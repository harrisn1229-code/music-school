# 26 Songs That Only Use Two Chords

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zpURr5COwI4)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zpURr5COwI4)

## Synopsis

A functional-harmony deep dive using the minimal case (two-chord songs) to isolate exactly what tonic, dominant, and subdominant function each contribute — because with only two chords, every bit of a song's harmonic tension and release has to come from those two chords alone, the underlying mechanism is unusually easy to hear in isolation.

## Techniques

- **I–V as the strongest possible two-chord pairing, and why**: tonic (I) is a point of full rest; dominant (V) creates strong pull specifically because it contains the leading tone (a semitone below the root, "desperately" wanting to resolve up) — especially pronounced when V carries a dominant 7th. Two chords are enough for a complete tension/release cycle because the two functions are maximally contrasted.
- **I–IV as a "softer" two-chord pairing, and the precise functional difference from I–V, stated as a matter of degree, not kind**: IV has subdominant function — it still wants to resolve to I, but "recommends" rather than "demands" it, producing a more relaxed/mellow feel than the more urgent I–V. **I–ii is functionally EQUIVALENT to I–IV**, not a third distinct pairing — ii and IV share the same subdominant function (explained by their proximity: ii and IV are only one note apart, e.g. Dm vs. F major sharing two of three notes) — two flavors of the same underlying progression, not two different devices.
- **A minor V weakens dominant function specifically because it flattens the leading tone**, dampening (not eliminating) its pull to resolve upward — the result functions more like a second subdominant chord than a true dominant. Worked example: "Falling" (Alicia Keys) is built on I–v (minor five) rather than I–V, producing a gentler, less urgent resolution pull despite the same root movement as a "real" I–V song.
- **A philosophical point on what counts as "a chord," worth keeping as a general framing for any progression analysis**: extensions/alterations to a chord (C→C6→C7→Cadd9) are argued to be flavor variations on ONE chord, not separate chords, as long as the underlying function (here, tonic) doesn't change — "different shades of the same color." Applied directly: "You Never Can Tell" (Chuck Berry) is called a two-chord song (C, G7) even though the piano part actually alternates C and C6, because C6 doesn't change the chord's tonic function. Two counter-examples that DON'T qualify as pure two-chord songs despite reputation: "Dreams" (Fleetwood Mac) resolves to a vi chord (Am) later in the song, breaking the pure IV-V pattern; "Tomorrow Never Knows" (Beatles) drones on C throughout but superimposes a B♭ chord (reading as a C11 rather than a true second chord) at the end of each verse — presented as a genuinely debatable edge case for where "chord" stops meaning "root-position triad" and starts meaning "any added color."
