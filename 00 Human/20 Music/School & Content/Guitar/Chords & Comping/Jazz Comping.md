# Jazz Comping

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Chord Voicings]], the rhythm/accompaniment side. Comping = accompanying, not soloing — the goal on every idea below is something solid and cohesive that supports whatever else is happening, not the flashiest thing playable in the moment. No home existed yet for comping-specific technique (as opposed to voicing vocabulary, which lives on [[Chord Voicings]]) — created from three concrete comping moves surfaced across [[BODY AND SOUL Guitar Lesson (chords, tab, scales, melody) No. 18 of Top 25 Jazz Standards for Guitar|Body and Soul]] and [[SOMEDAY MY PRINCE WILL COME Guitar Lesson (tab, chords, scales) Top Jazz Standards for Guitar No. 17|Someday My Prince Will Come]].

- **Five named comping rhythms, isolated on one static chord before being taken through the changes — "rhythm is much more important than the notes."** Practice method for all five: drill the rhythm alone on a single shell voicing first (metronome on 2-and-4), THEN move it through a full progression with real (drop-2) voicings. **1. Charleston** — hit on beat 1, then the "and" of 2; the foundational reference groove, but static ("just sits there"). **2. Push/anticipation** — chord on beat 1, a longer sustained chord on the "and" of 3 that pulls forward into the next bar's change; creates the forward momentum Charleston lacks. **3. Downbeat-anchored, soloist-friendly** — mostly straight downbeats tied together by a simple 2-note melody (the melody makes the rhythm easier to internalize); deliberately unobtrusive, safe behind a soloist since it avoids syncopation. **4. Off-the-one** — hits on the "and" of 1 and on beat 3, skipping beat 1 entirely — an entry point into offbeat comping generally (namechecks Red Garland's further extreme: anticipating the *next* bar's harmony on the "and" of 2 and "and" of 4). **5. Response** — hits on beat 2, beat 3, and the "and" of 3, built as a call-and-response reply to a soloist phrase resolving on the "and" of 4 or beat 1; explicit caution against over-elaborating a response rhythm — comping supports the soloist, doesn't compete with them ("an easy way to get fired"). → [[Chord Voicings]] — Jens Larsen, [[5 Comping Exercises for Jazz Rhythm on the Blues]]
- **Voicing-multiplication by top note — the single highest-leverage comping fix for feeling "stuck" on one chord.** For any static chord, find 3–4 different inversions whose only meaningful difference is *which chord tone sits on top* (root, 5th, 3rd, ♭7/shell top) — the listener perceives a static chord largely through its top note, so moving from 1 voicing to 2 "doubles the value," to 3 "triples the value," with almost no new theory required. **Modal comping over a static minor vamp:** a lead-sheet "Cm11" over a modal tune is shorthand for quartal/modal harmony rooted in Dorian, not a fixed chord — so any diatonic chord from the parent major scale can substitute over the same vamp and still work (worked moving up a whole step, e.g. to the tune's Dm-family voicings). **Turnaround vocabulary chunking:** rather than reharmonizing a 4-chord turnaround chord-by-chord live, memorize the whole string as one unit, then build 2–4 total alternate "strings" (changing mainly the top note across all four chords) and switch between complete strings — the realistic path to using new vocabulary on a real gig. **A function-agnostic half-step approach-chord trick:** insert a chord a half-step above OR below any target chord as a quick passing/crunch color — works purely because it resolves by half-step, independent of naming its "correct" function — a simpler sibling of this page's tritone-substitution walking-dominant bullet above, usable before formalizing it into a named substitution. **Right-hand articulation as an independent variety axis:** the same voicing string played as a groove pattern vs. a straight strum vs. arpeggiated changes the perceived sound without changing a single fretted note — combined with 3–4 voicings, multiplies into many usable variations from a small practice investment. → [[Quartal Chord Voicings]], [[Chord Substitution]] § *Tritone substitution* — Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared, [[Private Jazz Guitar Comping Lesson Over Footprints by Wayne Shorter]]
- **Walking half-step-above dominant chords into any change, as pure decoration — no theory required to use it.** A dominant 7th a half-step above ANY target chord can resolve down into it (it's the [[Chord Substitution|tritone sub]] of that target's real V), so you can insert one — as a full chord or just its root note — in front of any chord in any tune, even where nothing is written, purely because it sounds good. Worked live across a tune's first four bars: a half-step-above walking chord dropped in before every single target chord change. Usable in comping (adds harmonic embellishment and density "players love") or in lead lines (superimposed as a passing color even if no one else in the band is playing it). Density is a taste call — sparse-and-occasional or constant-walking-chords-throughout both work; the exercise is to drill it enough that you can dial it either way on purpose. → [[Chord Substitution]] § *Tritone substitution* — Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared, [[BODY AND SOUL Guitar Lesson (chords, tab, scales, melody) No. 18 of Top 25 Jazz Standards for Guitar]]
- **A reliable jazz-waltz (3/4) comping backdrop: bass note alone on beat 1, chord stabs on the "and" of beat 2 and on beat 3 — "1, and-of-2, 3."** Offered as a genuine fallback, not just a starting point: "if you only do this the whole time, it's still going to sound great." One named variation — play the bass note and chord together on beat 1, then the chord alone twice more (on beats 2 and 3) instead of the syncopated "and" placement. Solves a specific, common problem: most working jazz vocabulary lives in 4/4, so 3/4 can throw a player off if it isn't drilled on its own with a dedicated go-to pattern to return to. → [[Groove and Microtiming]] — Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared, [[SOMEDAY MY PRINCE WILL COME Guitar Lesson (tab, chords, scales) Top Jazz Standards for Guitar No. 17]]
- **Comp a dominant-7♭9♭13 with a half-diminished shape built on its ♭7 — one fretboard-study target, two usable voicing vocabularies.** F half-diminished (F–A♭–C♭–E♭) reduces exactly to ♭7-♭9-♭13-3 of G7 — every note in the shape is a legitimate G7♭9♭13 tone — so any inversion of an F half-diminished grip already known from elsewhere on the neck can be dropped straight onto a G7♭9♭13 without modification. Practical comping payoff: investing fretboard time in half-diminished inversions (valuable on their own, as the ii of a minor ii–V–i) pays double duty as rootless altered-dominant comping vocabulary a major 3rd below the half-diminished's root. → [[Chord Voicings]] § *From videos*, [[Diminished Chords]] § *From videos* — Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared, [[SOMEDAY MY PRINCE WILL COME Guitar Lesson (tab, chords, scales) Top Jazz Standards for Guitar No. 17]]
