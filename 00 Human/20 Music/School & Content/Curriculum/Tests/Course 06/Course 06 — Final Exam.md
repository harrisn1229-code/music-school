> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **How fast the chords change, independent of tempo — loop production wants it slow**
> Slow means one or two chords spread across several bars.
>
> **Q2.** **The harmonic rhythm went from slow to fast — the chorus now demands attention on the changes**
> Tempo didn't move, so the difference is entirely in the rate of chord change. Used deliberately that's a real lift into a chorus; used by accident over a repeating loop it's exhausting.
>
> **Q3.** **Rhythm · melody and topline · timbre · arrangement**
> Arrangement means scheduled absence as much as addition — dropping an element moves a track as much as adding one.
>
> **Q4.** **Repetition is the feature; staleness comes from nothing above the chords changing**
> *Rubric (5):* says predictability is what frees the listener's attention for the topline and groove (2) · says staleness is caused by the layers above the harmony being static, not by the progression (2) · gives a concrete diagnosis, such as listing everything that varies across eight bars other than the chords (1)
> The two claims only look contradictory if you assume the progression is supposed to be carrying the section. It isn't — its job is to become a bed the ear stops tracking after about two passes, and it does that job better the more predictable it is. What goes stale is everything that was supposed to move once the harmony stopped: the drums that never vary, the pad that never opens, the vocal that sits in one register. So the diagnosis is a list, not a rewrite. Play eight bars, write down every element that differs at the end from the start, and fix the emptiness in that list. Rewriting the chords when the list is empty just buys a loop that goes stale in a different key.
>
> **Q5.** **A sustained bass note held while the chords above it change — the tonic or the dominant**
>
> **Q6.** **The dominant is held and the drop is the resolution**
> Sitting on V and refusing to resolve is what builds the tension; the drop pays it off. The device is about four centuries older than the genre.
>
> **Q7.** **`C – G/B – Am – F` — the bass descends C–B–A–F while the chords stay I–V–vi–IV**
> Nothing above the bass has been rewritten. All the movement is in the bottom voice.
>
> **Q8.** **Because the ear takes the lowest note as the root**
> Changing the bass changes the perceived chord even when the notes above it are static, which makes it the cheapest way to get four chords to feel like eight.
>
> **Q9.** **Reharmonize the last bar · insert a secondary dominant · move the bass — the first is highest-leverage**
> Reharmonizing the last bar changes where every pass lands, so it reshapes the whole cycle rather than one moment in it.
>
> **Q10.** **Put E7 in front of it — the secondary dominant adds a leading tone and a tritone pulling into Am**
> *Also accepted:* E major · V/vi · the secondary dominant of the vi
>
> **Q11.** **The 3rd — the note that decides major or minor**
> Removing it stops the pad competing with the topline over the chord's identity, which matters more here than anywhere else because the same voicing is heard dozens of times.
>
> **Q12.** **The 9th isn't in the triad, so it colours without redefining anything**
> *Rubric (5):* says the 9th sits outside the triad, so it can't change the chord's quality or function (2) · says it therefore adds colour rather than redefining the harmony (1) · says the root and 3rd are the notes that state the chord, so landing on them reads as settled while the 9th reads as open (2)
> Function is carried by the root and the 3rd — one says which chord, the other says what kind. A topline landing on either of those is agreeing with the harmony and the ear hears arrival. The 9th agrees with nothing in particular: it's consonant enough not to fight, and outside the triad enough that the chord underneath is unchanged by it. What you get is a note that sounds like it could still go somewhere, which is exactly why toplines on the 9th read as more emotive and less conclusive.
>
> **Q13.** **I–V–vi–IV · vi–IV–I–V · IV–I–V–vi · V–vi–IV–I — only which chord is heard as home**
> The pitch content is identical across all four. Whichever chord the loop starts on and returns to becomes the tonic.
>
> **Q14.** **All three functions present · IV–I plagal at the loop point · the chords share notes**
> I and vi are tonic, IV is subdominant, V is dominant, so the full cycle fits in four chords. The plagal seam closes gently enough to repeat, and the shared notes give smooth voice leading around the loop.
>
> **Q15.** **Relative major and minor — no pivot chord, no new material, no modulation machinery**
> The relative minor of C major is A minor. They share a key signature, so the same seven notes are available and only the resolution target moves.
>
> **Q16.** **Start the same loop on the vi and end it there**
> Same four chords, rotated. If the feel is right you're finished and nothing was written.
>
> **Q17.** **Mixolydian — major with a ♭7 — or borrowed from the parallel minor**
> Both readings are valid and produce the same chord.
>
> **Q18.** **No leading tone, so nothing pulls up into the tonic**
> Motion without the dominant's gravity is what the word "modal" is describing.
>
> **Q19.** **The borrowed ♭VII and descending chromatic bass under a held chord**
> *Also accepted:* the line cliché as the second device · "Michelle" or "Something" named as examples
>
> **Q20.** **B natural against B♭ — Dorian versus natural minor, and Dorian lifts where Aeolian stays dark**
> The raised 6th is what makes the chord on the 4th degree major, and that major IV under a minor tonic is the whole colour difference.
>
> **Q21.** **♭VI–♭VII–i, which in A minor is F, G and Am — the bed under a lot of trap**
> *Also accepted:* metal · film scoring · drill
>
> **Q22.** **The characteristic note is missing or the parent's dominant is dragging the ear home**
> *Rubric (5):* diagnoses either an absent characteristic note or a functional dominant pulling back to the parent key (2) · says the characteristic note must be emphasized, since without it the mode is inaudible (1) · says the chord that note produces must actually appear, and the parent's V must be avoided (2)
> A mode isn't a set of notes, it's a set of notes plus a tonal centre the harmony keeps confirming — and the same seven notes will confirm seven different centres depending on what the chords do. If the vamp keeps resolving toward the parent major, the parent major is what you'll hear no matter which scale the melody came from. Fixing it is harmonic, not scalewise: make the ♮6 audible, make sure the major IV it produces is actually in the loop, and take out anything that functions as the parent's dominant. Think of it as D minor with a raised 6th rather than as the white notes starting from D, and the writing tends to fix itself.
>
> **Q23.** **A chromatic mediant — a 3rd away with a quality the key doesn't provide**
> In C those are E, A, E♭ and A♭ major. They don't sound random because each shares a common tone with the tonic — one note holds while everything else shifts — and because they have no dominant pull, they arrive rather than resolve. That reads as a lift or a scene change, which is exactly what bar 4 was asking for.
>
> **Q24.** **Truck driver if the lift should be heard · modal shift or its own V if it shouldn't**
> The truck driver's modulation is the blunt unprepared step up; the subtler pair are shifting the mode instead of the key, and approaching the new tonic through its own dominant.
> *Rubric (5):* describes the truck driver as an unprepared step up that reliably raises energy and openly announces itself (2) · names two subtler options from modal shift, approaching the new tonic through its own dominant, or pivoting on a shared chord (2) · says the choice is decided by how much the lift should be noticed (1)
> All three raise the last chorus; they differ in how visible the seam is. The truck driver's modulation shoves everything up a step with no preparation, which is why it works and why it reads as a gimmick — nothing hides it. A modal shift keeps the tonic and changes the collection, so the colour changes dramatically while the ground stays put and no key change is really happening. Approaching the new tonic through its own dominant costs one chord and makes the arrival sound intended rather than sudden. What decides is not which is more sophisticated but whether the song wants the lift to be heard as an event. A stadium-final chorus can afford to announce itself; a track built on subtlety cannot, and there the pivot or the modal shift is the honest answer.
