## What's taught

### 1. Slap is optional — not a prerequisite for being a real bassist
Thundercat built one of the most recognizable bass identities in modern music with almost no slap technique, reportedly under direct pressure not to (Chris Dave's snare-drum threat, one Instagram-post exception, and an earlier Suicidal Tendencies stint where he slapped "under the radar"). BassBuzz's own framing: most music outside a specific handful of slap-heavy artists doesn't use the technique at all, so treating slap as mandatory vocabulary for a "well-rounded bassist" is a myth — it's a stylistic choice, not a floor. This is a narrative/motivational point, not a technique with settings to extract, so it isn't routed into the slap-technique notes ([[Ghost Notes (Slap Bass)]], [[Slap Thumb Technique — Angle, Anchoring, and Muting]], [[Teeter-Totter Slap-Pop Technique]], [[Melodic Slap Lines — Avoiding Disco Octaves]]) — no new slap mechanic is actually taught here, just the choice *not* to use one.

### 2. Bass chords — three hacks that make them work
Full mechanism, worked examples, and settings routed to **[[Bass Chords and Double Stops]]** (a new "Thundercat's three hacks" section added this pass, since the existing note's core mechanic — register — is exactly hack #1's underlying logic, extended here with two genuinely new mechanisms: extra strings, and a live doubling effect):
1. **Design the song around the bass chords, don't retrofit them.** Because Thundercat is his own lead singer/songwriter, he can build the arrangement around a chord-heavy bass part from the start; fitting the same chord explosion into an already-arranged song (existing drums/guitar/synths) would just be mud, however cool it sounds isolated.
2. **The sixth string buys chord-tone spacing a 4-string can't reach.** A standard 4-string forces 2–3 note voicings to avoid mud in one hand position; Thundercat's signature 6-string lets a 4-note chord spread out more, avoiding the mud a cramped 4-string voicing of the same chord would produce (worked example: the "Tron Song" chord, written for his cat Turbotron).
3. **A live octave-up effect doubles the chord an octave higher**, pulling it out of bass register into guitar register so the ear can actually parse what's happening in the chord instead of it reading as a blur.

### 3. Playing fast — three trainable habits, not raw talent
Explicit deliberate-practice framing: BassBuzz is direct that there's no shortcut ("no amount of BassBuzz videos" substitutes for years of practice), but names three concrete levers that get you closer, all new to the vault's bass-technique layer and captured in a new note, **[[Playing Fast on Bass — Efficient Technique, Setup, and Bridge Plucking]]**:
1. **Relaxed, efficient fretting-hand technique** — minimize motion to reach each note rather than reaching with big, loose movements.
2. **Low-to-medium action at setup** — strings closer to the fingerboard mean smaller, more efficient fretting movements and less resistance to plucking.
3. **Pluck near the bridge** — less string give there means the plucking fingers do less work per note, which raises the achievable speed ceiling.

### 4. Restraint on demand — the "shred vs. bed" switch
Thundercat can shred (Jaco Pastorius/Stanley Clarke lineage, "Friend Zone" as the worked example of simple-then-nuts within one song) but chooses simple, supportive playing when a song calls for it — the Mac Miller collab "What's the Use" is the worked example of near-verbatim riff repetition in service of the song rather than showcasing. Two concrete, trainable steps given for making that switch on purpose:
1. **Play fewer notes by listening first** — deliberately listen to bandmates more than to yourself; framed as a trainable habit, not an innate trait.
2. **Use repetition to push the bass into the background** — Silk Sonic's "After Last Night" is the worked example: a simple, heavily repeated riff with almost no fills keeps listener attention on the vocal, and repetition also makes an eventual fill (which Thundercat does deliver at that song's end) land harder by contrast.

### 5. Thinking about bass melodically by singing first
Two-part mechanism for why Thundercat's simple lines still read as melodic rather than merely functional:
1. **Sing/hum the idea before playing it** — even just internally. BassBuzz's own before/after demo: aimless finger-wiggling inside a scale vs. humming an idea to yourself first, then playing that — described as an obviously more singable, catchy result. Sometimes the bass line literally copies the vocal melody outright (worked example: "Captain Stupido").
2. **Call and response with the lead vocal** — trading the "call" (sung line) for the "response" (bass fill) in the gaps, the same blues-tradition device named directly via B.B. King as the reference point. Singing while playing forces this naturally, since it's too hard to fill *while* singing, so a bassist has to learn to wait for the gap.

### 6. Tone-shapeshifting via effects pedals
Full mechanism, worked examples, and gear-tier starting points routed to **[[Bass Tone Chain Priority]]** (a new worked example added to its existing Link 7 — Pedals section) and **[[Sound Selection Guide]]** (Bass section, a new sourced entry):
- **Effects give the bass an artificial/processed character** that matches surrounding synths rather than sitting as an obviously "real" instrument.
- **Effects let multiple simultaneous bass tracks coexist without clashing** — normally a bad idea (competing low-end lines read as mud), but distinct effect chains give each layer its own identifiable sound. Worked example: "Oh Sheit It's X" (Apocalypse album) reportedly layers at least four bass tracks, each with a distinct effect treatment; BassBuzz demonstrates the failure mode directly by recreating the same passage with all four tracks sharing one plain tone, and it collapses into mud.
- **An envelope filter gives the bass a "wow wow," talking-voice character** — named directly as the source of the "It Is What It Is" tone, tied back to the video's singing/vocal-thinking theme (an effect that makes the instrument itself sound more like a voice).
- **Cheap starting gear, named directly:** a budget multi-effects unit as the lowest-friction entry point (BassBuzz's own long-term unit, a Boss ME-50B); the BassBuzz forum's own favorite budget option, old Zoom multi-effects units; free DAW effects plugins as a no-hardware option.
