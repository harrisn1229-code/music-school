# 3 Picking Mistakes That Are Slowing You Down!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CCCd7b3cGuA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CCCd7b3cGuA)

## Synopsis

Three subtler alternate-picking speed limiters found through personal practice and recurring student questions: syncing hand *intensity* (not just timing), a picking-angle inconsistency across strings caused by an anchored wrist, and picking motions that are simply too large to speed up further.

## Techniques

- **Decouple motion intensity between the hands — sync timing, not tension.** A real but under-discussed realization: players correctly sync picking-hand *timing* to the fretting hand (every stroke lands on the correct note), but often also unconsciously sync motion *intensity/tension* between both hands — digging in harder with the pick at speed causes the fretting hand to cramp up too, even though the fretting hand doesn't actually need more force to keep pace. Fix: deliberately keep the fretting hand relaxed even while picking harder/faster. Practiced with 4-notes-per-string 16th-note licks inside a daily 15-minute 200+ BPM alternate-picking speed block.
- **String-to-string picking-angle inconsistency, caused by an anchored wrist plus an arcing motion.** Diagnosed from a recurring pattern in student messages: some players find high strings easy and low strings fall apart, others report the exact opposite. Root cause: when the wrist stays anchored on the bridge and the hand simply arcs across the strings, the picking angle can end up good (low resistance) on one end of the neck and nearly parallel to the strings (high resistance, hard to speed up) on the other — an effect players often don't notice they're doing. **Fix: move the picking hand along as you transition between strings** (borrowing the same principle sweep picking uses) to keep a consistent angle on every string, rather than anchoring the wrist and letting an arc change the angle string to string. Anchoring is fine for material confined to 2-3 strings — the fix matters specifically for long scale runs crossing all six strings.
- **Picking motion size, with a vocal-technique analogy.** Just as beginner singers tend to sing louder when reaching for higher pitches, guitarists tend to pick with a bigger, more aggressive motion when trying to play faster — but a big motion is inherently harder to speed up and far more fatiguing over a full take, working directly against the goal. Daily finisher drill: at the end of a speed-training block, deliberately minimize picking-motion size while staying fast, worked across all strings up to the 24th fret specifically for endurance. Reported result: noticeably better endurance, control, and cleaner sound (less unwanted string noise) once the motion size dropped.
