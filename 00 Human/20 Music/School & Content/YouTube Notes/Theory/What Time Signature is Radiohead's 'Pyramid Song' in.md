# What Time Signature is Radiohead's 'Pyramid Song' in?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MdZSOoOF5Ms)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MdZSOoOF5Ms)

## Synopsis

A viewer-requested deep dive answering a genuinely counterintuitive question: the sheet music says 4/4, but the song feels wildly irregular. Works out that the "odd" feeling comes from three stacked, independent devices — a clave-like fixed accent pattern, chord changes landing on a shifting/inconsistent beat within that pattern, and swing feel — none of which actually require an unusual time signature to notate, and argues 4/4 is in fact the clearest notation choice available.

## Techniques

- **The rhythm itself, clave-style**: a symmetrical 2-bar repeating pattern — two dotted crotchets, a minim, two more dotted crotchets (in quavers: 3-3-4-3-3). Functions like a **clave** (the Latin American technique of a short 1-2 bar rhythm repeated as the piece's structural glue) even though the song isn't Latin — flagged as likely coincidental, but notable that the pattern is identical to a slowed-down bossa nova rhythm.
- **The real source of the "disorientating" feel — inconsistent chord-change placement over a consistent rhythm**: the underlying 2-bar rhythmic pattern repeats identically throughout, but WHERE the chord changes fall within that pattern keeps shifting from repetition to repetition. A beat where the chord changes reads as more rhythmically accented than a beat where it stays the same — so even though the skeleton rhythm never varies, the perceived accent pattern keeps changing because the harmony is landing in different spots each time.
- **Why 4/4 is actually the clearest notation choice, not a cop-out**: any rhythm can technically be written with no time signature/bar lines at all (all rhythmic information already lives in the note durations) — a time signature exists purely to make the music easier to read, not because the music requires one. An alternative notation alternating 3/8 and 4/8 would track the actual accent pattern more literally, but would obscure the fact that the underlying 2-bar pattern repeats consistently, and would require constantly-changing time signatures throughout the score. 4/4 doesn't perfectly align with every accent, but it makes the repeating 2-bar structure visually obvious — the actual communication goal of notation.
- **Swing, the third stacked layer**: Pyramid Song's eighth notes are swung (unequal long-short pairing) rather than straight — demonstrated by playing the song against a swung vs. a straightened metronome. Traced to a direct, named influence: Thom Yorke stated in a June 2001 Mojo interview that the song was him "totally obsessed by a Charlie Mingus song called Freedom" and trying to duplicate it — an early demo reportedly even included handclaps lifted from "Freedom" before being cut for sounding "naff."
- **Overall takeaway**: the song is genuinely just 4/4 with a swung, clave-like repeating accent pattern whose harmony keeps landing in a different spot each time — a case study in how "sounds rhythmically impossible" and "is actually simple on paper" can both be true depending on which layer you're listening for.
