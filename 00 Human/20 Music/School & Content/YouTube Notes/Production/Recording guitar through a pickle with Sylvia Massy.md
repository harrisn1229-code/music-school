# Recording guitar through a pickle with Sylvia Massy

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FpEJXHvaqJ0)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FpEJXHvaqJ0)

## Synopsis

A short Mix with the Masters studio demo: producer/engineer Sylvia Massy, tracking guitar overdubs with a guitarist referred to as "Buzz" (his own passing reference to a Melvins song tuned to low C strongly suggests Buzz Osborne of the Melvins), rewires the SPEAKER OUTPUT stage of a guitar rig — after the power amp, before the actual monitoring speaker — to run through an unconventional passive resistive load: first a pair of dill pickles, then an incandescent light bulb. The premise is electrical rather than digital: dill pickles are salty/conductive enough (high sodium content) that a strong enough signal driven through them can make them glow like a light bulb (Massy references having seen a pickle plugged straight into household AC light up), and passing a guitar/bass signal through that variable, imperfect conductive medium colors the tone in a way no plugin recreates. The value here isn't a specific setting — it's the underlying idea (**any conductive found object can be an insert point in a speaker-output signal path**) plus the concrete rig used to do it safely (splitter → preamp stage → power amp → resistive load in the speaker cable → power soak (attenuator) → isolated return speaker). The pickles didn't visibly light up on camera, but the feedback tone through them was kept as a usable take; the light bulb variant, tried afterward in the same signal position, produced a result both Massy and Buzz liked enough to record and double a full guitar solo through it ("that's a nice distortion... pretty freaking awesome sound").

## Techniques

- **The core idea: insert a passive conductive "found object" directly into the speaker-output signal path — after the power amp, before the final return speaker — as a hardware distortion/coloration stage with no plugin equivalent.** The character comes from a genuinely nonlinear, variable-resistance physical medium (a vegetable, a lamp filament) reacting to real current, not from digital modeling. Framed explicitly as a way to "enhance the sound of the feedback" on a guitar overdub — a texture move, not a corrective one. → [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[TEXTURE Slot]], [[LEAD Slot]]
- **Full signal chain used (the actual rig, useful even without owning the specific units):** guitar → a signal splitter → split 2 into the front input of a **GK (Gallien-Krueger) bass amp, used purely as a preamp** (not through its own speaker) → output into a **Crest power amplifier** → the Crest's **speaker output** routed through the resistive load (pickles, later the light bulb) → the return of that speaker cable goes into a **power soak** (a load box/attenuator) → power soak output feeds the actual monitoring **speaker, isolated in another room**. The power soak is the key enabling piece: it lets the engineer turn the *monitoring* speaker's volume down independently of how much signal/energy is being driven through the resistive load — "we're putting all the energy into the pickles" while the room itself doesn't get blasted. No owned equivalent for the GK/Crest/power-soak rig is tracked in this vault (Gear index has no bass-amp-as-preamp, standalone power amp, or attenuator/load-box entry) — logged as the general lesson rather than mapped to owned gear.
- **Safety practice: unplug the amp rig from the wall entirely before cutting/rewiring the speaker cable, and verify polarity before reconnecting.** The speaker cable was cut in half and the leads separated; the ridged conductor was identified as positive by feel before either pickle was wired in ("that has the ridge on it, so this is the positive side"). Basic discipline worth carrying into any DIY hardware-modification tracking trick.
- **Object choice — dill pickles, specifically for their sodium content.** Reasoning stated directly: "there's a lot of sodium in these, and the sodium actually will light up" — Massy references pickles lighting up like a light bulb when plugged straight into household AC (120V/60Hz) as the basis for expecting the same from a strong guitar/bass signal. Two pickles were wired in — one on the positive lead, one on the negative — roughly matched for size ("I don't think it really matters, but what the heck").
- **Lower frequencies drove the effect harder than higher guitar notes — cited directly in the session ("the lower frequencies work better").** On guitar, a strong sustained feedback squeal (not a picked note) was the target; opening the studio door was used to help sustain feedback longer by giving the amp more room to couple back into the strings. *(qualitative — no numeric threshold given for what counts as "low enough")*
- **Guitar-through-pickles result: no visible light-up, but a usable, good-sounding feedback tone was kept as a take** — "they're not lighting up, but it sounds pretty good... why don't we just record it going through the pickles." The visual gimmick (lighting up) and the usable audio result are two separate outcomes; the take was worth keeping even when the light-up didn't happen.
- **Switched from guitar to BASS specifically to chase the light-up effect, reasoning from AC mains frequency: a low B is close to 60 Hz, the same frequency household AC runs at (the frequency that's known to make a plugged-in pickle glow).** Explicit exchange: "that is that 60 hertz is a low B — so should I tune down to a low B?... or as close as you can get to 60 hertz, because if you plug a pickle in the wall it'll light up like a light bulb." A passing reference that the Melvins tune even lower, to C, for comparison. *(This is a genuinely transferable idea beyond pickles: if a resonant/nonlinear physical object has a known-sensitive frequency, tune the source instrument toward that frequency rather than assuming any pitch will drive the effect equally.)* → [[Bass Design]], [[SUB Slot]]
- **Bass-through-pickles: a sustained, held note ("you gotta have a sustained note") got a visibly stronger reaction than a picked/plucked attack** — the take is treated as a success in the room (excited reactions, "look at that," someone — reportedly Buzz — eating a pickle afterward), though the transcript never explicitly confirms the pickles visibly lit up on this pass either; logged as ambiguous rather than a confirmed light-up.
- **Follow-up variation, same signal position: swap the pickle load for a plain incandescent light bulb.** Reasoning given for expecting it to work: "it should work though, because you can drive a drill with it" — i.e., the power amp has enough current capacity to spin a drill motor, so it should be more than sufficient to light (and drive signal through) a light bulb's filament. The different filament material was expected to color the tone differently from the pickles — stated as an open question going in ("the different filament material should be interesting sonically, I haven't really heard this"). → [[Saturation and Distortion]]
- **Light-bulb-through-guitar-solo: a full guitar solo was recorded with the light bulb inline in the speaker-output path, monitored through headphones and cued to the existing track — described as "a nice distortion" and, after playback, "pretty freaking awesome sound."** The take was kept ("save that on a playlist") and then **doubled** (recorded a second pass) through the same light-bulb signal path for a stacked lead layer — the same double-tracking instinct used on a normal amp tone, just applied to this experimental signal chain. *(no gain/volume settings given — purely a qualitative "it sounded good, do it again" result)* → [[Layering and Stacking]]
- **General transferable takeaway:** treat the speaker-output stage of any tube/solid-state rig (after the power amp, before the final speaker) as an insertable point for experimentation — a load box/power soak makes this safe to do at controlled, non-destructive volume, and any sufficiently conductive found object (produce, a lamp, presumably other household items) is fair game as a passive coloration stage with a character no plugin will replicate. Sylvia Massy is known for this kind of physical/found-object studio experimentation more broadly; this video documents one specific instance of it.
