# How to get SO GOOD at guitar it feels ILLEGAL (Using Neuroscience)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7SNcy_GprQU)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7SNcy_GprQU)

## Synopsis

A "how to practice" video rather than a technique/song lesson — Daniel Seriff (25,000+ lessons taught) argues that 10,000 hours of undirected repetition ("massed practice" — drilling one thing over and over until your brain checks out) doesn't work, and lays out a full replacement system built on named deliberate-practice/learning-science concepts, each tied back to a concrete guitar drill: interleaved practice (rotating between several short blocks instead of one long grind) run from a forward-planned practice journal, 15–20 minute session caps enforced with a physical kitchen timer, the "additive approach" (Anders Ericsson's chunking, worked on a chord progression one chord at a time), practicing hands separately (fretting-hand shape alone, then muted picking pattern alone, then air-strum plus fretting before finally playing it), going directly to the trouble spot instead of restarting from the top when something breaks down, deliberately practicing at the edge of ability (Daniel Coyle's "deep practice"/myelin framing), catching the "illusion of knowing" (recognizing a concept intellectually isn't the same as being able to play it cold), and building strong mental models/audiation (hearing, seeing, and singing a phrase before playing it). He name-checks several learning-science books throughout (*Make It Stick*, *Peak* by Anders Ericsson, *The Musician's Way* — misheard/garbled by auto-captions as "Jeroen Klijsen" but the described author-and-teacher bio matches **Gerald Klickstein**, whom Seriff also separately credits by name later for the hands-separate method, so this is very likely one person referred to two ways by the transcript — *The Talent Code* by Daniel Coyle, *The Art of Learning*, *The Inner Game of Music*) as the intellectual scaffolding, but the video is dense with actual step-by-step drills, not just narrative — this page and the routed notes below extract those drills concretely rather than just the neuroscience framing.

## What's taught

- **Massed vs. interleaved practice (~1:00–2:30).** Massed practice = repeating the same thing over and over trying to perfect it; feels productive (fingers moving, time being put in) but the brain checks out and you stop actually learning. **Interleaved practice** = rotate between different things within one session — instead of 20 minutes straight on one thing, do 2–3 minutes each on five different things, then cycle back through all five again next session. Sourced to *Make It Stick* and *Peak*. Mechanism given: the *cycling itself* forces **recall** — every time you come back to something you're quizzing yourself, and that retrieval attempt is what builds deeper learning than uninterrupted repetition would. Explicitly frames the discomfort ("it feels harder... feeds your ego a little less") as *where the learning is happening*, not a sign to switch back to something comfortable.
- **The practice journal — plan forward, not log backward (~2:30–4:15).** A plain notebook or doc is enough; the key discipline is that you write down **what you're going to work on before the session**, not what you did after. You'll typically work the same items again tomorrow and the day after to bring them to an adequate level, so the journal is a rolling plan, not a diary. Two payoffs of writing it down: (1) you show up with a plan instead of "picking around," which defaults you back to whatever's already comfortable; (2) you start noticing patterns — which things keep coming up as challenging, which you've been avoiding, which you thought you knew but keep getting wrong when you come back to them. **Five categories to rotate a journal/session across:** skills and technique, theory and ears, creative application, and tunes/tracks/licks/lines (Seriff names four explicitly and references "five different categories" — the transcript doesn't fully enumerate the fifth cleanly; treat as roughly technique / ear-training / theory / creative application / repertoire-tunes-licks).
- **Session length cap + hard timer (~4:15–5:15).** Practice in short chunks, not long unbroken stretches — **15 to 20 minutes is about the maximum** before focus drops off completely and you're "just spinning your wheels." Multiple shorter sessions across a day beat one long marathon every time. Seriff uses a **physical cheap kitchen timer** to enforce this, set to specific block lengths (he references 30 seconds, 1, 2, 3, and 5 minutes as block sizes he actually uses) — when it goes off, he moves on, no exceptions.
- **The additive approach — chunking (~5:15–7:15), credited to Anders Ericsson's *Peak*.** Worked example: a 4-chord progression (G, Em, D, C shown). Don't play through the whole thing first. (1) Play just the first chord alone, 3–5 times, with a pause between reps, until it's solid. (2) Add the second chord — G then D — still not playing through, just adding the new piece onto the solid first piece, pausing between reps. (3) Add the third chord (G, Em) the same way. (4) Add the final chord (G, Em, C) the same way, arriving at the full progression only once each piece was solid on its own. Generalizes to licks or any material, regardless of difficulty — build small chunks additively rather than attempting the whole thing and hoping repetition smooths it out.
- **Hands-separate practice (~7:15–9:30), credited to studying classical guitar with Gerald Klickstein.** For anything technically demanding: (1) work the **fretting-hand shape alone** until it feels solid. (2) **Mute the strings** and drill the **picking/strum pattern alone** (his worked example: down-down-up-up-down-up). (3) **Fret the chords with the left hand while miming the strum pattern in the air** with the right — combining the two without yet actually sounding the chord. Only after that does he actually play it. At each stage, aim for **3–5 clean repetitions** before moving to the next stage.
- **Target the trouble spot directly, don't restart from the top (~9:30–10:30).** Most players, when they mess up mid-passage, either restart from the beginning or glaze over the mistake — which means the opening of a piece gets drilled far more than the actual problem spot, and the trouble never actually gets fixed. Instead: **go straight to the specific chord/phrase that broke down** ("every time you hit the C chord, you botch it — go straight to the C") and isolate and drill *that*, not the whole passage around it.
- **Practice at the edge of your ability — "deep practice" (~11:00–13:00), credited to Daniel Coyle's *The Talent Code*.** If material feels fully comfortable and familiar, you are not practicing, you are just playing — real growth requires pushing tempos and material that are just slightly out of reach. Discomfort here isn't a problem, it's a signal that you're in the zone where improvement happens. Neuroscience framing given (qualitative, not verifiable from a caption transcript, but stated plainly): there's no literal "muscle memory" — what practice builds is neural pathways insulated in **myelin**, and practicing at the edge of capability is specifically what causes that myelination, which is why signals travel faster from brain to fingers and great players look effortless.
- **The "illusion of knowing" (~13:00–14:00).** Recognizing a concept intellectually ("oh yeah, I know that") is not the same as being able to actually play it. Real mastery = playing it on demand, cold, without hesitation, including outside comfortable/familiar contexts — if you can only play something under familiar conditions, it isn't mastered yet. Corollary: watching lesson videos alone won't make you better — you have to put hands on the guitar, sound bad, diagnose why, and fix it.
- **Mental models / audiation, away from the instrument (~14:00–15:30), cross-referenced to *Peak*, *The Art of Learning*, *The Inner Game of Music*.** The ability to hear music clearly in your head before playing it — visualize the fretboard, say note names, see triads/CAGED shapes — without touching the guitar. Concrete drills: **hum or sing a phrase before playing it** (relayed from a piano-session conversation: "if you can't hum it or sing it, you shouldn't be playing it"); **close your eyes and visualize the fingering before executing it**. Framed as the most underused practice method available, usable anywhere, anytime, away from the instrument entirely.
