## What's taught

- **Resynthesis — analyzing a sample into editable "frames," unlike ordinary sampling**: a sampled sound (demonstrated on a spoken vowel and a sung vocal note) is analyzed and broken into a sequence of short "frames" (~5-10 ms each; one worked example used 29 frames), each frame capturing that instant's harmonic content as its own mini-FM/wavetable patch. Frames can be stepped through individually and EDITED — attack time, pitch, harmonic content — something a raw audio sample doesn't allow ("it's like putty," able to be reshaped in the middle of the sound, not just at the edges). Frames can also crossfade into each other for smooth transitions, or cut directly frame-to-frame.
- **Because resynthesis reconstructs harmonic content rather than replaying audio, pitch-shifting a resynthesized frame does NOT change its playback speed** — unlike a conventional sample, which slows down as it's pitched down. A genuinely different sound-design behavior worth knowing when choosing between sampling and resynthesis for a part that needs to be played across a wide pitch range.
- **A resynthesized frame's harmonic content is directly hand-editable via the same coefficient buttons used for building FM/additive waveforms from scratch** — you can dial in a textbook sawtooth/square/triangle harmonic series on a resynthesized frame exactly as you would a synthesized one, since under the hood it's the same harmonic wavetable engine.
- **Voicing a resynthesized sample as 4 detuned/delayed copies of a single frame** — pulling ONE frame out of a longer vocal sample and quadrupling it with individual detune, delay, and vibrato per copy turns a single instant of a sample into a chorused, sustained pad-like texture.
- **Polyphonic aftertouch/pressure, borrowed hardware**: the Synclavier's velocity+polyphonic-pressure keybed (letting you apply pressure to one held key without affecting others — a CS-80-like expressive capability, again resurfacing on modern MPE-style keyboards) came from modified pressure sensors originally developed for a Sequential Prophet T8, integrated by an outside engineer (Jeff Tripp) rather than designed in-house — another example of Synclavier's habit of adopting/adapting third-party hardware (also true of its original monitor/computer, sourced from an Able 60) rather than building everything from scratch.
- **Random stereo panning per partial** as a built-in modulation option (alongside a synced sine-wave stereo sweep), independently assignable per partial/timbre — a quick way to spread a multi-partial patch across the stereo field without external panning automation.
- **Cost/scale context**: RAM cost roughly $450-500/megabyte at the time — a 10-32 MB sampling system could run into the hundreds of thousands of dollars, which is why fully-loaded Synclavier systems were genuinely elite-priced instruments rather than artificially marked up.
- **Modern product line, and the one thing finally added that never existed on the original**: Arturia Synclavier V (Cameron Jones' own reimplementation of the original code, not a third-party emulation) → Synclavier Go (iPad, touchscreen recreating the physical buttons) → Synclavier Regen (a modern hardware desktop unit with virtually unlimited voices, both FM and sample playback) — and Regen is the first Synclavier product to add **actual analog-style resonant filters**, something New England Digital had deliberately never included on the original hardware (see [[Synclavier Under the Hood Tour]]).
