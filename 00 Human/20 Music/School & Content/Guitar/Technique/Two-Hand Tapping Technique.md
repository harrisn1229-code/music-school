# Two-Hand Tapping Technique

Right-hand tapping used as a scale-extending and phrasing device, not just as a flashy solo trick — the fretting hand holds a familiar scale shape while the tapping hand adds notes the fretting hand isn't reaching, or deliberately clashes against it for tension.

## Core techniques

- **Combine two scales via hand split.** Fretting hand plays a familiar scale box; tapping hand adds the *other* scale's distinguishing notes. Worked example: A minor pentatonic box (5th fret, low E string) in the fretting hand, tapping hand adding B (major 2nd) and F♯ (major 6th, the note that makes it read as Dorian rather than Aeolian) — same underlying note collection as C major/A Aeolian, reframed as A Dorian by which notes get emphasized and which chord it resolves against. General lick-writing move: keep a scale shape you already know under the fretting hand, tap in a second scale's color notes rather than relearning a new fingering from scratch.
- **Match tapped-note articulation to the surrounding legato so the tap disappears into one continuous stream.** The goal — and what marks an advanced player — is that a listener can't identify which notes were tapped versus hammered/pulled. Achieved by matching note spacing/timing and attack between the tapped note and the surrounding hammer-ons/pull-offs, not by any special tapping-specific technique.
- **Vary rhythmic phrasing between ascending and descending passes of the same lick** — e.g. a tap-then-pull-off grouping one direction, a different rhythmic grouping the other — adds interest without changing which notes are played.
- **Deliberately let tapped notes overlap for dissonance.** Tap a minor 2nd or major 7th interval (a semitone apart) against a sustained note and let both ring together rather than muting one — a real, usable tension effect, not a mistake to avoid.
- **Double tapping**: tap a note, pull off, hammer the same note back on, repeat rapidly for a fast even stream. Main risk is unwanted ringing from neighboring strings — practice on an outer string (low or high E) first, where there's only one neighbor to control, before moving to inner strings.

- **A tapping harmonic, plus multi-finger (middle + ring) tapping to extend single-string note count beyond fretting-hand reach.** Instead of tapping down on the fretboard, lightly *touch* the string exactly one octave above a fretted note without pressing it down — produces a harmonic rather than a fretted pitch, a distinct sound and technique from a normal tap.
