# Multifunctional Voicings

Part of [[Jazz Harmony]] — a reharmonization concept sitting alongside [[Chord Voicings]]'s "one physical shape, several harmonic identities" rootless-voicing principle and [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]'s "one shape, eight identities," but framed here as a *deliberate comping strategy* rather than an analytical curiosity: **one memorized voicing, played off different chord tones of a target chord, does the work of several separate shapes.** Nathan Borton's name for a device he draws from Barry Galbraith's *Comping Etudes* — a book of comping-focused jazz-guitar etudes (with audio recordings featuring Galbraith and bassist Milt Hinton) built around exactly this kind of practical, playable vocabulary rather than abstract theory. The payoff is efficiency: fewer shapes to memorize, more harmonic mileage from each one — "it's not about memorizing endless chord shapes, but rather understanding how many different chords you can imply with just one versatile shape."

## The core mechanism
Take a single chord-voicing shape (any of the standard families — min7♭5, half-diminished, maj7♯5, dominant 9 — see [[Chord Voicings]]) and play it starting from a **different chord tone of the target chord** each time. The shape's fingering never changes; only which note you're mentally treating as the root shifts — same underlying logic as [[Chord Voicings]]'s rootless-voicing reinterpretation and [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]'s root-reassignment table, but the *destination* here is always "which dominant/target chord does this shape become," not just "which chord symbol could this be."

## Three worked shapes, three functions each

**1. A minor7♭5 shape → three separate roles**, from Galbraith's *Tangerine* etude (a ii-V-I comping line):
- Played off its own root → a plain **minor 7♭5** (e.g. F m7♭5).
- Played off the **♭7 of a dominant chord** → a **highly altered voicing for that dominant** (worked example: the F m7♭5 shape used under a G7, since F is G7's ♭7 — reads as G7alt).
- Played off the **3rd of a dominant chord** → a **dominant 9 voicing** *(general starting point — stated as a recurring move throughout the book, not tied to one specific root in the source)*.

**2. A half-diminished shape → root, then altered-dominant ♭7 role, resolving to I:**
- Played off its own root → a plain half-diminished chord.
- Played off the **♭7 of the dominant** → an altered voicing for that dominant (worked example: played from A♭, the ♭7 of B♭7, to produce a B♭7-altered sound) — then resolves to the I chord. This is the same "shape reused off the ♭7 of the V chord" move as shape 1's second function, confirming it as a repeatable rule for any min7♭5/half-diminished-family shape, not a one-off.

**3. An A♭ major7♯5 shape → root role, then altered-dominant 3rd role:**
- Played off its own root → a plain **maj7♯5** chord.
- Played off the **3rd of a dominant chord** → another altered-dominant color (worked in a 2-5-1 line: the same shape used off the 3rd of the V7 to color it altered before resolving).

## A fourth device: reusing a dominant-9 shape at its tritone substitute root
From Galbraith's minor-blues etude: a **familiar dominant 9 shape** (the kind you'd normally finger as, e.g., a B♭ dominant 9) is instead played **rooted a tritone away**, functioning as an **altered voicing of the "real" dominant** — here a B♭ dominant-9 grip used to voice an **E7♯9♯5**, because B♭ is E7's tritone substitute (same guide tones, shared altered tensions — see [[Chord Substitution]] for the underlying tritone-sub theory). This is the same "one shape, reused at a different root, new function" logic as the three shapes above, but the destination root is chosen via tritone substitution specifically rather than "the same chord's own chord tone."

## Worked 2-5-1 lines that chain these together
Three full comping lines from the book layer this device on top of [[Jazz Comping — Rhythmic Motif and Walking Bassline]]'s Charleston rhythm (see that note's "Charleston off different beats" addition, same video):
1. A **Charleston starting on beat 3**, D7 voiced via the half-diminished-off-the-♭7 trick (shape rooted a half step below D, i.e. off C — the ♭7 of D7 — to sound altered), resolving to a **quartal voicing off G major built off the 3rd** (→ [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]).
2. A **5-1 line mixing Charleston with quarter and half notes**: the dominant chord first voiced with the half-diminished-off-♭7 trick (shape 2 above), then the new A♭maj7♯5-off-the-3rd trick (shape 3 above), resolving to a **A minor 9 voicing using open strings** — a specific fingerboard-knowledge move worth remembering as a go-to open-position A minor 9 shape.
3. The **minor-blues etude's two V-I ideas**: the first uses one of the multifunctional shapes above voiced as an E7; the second is the tritone-substitute dominant-9-as-altered-E7 device described above; the phrase again resolves to the same **open-string A minor 9** voicing as line 2 — confirming it as Galbraith's recurring resolution shape, not a one-off.

## A fifth device: the "magic chord" — one voicing, five identities via melodic minor
A different construction route to the same "one shape, several harmonic identities" payoff, this time built from a **scale** rather than derived from a chord-tone family: voice the notes of **C melodic minor** into a single chord shape (nicknamed "the magic chord") and the *same* physical voicing reads as five different chords depending purely on which note the bass plays underneath it:
- Bass on **C** → **C minor(major7) 6/9** (Cm(maj7)69) — the tonic reading.
- Bass on **F** → **F13(♯11,9)** — a Lydian-dominant-flavored reading a 4th up.
- Bass on **B** → **B altered** (B7alt) — the melodic-minor-up-a-half-step-from-the-7th-degree altered-dominant reading.
- Bass on **A** → **A minor7♭5(natural 9)** — a half-diminished reading with a natural 9 (from being drawn straight out of the parent melodic minor rather than a "pure" locrian ♮2 chord).
- Bass on **E♭** → **E♭ major7♯5(♯11)** — a lydian-augmented reading.
This is the same underlying mechanism as the four devices above (fix the notes, change what the bass implies as root), just entered from "harmonize a melodic minor scale into one grip" instead of "start a min7♭5/half-diminished/maj7♯5/dominant-9 shape off a different chord tone." **Worked as a full 2-5-1**: the one C-melodic-minor voicing covers the ii (via its A m7♭5(♮9) reading), the V (via its B altered reading, or combined with a tritone-substitute-flavored bass choice), and resolves — the whole progression playable off one memorized shape by changing only the bass note under it. Also explicitly stacked with [[Breaking Up Voicings]] (fragment the magic-chord voicing itself instead of striking it whole) and [[Counterpoint Line Generation]] (generate a two-voice line first, then harmonize it with the magic chord's five readings) — see those notes for the companion devices from the same lesson.

## Why this matters for comping efficiency
The whole point is vocabulary compression: instead of memorizing a distinct shape for every altered-dominant color you might need, memorize a handful of core 4-note shapes (min7♭5, half-diminished, maj7♯5, dominant 9) and learn **which chord tone to start each one from** to get a different, usable harmonic result. That's fewer total shapes under the fingers with more comping mileage per shape — directly analogous to [[Chord Voicings]]'s rootless-voicing reuse and [[Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords]]'s one-triad-many-chords logic, just applied specifically to altered-dominant sounds.

## A sixth device: chromatic-slide reharmonization of a rootless 9 chord (blues rhythm-guitar version)
A guitar-mechanical, blues-specific route to the same "one grip, several identities" payoff, from a different channel/lineage than the Galbraith devices above: take a **rootless dominant 9 shape** (3rd–♭7–9th–5th, no root — the same base grip documented on [[Rootless 9th Chords and Chromatic Movement in Blues Rhythm Guitar]]) and **slide the whole shape up a half step**. Because the four notes don't change, only which root they're heard against does, so the identical grip reads as any of:
- Its own dominant 9 (unmoved).
- A dominant 9 a half step up (the moved position's most literal reading).
- A half-diminished (m7♭5) chord, off a different root again.
- A minor 6 chord, off yet another root.
- An altered dominant a half step *below* the moved position (reading the moved shape's ♭7/major 3rd/♭13/♭9 against that root instead).
Worked example (rootless G9 slid up a half step): reads as A♭9 (A♭ mixolydian), Cm7♭5 (C locrian), E♭m6 (E♭ dorian-family), or D7alt (D altered — the source's own favorite reading, and the one demonstrated in the solo). One chromatic slide, four to five usable harmonic destinations depending on how the soloist/comper chooses to hear it — the same economy-of-shapes payoff as the Galbraith devices above, reached here from a **half-step chromatic slide** rather than "start the same shape off a different chord tone."

## A seventh device: the half-diminished shape's six-way ambiguity, worked into a full progression
A single root-position half-diminished shape (top four strings: root-♭5-♭7-♭3) is the richest example of this page's whole theme found yet — the source calls it his "favorite example" of one-shape-many-identities specifically because it supports **six** distinct chord readings depending purely on which of its four notes is treated as the (possibly unplayed) root:
- Its own root → plain **half-diminished** (m7♭5).
- A different note as root → **minor 6**.
- Another note as root → **dominant 9**.
- Another note as root → **dominant 7 ♭13 ♭9**.
- The *same* root as the previous reading, but reinterpreting the "♭13" note as a **♯5** instead → **dominant 7 ♯5 ♭9** — identical notes, identical assumed root, different scale/label (stated as an important distinction because it changes which scale a soloist would draw from, even though nothing physically changes in the grip).
- The remaining note as root → **major 13 ♯11** (= maj7♯11).

**Worked as a full held-voicing progression** (the payoff move — the same top shape reused across an entire chord progression rather than one chord at a time): shell voicings first, **D major 7 → B minor 6 → E7 → B♭7 → resolve to A major 7**; then the *identical top-note shape held throughout*, only the assumed root/bass moving underneath — **D major 13♯11 → B minor 6 → E9 → B♭7(♭13,♭9) → A major 7**. Demonstrates a chord progression that sounds like genuine harmonic motion in the bass while the guitar's rootless voicing on top never physically moves; explicitly not framed as a sound to sit on constantly, but as a deliberate static-color option to have on tap. → [[Chord Voicings]], [[Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords]] — Jared Borkowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared), [[Rootless Voicings - WOW!]]

## An eighth device: a static triad held over a moving chord progression (songwriting application)
The same "one shape, many identities" mechanism run in the opposite direction and toward composition rather than jazz comping: instead of picking a different root for the *same fixed voicing* chord to chord, pick a **fixed small triad shape** and find a sequence of *different* underlying chords that each legitimately contain it, so a genuinely moving progression can carry one repeating melodic/harmonic idea on top. Worked on an arpeggiated **A minor triad** held constant against five different underlying chords, each one shown to actually contain the Am triad: **Am** (plain) → **F♯ half-diminished** (contains Am) → **F major 7** (contains Am, via this page's own root-replaced-with-7th mechanism) → **C major 6** (this specific voicing of C6 contains Am) → **D minor**, reasoned chord-tone by chord-tone: Am's root becomes Dm's ♭7 (→ Dm7), Am's 5th becomes Dm's 9th (→ Dm9). **Arrangement-simplification move layered on top:** rather than playing each full chord underneath, play only the **1st and 3rd** of each chord in the sequence — a repeating two-note fragment under a moving progression, described as producing a deliberate "energy build" from the repetition. A concrete demonstration that this page's whole device family applies to writing a part, not just comping a jazz standard. → [[Chord Voicings]] — Jared Borkowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared), [[Rootless Voicings - WOW!]]
