# 5 Popular Beginner Bass Lines (Are You Playing Them Wrong?)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=crO-k0sR4TM)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=crO-k0sR4TM)

## Synopsis

A BassBuzz "diagnosis" video with a sharper angle than a standard riff-tab lesson: Josh picks five iconic, genuinely famous bass lines that *look* trivially easy on paper (a handful of open strings and a couple of frets each) and shows, via a running "Noob Josh" bit, the **one specific detail** beginners reliably get wrong on each — not wrong notes, but wrong *rhythm*, wrong *slide mechanics*, wrong *note length*, or wrong *articulation*. Each riff is used as the vehicle for teaching one transferable rhythmic/technical concept in real depth (16th-note syncopation, big-slide mechanics, quarter-note triplets, note length/staccato-vs-legato, hammer-on technique) rather than just handing over a tab — and each section closes with a short "now generalize this to your own bass lines" demo, so the video does real double duty as both a repertoire lesson and a five-part technique curriculum. A NoteFlight slow-play-along score for all five riffs is mentioned repeatedly as a companion resource (external, not pulled into the vault). Genuinely one of BassBuzz's more theory-dense beginner videos — three of the five riffs get a full rhythmic-notation breakdown (16th-note counting, quarter-note-triplet math) rather than "just feel it by ear."

## What's taught

- **[~0:00–0:44] Framing.** Five iconic, recognizable riffs that sound impressive but get "butchered" by beginners missing one specific detail each — not the notes, always something else (rhythm, slide tracking, note length, articulation). Video is explicitly built to also teach transferable concepts, not just five tabs.

- **[~0:44–4:56] Riff 1 — "Another One Bites the Dust" (Queen, bassist John Deacon) — 16th-note syncopation.**
  - **The diagnosis:** beginners typically get the *notes* right but not the *rhythm* — this riff runs on **16th-note syncopation**, the rhythmic backbone of funk and disco, and without understanding it a player fudges the timing (notes landing a little early or late) even when playing "by ear" gets them close.
  - **16th notes, defined and counted:** divide each quarter-note beat into four equal parts, counted **"1 e and a, 2 e and a, 3 e and a, 4 e and a."**
  - **Syncopation, defined:** accenting notes on the off-beat (the "e" and "a" subdivisions) instead of squarely on the beat — "creates an edgier, funkier rhythmic feeling." Straight quarters (`1 2 3 4`) or even eighths (`1 and 2 and`) are the un-syncopated baseline this contrasts against.
  - **Where it lands in this specific riff:** the second bar enters on the **"a" of beat 4** (not squarely on beat 1) and then accents the **"a" of beat 2** — full riff counted as "One, Two, Three and Four beat and A, One and Two E and A."
  - **Fingering:** open E string for most of the riff, plus two fretted notes — **index finger, 3rd fret of the E string**, and **pinky, 5th fret of the E string**. That's the entire fretting-hand vocabulary for the riff.
  - **Generalizing it — "funk up" any plain bassline:** take a square, on-the-beat line and move individual notes onto the "e" or "a" subdivisions instead of leaving them on the beat. Demonstrated as a two-step escalation on a plain example line — move one note (the 3rd note) onto the "e" of beat 3 → audibly funkier; then move a second note (the 2nd note) onto the "a" of beat 2 as well → funkier still. Framed as a simple, repeatable move (not a whole new vocabulary to learn) for adding funk character to an otherwise flat line, and works for both *playing* an existing syncopated riff accurately and *writing* your own. → new content: [[Syncopation and Metric Displacement]].

- **[~4:56–8:02] Riff 2 — "London Calling" (The Clash, bassist Paul Simonon) — the big slide.**
  - **The diagnosis (two distinct beginner failure modes shown):** (1) missing the slide entirely and just playing the two notes discretely, and (2) attempting the slide but getting visually lost partway through and landing on the wrong fret.
  - **Slide mechanic #1 — fret-and-hold:** fret the **2nd fret**, keep pressure down, and slide the same finger up to the **7th fret** (the destination) — in slow motion it's audibly one fret at a time, but at tempo it reads as one smooth glide. Pluck happens once, at the start.
  - **Slide mechanic #2 — look-ahead tracking:** once the starting note (2nd fret) is fretted, move your **eyes** to the 7th-fret destination *before* the slide starts, then move the finger to where the eyes are already looking, and pluck on the beat — "play the note, move your eyes, then move your finger to where your eyes are." This is the same core look-ahead principle [[Bass Slide Technique]] already documents from a different riff ("Feel Good Inc."), now with a second, bigger worked example (5 frets of travel vs. that riff's shorter slide) and its own specific fingering.
  - **Full fingering for the riff:** index on the 2nd fret of the A string, slide to 7th fret (index); pinky on the 10th fret of the A string while the index stays down on the 7th fret (sets up a fast position change); back to 7th (index), back to 10th (pinky), roll the pinky over to the 10th fret of the D string; then back down — index 2nd fret slides to index 7th fret again, same pinky/index/pinky pattern, then middle finger on the 8th fret of the E string to close the phrase.
  - **Generalizing it — slides as a free character add:** demoed on a plain example line — adding slides "without even having to change the notes" gives a bland line noticeably more character and makes the playing read as more confident/committed. → new worked example added to [[Bass Slide Technique]].

- **[~8:02–11:59] Riff 3 — "Seven Nation Army" (The White Stripes; originally a pitch-shifted guitar riff, not bass) — the quarter-note triplet.**
  - **The diagnosis:** looks trivially simple as a repeated note pattern, but the actual rhythm is a **quarter-note triplet** — three evenly-spaced notes across a two-beat span — and beginners who don't recognize that as a distinct rhythmic unit butcher the timing.
  - **The math, worked step by step:** take the two beats in question (beats 3–4), divide *each* beat into three even parts (counted "3 and a, 4 and a" — six slots total), then play only the **1st, 3rd, and 5th** of those six slots (i.e. skip the 2nd, 4th, and 6th) — giving three evenly-spaced hits across the two-beat span instead of the six you'd get if every slot were played. Full first-bar count: **"One, Two and Three and A, Four and A"** — the first two beats divide in twos (straight eighths), the second two divide in threes (the triplet).
  - **Fingering:** pinky on the 7th fret of the A string (played twice), index on the 5th fret of the D string, pinky on 7th fret of A again, index on 5th fret of A, shift to middle on the 3rd fret of A, index on the 2nd fret of A — full sequence **pinky, pinky, index, pinky, index, middle, index**. Explicitly flagged as one of several valid fingerings people use for this riff (alternatives given: move the G up a string, or move the C/B to the E string to eliminate the position shift) — the one thing flagged as a genuine mistake is playing the whole riff in open position on the thin strings, which reads "wimpy" rather than driving.
  - **Generalizing it — triplets as a quirk generator:** demoed on a plain straight-eighths example line — adding a triplet in gives it something "catchier" than dividing every beat in twos, described as making an otherwise-square line "quirkier." → new worked example added to [[Time Signatures & Note Values]] (Tuplets section — this is a real-song super-triplet, a triplet's duration stretched across two beats rather than confined to one).

- **[~11:59–14:58] Riff 4 — "Walking on the Moon" (The Police, bassist Sting) — note length (short/long/long).**
  - **The diagnosis:** beginners nail the notes *and* the rhythm on this one but still sound wrong, because they miss **note length** — specifically, cutting the *first* note of each three-note group short and holding the middle note long, when it should be the reverse.
  - **Correct pattern, confirmed against the actual waveform:** each three-note phrase is **short, long, long** — cut the first note of the group short, hold the second and third notes long. The common beginner error inverts it to **long, short, long**, cutting the *middle* note short instead of the first.
  - **Why beginners get it backwards:** there's a fretting-hand note change (C to D) right at that spot in the phrase, and beginners unconsciously leave a gap of silence there while their fingers reposition — that gap lands on the wrong note in the group and reads as the middle note being cut short.
  - **The governing principle, stated directly and worth quoting whole:** "note length should be chosen based on the sound you want, not by what your fingers want to do" — matches [[Connected vs. Disconnected Bass Phrasing]]'s existing "match the source, don't default to short notes" rule almost verbatim, from a different video.
  - **Fingering, phrase by phrase:** Phrase 1 — index finger, 3rd fret of A string, played **short** (lift immediately after plucking to cut it), then index presses again and stays down while the pinky adds the 5th fret of A — the short-long-long shape realized as "lift, press-and-hold, add." Phrase 2 — middle finger, 3rd fret of D string, cut short the same way; then index on the 2nd fret of D, held ringing until the last possible instant before plucking the 3rd fret of A with the middle finger — full phrase-2 fingering **middle, index, middle**.
  - **Generalizing it — note length as its own expressive axis, independent of notes/rhythm:** demoed on the same simple quarter-note line played three ways — all long, all short, and alternating short/long/short/long — each version described as producing "very different feelings" with the *identical* notes and rhythm untouched. Explicitly recommended as a go-to tweak "if you like the notes but something still feels a little off." → new worked example added to [[Connected vs. Disconnected Bass Phrasing]].

- **[~14:58–18:09] Riff 5 — "Killing in the Name" (Rage Against the Machine, bassist Tim Commerford) — hammer-ons, and closing.**
  - **The diagnosis (two distinct failure modes):** hammer-ons played so weakly they're barely audible, or skipped entirely in favor of plucking every note — the second option is called out as sounding "really weird" on this specific riff, since the line is built around the hammer-on's tonal contrast.
  - **Hammer-on definition (general mechanics):** sound a note by striking the fret firmly with the fretting hand alone, without a matching pluck.
  - **The fix for weak hammer-ons — fret harder than normal, and don't lose the anchor note:** press hard enough to actually sound the string (described as "as hard as you can" until strength builds with practice), *and* keep the previous fretting finger held down on its note so the note before the hammer-on doesn't die early — losing that anchor finger is a second, separate way a hammer-on reads as weak, beyond just insufficient hammer force. Explicitly framed as physically tiring at first, with strength building through repetition.
  - **Tuning note:** the riff is played with the **E string tuned down a whole step to D** (full tuning **D-A-D-G** low to high) — called out as a common tuning choice across Rage Against the Machine's catalog generally, not unique to this one song.
  - **Full fingering:** open low D → index on 3rd fret of A, hammer-on to pinky on 5th fret of A (first hammer-on: pluck the 3rd-fret note, then hammer the 5th fret as hard as possible) → open low D again → index on 3rd fret of D, hammer-on to middle finger on 4th fret of D → pinky on 5th fret of A (plucked) → open D string → index on 2nd fret of E, hammer-on with middle finger to 3rd fret of E → pluck the 2nd fret of E again → loop back to the top.
  - **Generalizing it — three reasons hammer-ons matter beyond this one riff:** (1) a second articulation option beyond constant plucking, so a line doesn't have to be "pluck, pluck, pluck" the whole way through; (2) notes glide into each other more smoothly than an all-plucked line; (3) it's easier to play fast, since the picking hand doesn't have to strike every single note. → new worked example added to [[Hammer-Ons and Pull-Offs (Bass)]].
  - **[~17:30–18:09] Closing.** A recurring "Noob Josh" comment-bait gag about subscribing/liking/notification-bell, closing on a nonsense hammer-ons tongue-twister callback ("some of those who work hammer-ons are the same who play hammer-ons...").
