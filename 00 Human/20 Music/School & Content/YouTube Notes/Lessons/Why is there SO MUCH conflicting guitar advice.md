# Why is there SO MUCH conflicting guitar advice?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_8B8wWdVtfc)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_8B8wWdVtfc)

## Synopsis

Brandon D'Eon walks through seven commonly-contradicted pieces of guitar advice and gives his own resolved position on each, generally landing on "both sides are right depending on your goal" rather than declaring a single winner. The practical value is in the resolutions themselves, several of which are concrete enough to act on.

## Techniques

- **Debate 1 — Are scales needed for soloing?** Resolution: goal-dependent. If your soloing goal is simple fills/riffs around chords and you have a strong ear, you can get far without formal scale knowledge. If the goal is full-fretboard, any-key improvisation, scale knowledge is a hard requirement, not optional — his own experience: fretboard-wide scale knowledge is what expanded his improvising, specifically because his goal required it.
- **Debate 2 — Practice with clean tone vs. distorted tone.** Resolution, split by hand: **practice left-hand techniques (legato, trills) with a clean tone** — distortion masks left-hand sloppiness that a clean signal exposes immediately. **Practice right-hand techniques (alternate picking, sweep picking) with a distorted tone** — distortion exposes muting/noise mistakes (open-string ringing, sloppy sweep muting) far more harshly than a clean tone does, since those artifacts get amplified rather than hidden.
- **Debate 3 — Slow-and-gradual metronome increases vs. short fast speed bursts, for building speed.** Resolution: use both, sequentially. Push straight to a target tempo beyond your current plateau (the "fast methodology" — deliberately sacrificing accuracy at first, on the logic that faster playing recruits different hand/forearm muscles that never activate at a comfortable tempo) to break the plateau, **then** stay at that new tempo and grind for accuracy using the "slow methodology" mindset until it's clean. Neither approach alone is sufficient in his own experience — the fast jump breaks the ceiling, the subsequent accuracy grind makes the new tempo usable.
- **Debate 4 — Learn techniques through songs vs. through dedicated exercises.** Resolution: technique-dependent, based on how "musical" isolated reps of it can be. **Bending** is named as better learned through songs (e.g. "Comfortably Numb") — most bending exercises aren't musical enough to sustain engagement, and the technique's core elements (thumb placement, wrist pivot, tuning accuracy) transfer fine from real playing. **Sweep picking** is named as better learned through dedicated exercises — its failure modes (notes ringing together, pick-angle consistency, muting, note-spacing evenness, hand-sync) are numerous and specific enough that isolated, repeatable drilling outperforms picking it up incidentally from songs.
- **Debate 5 — Is the CAGED system a useful learning tool or a crutch?** His own resolved position: leans toward "crutch," though he flags it as useful for beginners specifically. The critique: CAGED teaches memorized shapes, not the underlying notes that make up a chord or scale, which breaks down the moment you need a voicing or scale position outside the five CAGED shapes — worked example: identifying which CAGED shape a given Phrygian scale position belongs to is genuinely ambiguous (two different CAGED shapes both technically "fit" the same notes), which undercuts CAGED's claimed clarity. His alternative: learn the actual notes/scale-degree construction directly rather than routing everything through five memorized shapes. *(Contrast with this vault's own CAGED-based material — [[CAGE System Scale Shapes]], [[Positional CAGED Soloing with Matched Modes and Arpeggios]] — which treats CAGED as a practical organizing tool rather than a crutch; flagged as a genuine pedagogical disagreement worth being aware of, not a vault error.)*
- **Debate 6 — Does music theory help or hurt musicianship/creativity?** His resolved position: theory helps, with the same goal-dependent carve-out as Debate 1 — if your only goal is playing existing songs for entertainment, theory isn't strictly required. He's dismissive of the "theory kills creativity" claim as usually post-hoc justification from players who never learned theory, rather than a real trade-off.
- **Debate 7 — What does "phrasing" actually mean? (Definitional, not a real disagreement.)** Two incompatible definitions circulate under the same word: **phrasing = the content of your lines** (which notes/phrases you choose to play — "fix your phrasing" means play different material) vs. **phrasing = how you play a given line** (dynamics, timing, articulation — same notes, delivered differently, the way the same sentence can be spoken multiple different ways). His own usage (and what he learned in music school) is the second definition. Flagged as worth clarifying explicitly whenever the word comes up in feedback, since the two definitions produce completely different practice actions.
