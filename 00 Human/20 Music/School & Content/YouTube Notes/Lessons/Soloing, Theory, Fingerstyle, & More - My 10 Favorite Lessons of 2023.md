## What's taught
Countdown order, #10 → #1, each with the specific clip content he shows:

- **#10 — Soloing over a I–vi–IV–V progression: target a chord tone of the ONE differently-functioning chord, not every chord.** From "How to Play a Guitar Solo Over Simple Chord Progressions" (Sept 5, worked in key of E: E major–C♯minor–A major–B major). Core method: use a phrasing structure of **AAAB** — play a short idea, repeat it (loosely) three times over the I, vi, and IV chords (which "can all kind of be the same harmonic function"), then on the V chord specifically, land a **chord tone of the V** at the end of the phrase, since the V is "the one that's actually different." The earlier, more advanced step this simplifies: after mapping out every chord's chord tones, solo completely freely over the changes (any scale — pentatonic, blues, major, "whatever weird stuff") and just make sure to land back on a chord tone at phrase starts/ends — the chord-tone map is what makes free playing still sound intentional rather than random. → [[Guide Tones]]
- **#9 — Whole tone scale over a dominant 7♯5 chord.** From "The Dream Sequence Scale: Whole Tone Scale Guitar Shapes and How to Use Them" (Jan 24) — covers the scale's history, all its physical fretboard shapes, and its most common modern use case: improvising over a dominant 7♯5. Demonstrated over C7♯5. Describes the sound qualitatively: "nebulous," "open-ended," "no beginning, no ending, it's just the same everywhere you go," an "absolute ungrounded" sound with no home base even though C is technically the chord's root — nicknamed the "dream sequence sound" for that reason (its symmetry means every rotation is the same scale transposed, so it never resolves the way a normal scale does). → [[Scale Modes Reference]] (already documents Whole Tone as 7♯5/"Lydian Dominant ♯5")
- **#8 — Fill gaps between voice-led chord tones with passing chromatic notes for advanced-sounding harmony, without needing to name the theory.** From "Composing With Smooth Voice Leading: Inverted Chords Guitar Composing Tutorial" (March 14) — thinking on guitar "like a composer would think when writing for an orchestra, string quartet, or choir": every note moves the smallest distance possible when chords change (voice leading via inversions), rather than jumping between full chord shapes. The clip's specific extension: after finding a smoothly voice-led progression, **add a note that fills the gap between one voice's start and end pitch** — a half-step motion needs no filling, but a whole-step or minor-third gap gets a passing note in between. Demonstrated live and unplanned, moving single notes by a half step at a time to morph one chord into another (e.g., lowering one note turns a shape into G minor; moving a different note creates a sus chord before resolving) — framed as literally how harmony as an idea was invented: "just moving melodies because you wanted the melody to move, and the harmony ends up being something different because the melody moved." → [[Chord Inversions]]
- **#7 — Lydian dominant (4th mode of melodic minor) over a dominant 7♯11 chord.** From "Modes of Melodic Minor Scale on Guitar and What Chords to Play Over Them" (Jan 10) — each melodic-minor mode pairs to one specific chord type; this clip demonstrates the 4th mode (Lydian dominant) over a plain dominant 7♯11. Practice approach: outline the underlying dominant-7 chord tones first (as with any chord-tone-mapping approach), then add the mode's extra color notes around that skeleton. Called out as **his most-used melodic-minor mode** specifically because dominant 7♯11 chords come up more often in real playing than the other five modes' target chords. → [[Scale Modes Reference]] (documents this as the IV-degree mode: "7 / Lydian ♭7" — same mode, "Lydian ♭7" ≡ "Lydian dominant")
- **#6 — Fingerstyle thumb independence: hold a constant root-note bass under a moving/switching-chord upper part.** From "Enhance Your Finger Picking With These Thumb Independence Guitar Exercises" (June 13) — series of exercises for playing/hearing two independent parts on one guitar. Clip shown = exercise 3: thumb plays the current chord's **root only**, switching to the new chord's root exactly when the chord changes (suggested rhythm: 4 counts, i.e. 2 measures, per chord before switching) — open strings are ideal for this where available. Progression path: once solid, add scale notes above the held root ("what's the G scale above that"); applied to a full song (his example: "What a Wonderful World," changing every chord) this root-per-chord thumb technique is described as "one or two steps away from a solo guitar arrangement" — the next step being to replace the plain quarter-note-driving bass with the occasional fuller chord shape. → new note: [[Fingerstyle Thumb Independence]]
- **#5 — Any triad (in-key or not) can safely take the pentatonic scale built on its own root.** From "What Scales Go With What Chords: Finding the Right Scales for Chords on the Guitar" (May 9) — the practical shortcut for improvising/writing melody without knowing (or needing to know) the overall key: for **any** triad that shows up in a progression, major or minor, in the stated key or borrowed from outside it, playing the matching pentatonic scale off that triad's own root (major pentatonic over a major triad, minor pentatonic over a minor triad) "will sound amazing" and automatically tracks the changes. Demonstrated over E major → C minor → B major → (an out-of-key) C major, switching pentatonic scale each time by ear rather than by key analysis. → [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]]
- **#4 — Artificial harmonics: fret normally, touch 12 frets above with the picking hand, then pluck.** From "Guitar Harmonics In-Depth Guide: Easy, Pure and Beautiful Sound" (Nov 7) — full explainer on natural + artificial harmonics/overtones; clip demonstrates artificial harmonics played through a blues scale run, an octave higher and much brighter/bell-like than the normally-fretted pitch. Technique: fret a note as usual, then with the **picking hand**, lightly touch the string exactly 12 frets above the fretted note (same touch as a standard natural harmonic) while plucking — same hand does both the touch and the pluck. Three named plucking methods for the same touch: **(1)** hold the pick normally, extend the index finger to touch the node, pluck with the pick; **(2)** same touch, pluck with the ring ("a") finger instead — common in classical guitar; **(3)** same touch, pluck with the thumb. Any finger can touch, any finger can pluck — these three are just the most common combinations. → new note: [[Guitar Harmonics]]
- **#3 — Walking connective bass notes between chords, using 1 to 4 scale (or chromatic) notes.** From "Walking Between Chords on Guitar for Songwriting and Arranging" (April 11, his best-performing video of the year) — multi-step lesson building basslines that connect one chord to the next using 1, then 2, then 3, then 4 connecting notes, using both in-scale and chromatic (out-of-scale) passing tones. Clip shown = the 3-connecting-note version: on beats 2–3–4 of the bar before a chord change, play three ascending scale notes leading up into the next chord's root (worked example walks up into A minor) — described as giving "a whole different flavor" versus landing on the chord cold. → [[Bassline Writing]]
- **#2 — Two go-to moveable chord shapes cover most pop songs.** From "Play Any Song With Just Two Chord Shapes 🤯" (Jan 17, his 2nd favorite) — really doable with one shape, but two is more practical; these are his personal default go-to voicings for playing/writing/learning songs and mapping chords in a key generally (not song-specific shapes). Clip demonstrates the two shapes, moved to different frets/roots, playing through "Don't Go Chasing Waterfalls" (TLC) and "Crazy" (Gnarls Barkley) with the shape and chord name captioned on screen as he plays. → [[Chord Voicings]]
- **#1 — Choosing between two theoretically-valid scales by feel, not just correctness: Mixolydian vs. blues scale over a D7.** From "What Scale to Play Over What Chord: Choosing What Scale to Use" (May 30, his #1 favorite) — about the case where **multiple scales are all technically correct** over a chord, and the real skill is choosing between them by ear/feeling rather than defaulting to "whichever is most correct." Demonstrated over a D7 vamp: **Mixolydian** — the scale the dominant-7 chord is actually derived from — sounds "sweet," has the major 3rd, reads as a "smug smile" facial expression in his framing; the **blues scale** (with its ♭3) sounds like "attitude" — the ♭3 against Mixolydian's natural 3rd is technically "a wrong note," yet is standard vocabulary over dominant chords in blues/rock, which is exactly why players **bend into/out of that ♭3** rather than sit on it squarely (the bend IS the acknowledgment that it's outside the "correct" scale). Framed explicitly as an art-not-craft decision: practice absorbing/feeling which scale communicates the emotion wanted in the moment, not just which one is theoretically allowed. → [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]]
