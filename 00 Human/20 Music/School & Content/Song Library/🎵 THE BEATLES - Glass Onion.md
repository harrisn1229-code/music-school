# Glass Onion — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

A short talking-blues patter song whose harmony **never establishes a home key by cadence**. A minor asserts itself, but there is no V–i and no iv–i anywhere; the only prop is the ♭VII–i implied at the top of each verse, which is thin evidence. Five chords total, and only two of them are diatonic to the key.

**Form.** Deliberately stick-figure: no intro, an unrelieved chain of verse/refrain pairs, immediate phrase repetition inside every section. The **verse runs an unusual 9 bars** in an AA-A'A'' pattern with the third phrase a bar longer than the rest; the refrain is **6 bars**; the bridge is **10**, eight of them static. The non-sequitur outro is what saves the song from complacency.

**Harmony.** The verse is a sustained lean toward the relative major that never arrives ([[HAR-C10 — Tonicization vs modulation]]), built from stacked secondary functions doing the work of a modulation that never happens ([[HAR-C07 — Secondary dominants]]). The bridge is the clearest single device: eight bars of **tonic pedal** with a chromatically rising middle voice climbing over it ([[POP-C03 — Pedal point]]). The rise manufactures expectation and then declines to pay it off — nothing harmonic is happening behind the suspense. The outro's chromatic stream of dominant sevenths is a disguise rather than a non sequitur: it's the refrain's opening in altered dress, and the glissando delivery was already planted in the second halves of the verses, which is why it works on reflection rather than on impact.

**Melody.** Minor-key blues writing leaning hard on the **flat fifth**, which is also the top note of the song's most-used outside chord — so the tune's most characteristic pitch is supplied by the harmony rather than fighting it.

**Arrangement.** Heavily overdubbed and indistinct on the surface, but worth straining for: a bowed-string glissando on one specific chord change, a ghost falsetto as verse 2 closes, a moment of dead clean silence before the final verse, and a quoted recorder lick held back until the phrase *after* the lyric that names it.
