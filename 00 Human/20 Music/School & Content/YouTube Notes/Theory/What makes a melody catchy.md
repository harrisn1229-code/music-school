# What makes a melody catchy?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nxXh8PU4KkI)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nxXh8PU4KkI)

## Synopsis

A theory of the "earworm" — why some melodies get stuck in the head involuntarily — built around two claimed necessary features (easy to sing, and repeated), with a linguistic explanation for why humans are wired to latch onto pitch patterns at all. Explicitly separates "catchy" from "good": many beautiful melodies aren't earworms, and many earworms (Baby Shark) aren't musically desirable — catchiness is a distinct, narrower property.

## Techniques

- **Catchy ≠ good — two separate axes.** A melody can be catchy without being musically strong (Baby Shark), and a strong melody can fail to be catchy. Worth stating explicitly when evaluating a hook: "does this stick" and "is this good" are different questions.
- **Singability driven by interval size: seconds through fifths are easy to sing involuntarily; sixths and beyond take deliberate vocal effort.** A wider melody can still be catchy, but it's fighting the mechanism rather than using it — echoes the existing note's "leaps (a third or bigger) are hard" guidance with a slightly looser threshold (fifths still counted as easy here).
- **Rhythm/tempo has a catchiness sweet spot in both directions, not just "too fast is bad."** Notes packed too tight make a tune hard to sing along to; notes spaced too far apart make the listener consciously aware they're "waiting" for the next note with no pulse behind them to fill the gap — both failure modes reduce the involuntary-hum effect, not just density.
- **A melody written for an instrument, not a vocalist, often has no built-in breathing room and resists being sung along to** — the example given is Coldplay's "Clocks" piano riff: melodically catchy, but there's no gap anywhere in the phrase for a singer to breathe, so singing along to it is physically uncomfortable even though the tune itself is an earworm.
- **A named 4-phrase pop-chorus shape for repetition without monotony: A, A' (same phrase, subtly changed ending), A (exact repeat), B (new closing phrase).** Phrase B does two jobs at once — it resolves onto the tonic (giving the passage a sense of landing) and it breaks up what would otherwise be A repeated a tiring number of times in a row, acting as "respite" before the whole shape can loop again. Worked example: the chorus of "I Wanna Dance with Somebody" (Whitney Houston).
- **Macro-repetition (hearing the same recording on loop, e.g. background music at a shop, or game music during long play sessions) is often enough on its own to embed a tune**, independent of the melody's own internal structure — cited as part of why video-game music in particular tends to lodge itself, since exposure is long and attention is split elsewhere.
- **The "why" mechanism proposed: catchiness rides on the same cognitive machinery humans use to process spoken language, not a separate musical faculty.** Humans track small pitch changes in speech to extract meaning (most explicitly in tonal languages like Mandarin, but pitch-for-meaning shows up in every language — e.g. rising pitch signaling a question) — the claim is that a melody gets "learned" involuntarily via the same pitch-tracking wiring used for language acquisition, which is why repeated exposure alone (as with a language) is enough to make it stick without any deliberate memorization.
- **A worked catchy-vs-uncatchy demonstration**: the catchy version used small intervals (2nds/3rds/5ths) plus sequence and antecedent/consequent structure; the deliberately uncatchy version used wide, awkward leaps (a flat 9th), a fast relentless rhythm, and zero repetition — a compact checklist of the four levers (interval size, rhythm density, sequence, repetition) in one A/B pair.
