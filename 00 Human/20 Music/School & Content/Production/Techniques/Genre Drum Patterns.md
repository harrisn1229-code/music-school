# Genre Drum Patterns

**What actually distinguishes one genre's drums from another's** — tempo, where the backbeat lands, and how the hats subdivide. Three parameters explain most of it.

Companion to [[Rhythm and Groove]] (mechanics), [[Pocket and Microtiming]] (feel) and [[Syncopation and Metric Displacement]] (the devices). The chain-level treatment of each element lives in `Production/Chains/2 Drums/`.

## The three parameters

Almost every genre distinction in drum programming reduces to:

1. **Tempo** — the written BPM.
2. **Backbeat placement** — where the snare or clap lands.
3. **Hi-hat subdivision** — how the beat is divided above the kit.

Get those three right and a pattern reads as its genre before you've chosen a single sample.

## Half-time: the concept that explains trap

**Half-time means the backbeat moves to beat 3 instead of beats 2 and 4, so the groove feels half as fast at the same tempo.**

The ear judges groove speed almost entirely from snare placement. A snare only on beat 3 reads as a backbeat at half the actual BPM — so a track written at 140 *feels* like 70, while the hats still get to subdivide at the fast tempo.

That's the trick that defines trap: **fast enough for busy hi-hats, slow enough to rap over.** Written at 140, felt at 70. Both at once, which is not possible any other way.

The perception trick is already noted in [[Time Signatures & Note Values]]; this is the same fact from the arranging side.

## Boom-bap vs trap, the short version

The two giveaways, in order:

1. **Hi-hat activity.** Boom-bap hats are simple — straight 8ths with an open hat on the offbeat. Trap hats subdivide into 16ths with rolls in 32nds and triplets. If the hats are busy, it's trap.
2. **The half-time backbeat.** Boom-bap snares on 2 and 4 at ~90. Trap snares on 3 at ~140.

Everything else follows. Boom-bap's drums are usually **sampled and deliberately un-quantized** — the swing comes from the source break and from laying back (see [[Pocket and Microtiming]]). Trap's drums are **programmed and tight**, with the looseness pushed into the hats rather than the kit.

The other structural difference worth knowing: in boom-bap the kick and the bass are separate parts. **In trap the 808 is both** — it's the kick and the bassline in one element, which is why 808 note length and glide matter so much and why [[Bassline Writing]] and [[Bass Design]] are the same conversation in that genre.

## Trap hi-hat rolls

The strand's most specific gap (RHY-09 — you had triplets and not the rest).

**The foundation is straight 8ths or 16ths on the closed hat.** Rolls are ornaments *on top of* that foundation, not the foundation itself.

The subdivisions in play:

- **16ths** — the default bed.
- **32nds** — the standard roll. Two hits where one was.
- **Triplets** — 8th, 16th or 32nd triplets. The "rolling" character that reads as trap rather than as a fill.
- **Faster bursts** — 64ths for a moment, then snap back.

**What makes a roll work:**

- **Velocity ramps.** A roll at uniform velocity sounds like a stutter. Ramp up into the landing or down out of it.
- **Land somewhere.** Rolls resolve — usually into the next bar or onto the snare. A roll that just stops sounds unfinished.
- **Sparingly.** Every bar is exhausting; every 2 or 4 bars is a hook.
- **Pan the aux hats.** Keep kick, snare and 808 centred; extra rolls, open hats and percussion belong off-centre — per [[Kenny Beats - Making Trap Drums More Interesting]] and [[Width and Stereo]].

## West Coast / Mustard Bounce — ~90–100 BPM

The DJ Mustard/modern-LA-bounce build order (see [[Rap Subgenres]] West Coast/G-Funk section for the wider genre context). What makes it read as "the Mustard bounce" is the *sequence* the kit is built in, not any single sample choice.

- **Verse/intro: snap + cowbell + rim only.** The bare-bones bounce — no kick yet.
- **Add a tom as a section-transition device**, not a rhythmic element — its job is to signal an upcoming change (e.g. into the hook), so it's placed at transition points rather than woven through the groove.
- **Hook: layer the snap with a clap** (doubling the backbeat hit, not replacing the snap), **add a hi-hat specifically for movement**, **add a spaced-out kick** (kick sits in the gaps rather than driving a dense pattern), and **finish with a long, deep synth bass or 808** carrying the low end.
- **Darker/uptempo variant:** same ~100 BPM pocket, but the hi-hats are played at **double speed** — doubling only the hat rate (not the BPM) is what reads as "more energy/uptempo" while keeping the same tempo feel; closes with a **simple 808** instead of a synth bass.
- **Pop-hit variant (for outside artists, e.g. Rihanna/Post Malone-style Mustard productions):** the kit strips down to **very spaced out** — a reverbed rim, jittery/irregular hi-hats, a basic kick, and a distorted/tinny bass carrying the hook — deliberately sparse to leave maximum room for the vocal.

## UK Drill — ~140–145 BPM (drum build order)

loner's from-scratch drill build order (see [[Rap Subgenres]] Drill section for the wider genre identity, including the dark-chord-voicing trick that pairs with this kit). Melody/harmony are locked in FIRST — drums come only once "I didn't want to do any more melodics."

- **808 first** — the low-end/bass foundation goes down before any other percussion; slides get added afterward in a separate audition pass (see below), not written in up front.
- **Hi-hat second, treated as the single most important sound-selection call in the whole kit** — "if you're making a drill bit you're going to want to make sure your hi-hat is super fire," both the sample AND the pattern, because it's carrying most of the bounce. Two knob moves on the chosen hat: one pushed up (a character/tone control) and a second pushed up that pitches the hat down. Its timing is shifted back off-grid, but by **less than his normal amount** — a busy, already-layered hi-hat part reads as "off" if the microtiming shift is pushed as far as he'd use on a sparser part, so the more elements already stacked on the hat, the smaller the off-grid nudge should be. *(general starting point — exact ms/tick shift not given)*
- **A basic rim shot**, then **a roll built from a "counter snare" plus a triangle hit** — a small percussion cluster added purely for extra texture, not a rhythmic centerpiece.
- **A granular percussion layer for a deliberate switch-up in texture** — rather than another straight one-shot, a perc sample is dragged into a granular device (owned lane: [[Granulator II]] / [[Granulator 3]]), the grain settings tweaked, and a preset used — reached for specifically "to switch up and do something a little bit different" from the rest of the kit.
- **Kick LAST, picked purely for maximum hit ("a super hard hitting one") and left on a deliberately spaced-out pattern** — because the percussion layers already built above it are filling the rhythmic space, the kick doesn't need density, just impact where it lands.
- **808 slides added in a separate audition pass, after the beat already exists.** Stretch the 808 across the whole beat first, then play it back and listen; wherever a slide feels right by ear, use the chop tool to cut the 808 pattern at that point, then right-click → **Make Unique** to detach that one segment into its own independent instance (Ableton-equivalent: consolidate/duplicate the clip, or isolate the MIDI note) so only THAT slide can be edited without touching the rest of the pattern. Full pitch-slide mechanics (warp mode, clip transposition envelope) are on [[Bassline Writing]].

→ [[Rap Subgenres]], [[Bassline Writing]], [[Sound Selection Guide]], [[Granulator II]] — loner, [how to make hard drill beats with synths from scratch (headie one, drake etc.)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rcJ-j6X9bAU)

## House

Included because it's the clean contrast case and the [[You Suck at Drums No. 5: How to make SICK House Drum Beats|"You Suck at Drums" house episode]] is already in the vault.

**Four on the floor** — kick on every quarter — plus the **offbeat open hat** on every "and", which is the genre's signature and a pure [[Syncopation and Metric Displacement|syncopation]] device: the hat lives entirely in the gaps the kick leaves.

Clap or snare on 2 and 4, layered with the kick rather than replacing it. **Everything tight** — this is a genre where machine precision is the aesthetic, so the feel lives in the swing setting, not in microtiming.

- **Extra low-velocity ghost notes layered onto a straight four-on-the-floor kick add swing beyond the DAW's own groove/swing setting** — a Drake-*Honestly, Nevermind*-inspired remake programmed additional low-velocity kick hits alongside the main four-on-the-floor pattern specifically for bounce, rather than relying on swing quantization alone. Paired with a delay on the main hi-hat (traced by ear to "Texts Go Green") and a THIRD hi-hat part used only as an end-of-loop fill, plus a vocal-chop delay deliberately set to a DIFFERENT time value than the hi-hat delay — that time mismatch called out as "a massive key to creating swing." → [[Kick Design]], [[Delay]], [[House - EDM|House / EDM]] — loner, [how to make authentic house beats for drake's 'honestly, nevermind'!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E63Q8-gXoMU)

## The acoustic-kit side — seven played styles

Everything above is *programmed* drums. The played, kit-based equivalents come from the [[Drums Cheat Sheet - Enrichment|Drums Cheat Sheet]] poster, which prints four one-bar grooves for each of seven styles. The same three parameters explain them — and the panel is a good demonstration that hi-hat subdivision, not kick placement, is what makes a groove read as its genre.

| Style | Meter | Hats / ride | Backbeat | The tell |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Rock** | 4/4 | Straight **8ths** on closed hat | Accented **2 and 4** | Hats and snare are constant across all four patterns; only the **kick** moves |
| **Funk** | 4/4 | **16ths**, with an open hat (`o`) | 2 and 4, surrounded by **ghost notes** | Parenthesised ghost snares + 16th-placed kicks — this is the Rock box with the gaps filled in |
| **Jazz / Swing** | 4/4 | Ride "spang-a-lang" under **triplet brackets** | Snare mostly rests / comps | **Hi-hat foot on 2 and 4** (X below the staff); the kit is accompaniment, not a grid |
| **Slow Blues** | **6/8** (12/8 feel) | Triplet 8ths throughout | Backbeat over a pulse kick | The only compound-meter box on the sheet |
| **Hip Hop & R&B** | 4/4 | Mixed 8ths and 16th pairs | Accented 2 and 4 | Busiest kick figures of the seven; hi-hat foot appears in one pattern |
| **Shuffle** | 4/4 | **First + third triplet partial** under triplet brackets | 2 and 4, with ghosts between | Written as triplets, not as swung 8ths — the meter tells you the feel |
| **Bossa Nova** | 4/4 | Continuous **16ths** | **Rim click / cross-stick**, not snare | Cross-stick clave pattern + hi-hat foot on 2 and 4 |

Two things worth stealing for programmed work: **ghost notes are the funk/rock dividing line** (not the kick pattern), and **cross-stick is a separate articulation**, not a quiet snare — both are notated as distinct symbols, per [[Drum Notation]]. The sticking that produces these grooves is in [[Drum Rudiments]].

## Building any of them

The energy-tier method from the "You Suck at Drums" series generalizes across all four, and it's the most useful structural idea in the set:

**Intro → A → B → Outro, adding exactly one new percussion layer per section.** Density ramps progressively rather than starting busy. The outro drops the newest and harshest layers first — a comedown from peak energy, not a reset to the intro. See [[Arrangement and Song Form]].

Kenny Beats' version of the same point: **sections, not one loop.** "Sicko Mode is three different beats in one of the biggest songs of our generation." And the counterweight worth keeping — **artists are the simplicity referee**; a Lil Baby session ran on 5–7 sounds.

## Reggae/dub: kick placement as the whole genre family

Same three-parameter logic as the boom-bap/trap table above, but the dub family is defined by **kick placement alone** — one instrument, four positions, four sub-genres. Per [[Pocket Operations - Enrichment|Pocket Operations]]:

- **Half Drop** — kick on beat 1 only.
- **One Drop** — kick on **beat 3 only**, skipping beat 1 entirely — the genre-defining reggae move (the "drop" is the omission, not an addition).
- **Two Drop** — kick on beats 1 and 3.
- **Steppers** — kick on every beat (four-on-the-floor equivalent for reggae).

All four share a rim/snare hit anchoring beat 3 and closed hats on the off-8ths — only the kick pattern changes, the same "one parameter, four genres" idea as boom-bap-vs-trap above.

## Reggaeton / Dembow: a texture built from stacked generic percussion, not a single pattern

Unlike the reggae/dub family above (one parameter — kick placement), the dembow rhythm that defines reggaeton is a **layered percussion texture**: several "click" one-shots stacked together, where the genre's identity lives in the *combination*, not in any one sample. Per a Mix with the Masters reggaeton mixing breakdown (transcript low-confidence — machine-translated, described qualitatively):

- **The clicks are deliberately ordinary — sourced from the same online percussion libraries every other reggaeton producer draws from — and expected to sound the same as everyone else's in isolation.** The individuality comes entirely from how they're stacked and processed together, not from finding rarer samples.
- **A worked four-layer dembow click stack:** (1) a "12-beat" layer pitched/EQ'd up for extra top-end air; (2) the "classic" dembow pattern on four beats, phase-inverted to resolve a clash with the other layers and low-cut to leave room for them; (3) a layer carrying some distortion that pushes the kick's punch forward, more overtly "reggaeton" in character than the others; (4) a layer emphasizing the kick's own fundamental note, pitched low, reinforcing weight under the click texture. None of the four read as distinctive soloed — the composite pattern is the point.
- **Grid-locked by default; swing loosens it.** The dembow pattern as typically programmed reads as fairly mechanical/grid-locked; applying the DAW's swing function to it (FL Studio in the source) softens that locked feel without changing the underlying pattern. *(no swing percentage given)*
- **No dedicated kick-vs-bass sidechain, by choice, in this example** — a simple-arrangement, low-processing-count philosophy ("making everything as simple as possible") extends to skipping the duck that a house/EDM kick-bass relationship would default to.

## Hybrid breaks: chain a base pattern with a swappable ending

A structural idea worth stealing regardless of genre, from [[Pocket Operations - Enrichment|Pocket Operations]]: build **one base break plus several interchangeable "ending" bars**, and close a phrase with whichever ending fits the transition rather than committing to one fixed break. Same "same loop, swap the tail" logic as the section-transition fills in [[Arrangement and Song Form]] — treat break endings as a small swappable library, not one-offs. Full worked routine (the book's base pattern + all 8 endings, translated to beat position, plus how to apply it in hip-hop/trap/EDM): [[Break-Plus-Cadence Programming]].

- **Drum layering order for a gritty trap beat: snares first, then 808, hats, then percussion.** Build the kit in that sequence to match a gritty sample's texture rather than starting from the kick/808. → [[Kick Design]] — loner, [these '100 GIGS' production tricks are INSANE!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IF0K7RVi_rk)
- **Half-time as a mid-session diagnostic, not just a genre choice — trying it on the drums fixed a beat that felt "off" and triggered a "less is more" strip-back of other elements.** A live application of the [[#Half-time: the concept that explains trap|half-time-defines-trap]] mechanic above, used as a troubleshooting move rather than a planned arrangement decision. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]] — loner, [these '100 GIGS' production tricks are INSANE!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IF0K7RVi_rk)

- **The *Rodeo*/Travis Scott hybrid: trap programming layered with boom-bap elements, worked example.** A two-layer trap kick (hard layer + light top layer, see [[Kick Design]]), a hi-hat programmed to follow the bassline's own rhythm rather than a stock 16th-note pattern, a Tom hit standing in for a snare at certain points in the groove (two Toms used together to build a small fill/drum groove, directly inspired by "Impossible"), and the pattern finished off with a straight boom-bap snare + open hat. The reusable idea: don't pick "trap drums" OR "boom-bap drums" — layer/alternate elements from both kits within the same beat. → [[Kick Design]] — loner, [the secrets behind mike dean's 'rodeo' production!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V9FhndW8t74)
- **Drum-bus reverb only during build-ups (otherwise dry), and reuse one settled channel-strip chain across an entire album for cross-track glue.** On a UK-garage two-step drum bus, bring reverb in specifically for build-up sections rather than running it as a constant send; separately, stick to one finished chain (glue comp → tape → saturation → EQ → limiter barely touching) across every song on a record so the whole album feels consistent, accepting that as a trade-off against trying something new per track. → [[Reverb]] — Disclosure (Sol State), [Disclosure makes a classic UKG drum beat](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=e50an_umecA)
