## Synopsis

A 15-minute theory-first lesson from Jens Larsen tackling the altered scale (the 7th mode of melodic minor) from the angle he thinks most players get wrong: practicing it as "just another scale to loop over a static chord" instead of understanding *why* every note in it wants to move. His core reframe is that playing an altered chord is a deliberate harmonic statement — "I'm about to sound dissonant on purpose" — so every note in the scale needs a stated resolution target, not just a fingering. The lesson works through a full degree-by-degree resolution map of G altered → C major (spelled from A♭ melodic minor), explains why the altered chord itself isn't diatonic to its own parent scale (so you need substitute arpeggios, not the chord's own arpeggio, to outline it), and closes with three worked 251 lines (Dm7–G7alt–Cmaj7) that each demonstrate a different way of applying the resolution logic — enclosures, quartal stacks, tritone substitution, and a diminished-triad sequence. ## What's taught

- **The altered scale is the 7th mode of melodic minor, and G7 altered is spelled directly from A♭ melodic minor** — G altered = G–A♭–B♭–B–D♭–E♭–F–(G) (root, ♭9, ♯9, 3rd, ♯11, ♭13, ♭7), the exact same seven notes as A♭ melodic minor starting a half-step below the tonic. → [[Scale Modes Reference]] (Melodic Minor table, row VII — "Locrian ♭4, ♭♭7")
- **Core reframe: don't practice the altered scale as a static loop over one chord — practice it as seven notes that each have a named resolution target**, because playing an altered dominant is inherently a "make it stick out, then resolve it" statement, not a neutral color choice. Nearly all altered dominants (his estimate: "99% of the time") are functioning as the V of a real cadence, not sitting unresolved — so the scale's whole point is what it resolves *into*.
- **Full degree-by-degree resolution map, worked over G7alt → C major** (a ii–V–I: Dm7–G7alt–Cmaj7). Each altered-scale note gets a stated destination on the C major chord:
  - **A♭ (♭9)** → resolves to **G**, the 5th of C major.
  - **B♭ (♯9)** → resolves to **B**, the major 7th of Cmaj7 — either directly, or by passing through the ♭9 (A♭) first.
  - **B (natural 3rd of G7)** → can simply **stay** as the major 7th of Cmaj7 (a chord tone shared by both chords), or resolve further, though a full stop there "sounds a bit final."
  - **D♭ (♯11)** → resolves to **D**, the 9th of Cmaj7.
  - **E♭ (♭13)** → resolves **up to E** (major 3rd of C) **or down to D** (9th) — two valid directions.
  - **F (♭7)** → resolves **down to E** (major 3rd) **or up to G** (5th) — also two valid directions.
  - **G (root of G7)** → doesn't really resolve at all, because it's *already* a chord tone shared by both G7 and C major (the 5th of C) — it's the one note in the scale that's already "home."
  - Stated payoff: once every note has a known destination, you always know where a line needs to *end* — the last note has to be dealt with deliberately — and these mapped resolutions are "the most smooth way to do that." Bigger-interval resolutions are possible but harder to control. → [[Altered Chords]], [[Guide Tones]]
- **Why you need substitute arpeggios for an altered dominant: the dominant chord itself isn't diatonic to its own parent scale.** Running the diatonic 7th chords of A♭ melodic minor, there's no plain G7 in the list — G7(altered) isn't one of the seven diatonic chords built from A♭ melodic minor, so soloing "off the chord's own arpeggio" isn't an option the way it is for an ordinary diatonic chord; you have to borrow a *different* diatonic chord's arpeggio from the same parent scale instead.
- **Two go-to substitute arpeggios for an altered dominant, both diatonic to the same melodic-minor parent:** (1) **F half-diminished** (the vi° chord of A♭ melodic minor, built off G7's own ♭7) — chosen because "it has the sound of the dominant" plus some of the good alterations; (2) **B major7♭5** (built off the parent scale's ♭III, enharmonically C♭) — again carries the chord's essence (guide tones) in two of its notes, with the other two coloring it nicely. Recommendation: practice all seven diatonic arpeggios of the parent melodic-minor scale if you want full fluency, but these two are the most immediately useful for the altered-dominant sound specifically. → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]
- **Simple 3-note 251 licks as a starting framework**: build a line by placing one note over the ii chord and one note over the V (dominant) chord, using the resolution logic above to choose which altered-scale note lands where, then improvising the connective material in between. Presented as a deliberately minimal starting template — get the "load-bearing" notes placed first, fill in style/decoration once the skeleton is solid.
- **Worked example 1 (Dm7–G7alt–Cmaj7):** the D minor 7 line opens with a **chromatic-style enclosure of leading notes** targeting the root of Dm7 (not a strict chromatic enclosure — a mix of scale-tone and half-step leading notes). The G7alt line is a **sequence built from an A♭ minor triad plus an F diminished arpeggio fragment**.
- **Worked example 2:** the Dm7 line is built as a **stack of 4ths** (quartal voicing/line, the same quartal-line idea Larsen references from an earlier lesson — see [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]). The G7alt line is mostly **scale-based** (straight through the altered scale) and closes with a **trill** ornament — flagged as a genuinely idiomatic move for this style, worth having under the fingers for other "part-theme"-style lines, not just this example.
- **Worked example 3:** the Dm7 line is a straightforward diatonic line. Over G7, instead of the F half-diminished/B major7♭5 pair from above, this example reaches for **D♭7 — the tritone substitution of G7** ("the altered sound and the tritone-substitution sound are very related," so a tritone-sub arpeggio naturally fits an altered-dominant context) — followed by a **drop-2 voicing of A♭ minor-major7(add9)**, then resolves to G. So the full G7alt line = D♭7 arpeggio → A♭ minor-major7(9) drop-2 shape → resolve to G. → [[Chord Voicings]] (drop-2 voicing type), [[Chord Substitution]] (tritone substitution)
- **Closing practice framing:** the goal is to internalize the resolution logic deeply enough that "which note resolves to which note" stops being a conscious calculation — get there by building and playing enough lines that the resolutions become automatic in the fingers and ear, not by memorizing the map as trivia.
