# Bass Slide Technique

BassBuzz's quickfire answers to the standard beginner slide questions — pressure, tracking, discomfort, and where to actually start a slide from — taught through the slide in "Feel Good Inc." but framed as general slide mechanics that apply beyond that one riff. Slides are called out as "maybe the most fun thing about being a bass player," but also one of the things that trips beginners up most, because the questions ("do I press down the whole time? how do I not get lost? why does it hurt so much?") don't have obvious answers from watching someone else play.

## Pressure: full contact, but don't over-press

Press down for the entire slide — don't release partway and reapply — but keep the pressure to what's needed to sound the note, not more. Over-pressing with the fretting fingers or thumb is the specific failure mode that gets a hand physically **stuck** partway through a slide, unable to glide smoothly to the destination fret.

## Tracking: look at the destination, not the start

Look at where you're going, not where you came from. The eyes (and, by extension, the hand's sense of where it's headed) should already be oriented toward the landing fret before or during the slide, rather than staying focused on the starting position — the classic way to "get lost" mid-slide is tracking the wrong end of the motion.

## Discomfort is expected, not a sign of doing it wrong

Slides scrape skin against the string and fret wire, and that stings until the fingertip toughens up with repeated playing. Framed matter-of-factly as "part of your hazing" — an expected, temporary cost of learning slides, not a technique flaw to fix.

## Finger choice: whichever minimizes what comes next

Any finger can technically execute a slide, but the practical choice is whichever finger leaves the hand best positioned for **what happens immediately after** the slide lands — for the worked example in [[🎵 GORILLAZ - Feel Good Inc]], that's the **pinky**, because it requires the least additional hand-position shifting once the slide resolves. Pick the finger by looking one move ahead, not by habit.

## A slide with no leading note is about the destination, not the origin

Notation cue: when a slide is written with a scoop mark and **no note leading into it** (nothing to slide *from* in the written line), that's a signal the slide is defined by its **landing fret**, not its starting fret — the exact starting position is close to arbitrary as long as it's a reasonable distance away.

**Starting-distance sweet spot:** don't start the slide one fret away (reads as anticlimactic — too small a gesture to register as a slide to the ear) and don't start it the maximum possible distance away (excessive, muddies the gesture) — pick a middle distance, "some frets away," that gives the slide an audible sweep without either extreme.

**Execution sequence:** fret the starting position, pluck **at the start**, then immediately begin sliding toward the destination fret — the pluck happens once, at the origin, not re-struck on arrival.

There's a small audible gap right at the finger switch, explicitly flagged as a non-issue: "you can't really hear that in context." - **A second, bigger worked slide confirming the same fret-and-hold + look-ahead-tracking method already documented above from "Feel Good Inc.," this time on "London Calling" (The Clash, bassist Paul Simonon).** Two distinct beginner failure modes shown: missing the slide entirely and playing the two notes discretely, or attempting it but getting visually lost partway through and landing on the wrong fret. **Mechanic #1, fret-and-hold:** fret the 2nd fret, keep pressure down, slide the same finger up to the 7th fret — five frets of travel, a bigger gesture than this note's "Feel Good Inc." example. **Mechanic #2, look-ahead tracking:** once the starting note is fretted, move the eyes to the 7th-fret destination *before* the slide starts, then move the finger to where the eyes are already looking, and pluck on the beat — restated near-verbatim as "play the note, move your eyes, then move your finger to where your eyes are," the same core sequence as this note's existing tracking method. **Full riff fingering:** index on the 2nd fret of the A string, slide to 7th fret; pinky on the 10th fret of the A string while the index stays down on the 7th fret (sets up the fast position change); back to 7th (index), back to 10th (pinky), roll the pinky over to the 10th fret of the D string; then back down — index 2nd fret slides to index 7th fret again, same pinky/index/pinky pattern, closing on the middle finger at the 8th fret of the E string. **The generalization:** demoed on a plain example line — slides add noticeable character "without even having to change the notes," and are framed as making the playing itself read as more confident and committed. → BassBuzz, [[5 Popular Beginner Bass Lines (Are You Playing Them Wrong?)]]
- **Two distinct slides, same riff, worked at two different levels — a small connective slide and a "monster" slide with its own timing quirk.** BassBuzz's 5-level breakdown of Nirvana's "Smells Like Teen Spirit" (bass by Krist Novoselic) teaches both: **(1) the small "spicy" slide** worked into the main riff at Level 2 — fingering sequence "index, slide, middle, middle, slide": index frets the 1st-fret note, holds down while sliding *up* to the 4th fret of the E string (caught by the middle finger), then the middle finger plays the 4th fret of the A string and slides back down to the 1st-fret index position — this is the same small, character-adding slide already cited above (from [[Why Beginner - Intermediate Bassists Sound “Karaoke-y”]]) as the "chocolate sauce" example on this song, now with its full worked fingering. **(2) The "monster" slide** at Level 5, a distinct and much bigger move in the intro: index finger on the 1st fret, slide up to the **6th fret**, then back down to the 1st fret. **Timing quirk unique to this slide:** on the studio recording it starts *later* than a normal beat subdivision — as if the beat were divided into a triplet and the slide begins on the triplet's last third rather than an even subdivision — which Novoselic doesn't reproduce the same way live (it comes in earlier there). Framed as maybe-intentional, maybe-just-sloppy, but either way part of what makes the studio take "quirkier and a little less rhythmically predictable" than a metronomically clean cover. → [[Pocket and Microtiming]] (the late/triplet-feel entry as a studio-microtiming data point) — BassBuzz, [[The 5 Levels of Smells Like Teen Spirit (For Bass)]]
- **Choose fingering that physically enables the slide, not just fingering that's convenient in the moment — a slide that isn't built into the fingering has nowhere to go.** In the chorus of "Yellow" (Coldplay, bassist Guy Berryman), the natural slide is set up by shifting *up* to play the 9th-fret notes with the index finger, which leaves the hand positioned to slide back down naturally to the 6th fret and land back at the starting note. Playing the same passage all in one position (no shift) removes the slide entirely — there's nowhere left to slide *to*. Same logic applies to note/string choice: moving a note to a different string to avoid a position shift (e.g. relocating a G♯ from the 6th fret of the D string to the 11th fret of the A string) also kills the slide, because the string crossing breaks the slick downward glide. When adapting a slide-bearing line, check that both the fingering *and* the string choice still leave a slide-able path before committing to a fingering. Further subtle-slide examples cited: **Bad Decisions** (The Strokes, bassist Nikolaï Fraiture) and **Smells Like Teen Spirit** (Nirvana, bassist Krist Novoselic) — small, subtle slides (not big dramatic ones) that add character without calling attention to themselves; framed as the "chocolate sauce" that turns a plain ("vanilla") line into something more expressive. → BassBuzz, [[Why Beginner - Intermediate Bassists Sound “Karaoke-y”]]
