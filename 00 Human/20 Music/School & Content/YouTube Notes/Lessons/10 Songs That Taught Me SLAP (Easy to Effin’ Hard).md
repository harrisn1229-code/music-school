## What's taught

- **[~0:00–1:20] Song 1 — unnamed (song title not stated aloud; described only as "a super common formula for a simple funky slap line" over a chord progression).** The first full song Josh learned that put slap fundamentals to work: **slapped root notes + popped octaves over a chord progression** — named as an extremely common funk slap formula that recurs throughout the rest of the video. Gives a **3-step recipe for building infinite slap basslines** from this formula: (1) make up a short sequence of a few notes — random, or from a scale (minor pentatonic given as the example); (2) throw in some octaves (the higher repeat of the same note name, always reachable by going up two strings and up two frets); (3) apply slaps and pops to the result. → [[Melodic Slap Lines — Avoiding Disco Octaves]] (this is the "before" starting point that note's disco-octaves refinement builds on).

- **[~1:20–3:15] Song 2 — "Higher Ground" (Red Hot Chili Peppers, covering Stevie Wonder); bassist Flea.** The first slap tune that gave Josh the "I can't believe I'm playing this" feeling. Same slapped-root/popped-octave formula as song 1, but Flea plays it with a **slap-slap-pop pattern in 12/8 time** — a tripletty three-notes-per-beat feel, counted "one two three four five six seven eight nine ten eleven twelve" rather than the far more common two- or four-notes-per-beat groove. Named as a spot where even intermediate/advanced slappers stumble, because the ear defaults to the common feel; the fix given is to learn the **thumb-thumb-pop** shape as a discrete pattern rather than trying to count triplets live. → [[Teeter-Totter Slap-Pop Technique]] (new "From videos" bullet — the 12/8 rhythmic-feel variant of that note's core motion).

- **[~3:15–6:15] Song 3 — "The Awakening" (Les Claypool live bass solo; a cover of a tune by Dexter Redding, Otis Redding's son — title confirmed on-screen per the video).** Discovered as a mislabeled bootleg on LimeWire years before Josh learned its real title/origin. The main lesson: **slap can be genuinely melodic, not just slap-pop-octave gymnastics.** The video's stated reasoning ties directly to how humans perceive melody: close-together notes read as "singable" (compares directly to how anyone can hum "Ode to Joy" from the sheet music's tight vertical note spacing) while wide octave jumps read as exciting/percussive but not melodic. The concrete tool for this: a **thumb-hammer pattern** — slap a note with the thumb (fretted or open string), then hammer on to the next note by smacking it hard enough to ring without a fresh pluck. Claypool's showcase use stacks **three thumb-hammers in a row** mid-bar. Framed explicitly as a speed trick too: it doubles note density without speeding up the slapping hand itself. Practice recipe: pick a scale (minor pentatonic named as the slap-classic choice) and experiment with thumb-hammer starting from different scale degrees to generate original licks. → [[Hammer-Ons and Pull-Offs (Bass)]] (new "From videos" bullet, full mechanics) and [[Melodic Slap Lines — Avoiding Disco Octaves]] (the melody-perception reasoning this song supplies).

- **[~6:15–8:35] Song 4 — unnamed (Larry Graham, called "the grandfather of slap bass"; song not stated aloud).** The claim: everybody in slap's lineage (including Claypool, per the video, despite his reputation as an idiosyncratic player) traces their technique back to Graham. The lesson here isn't a hand mechanic — it's **arrangement**: a song built entirely from great riffs still gets boring after 30 seconds without **contrast** — in space, volume, or tone — between sections. Worked example: a spacious, restrained intro riff (lots of rests, longer notes) is deliberately held back for roughly the first 20 seconds, so that when the intensity escalates — denser notes, a more driving rhythm, Graham's signature aggressive slides — the payoff actually registers as a build rather than just "loud." → [[Slap Bass Dynamics — Contrast, Force, and Restraint]] — new technique note, tool #1.

- **[~8:35–10:15] Song 5 — unnamed (Louis "Thunder Thumbs" Johnson, "Larry Graham's other grandfather-of-slap peer"; song credited to "a small indie artist you probably haven't heard of" — deadpan joke, artist not named).** The lesson: **hit force is a legitimate tone control, not just a volume knob.** Johnson is described as hitting even harder than famously hard slappers like Flea, and the video is explicit this isn't just louder — it's audibly edgier and more aggressive. Directly addresses a common beginner fear ("Josh, am I slapping too hard?") with a flat no — there's no such thing as slapping too hard, only losing control. A/B demo given: the same riff played at medium-volume slap-pop vs. a full "Thunder Thumbs" impression, described as sounding "way more raw and intense." → [[Slap Bass Dynamics — Contrast, Force, and Restraint]], tool #2; also reinforces [[Bass Tone Chain Priority]] Link 1 (harder attack → growlier tone), the general (non-slap) version of the same finding.

- **[~10:15–12:20] Song 6 — unnamed 1979 funk track (bassist Nate Phillips; Josh originally thought it was a Victor Wooten original because he'd heard Wooten quote it in a solo — near-certainly "Ear Food," per the description's period/style match, but not stated aloud in the transcript, so treated as inferred).** Lesson: a **slap-fingers hybrid technique** for less-predictable, contrast-driven lines. The first half of the riff is played fingerstyle (soft, mellow, "not really any slap going on") and the second half switches to slap (edgy, percussive) — described as another variation on Larry Graham's contrast principle, but achieved by switching *technique* rather than dynamics. Flagged as awkward at first regardless of thumb orientation (up or down); drilling sequence given: (1) practice just the position switch, slowly, with no notes; (2) add simple content (root-octave fingerstyle, then the same idea slap-pop); (3) practice switching back and forth; (4) get fancier with the notes and gradually speed up the transition. → [[Slap Bass Dynamics — Contrast, Force, and Restraint]], tool #3.

- **[~12:20–14:00] Song 7 — unnamed (Marcus Miller, with saxophonist Hiram Bullock; Josh learned it in his parents' garage using a Tascam CD slow-downer).** Lesson, framed as "how to slap without getting fired": **ensemble restraint.** For the first six beats, Miller lays back on low root notes while the sax holds the melodic spotlight, joins the melody for a couple of beats, then plays a unison lick with Bullock. The mechanism given is simple and stated directly: keep listening to the rest of the band, and don't play busy slap fills when someone else is singing or carrying the melody. Named as the actual reason so many bassists "blow it" — not bad technique, but tuning out the band because slap is fun to play, which wrecks rhythmic sync and reads as overplaying even when every note is clean. Video points to Miller's *Live in Japan* release for a jaw-dropping bass solo version of the same tune. → [[Slap Bass Dynamics — Contrast, Force, and Restraint]], tool #4.

- **[~14:00–15:40] Song 8 — unnamed (Mark King, Level 42; "the classic Mark King machine-gun triplet"; Josh covered this same riff in an earlier video "10 years ago" per the transcript).** The riff itself is fast, dense hand-slapping — but the video's actual payload is a **practice-methodology quote from a Mark King interview**: *"playing fast really is — start very slow, and get faster."* Mechanism: playing a phrase ridiculously slow gives enough time to notice and correct small inefficiencies in the motion, and those small efficiency gains compound into real speed over time — framed as the real secret behind "impressive-sounding" fast patterns that "aren't actually that hard" once played correctly and gradually sped up. → [[Goal-Tiered Practice and the Self-Taught Bass Commandments]] (new "From videos" bullet, third independent case study for the start-slow checklist).

- **[~15:40–17:15] Song 9 — unnamed drum-and-bass track (Squarepusher; Josh first heard it in college).** Framed as a pure **speed benchmark/self-test**: quarter-note tempo of 190 BPM, with bursts of 16th notes in the slap line — too fast for most slappers to manage even at eighth notes, per the video. The takeaway: if a player can execute near this tempo, their fundamental slap-pop motion is efficient enough that it transfers directly to less extreme, more commonly-played fast lines — named examples given are "Nobody Weird Like Me" (Red Hot Chili Peppers) and "Detroit" (Marcus Miller). Reiterates Mark King's advice from song 8 as the way to actually get there. → [[Goal-Tiered Practice and the Self-Taught Bass Commandments]] (folded into the same bullet as song 8, as a benchmark data point).

- **[~17:15–end] Song 10 — "Classical Thump" (Victor Wooten; an étude Wooten wrote for himself).** The video's climax and Josh's most personally formative song: an étude ("a piece specifically designed for practicing something") built from a smorgasbord of advanced slap patterns well outside the slap-pop-octave vocabulary the video started with. The single most important pattern to learn from it, per the video: **thumb-hammer-pluck**, demonstrated at bar 17 — a three-motion cycle (slap, hammer-on, pop) that never has to repeat the same motion back-to-back. Because none of the three motions needs to immediately recur, interleaving them creates the effect of far greater speed and complexity than any single motion is actually playing at — demoed live, layer by layer: slap-only eighth notes, then add pops (doubles the apparent speed), then add hammer-ons (faster again), with no individual motion actually sped up. Josh credits the song directly with shaping who he became as a bassist, and describes spending roughly eight hours straight with Wooten's transcription book as a teenager, "without eating or peeing," to learn it — offered as his own case study for how genuinely inspiring material sustains practice motivation. → [[Hammer-Ons and Pull-Offs (Bass)]] (new "From videos" bullet, full mechanics) and [[Goal-Tiered Practice and the Self-Taught Bass Commandments]] (the motivation anecdote, same bullet as songs 8–9).
