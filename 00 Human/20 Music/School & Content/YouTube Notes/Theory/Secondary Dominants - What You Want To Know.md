# Secondary Dominants - What You Want To Know

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8dp1upexGCQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8dp1upexGCQ)

## Synopsis
A concise jazz-guitar theory lesson from Jens Larsen walking through secondary dominants from three angles: what they are, how to find them inside a real standard, and how to use them in comping and soloing. He opens from first principles — in C major, the only diatonic dominant is G7 and it only resolves to the tonic, so a secondary dominant "borrows" a dominant 7th for any other scale degree (V7/vi = E7 → Am, V7/IV = C7 → F). The middle section is a full worked analysis of the first half of "There'll Never Be Another You" (key of E♭ major): find every dominant-quality chord in the chart, then test each one against whether it actually resolves down a fifth to determine which are secondary dominants versus decorative/non-functional dominant-quality chords. He explicitly frames this as the general method for learning any standard — spot the secondary dominants first, and the rest of the chart usually reduces to plain diatonic chords. Two practical payoffs get real depth: (1) a clean two-case rule for which scale to solo over a secondary dominant with (major target → that key's major scale; minor target → harmonic minor built off the target), plus a third case (V7/V, "the dominant of the dominant") that gets its own Lydian dominant treatment; and (2) two comping/soling patterns for inserting secondary dominants that aren't already written into a chart — as a passing chord right before a target chord in an existing progression, and as an embellishment dropped into a long static vamp. Efficient, well-sequenced lesson with real chord-chart numbers throughout, not just abstract theory-speak — a strong companion to the vault's more example-dense secondary-dominant videos.

## What's taught
- **The core mechanism** — in a key, there's only one native dominant (V7, resolving to I); a secondary dominant is any other scale degree's own V7 "borrowed" and pointed at that degree instead — worked examples in C major: **E7 → Am** (dominant of vi) and **C7 → Fmaj7** (dominant of IV) *(~0:40–1:40)*
- **Finding secondary dominants inside a real standard, step by step** — worked on the first half of "There'll Never Be Another You" (key of E♭ major): (1) list every dominant-7th-quality chord in the chart — here G7, E♭7, D♭7, F7, B♭7; (2) throw out the chord that's just the plain diatonic V (B♭7 — that's the key's own dominant, not a secondary one); (3) for each remaining dominant chord, check whether it actually **resolves down a fifth** to something — that's the test for whether it's really "functioning" as a dominant, not just dominant-quality by chord spelling *(~3:20–5:30)*
- **G7 → Cm7 identified as V7/vi (secondary dominant for the 6th degree)**, E♭7 → A♭maj7 identified as **V7/IV** (secondary dominant for the 4th degree), and **D♭7 flagged as a false positive** — it's dominant-quality by spelling but doesn't resolve down a fifth (it moves to E♭, not G♭), so it's non-functional and NOT a secondary dominant despite looking like one on paper — a concrete worked example of the resolves-down-a-fifth test failing *(~4:10–4:40)*
- **F7 → B♭7 identified as "the dominant of the dominant" (V7/V)** — flagged as a very common secondary dominant in major-key standards, and specifically common in this position of a form (i.e., recognizing the slot itself speeds up learning new charts that reuse the same form move) *(~4:40–5:00)*
- **Secondary dominants usually don't travel alone — they arrive as full ii–V cadences.** In the same chart: **Dø7 → G7 → Cm** (the ii of the secondary dominant, not just the dominant by itself) and **B♭m7 → E♭7 → A♭maj7** — the practical takeaway stated directly: expect to see the ii chord in front of a secondary dominant just as often as the bare dominant alone → [[The ii-V-I]] *(~5:00–5:20)*
- **Scale/extension choice for a secondary dominant resolving to a MAJOR chord** — treat it exactly like the plain dominant of that target's own key: play the target's major scale, giving a **9 and 13** as the natural available extensions. Worked twice: C7 → Fmaj7 uses F major scale (C7 with 9/13); E♭7 → A♭maj7 in the standard uses A♭ major scale, chosen because "it fits better with the rest of the key" (E♭ major) → [[Secondary Dominants]], [[Extended Chords]] *(~6:00–6:40, restated ~8:00)*
- **Scale/extension choice for a secondary dominant resolving to a MINOR chord** — use the **harmonic minor scale built on the target chord's root**, not the target's natural minor. Worked twice: E7 → Am uses A harmonic minor (E7 with **♭9 and ♭13**); G7 → Cm7 in the standard uses C harmonic minor (same ♭9/♭13 pair — here specifically A♭ for the ♭13 and A♭ again... actually flagged in-video as "the ♭13 is E♭ and the ♭9 is A♭," both diatonic to the home key of E♭ major, which is given as the reason this scale choice sounds so at-home over the secondary dominant) → [[Secondary Dominants]], [[Scale Modes Reference]] § Harmonic Minor *(~6:40–7:30)*
- **Lydian dominant over the "dominant of the dominant" (V7/V)** — a third, distinct case from the major/minor split above: when the secondary dominant IS the five-of-five, especially when it sits for a longer stretch (his example: two full bars), the matching sound is **Lydian dominant** (the ♯11 sound), not the plain major-scale treatment — cited as audible directly in the melody of "Take the A Train" (the ♯11 lands as a melody note) and also present in "There'll Never Be Another You" and "Girl from Ipanema" in the same harmonic slot → [[Scale Modes Reference]] § Lydian Dominant *(~7:30–8:20)*
- **Comping technique 1 — insert a secondary dominant as a passing chord directly in front of its target, inside an existing progression.** Worked over a plain ii–V–I in C (Dm7–G7–Cmaj7): drop a secondary dominant in front of the ii chord itself (A7 → Dm7), AND/OR turn the ii chord into a dominant of the V (D7 → G7), AND add one more secondary dominant ahead of that (E7 → A7) — stackable, and explicitly framed as *not* something a soloist needs to treat as a real solo target, just an added comping color pulling toward the next chord *(~8:40–9:30)*
- **Comping technique 2 — drop a secondary dominant into a long static vamp for movement.** Over an extended Gm7 vamp, insert D7 (V7/i, resolving back to the Gm7) on the weak part of the bar, then resolve back — described as a simple, low-risk way to add motion/interest to a chord that would otherwise just sit; can be extended into a full mini-cadence by adding A7 → D7 → Gm7 (essentially a full ii–V into the vamp chord) *(~9:30–10:20)*
- **Same vamp technique applied to soloing, with a concrete arpeggio substitution** — over a long Gm7 vamp (his example: the kind of 4-bar tonic-minor stretch found in tunes like "Airegin" or "April in Paris"), insert D7 in the solo line on the weak part of the bar using the **F♯ diminished arpeggio as a stand-in for D7** (F♯dim7 shares three notes with D7♭9), resolving back into Gm6/Gm7 — and the same "stack another secondary dominant in front" trick from comping applies here too (A7 → D7 → Gm) *(~10:20–11:20)*
- **General framing / practice philosophy** — don't learn secondary dominants as an isolated puzzle to solve when written out; the payoff is recognizing them by ear in real songs the same way a 12-bar blues becomes instantly playable once you recognize the form — tie theory to actual tunes you know so it becomes something you *hear* coming, not just something you can analyze on paper *(~1:40–3:20, ~11:20–end)*
