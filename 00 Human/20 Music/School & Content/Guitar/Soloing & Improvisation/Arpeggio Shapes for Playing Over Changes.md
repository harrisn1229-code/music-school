# Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes

Part of [[Guitar Positions]] and the guitar side of [[Chord Voicings]] — a system for **outlining a non-diatonic or fast-moving chord progression in a solo**, built around CAGED bar-chord shapes rather than a single scale stretched across the whole progression. Pairs directly with [[Secondary Dominants]] and [[The ii-V-I]]: the harmonic analysis those pages describe (spotting a secondary dominant or a 251 inside a scary chart) is what tells you *which* arpeggio shape to reach for at each chord; this page is the physical, on-the-neck half of that same skill.

## The core idea: arpeggiate each chord in position, not scale over the whole progression
When a progression isn't cleanly diatonic to one key — it's full of secondary dominants and borrowed ii-V units — a single parent scale won't outline the changes convincingly. Instead, address each chord **individually** with its own arpeggio (root-3rd-5th-♭7, in whatever order the position gives you), chosen from whichever **CAGED bar-chord shape sits physically closest** to the shape used for the previous chord, so the hand barely moves between chords instead of jumping to a fresh spot on the neck for every change.

## Picking the shape: closest CAGED grip, not the "textbook" one
Worked example targeting **C7 → E7 → Am7**, all found within a few frets of each other:
- **C7** — root-position arpeggio off a **C-shaped bar chord**. Ascending pattern from the root: **root – 3rd – 5th – ♭7 – root – 3rd – 5th – ♭7 – root**.
- **E7** — not a fresh E-shape from scratch, but the **closest E-shaped bar chord to the C shape just used**. Because of where that shape sits, the lowest available note in the position is the **5th** (B), not the root, so the arpeggio pattern shifts to start there: **5th – ♭7 – root – 3rd – 5th – ♭7 – root – 3rd – 5th**.
- **Am7** — played as a **D-minor CAGED shape moved up the neck** (the relative-minor trick: reuse the CAGED *minor* shape rather than inventing a new fingering for the minor chord). Lowest note is the **♭3** (C), so the pattern starts there: **♭3 – 5th – ♭7 – root – ♭3 – 5th – ♭7 – root – ♭3**.

Same logic continues through any chunk of the progression — e.g. **Gm7 → C7 (or C13) → Fmaj7** (a major ii-V-I) and **Bm7♭5 → E7 → Am7** (a minor ii-V-i): each chord gets the CAGED-family shape that's already closest to hand, not the "default" open-position grip for that chord.

## Two-stage drill: out of time, then in-time voice leading
1. **Out of time first.** Just run each chord's arpeggio shape up and down repeatedly on its own, no backing track — pure finger/eye/ear training on the shape before trying to use it musically.
2. **Then, in time, a voice-leading exercise over the backing track.** Play continuously through the changes, switching arpeggio shape exactly at the chord change, but **stay in the direction you're already moving** rather than resetting to the root each time — if you're ascending through beat 3 of the current chord, keep ascending into the next chord's shape; if descending, keep descending. This is harder than the out-of-time drill but is the actual payoff: it exposes the real chord-tone connections between adjacent chords. Starting the exercise on a different beat/chord-tone each pass produces a different fingering and a different line every time — described as "endless possibilities," and the closest guitar-neck analogue to piano-style voice leading between chord tones (see [[Chord Inversions]]'s smoothest-motion principle, applied here across a solo line instead of a comped chord).

## Chunk-then-connect: the actual "breaking it down" strategy
Work each 2–3-chord harmonic unit (a secondary dominant, a major or minor 251) as its **own small, manageable piece** until it's comfortable in isolation, THEN tie adjacent chunks together into longer phrases, THEN connect the whole progression. This is the practice-side mirror of the harmonic-analysis side on [[Chord Progressions]] and [[Secondary Dominants]] — isolate the small device you can already name, get fluent on its arpeggios alone, then stitch chunks together rather than trying to swallow a long progression whole. "The real key to practicing these long progressions... take these little pieces and work vocabulary in a way that's manageable, then start tying it together."

## Lick-splicing across shapes
Once a chunk's arpeggio shapes are comfortable individually, take fragments from different shapes and recombine them freely instead of always playing a shape start-to-finish — e.g. the top part of a Gm7 shape, the middle part of a C7 shape, and the bottom part of an Fmaj7 shape, spliced into one line. This is presented as how real improvised vocabulary gets built once the raw shapes are under the fingers — the shapes are a roadmap of chord tones, not a fixed lick to play verbatim every time.

## Full five-CAGED-position mapping system (blues I-IV-V, all six strings)
A complementary, simpler-to-start method to the chunk-then-connect approach above: rather than chasing a moving progression chunk by chunk, map **every chord of a static, repeating progression into every one of the five CAGED positions**, learned across all six strings, so from any string in any position you can find the nearest chord tone of the next chord without leaving that position. Worked over a 12-bar blues in **A** (A7-D7-E7, the exact I7-IV7-V7 example on [[Blues Form and Harmony]]): in each of the five CAGED-derived positions, find and drill the dominant-7 arpeggio (root-3rd-5th-♭7, ordered by whichever tone the position's lowest string puts first) for **all three** chords, using that position's CAGED bar-chord grip as the fingering anchor — then play a short demonstration solo confined to that one position, using the arpeggios as target/outline tones for the changes. Because there are only five CAGED shapes total (C-A-G-E-D) and three chords need a shape each in every position, the same handful of physical shapes recur constantly, just repositioned and reassigned to a different chord each time — "there's actually only five shapes, so you're going to repeat yourself a lot." The payoff insight, reached only after all five positions are mapped: the *same* physical shape keeps recurring for *different* chords in different neck locations, so the real skill is recognizing and recalibrating five shapes, not memorizing fifteen. **Practice sequencing tip that generalizes to the chunk method above too:** drill each position/chord in isolation first, then move directly between the position's own chords (A7→D7→E7 and back) until it needs no conscious thought while still tracking exactly which chord tone you're on. Portable to any tune: swap the chord sequence and quality and the same five-position mapping process still applies. ## Single-chord-type ladder: one chord type, all five positions
A third complementary approach — for mastering a **chord *type*** cold across the whole neck rather than outlining a moving progression or mapping one static progression's changes: pick one chord type (major7, minor7, dominant7, diminished7, half-diminished, minor6…), map its arpeggio shape in **all five CAGED-derived positions**, and run the same five-step ladder on each position in turn before moving to the next chord type:
1. **Map root-to-root** — straight up and down the shape, repeating/pausing on roots, pure fingering/positional mapping.
2. **Drill a melodic pattern off each chord tone** — from the lowest chord tone, up to the next and back, off every chord tone in the shape, seamlessly in time.
3. **Improvise with constant chord-tones-only notes** — an unbroken stream (straight eighths work well), no stopping, to test whether the shape is truly internalized; slow down when it breaks.
4. **Improvise with musical phrasing, still chord-tones-only** — real musical statements (idea, space, answering idea — "measure on, measure rest" is a good default), landing phrases on the root for cohesion.
5. **Add non-chord tones freely** — once chord tones are automatic, explore every other note in the position by ear alone; a note that sounds "wrong" is usually about *where* it landed rhythmically, not that it's unusable. Deliberately scale/mode-name-agnostic — the philosophy is that ear-driven exploration around solid chord tones is a more advanced skill than scale recall, and it produces modal-sounding lines without needing to name the mode.

Practice steps 3–5 against a static looped vamp of the target chord so every added note is judged against a real sustained harmonic context. Contrast with the two methods above: those connect *different* chords across a moving progression; this ladder drills *one* chord type across positions before ever introducing a second chord — complementary halves of full arpeggio fluency. Distinct also from [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]'s ladder, which targets a whole moving progression's downbeats/phrasing rather than one static chord shape position by position.

## Daily diatonic arpeggio drilling (box, vertical, two-octave)
A fourth complementary method — distinct from the three above in that it doesn't target navigating a *moving* progression at all; instead it's a **daily internalization drill for every diatonic arpeggio of one static key**, meant to be repeated across every key and mode over time rather than solved once. Worked over **B♭ major** (2 flats — B♭, E♭):
1. **Horizontal box-position pass.** Place all four fretting fingers on the neck to form a fixed box shape; moving outside the box is the cue to change strings, not to stretch a finger. Starting on the low E string, play a one-octave **B♭maj7** arpeggio, then work through every other diatonic arpeggio reachable within that same box up to the end of the fretboard; when the box runs out of room, shift the whole box up (or down) one fret to the **next diatonic arpeggio in the key** (B♭maj7 → Cm7 → …), again starting on the low E string, and repeat for every arpeggio of the key. Vary the drill by direction: all-descending instead of all-ascending, or alternating (ascend one arpeggio, descend the next).
2. **Vertical, single-string-set pass, starting on every chord tone.** Instead of moving horizontally within a box, run one arpeggio up and down a single string set, starting not just from the root but from **every chord tone in turn — root, 3rd, 5th, 7th** — repeated across the E, A, D and G strings for full coverage. The starting-tone shapes chain vertically into two-octave range simply by repeating the same one-octave shape an octave higher (e.g. a two-octave B♭maj7 starting on the 3rd is the root-position-from-the-3rd shape, doubled).
3. **Two-octave patterns with named starting fingers.** Full two-octave arpeggio shapes anchored to a specific starting finger per starting chord tone, opening up more of the fretboard than a root-only shape. Common starting-finger defaults (transferable rule of thumb): **root → 2nd finger, 3rd → 1st finger, 5th → 2nd finger, 7th → 3rd finger** — other fingerings are possible and will alter the resulting shape, these are just the defaults.
4. **Visualize, don't rely on muscle memory** — actively picture each arpeggio's shape and chord-tone identity within each box/position rather than letting the fingers run on autopilot; the stated payoff is that this trains the *ear* for the key, giving instant access to its essential sounds, not just finger recall.

Framed explicitly as never "finished" — an ongoing, lifelong practice meant to run daily across every key and mode, not a drill to solve once. Complements rather than replaces the three methods above: those three target *navigating a moving progression*; this one targets *fully internalizing every diatonic arpeggio of one static key* so the shapes are already automatic before they're ever needed against a moving chord. ## Half-step approach on every arpeggio tone (not just one target)
A bebop-era spin on soloing off an arpeggio shape: instead of approaching a single chord tone, precede **every note of the arpeggio** with a half-step approach from below, resolving the final approached note into the target chord's 3rd. Common over dominant chords. *(general starting point: approaching every tone of a G major arpeggio by a half step, then resolving similarly into the 3rd of the I chord.)*
- **Donald Byrd (solo on "Lex")** — walk up a **dominant 9th arpeggio**, land a **five-note enclosure** on the dominant chord's **7th**, then apply the half-step-approach-per-tone device to the same dominant-chord arpeggio.
- **Wes Montgomery (solo on "D Natural Blues")** — opens on a classic Wes minor-9 shape (a device he leans on often over minor 9 chords), adds an **enclosure of the 3rd**, then reaches an **altered dominant** (♯5, ♯9 added) outlined largely as an **E♭ major triad over the dominant root** — finished with the half-step-approach-per-tone device applied to each triad note.
