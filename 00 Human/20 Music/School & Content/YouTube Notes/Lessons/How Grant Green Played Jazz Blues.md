# How Grant Green Played Jazz Blues

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BNQiLjJ4N4w)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BNQiLjJ4N4w)

## Synopsis

Jack Ruch transcribes and analyzes two Grant Green blues-solo excerpts — the opening chorus of "Miss Ann's Tempo" and the first chorus of his solo on "Number One Green Street," both swing/jazz blues in B♭ — to extract reusable **jazz-blues vocabulary** rather than just admire the playing. A second Jack Ruch video, [[Transcribing Licks And Making Them Your Own]], revisits an overlapping stretch of the same two solos plus a third tune ("Blues for Willarine") with a methodology focus — how to rework a transcribed lick into your own, including a minor-third/half-step transposition device not covered here. This is a direct companion to [[The Key To Understanding A Jazz Blues]] (same channel, same B♭ jazz-blues form, same 6-2-5-1 turnaround, and that video names Grant Green as one of its two headline jazz-blues players alongside Wes Montgomery) — where that video builds one worked line from scratch, this one reverse-engineers Green's actual recorded vocabulary over the identical harmonic terrain and finds the same core devices independently: hit the ♭9 over the VI7 chord for the altered/bebop color, and chromatically approach the tonic's chord tone to close the turnaround. The most valuable transferable idea isn't a single lick but the **method** stated at both the start and the end: learn a handful of these vocabulary phrases, and over time — through repetition and by tying fragments together in different combinations rather than replaying them verbatim — they blend into your own playing and start appearing on their own in improvisation. Ruch also states a concrete on-ramp for anyone newer to this: learn the arpeggios of the underlying chords first, and the licks in this video will turn up naturally inside those chord tones.

## What's taught

- **Opening-phrase blues vocabulary over the I chord (Miss Ann's Tempo, bars 1–4, B♭).** Starting from a chord-shape fingering: slide the **minor 3rd up to the major 3rd** (D♭→D over B♭), then the **5th up to the 6th up to the root** (F→G→B♭) — a classic blues-opener slide sequence. Then descend into **B♭ minor pentatonic/blues-scale** territory: **♭7 down to the 5th** (A♭→F), then a slide from the **4th up to the ♭5** (E♭→E, the blue-note passing tone) coming back down to the **♭7** — the whole thing framed as "a really, really classic and common way to start out a blues solo," i.e. reusable opening vocabulary, not tune-specific. → [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]] (the ♭5-as-passing-tone device, the minor-3rd→major-3rd blend)
- **The I→IV chromatic pivot: the major 3rd of the I chord becomes the ♭7 of the IV chord a half step below.** Named "a really common thing that he does a lot": over B♭ (I), slide up to the **major 3rd (D)**, then simply **drop that same note a half step to D♭** — which reads as the plain scale tone over B♭ but *becomes the ♭7 of the E♭ (IV) chord* the instant the harmony changes underneath it. One note, reinterpreted by the chord change rather than replayed — a cheap, guitar-idiomatic way to nail the I→IV transition without a scale change. Confirmed in **both** transcribed excerpts (same device, same D→D♭ half step, in "Number One Green Street" too) — genuinely part of Green's core vocabulary, not a one-off. → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]], [[Blues Form and Harmony]]
- **The VI7 turnaround approached with maximum simplicity: just the root.** Getting from I back around to the 6-2-5-1 turnaround (B♭7 → G7 → Cm7 → F7 → B♭7), Green's move into the VI chord (G7) is nothing more than **landing on its root, G** — no decoration, immediately followed by the harder vocabulary below. A useful reminder that not every chord change needs to be outlined elaborately; sometimes the plainest possible landing is the right call right before a denser phrase. → [[Turnarounds]] § *VI7*
- **The signature 6-2-5-1 line: target the ♭9 over the VI7, dense with enclosures and chromatic pedal tones.** Over G7 (VI), the line targets the **♭9 (A♭)** — called out directly as "a really nice sound" — built from "a lot of enclosures and chromatic kind of pedal-tone stuff." This is the same altered-dominant device [[The Key To Understanding A Jazz Blues]] extracted from Green's playing in the abstract (leap to the ♭9 for "a really cool altered, very jazz-bebop type of sound"); seeing it confirmed inside an actual transcribed Green solo (twice — it recurs almost identically in "Number One Green Street"'s 6-2-5-1) is good evidence this is genuinely a Green signature, not a one-off Ruch demonstration. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]] § *Target the guide tones* / *Enclosures*, [[Turnarounds]] § *VI7*
- **A distinct "common Grant Green / bebop phrase" bridging the IV-chord lick into the G7 line**, taught deliberately slow first, then at tempo with swing feel, and described as a phrase type Green "reinterprets and rearranges" across many recordings rather than a single fixed lick — Ruch's stated takeaway is to learn a few of these and start combining fragments of them freely once they're under your fingers, rather than memorizing them start-to-finish. → [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]] § *A more literal alternative*
- **2-5-1 arpeggio approach into the IV chord (Number One Green Street, bars 5–6).** To move from I to IV (B♭ to E♭), Green plays a lick built straight out of the **ii chord of the target IV** — a 2-5-1 of E♭ is **F minor 7 – B♭7 – E♭**, and the lick over that first bar is "right out of an F minor seven" arpeggio; Ruch's framing: "you can play any idea over that chord as kind of part of a 2-5-1" — i.e. treat the ii-chord arpeggio as the target, not a scale over the whole two-bar stretch. Same I→IV chromatic-pivot device as above shows up again on landing at E♭ (the ♭7/D♭ note). → [[Chord Substitution]] § *4. Interpolating a ii in front of any dominant*, [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]
- **Basic root-3rd-4th-5th-root-♭7 blues vocabulary over the I chord**, called out explicitly as "pretty standard blues stuff" — the plain scalar/chord-tone line Green opens "Number One Green Street" with after a rhythmic pickup phrase, before the more elaborate 2-5-1 and 6-2-5-1 material. Useful as the floor: not every bar needs the dense vocabulary above. → [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]]
- **Chromatic walk-up into the I chord's 3rd as the turnaround's closing move (Number One Green Street).** After the same G7 ♭9 → Cm7 arpeggio-triad → F7 line as in "Miss Ann's Tempo," Green closes the turnaround back to B♭7 by **walking up chromatically to the 3rd of the I chord** — a three-(or-so)-note chromatic approach landing squarely on a guide tone, the mirror image of the ♭9-then-chromatic-walk-to-root device on the VI7 above, now used to resolve the whole form rather than to color one chord. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]] § *Approach notes*, [[Guide Tones]]
- **Rhythmic pickup phrase leading into the top of the 12-bar form** — "Number One Green Street" opens with a pickup line that plays *into* bar 1 rather than starting squarely on it, a phrasing habit worth noting on its own (arriving at the downbeat mid-phrase rather than beginning cold on it). → [[Blues Lead Phrasing and Touch]]
- **Practice method, stated twice — once as a starting recommendation, once as the payoff:** (1) "If you just sit and learn the arpeggios to these chords, you can find these licks right inside of these chords... learn the arpeggios, and then learn some vocabulary like this, and see how it fits together" — arpeggio fluency as the on-ramp to hearing/finding this style of line, aimed explicitly at players newer to the style. (2) The long-game payoff: transcribe and sit with solos like these, and "over time it starts to blend in and become a part of your playing and starts to pop out in your improvising" — internalization through repetition and recombination, not memorization for its own sake, echoing (in a more literal, less concept-naming form) the transcribe-then-recombine method on [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]]. → [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]], [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]]

No plugin, effect, mixing, or sound/tone-selection content — pure instrument/theory pedagogy (guitar improvisation vocabulary transcribed from a specific jazz-blues player, over the exact 12-bar jazz-blues form already documented in [[Blues Form and Harmony]]).
