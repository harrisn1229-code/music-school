# KSHMR MUSIC PRODUCTION Q&A - Kicks, loudness, catchy melodies, vocalists, writer's block, saying no

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdipHMBxPlA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdipHMBxPlA)

## Synopsis

A rapid-fire Q&A with KSHMR covering six different questions in under 11 minutes: sound design vs. sound *selection*, how to build a coherent sonic palette by saying no to samples that don't fit, what actually breaks writer's block, how to pick and work with a vocalist, how to write a catchy melody (the pentatonic-scale thread running through modern pop/EDM hooks), and two mixing questions — getting a kick to cut through a drop and pushing loudness without audible clipping. The throughline across almost every answer is restraint and constraint: taste is expressed by what you refuse to use, not by what plugin you reach for, and the same idea shows up again at the end when he argues a strong idea with a rough mix will always beat a polished mix built on a weak idea.

## Techniques

- **Sound design is in service of taste, not a goal on its own** — learning to tweak a synth (reduce noise, shorten decay/release) just gives you more *options* to realize a palette you've already decided on; the skill itself isn't the point.
- **Build a sound palette by deciding what to say no to, not by collecting more samples** — pick a specific reference feel (his example: a "Peru meets psy-trance" hybrid), then actively reject anything outside it — a clean snare gets passed over for ethnic percussion that fits the Peru side, and only the hardest psy-trance sounds get used rather than the whole genre's palette; constraint is what produces a recognizable, personal sound, and producers with a distinctive signature are reliably using fewer, more deliberately-chosen sources rather than pulling from everything available. → [[Sound Palette]] — KSHMR (Sol State), [KSHMR MUSIC PRODUCTION Q&A](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdipHMBxPlA)
- **Don't treat writer's block as something to rest through — sit with it until it breaks.** His stated process: no "spa day" or self-care detour, just sitting in front of the computer, and if nothing comes, going to bed and repeating the same process the next day rather than stepping away from the work entirely.
- **What actually breaks writer's block is finishing something good, not waiting it out** — likens it to scoring a touchdown: once you remember what landing a genuinely good idea feels like, that memory pulls you back into a productive creative mode and generates momentum for the next idea. → [[Committing and Printing]] — KSHMR (Sol State), [KSHMR MUSIC PRODUCTION Q&A](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdipHMBxPlA)
- **Pick a vocalist for their songwriting, not just their voice** — most people know someone who can sing well, but a great voice isn't what makes a great song; good taste in melody/lyric writing is the harder-to-find, more important trait, with a strong voice as a bonus on top.
- **Work repeatedly with the same vocalist early on — it accelerates your growth as a producer faster than working solo.** Producing for one artist across many songs forces you out of working alone and into building every decision around someone else's vocal and the song you're making together, which teaches faster than isolated bedroom production; KSHMR credits his early years producing all of Dev's material (including "G6" and "Bass Down Low") as directly shaping how he now approaches his own music. → [[Melody Writing]] — KSHMR (Sol State), [KSHMR MUSIC PRODUCTION Q&A](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdipHMBxPlA)
- **Write the melody against chords you already like, rather than starting from a bare pitch idea** — lay down a chord progression first, then write melodic lines that move against it; this is the practical starting point behind most catchy top-lines.
- **The pentatonic scale is a recurring thread behind catchy radio/EDM melodies** — skipping the 4th and 7th scale degrees of the major scale (in C major: no F, no B) leaves the five-note pentatonic set that shows up repeatedly in hooks people call "catchy"; cited examples are Marshmello's "Happier" and Porter Robinson & Madeon's "Shelter." Paired with playing around the chord tone underneath and building a theme via repetition with variation rather than constant new material. → [[Melody Writing]] — KSHMR (Sol State), [KSHMR MUSIC PRODUCTION Q&A](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdipHMBxPlA)
- **Raise a kick's transient level specifically, not its overall volume, to make it cut through a drop.** Turning up a sound's whole volume eats headroom other elements need; boosting just the transient portion makes the ear latch onto the hit without meaningfully growing how much space it occupies in the mix — his practical kick-in-a-drop recipe pairs this with hard sidechaining everything else (especially clearing the kick's first few milliseconds — reach for an LFO-style tool if a compressor's sidechain is too relaxed to fully duck that opening transient). → [[Transient Master]] — KSHMR (Sol State), [KSHMR MUSIC PRODUCTION Q&A](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdipHMBxPlA)
- **Loudness without audible clipping starts with leveling individual sounds so none of them forces the limiter to overwork** — if the kick sits disproportionately louder than everything else, it's what triggers distortion before the rest of the mix even reaches the limiter; balance the loudest offenders down first. Simplify down to a few core elements (kick, bass, one or two more) each given clean individual space, so the compressor/limiter downstream has an easy job rather than fighting low-mid muddiness built up from too many competing sounds — multiband compression and heavier "sausage"-style saturation are extra mastering-stage tools layered on top of that groundwork, not a substitute for it. → [[Clipping and Loudness]] — KSHMR (Sol State), [KSHMR MUSIC PRODUCTION Q&A](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdipHMBxPlA)
- **Mixing can't rescue a weak idea — "you can't polish a turd."** Looking back at his own catalog, the songs that mattered to his career weren't the best-mixed ones; "Lychee" (G6) had real technical flaws in the mix and still worked, because the idea underneath it was strong. The idea is the foundation everything else, including the mix, sits on top of.
