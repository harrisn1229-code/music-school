> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **The idea was never bad — it was alone**
> Loop-itis is a misdiagnosis, not a skill gap, and it's the single biggest reason projects die in the folder.
>
> **Q2.** **A B section — not a new idea**
> A good idea missing the one other idea that points out what it is you're doing still sounds like a bad idea when it's on its own.
>
> **Q3.** **No amount of subtractive variation on one loop sustains a song — a second loop is unavoidable**
> *Rubric (5):* says subtraction alone won't carry a full song, so a genuinely second loop becomes necessary (2) · says most attempts at that second loop will be discardable (2) · says the discards are the cost of the method rather than evidence it's failing (1)
> The ceiling is worth stating plainly because the alternative is discovering it halfway through a project and concluding you've lost the ability to write. Scheduled absence buys a lot — enough to turn eight bars into a couple of minutes — but a song needs somewhere else to go, and eventually that means writing material that didn't exist. Most of what you write at that point will not survive. Expecting the mediocre attempts is part of the technique: you're not auditioning yourself, you're generating candidates, and the ratio is supposed to be bad. The failure mode isn't writing four B sections that don't work, it's stopping after the first one and calling the whole loop dead.
>
> **Q4.** **Duplicate the drums and strip everything else · write a complementary part · test the return**
> Bass, keys and melody all come out in step one, so the B section is written over the groove that's already working rather than against a blank clip.
>
> **Q5.** **Different chords · opposite density · different mood**
> A sad, loose and simple B under a full, happy and soulful A is the shape. Complementary means it answers the A section, not that it's merely unlike it.
>
> **Q6.** **The return — playing out of B back into A should feel like coming home**
> That lift on the way back is what you're listening for, not whether B is good in isolation.
>
> **Q7.** **Because the right B section makes the A section better — if the return is flat, B isn't finished**
> A B section that's merely different has done half the job. The one you keep is the one that recontextualizes what came before it, which is why the test is the step people skip and the step that decides everything.
>
> **Q8.** **The relative key and the withheld chord — both solve "new but not random"**
> They approach it from opposite ends: one guarantees the notes still fit, the other guarantees the arrival is unfamiliar.
>
> **Q9.** **Up three semitones from a minor root — identical scale tones, so the return can't clash**
> The relative major of A minor is C. Same seven notes, different centre, which means the contrast is real and the way back resolves cleanly without any planning.
>
> **Q10.** **Audit the chords already used, then open the bridge on a diatonic one the song never played**
> *Rubric (5):* describes auditing the chords already used across verse and chorus (1) · says to identify the diatonic chords the song has never played (2) · says to open the bridge on one of those, since its arrival alone signals somewhere new (2)
> In practice it's a five-minute job on a song you've already written. Go through the verse and the chorus and write down every chord that appears — say the song has used the I, the ii, the iii, the V and the vi and has never once played the IV. That unused chord is a resource the song has been saving without meaning to, because the listener has heard everything else in this context and not that. Open the bridge on it and the section announces itself before a single instrument changes. It costs nothing because it requires no new sound, no new tempo and no modulation plan — the chord is already diatonic, so it can't clash, and the effect lands on the first beat of the section rather than waiting for an arrangement change to arrive.
