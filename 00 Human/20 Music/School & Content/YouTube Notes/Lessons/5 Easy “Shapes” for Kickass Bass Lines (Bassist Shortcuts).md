## What's taught

### Shapes as chunking — the psychology
- A **shape** is a visual/finger pattern on the neck representing a fixed set of pitch *relationships* rather than fixed pitches — because it's movable to any root, the same shape reproduces the same music starting on a different note every time, letting a player move around the neck "without having to frantically think about note names and theory."
- Grounded in psychologist **George Miller's** concept of **chunking**: combining a bunch of individually hard-to-remember bits of information into one larger, easier-to-remember chunk. Demoed with a random letter sequence (hard to memorize) rearranged into recognizable words made of the identical letters (suddenly easy) — same information, chunked differently.
- **The "Another One Bites the Dust" proof:** two completely different sets of notes both read unmistakably as the same bassline, because it's the *relationships between the notes* — not the specific notes themselves — that the ear tracks. On bass, that relationship is represented visually as a shape, and because of how bass is tuned, a shape can be moved literally anywhere on the neck and still produce "the same music just starting on a different note."

### Why bass specifically is built for shapes
- **Piano:** five black keys and seven white keys per octave — an asymmetric keyboard — means moving a shape around doesn't always reproduce the same music.
- **Vocalists:** no visual reference at all.
- **Guitar:** the extra two strings (compared to bass) are tuned differently from the rest, so a shape doesn't always give the same result when moved.
- **Bass:** implied contrast (not stated as a separate rule in so many words, but the throughline of the whole video) — every shape demoed below is explicitly described as movable "anywhere on the neck," unlike piano, voice, or guitar.

### Root notes: the baseline before any shape
- **Root notes come from whatever chord the guitar or keyboard player is holding** — playing just those roots along with the chord changes ("chugging") is already "a perfectly valid bassline" on its own.
- Worked baseline demo over an **E, B, C♯ minor, A** progression (a "Don't Stop Believin'"-style chord sequence), played first as plain roots only.
- **Shapes open up the next step:** once roots are covered, a shape gives a structured way to visually find *non-root* notes to sprinkle around each root, for a more original/distinctive line than roots alone.

### Shape 1 — Root-fifth (works ~90% of the time, quality-blind)
- **Fretboard geometry:** go up a string and up two frets from the root to land on the fifth.
- **Why it's the default:** "the fifth is a great place to go because it adds some movement and spice to the bassline without making the harmony sound complicated," and it works on major or minor chords without needing to know which.
- **Worked demo:** "Rikki Don't Lose That Number" (Steely Dan) — played through in real time as a follow-along.
- **The "magic dots" mental model, stated directly:** "how I'm actually experiencing this as I play is... the notes in the shape are lit up, like there are magical colored dots on the fretboard... I don't need to think about what notes to play every time, I just imagine the dots of the shape and then I just plug my fingers onto those spots." It's fine not to know the other note's name while playing it — the shape does the work.
- **Jam demo over an A, D, E chord progression:** stamping the identical root-fifth shape onto each root, with nothing else changed, produces a usable rock-flavored line, a country-flavored line, and a more reggae-flavored line — three distinct genre feels from the one shape.

### Shape 2 — Octave (safe anytime, adds movement without new harmony)
- **Fretboard geometry:** from a root, go up two strings and up two frets to land on the octave — worked concretely (F on the 1st fret of the E string → another F on the 3rd fret of the D string; E♭ on the 6th fret of the A string → another E♭ on the 8th fret of the G string).
- **Why it's "safe anytime":** an octave shares the same note name as the root, one register up, so it adds movement without introducing any new harmonic color to clash against the chord.
- **Bonus utility — a note-naming shortcut, not just a bassline device:** if you already know the name of one note, moving the octave shape tells you the name of the other note for free — worked example: knowing the 3rd fret of the A string is C means the octave shape (up two strings, up two frets) tells you the 5th fret of the G string is also C.
- **Worked demo:** "Hot Pants" (James Brown).
- **Jam demo over the same A, D, E progression:** a slap-funk-flavored line, a disco-flavored line, and a pop-rock-flavored line, again all from the one shape relocated to each root.
- BassBuzz plugs the **Beginner to Badass** course (BassBuzz.com) here as where these and further shapes get taught alongside the underlying theory of why they work.

### Shape 3 — Major triad (1-3-5 of the major scale)
- **Fretboard geometry:** the first, third, and fifth notes of the major scale built on the root, forming a compact three-note shape.
- **Theory-optional by design:** "you actually don't even need to know your major scale in order to use this shape" — the shape is memorized and pattern-matched to the root directly; a beginner-scales video is pointed to as optional further context, not a prerequisite.
- **The piano contrast, made concrete:** a C major triad and an E♭ major triad are completely different hand shapes and fingerings on piano; on bass they're the *identical* shape, just relocated to a different fret — stated directly as "way easier on bass than on other instruments."
- **Worked demo:** "Three Little Birds" (Bob Marley).
- **Jam demo over A, D, E:** a walking bassline, a "little more ska-y" line, and a classical Alberti-bass-style broken-triad line, all built from the one shape.

### Shape 4 — Minor triad (1-3-5 of the minor scale)
- **Fretboard geometry:** the first, flat-third, and fifth notes of the minor scale built on the root — the same physical shape as the major triad with the third flatted.
- **Open-string equivalence demoed directly:** worked in E minor first from the 7th fret of the A string, then again using the open low E string instead — "it's the same shape, even though one of the notes is open instead of fretted."
- **Worked demo:** "Pipeline" (the Stevie Ray Vaughan / Dick Dale version).

### The root-movement-is-also-a-shape idea (bridge into Shape 5)
- Once a chord progression's roots are located once — A, D, and E in the running jam example above — the *movement between those roots* itself traces a fixed, memorizable visual pattern on the neck (a triangle, in this case), so a player doesn't need to re-find each root from scratch on every repeat, just recall the root-movement shape.
- **Named directly as "shapes layered on top of other shapes":** thanks to this chunking effect, a bassist only has to remember **two things** — the root-movement shape, and whichever bassline shape (root-fifth, octave, major triad) is layered on top of it at each point — rather than every individual note in the line.

### Shape 5 — "Supershapes" (combining/switching shapes within a bassline)
- **Central claim:** it's far more common to change shapes throughout a bassline than to stay on one shape the entire way through.
- **Worked demo, full breakdown — "Gangsters" (The Specials):** over A minor → A♭ major → A minor (a bar's rest) → A minor → A♭ major → A minor → root-fifth-on-A (repeating for the second half of the phrase), the line runs the **minor triad shape** on the A minor chord, the **major triad shape** on the A♭ major chord, and the **root-fifth shape** on the return to A — three different shapes from this video, combined in one real bassline.
- **The general recipe extracted from that example:** given a chord progression's qualities (here, A minor, A♭ major, A minor, A♭ major, A minor), root-fifth (Shape 1) is usable across all of them regardless of quality; the octave shape (Shape 2) is a further option not used in this particular bassline but available; the minor triad shape belongs on the minor chords and the major triad shape on the major chords, once the quality is actually known.

### The 3-step process for learning any new shape
Stated directly as generalizing past these five starter shapes to any future shape — scales, chords, arpeggios, or basslines — "there are a gazillion more to learn":
1. **Nail the fingering** — work out which fingers to use, and get comfortable fretting/plucking cleanly at different speeds.
2. **Memorize the shape** — start seeing the "magic dots" on the neck: look at how far apart the notes are in frets, and give the shape a name if that helps it stick.
3. **Learn the context** — figure out *when* you can use the shape; this is where theory and ear training come in. Shapes don't replace theory and ear training, "they just bring them into the real world, visually" — and learning context is framed as a genuinely never-ending journey, not a box to check once.

### Closing demo — superimposing a shape onto a scale
Taking the root-fifth shape (Shape 1) and playing it against every root of a minor scale, rather than just one static root, is shown to unlock a much wider set of playable lines from the same small two-note shape — "layering one shape on top of another shape" one more time, as a closing example of how far a small starter vocabulary keeps paying off once combined with scale material.
