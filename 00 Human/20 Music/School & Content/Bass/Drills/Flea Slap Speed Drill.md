# Flea Slap Speed Drill

A single two-bar octave pattern, chromatically walked up the neck to the 12th fret and back down, taught by BassBuzz as the speed drill Flea himself uses — Flea's own on-camera explanation of his slap/pop technique opens the video, then BassBuzz breaks the drill down and adds a tempo ladder and three composition recipes for turning it into original basslines. Distinct from — and a good speed-focused companion to — [[Slap Bass 5-Level Daily Drill]]: that drill layers *new technique elements* (pops, ghosts, hammer-ons, phrasing) one at a time; this one holds the technique constant (pure octave slap-pop) and drills pure **speed and endurance** by walking one fixed pattern chromatically across the whole neck.

*(Minutes is a general starting point, not from the source — the video explicitly does not play the full drill on camera "because it takes forever," so treat 10 minutes as a starting session length and extend once endurance allows.)*

## Flea's own technique, in his words

Before teaching the drill, BassBuzz cuts to Flea explaining his own slap/pop mechanics directly (from a separate clip): **"strike the low string with your thumb with the bone that's in the middle of your thumb,"** then pop by hooking the **middle finger** underneath the string and pulling up — Flea's own phrasing: *"I will slap down with my thumb and then with my middle finger it will go underneath the D string and pull up."* Full general mechanics (angle, anchoring, muting) are already covered in [[Slap Thumb Technique — Angle, Anchoring, and Muting]] — this is Flea's own worked description of the same slap-then-pop motion that note's "Fix 1–3" break down mechanically, plus BassBuzz's own "lightning round" refresher layered on top: fresh round-wound strings, low-to-medium action, tone knob up, rotate the forearm at the elbow to strike with the side of the thumb, set a finger under the target string, rotate the forearm back up to pop. That refresher motion — rotate down, strike, set finger, rotate up — is the same single-cycle idea as [[Teeter-Totter Slap-Pop Technique]], described here in Flea-specific language.

## The pattern

A repeating **two-bar** phrase, all notes played as **octaves** (the same note name, one octave apart):

- Ignoring the very first note, the pattern is a simple repeating cell: **pop–thumb–thumb, pop–thumb–thumb, pop–thumb–thumb...**, ending on **pop–thumb**, where that final thumb doubles as the first note of the next two-bar repeat — so mechanically it's really one repeating cell played continuously, not two distinct bars.
- Every note is an **octave** — the low note plus its higher repeat, found by going **up two strings and up two frets** from the low note (or, working the other direction, **down two strings and down two frets**). This is the same octave-shape fact documented in [[Octave Technique — Split-Finger Fretting and Duck-Wrist Prevention]]; see that note for the wrist/finger-split mechanics of jumping between the two notes.
- **Fretting rule: use index and pinky, not index and ring**, once you're past the open string (the open-E starting position obviously needs no fretting-hand octave note yet). BassBuzz flags this explicitly because some students default to index-ring — it "wastes energy" for this shape; index-pinky is the efficient choice. *(Not yet documented elsewhere in the vault — new detail from this video, distinct from the low/high split-finger anchoring in [[Octave Technique — Split-Finger Fretting and Duck-Wrist Prevention]], which is about alternating between two anchored fingers across a scale run rather than which two fingers hold a single stationary octave shape.)*
- **Chromatic walk:** play the two-bar cell **twice** starting on the open E-string octave, then move the whole shape up **one fret** and play it twice again, and so on — Flea takes this all the way up to the **12th fret**, then works his way back down the same way.

## Four things to watch while drilling

1. **Accuracy** — are you hitting only the strings you mean to hit?
2. **Consistency** — are all the notes ringing out clearly for their full duration, not getting choked/muted short?
3. **Breathing** — by the halfway point of a full pass most players have stopped breathing normally; a genuine practice reminder, not a joke aside.

## Why this exact pattern — it's a real Flea line, not just an exercise

BassBuzz points out the drill isn't an arbitrary made-up shape — it's lifted straight from real Red Hot Chili Peppers basslines:

- **"Get Up and Jump"** (from the Chili Peppers' 1984 self-titled debut) uses almost exactly this pop-thumb-thumb pattern, minus a small hammer-on at the end of the line.
- A related variant — the same **thumb-thumb-pop** cell, just starting on the thumb instead of the pop — shows up in **"Dark Necessities"** and in the Chili Peppers' cover of **"Higher Ground."**
- These are name-checked as pattern matches only (no tab/settings given in this video) — general reference for ear-training/pattern-recognition in the spirit of [[Steal Like a Bassist — Ear-Training from Known Melodies]], not full song breakdowns; no dedicated song notes created from this alone.

## Turning the drill into original basslines — three recipes

Once the pattern and tempo ladder are solid, BassBuzz gives three ways to use the same rhythmic cell to write actual lines/solos rather than just drilling the fixed octave shape. All three keep the **same pop-thumb-thumb rhythm** and swap what pitches go where — a sibling approach to the recipe-driven composition methods in [[Bass Pentatonic Riff-Writing Recipe]] and [[The Bingo Method — Number-Pattern Riff Generation]], but built specifically on the slap/pop rhythmic skeleton rather than a plain scale walk or number sequence.

1. **Static bass.** Leave every **slap** (thumb note) on the open E string — that's the fixed "static" bass note — and move only the **pops** around a scale of choice. Worked example: E minor pentatonic (E, G, A, B, D), one simple shape to frame the notes in, popping a randomly-chosen scale note on each pop while the thumb stays locked to open E.
2. **Moving octaves.** Instead of anchoring the slap to one fixed note, move the whole **octave shape** (both the slap note and its pop octave) around the scale — grab a lower-register run of the same E minor pentatonic scale for the slap notes, then use the same two-strings-up/two-frets-up shape to find each note's higher octave for the corresponding pop. Same rhythm throughout, just a moving root instead of a static one.
3. **Be the Flea.** The bigger lesson under the first two recipes: come up with your **own** one-or-two-bar combination of slaps and pops that sounds good to you, and drill *that* up to speed the same way — moving it around the bass the same way Flea's original pattern gets moved. The real takeaway isn't copying Flea's exact notes, it's **copying the mindset** he used to invent the drill in the first place — that's what actually transfers into being able to write original speed drills and slap lines later.

## How to run it

- Nail the mechanical fundamentals first — this drill assumes the [[Slap Thumb Technique — Angle, Anchoring, and Muting]] and [[Teeter-Totter Slap-Pop Technique]] basics are already in hand; it's a speed/endurance drill layered on top of clean technique, not a substitute for building it.
- Start at ~70 BPM, walk the full chromatic range (open through 12th fret and back) at that tempo before pushing the metronome up in small 2–3 BPM steps.
- Once the chromatic walk feels solid, spend separate reps on recipe 1 and 2 above using a scale relevant to whatever you're working on ([[Bass Scale Shapes — Major, Minor, Pentatonic & Blues]] for shape reference), then try recipe 3 with an original 1–2 bar cell of your own.
