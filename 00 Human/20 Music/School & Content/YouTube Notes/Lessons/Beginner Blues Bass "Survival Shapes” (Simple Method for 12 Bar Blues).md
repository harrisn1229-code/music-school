# Beginner Blues Bass "Survival Shapes” (Simple Method for 12 Bar Blues)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tiloGr59WOg)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tiloGr59WOg)

## Synopsis

The **origin video** for BassBuzz's "blues survival shapes" — a 2019 upload (older than the channel's later, more advanced "[[How to Play Blues Bass (in 5 Levels)]]," which reuses this exact Level-1 material almost verbatim and cites the same two shapes by the same name). Where the 5-Levels video treats the shapes as a launchpad into four escalating vocabulary layers, this earlier video stays deliberately narrow: teach two movable fretboard shapes (a "high" and a "low" triangle) that locate the IV and V from any I root with zero interval knowledge, teach the 12-bar form's I/IV/V bar layout well enough to know *when* to use each note, and stop there — explicitly deferring "spicier" vocabulary to later videos. It's the more didactic of the two: it works the high-shape math in **two** full keys (E, G) and the low-shape math in **two** full keys (C, F#) rather than one worked example per shape, and it's the only one of the pair that explicitly derives *why* the shapes work — walking a D major scale on camera to show that the high shape's IV/V are literally scale degrees 4 and 5 relative to I (same fret/string-up = the 4th degree, not a coincidence), and that the low shape's IV/V are the *same* two notes as the high shape, just voiced an octave lower. It also has a short, effective bit of ear-training theater — playing "Jump, Jive and Wail" (Louis Prima, as covered by Brian Setzer) with the chord progression digitally removed to demonstrate audibly why the 12-bar changes matter, not just intellectually. Closes with two full play-along passes (root notes only, quarter-note rhythm) over real recordings: "Killing Floor" (Howlin' Wolf) in A using the high shape, and "Pride and Joy" (Stevie Ray Vaughan) in Eb using the low shape — the same two reference points ("Killing Floor" and Tommy Shannon's SRV playing) that recur as vocabulary examples in the later, denser video. Pure instrument/theory content, beginner-facing, no production/plugin material.

## What's taught

- **[~0:00–0:30] Framing.** Promises a "stupidly simple" blues approach that deliberately withholds most theory on day one — you only need **three notes** (I, IV, V) and two shapes to survive a blues jam in any key, full stop.
- **[~0:30–1:15] The core premise.** A "shape" = a visual fret/string pattern that gives you a shortcut without needing to know intervals or key signatures. Two shapes taught: a **high shape** and a **low shape** — pick whichever fits the neck position your root note lands on.
- **[~1:15–3:00] High survival shape, worked in two keys.** From the root (I): **go up one string, same fret → that's the IV**; **from the IV, go up two more frets on that same string → that's the V.** Worked example 1: blues in E, root = **E, 7th fret A string** → IV = **A, 7th fret D string** → V = **B, 9th fret D string** (frets inferred from the shape math; not stated on camera for this key). Worked example 2, done fully on camera to prove the shape transposes: blues in G, root = **G, 3rd fret E string** → IV = same fret, string up → V = two frets further on that string. **Same shape, different key, just because you started on a different note** — that's the whole trick.
- **[~3:00–4:30] Low survival shape, worked in two keys — exact frets given both times.** From the root (I): **go down one string, back two frets → that's the IV**; **from the IV, go up two frets on that same (lower) string → that's the V**, which lands you back on **the same fret the root was on, one string down** — stated explicitly as a shortcut: *"you can also think of the V in the low shape as just being down a string, same fret from the I."* Worked example 1: blues in C, root = **C, 3rd fret A string** → IV = **1st fret E string** → V = **3rd fret E string**. Worked example 2: blues in F#, root = **F#, 4th fret D string** → IV = down a string, down two frets → V = up two frets from there (same shape, different key; explicitly called out that a sharp in the key name doesn't make it harder). **These two worked low-shape examples are the first time exact frets for this shape land anywhere in the vault** — see the update to [[Blues Bass Survival Shapes and Vocabulary Ladder]] below, which had flagged the low shape's fretting as "not given on camera" in the newer video.
- **[~4:30–6:15] Building a real bassline on top of the shape (worked in E).** Two demonstrated approaches: (1) **survival mode** — just plug away on the I with quarter notes (or any rhythm), and use the shape purely to know *which string* to jump to when the chord changes to IV or V; (2) **decorated mode** — keep the I/IV/V notes as fixed anchor points at the same shape positions, and "add stuff around the shape" (fills, rhythm, passing notes) between and around them rather than replacing them. Framed as the natural next step once the shape itself is automatic — the shape is scaffolding, not the whole part.
- **[~6:15–8:00] Theory: why the high shape works (optional but recommended).** Demonstrated in D: walk the D major scale on camera, count to the 4th scale degree (G) — it lands on the exact same fret, one string up from D, matching the high shape's IV. Count to the 5th degree (A) — it lands up two more frets on that string, matching the high shape's V. **Conclusion stated directly: the survival shapes are just the 1st, 4th, and 5th notes of the major scale built off whatever key you're in — "not a coincidence at all."** This is the reason the note is named I/IV/V rather than 1/2/3.
- **[~8:00–8:45] Theory: why the low shape works.** Same D major scale, same G (4th) and A (5th) — but for the low shape, find those same two notes **below** the root instead of above it: G on the 3rd fret of the E string, A on the 5th fret of the E string, both below D on the 5th fret of the A string. **Explicitly named: that's "down an octave"** — the low shape's IV and V are literally the *same notes* as the high shape's, just an octave lower, not a different harmonic relationship.
- **[~8:45–9:15] The 12-bar form, framed as "when to play the notes."** Named as the most common blues form, citing "The Thrill Is Gone" (B.B. King), "Hound Dog" (Elvis), and "Red House" (Jimi Hendrix) as songs that use it. Structure: 12 bars, grouped into **three chunks of four bars each**, that repeat over and over through a song.
- **[~9:15–10:30] "Jump, Jive and Wail" (Louis Prima, Brian Setzer version) — counted through the three 4-bar chunks live**, across a verse, a repeated chorus, and a solo, to make the "three groups of four" structure concrete and audible rather than abstract.
- **[~10:30–11:15] Why the chord progression matters — an audible A/B demonstration.** Plays the same song's vocal melody over **no chord progression at all** (comedically "stuck" — "why is this still happening?"), then over the **real** chord progression, to make the point stick: *"a chord progression gives us some destinations to travel to"* — the changes are what make a song feel like it's going somewhere instead of sitting on one note. A good, reusable teaching device for explaining harmonic movement to a total beginner without any Roman-numeral vocabulary yet.
- **[~11:15–12:00] The 12-bar I/IV/V map, with the turnaround variation named.** `I I I I / IV IV I I / V IV I [I or V]` — bars 1–4 stay on I; bars 5–6 go to IV, bars 7–8 back to I; the final four bars (**"the turn around"**) go V, IV, I, and then **either stay on I or go back to V for the closing bar** — called "a really common variation on the form." Flags that real songs have slight variations on this skeleton, but this is "a good starting point."
- **[~12:00–12:30] "Good Golly, Miss Molly" — same form, same progression, counted through live** as a second confirming example of the exact same I/IV/V map (including landing the turnaround on I here, one of the two named variations).
- **[~12:30–13:30] Play-along 1 — "Killing Floor" (Howlin' Wolf), 12-bar blues in A, high shape.** Root = **A, 5th fret E string** (chosen specifically because there's no string below to use the low shape — a nice practical rule: **if your root is on the lowest string you have, you're forced into the high shape**). IV = **D, 5th fret A string**; V = **E, 7th fret A string**. Played as straight quarter notes on the root only (not the song's actual guitar-doubling bassline — deliberately simplified so the shape's motion through the 12 bars is easy to track), with the turnaround ending on the V variation. Noted as the same song that inspired "The Lemon Song" (Led Zeppelin) — same fact independently confirmed via the chromatic-walkup angle on [[Blues Bass Survival Shapes and Vocabulary Ladder]] § *Level 4*.
- **[~13:30–14:30] Play-along 2 — "Pride and Joy" (Stevie Ray Vaughan), 12-bar blues in Eb, low shape.** Root = **Eb, 6th fret A string**; IV = **Ab, 4th fret E string**; V = **Bb, 6th fret E string**. Same simplified quarter-notes-on-the-root approach, explicitly contrasted with the real recorded bassline's "cool walking stuff" (Tommy Shannon's actual part, per the fuller breakdown on [[Blues Bass Survival Shapes and Vocabulary Ladder]] § *Level 5*) — this pass is root-note scaffolding only, not the finished line.
- **[~14:30–end] Wrap-up.** You now have both survival shapes and the 12-bar map — total beginners stick to the shape notes; more advanced players "plug" existing blues bass patterns/licks directly into the I/IV/V slots the shapes locate.
