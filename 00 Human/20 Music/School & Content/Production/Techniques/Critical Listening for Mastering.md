# Critical Listening for Mastering

**What it is:** the specific listening discipline mastering asks for beyond running tools correctly — five things to judge on every pass ([[#The five categories|Dynamics, Loudness, Timbre, Width, Depth]]), genre-aware processing judgment (the same move is right for one genre and wrong for another), and the ability to imagine the target sound before touching a device. Complements [[Referencing and Translation]] (the A/B/playback-check discipline) rather than replacing it — this note is about *what to listen for*, that one is about *how to check it honestly*.

## The five categories
1. **Dynamics** — the ratio/relationship between quiet and loud passages. Too dynamic → quieter details get lost on most consumer playback systems; too compressed → the listening experience gets fatiguing fast. [[Compression]], [[Compression Staging]], [[Ozone 9 Maximizer]], [[kHs Clipper]] all act here.
2. **Loudness** — the overall track level, inherently tied to Dynamics: two mixes with identical peak values but different dynamic range — the *less* dynamic one has to run louder to read the same. See [[Clipping and Loudness]] for the concrete LUFS targets and stack.
3. **Timbre** — the track's overall tonal character: frequency response plus the ADSR (mainly attack/decay of transients) plus harmonic/disharmonic distortion. Compressors, EQs, and exciters/saturators do the most work here — [[EQ]], [[Saturation and Distortion]].
4. **Width** — the ratio of centered vs. distinctly-left/right signal: identical L/R reads centered, differing L/R reads wide. Typically shaped with mid-side EQ/compression/saturation and stereo imagers (which also add psychoacoustic width tricks on top of straight M/S routing) — full toolkit on [[Width and Stereo]]. Too wide costs mono compatibility and reads unfocused; **too centered reads closed-in and lacking a natural sense of space** — a downside [[Width and Stereo]] doesn't otherwise call out explicitly, since that note's own "rules that keep width safe" section is mostly written from the too-wide side.
5. **Depth** — the ratio of perceived background to foreground (dry vs. wet) signal. The most complex category: on the surface it's just the dry/wet relationship, but perception of it is tied into Dynamics, Loudness, Width, and EQ all at once — see [[Depth and Space]] for the fuller four-cue breakdown (level, HF content, transient sharpness, wet/dry+pre-delay); this category is the umbrella those four cues live under.

## Genre-aware processing judgment
The same processing move is right for one genre and wrong for another — knowing *why* a genre sounds the way it does (its historical tone/gear) is what makes the call, not a fixed rulebook. Worked comparison from the source: **EDM** with an intentionally aggressive kick/snare/pluck → skip tape emulation (its subtle wave-shaping + high-frequency attenuation would counteract the intended aggression) and reach for a **Clipper** instead, since it imparts distortion to each transient and keeps the aggressive character → [[Clipping and Loudness]], [[kHs Clipper]]. **Jazz mastering** → tape emulation is the right call here (peaks attenuate gradually, shaped in a manner indicative of the genre's era — even tune the emulation toward the specific machine that era's jazz would have run through), and it works as a genuine **alternative to limiting**, not just a color pass; a Clipper here would be the wrong choice (no reason to impart aggressive distortion to transients, and no reason to push toward a loudness that would necessitate clipping in the first place) → [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[Kramer Tape]]. The general lesson: before reaching for a processor, know the genre and the gear/era it evokes, and let *that* decide the tool — not a one-size-fits-all mastering-chain default.

## Imagining the sound before it exists
The hardest of the three named mastering skills (with critical listening and genre knowledge) — knowing what you want the mix to become *before* you start shaping it. Practically: as you listen, actively imagine the target for each of the five categories above (how dynamic, how loud overall, how the transients/timbre should sound, the width, the depth) — then check that target against the genre and the playback context it's actually headed for. Two worked genre/context checks from the source: wanting a wide stereo image on an **EDM track bound for club systems** (which are often multispeaker mono) → reconsider how wide to actually make it, since the club playback context caps how much width will translate → [[Width and Stereo]], [[Referencing and Translation]]. Wanting a **pop song bright and aggressive** but knowing it'll be heard mainly over **earbuds** → be careful not to push it too bright, or it reads harsh on that specific playback chain → [[Referencing and Translation]].

## Chain-order takeaway
Before starting a mastering chain, have the five-category target and the genre call above settled — then the chain itself typically runs **corrective EQ → saturation → stereo imaging → maximization → clipping → limiting**, the same stage order [[Master Chain]] already follows (corrective EQ → glue → tape → imaging → density → clip → limit).

## From videos

- **"You have to see with your ears" — a working engineer's ears-first discipline, stated as a hard rule rather than a general preference: never reach for EQ or a compressor before listening first to decide whether the source actually needs it.** Neal Pogue frames this as a generational habit — self-described as coming from the pre-DAW "VU meter age" rather than today's "monitor age" — waveforms get used only for "fixing little things," never for judging whether a sound is active or already good; that call is by ear alone. Paired with a matching restraint principle: on a mix where the synths "already sounded good," he added almost no EQ — "I have no ego at all. If it already sounds good, I don't have to touch it." Same logic extends to the stereo bus: engage compression/EQ there only "if I need it... it's a reactive thing," not as an automatic final-stage step — worked chain and settings now on [[Master Chain]] (§8 [[Ozone 9 Maximizer]]). → [[Master Chain]], [[Ozone 9 Equalizer]], [[Ozone 9 Maximizer]] — Neal Pogue (Mix with the Masters), [[Understanding Kaytranada's vision with Neal Pogue]]
- **Judge an A/B by the decay tail, not the source tone — and treat trained-ear format identification as a real, compounding skill.** When comparing two processing/summing paths (or formats) on the same material, don't listen to the obvious hit/tone itself — listen to **the ring**: the tail of the reverb/echo/compression riding a snare (or any decaying element). That tail is where low-level resolution differences actually live; worked live A/B: an all-tube stereo-bus path reveals the most of that ring ("the tube again has even the highest resolution, it's even more than the solid state"), digital summing reveals the least, and API+tube together reveals the most of all. The stated payoff of training this listening skill: the better you get at identifying quality/resolution/emotion/distortion/harmonics, the more reliably you start hearing things like tape vs. digital source, and even which sample rate (44.1/48/96) a take was recorded at, purely by ear — "you can actually hear every one of those." → [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[Session Templates]], [[Master Chain]] — Jack Joseph Puig (Mix with the Masters), [Listening to the details with Jack Joseph Puig](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=20CbTBdY8sk)
- **The five-category critical-listening framework itself (Dynamics, Loudness, Timbre, Width, Depth), the genre-aware EDM-vs-jazz saturation call, and "imagine the sound before it exists" as the third mastering skill — the whole note above is sourced from this one video.** → [[Width and Stereo]], [[Depth and Space]], [[Clipping and Loudness]], [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[Referencing and Translation]], [[Kramer Tape]], [[kHs Clipper]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [Before You Master Music, Watch This](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tEqcGkgHgqc)
