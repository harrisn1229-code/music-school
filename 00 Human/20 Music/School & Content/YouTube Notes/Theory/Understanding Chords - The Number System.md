# Understanding Chords - The Number System

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=123uPY0lg1A)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=123uPY0lg1A)

## Synopsis
The on-ramp lesson for Roman-numeral/scale-degree thinking: why musicians number chords instead of always naming them, the fixed major-minor-minor-major-major-minor-diminished quality pattern that makes the numbers portable across keys, and why the 1-chord (tonic) is the anchor everything else is measured against. Companion/prerequisite to [[What Does It Mean To Be In a Key|What Does It Mean To Be "In a Key"?]] and [[The Basics of Complementary Chord Progressions]] in Max's beginner theory sequence — this is the third video in that sequence and explicitly sets up both.

## What's taught
- **Number chords by their scale degree instead of naming them** — the chord built on the 1st note of the scale is "the one chord," on the 2nd note "the two chord," etc.; same idea as [[Chords by Key]] and [[Chord Progressions]]'s Roman-numeral analysis, taught here in plain "one/two/three" spoken form rather than Roman numerals on the page.
- **The major/minor/diminished pattern is fixed in every major key** — I ii iii IV V vi vii° = major-minor-minor-major-major-minor-diminished, worked in both C major and G major to prove it holds regardless of key. Matches [[Diatonic Modes]]'s Ionian triad-quality table exactly (same 4+3/3+4/3+3 half-step logic lives there). I, IV and V are always major; ii, iii, vi are always minor; vii is always diminished.
- **Because the numbers carry their quality with them, a progression can be spoken/written in numbers only** — "4326" in the key of C decodes to F major, E minor, D minor, A minor without ever saying "major" or "minor" out loud, because any musician who knows the system already knows 4 is major and 2/3/6 are minor. This is presented as the standard professional shorthand for talking about progressions.
- **The number system is the fast path to transposing a progression** — a "1 2 3" progression sounds "the same, relatively speaking" in any key because the numbers are locked to chord quality; play it starting from G's tonic instead of C's and it's the identical shape, just higher/lower. Direct practical use: learn a progression you like as numbers, not chord names, so it ports to any key instantly. → [[Circle of Fifths]]
- **You still need to be able to name the actual chord**, not just the number — the number system and chord names work together (number tells you what to build, naming tells you what you actually played), useful when communicating with someone who needs the letter name.
- **The 1 chord (tonic) is the key center** — repeatedly emphasized as the "settled," "rested," "home" chord; wherever it lands in a progression is the most stable-feeling point, and being deliberate about *when* it lands is a compositional lever (developed further in the paired video, [[The Basics of Complementary Chord Progressions]]). → [[Keys]]
