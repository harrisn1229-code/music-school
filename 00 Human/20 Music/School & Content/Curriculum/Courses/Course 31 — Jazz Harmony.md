# Course 31 — Jazz Harmony

Teaches the four ideas jazz harmony actually runs on — the ii–V–I as its basic sentence, guide tones as the two notes that carry a chord, substitution as an engine with rules rather than taste, and reharmonization as the payoff — then the forms, the modal alternative and the melodic vocabulary built on top of them.

[[School Config]] records that **JAZ was expanded from "dip a toe" to comprehensive on 2026-08-09**, Nate's own call, and seventeen notes were written to back it. The notes landed; the syllabus never followed, and the strand sat with a full library, no course and no concepts until 2026-09-01. This course is that decision carried out.

**What it assumes**

- [[Course 29 — Chord Vocabulary]] — jazz harmony is chord vocabulary moving fast. You cannot substitute a chord you cannot spell, and the sevenths, extensions and alterations that course installs are the raw material here.
- [[Course 30 — Voicings & Comping]] — a guide tone is a voicing decision before it is a theory one, and half of this course is choosing which notes to leave out.
- [[Course 02 — Chord Function]] — substitution is defined in terms of function. Without tonic, subdominant and dominant, "shares the same function" is not a usable rule.

**What this unlocks**

- **The chords in the music you already make.** Neo-soul, jazzy hip hop and a great deal of modern R&B are jazz harmony arriving second-hand. This is how to write those chords deliberately instead of finding them by accident.
- **Reharmonization**, which is the fastest way to get a second section out of material you already have — the melody stays, the chords underneath change, and the repeat reads as development.
- **The library.** Seventeen notes in `Theory/Jazz/` stop being a reading list and become an ordered syllabus, with the tools shelf wired in: [[Auralia]] tests this material as listening, [[Teoria]] drills the chord qualities free.

> [!info] Where this course transfers, and where it does not
> Nate produces hip hop, trap, EDM and pop while studying a jazz-first library, so every lesson names the transfer where there is one — the tritone sub and the backdoor are already in the pop repertoire, guide tones are a mixing decision, and modal jazz is sixty years of solutions to the loop problem. What does not transfer is density: bebop's note rate is a genre convention, not a principle.

## The lessons

### Lesson 1 — Sevenths and the Central Cadence

**Read**
- [ ] [[Jazz Harmony]] → *The four ideas the whole subject runs on*, *Numerals in jazz vs. the classical style used elsewhere here*
- [ ] [[The ii-V-I]] → *The major version*, *The minor version*, *Chains — the thing that makes it worth learning*, *When the I doesn't arrive*, *Practical use outside jazz*

**The material**
- The premise before any rule: **[[JAZ-C01 — Sevenths are the baseline, not a colour|a bare "C" on a jazz chart means Cmaj7 or C6]]**. In common-practice harmony the triad is the default and the 7th is a colour; here the 7th is the default and the triad is the special case.
- Everything downstream assumes four notes. Guide tones need a 7th to exist; a tritone substitution has nothing to preserve without one. Reading a jazz chart as triads is not a simpler reading, it is a wrong one.
- The cell the language is built from: **[[JAZ-C02 — The ii-V-I, major and minor|Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7]]**, and in minor **Dm7♭5 – G7♭9 – Cm7**. The roots fall a fifth twice — the circle of fifths in miniature, and the strongest root motion in tonal music stacked back to back.
- The three minor-key differences are forced rather than chosen. The ♭5 is simply the 6th degree of natural minor; the ♭9 comes from harmonic minor, whose raised leading tone is what makes the V a dominant at all; and the tonic is ambiguous on purpose between m7, m6 and mMaj7.
- **The units chain**, which is what makes them worth learning: make the I of one unit the ii of the next, or drop a ii–V in front of any chord you want to arrive at. `| Dm7 G7 | Cmaj7 | Cm7 F7 | B♭maj7 |` is two of them, a whole step apart.
- The reading tip: **[[JAZ-C03 — Spotting a ii-V, and when the I never arrives|scan for any m7 followed by a dominant 7th a fourth above it]]**. Whatever it points at is the intended I — even when the I never arrives.
- Two cases where it does not. The **deceptive** move goes to vi or ♭VI instead. The **unresolved** ii–V moves straight to the next one, which is why a bebop head can look like it changes key every two bars when it does not.
- Outside jazz it shows up three ways: as the whole loop, as a turnaround pushing back to the top of a plain pop verse, or stripped to guide tones so it survives an arrangement with almost no chordal instrument.

**Concepts**
- [[JAZ-C01 — Sevenths are the baseline, not a colour]]
- [[JAZ-C02 — The ii-V-I, major and minor]]
- [[JAZ-C03 — Spotting a ii-V, and when the I never arrives]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — write the major ii–V–I in all twelve keys and the minor version in four. Then take a four-chord loop of your own and re-spell every chord as a seventh.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — build `Dm7 G7 Cmaj7 Cmaj7` as a four-bar loop in Ableton and let it run. Then rewrite the same four bars as triads and A/B them.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 31 — Lesson 1 Test]]

### Lesson 2 — Guide Tones

**Read**
- [ ] [[Guide Tones]] → *Why the 3rd and the 7th*, *Guide tones move by step — that's the whole trick*, *Shell voicings*, *Using it to write*, *The one exception*

**The material**
- The highest-leverage idea in the strand: **[[JAZ-C04 — The 3rd and the 7th carry the chord|two notes state a chord's quality]]**. The 3rd says major or minor, the 7th says major, dominant or minor. Together they name the chord.
- The root tells you nothing about quality and the 5th tells you almost nothing, which is why the 5th is the first note dropped from a voicing. This is why a bass and a horn can imply a full progression with no chordal instrument present.
- **The one exception is m7♭5.** Cm7♭5 and Cm7 share E♭ and B♭, so only the ♭5 separates them — a m7♭5 voicing that drops its 5th states the wrong chord.
- Now the mechanism: **[[JAZ-C05 — The 7th of one chord is the 3rd of the next|run the guide tones through Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7]]** and the lines go C → B → B and F → F → E. One voice holds while the other moves a half step, then they swap.
- Nothing leaps, and it is not luck — that is what root motion by fifths does. It is also the concrete test for a substitution: one that keeps the guide-tone lines stepwise is a good one, which is exactly why the tritone sub works.
- **Write the two lines before you voice anything.** Fix any leap larger than a step by inverting the chord or choosing a different extension, then decide the full voicings. Solving the horizontal problem first is faster and it produces smoother results.
- The practical form is the **[[JAZ-C06 — Shell voicings|shell]]** — root, 3rd and 7th, no 5th and no extensions, usually with the 7th below the 3rd. Extensions stack on top: shell plus 9, shell plus 13.
- On a dense arrangement a shell is often the *right* voicing rather than the simple one. A full five-note chord under a busy vocal is usually the mistake, and cutting a crowded chordal instrument to shells frees mids without losing any harmony.

**Concepts**
- [[JAZ-C04 — The 3rd and the 7th carry the chord]]
- [[JAZ-C05 — The 7th of one chord is the 3rd of the next]]
- [[JAZ-C06 — Shell voicings]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — write out only the 3rds and 7ths of a tune's changes as two horizontal lines. Circle every leap larger than a step and re-voice that chord until the leap is gone.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — comp a ii–V–I as shells in root–7th–3rd order under a vocal or lead you already have, then again as full five-note chords. Keep one.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 31 — Lesson 2 Test]]

### Lesson 3 — Substitution

**Read**
- [ ] [[Chord Substitution]] → *The governing rule*, *1. Diatonic substitution — chords a third apart*, *2. Tritone substitution — the important one*, *3. Backdoor substitution*, *4. Interpolating a ii in front of any dominant*, *Choosing between them*
- [ ] [[Tritone Substitution]] → *The mechanism*, *Pop/rock examples — found by ear, not by theory*
- [ ] [[Backdoor Progression]] → *The progression*, *Pop/R&B examples*, *Jazz standard usage*

**The material**
- **[[JAZ-C07 — Substitution's governing rule: function first|Two chords can substitute when they share enough tones and the same harmonic function]]** — and function comes first. A substitute sharing three notes that resolves the wrong way is worse than one sharing two that resolves right.
- Diatonic substitution falls straight out of it: two diatonic chords a third apart share two of three triad tones, so `Cmaj7 Cmaj7 Fmaj7 G7` becomes `Cmaj7 Am7 Fmaj7 G7`. vi is the strongest I-substitute; iii is the weakest, because it lacks the root and contains the leading tone.
- **The failure mode is substituting everything.** Against plain harmony a substitution reads as sophisticated; wall to wall it reads as directionless. The melody is the test — a substitution that clashes with the tune is wrong however good the voice leading.
- **[[JAZ-C08 — Tritone substitution: the shared tritone|G7 and D♭7 contain the same tritone]]** — B and F, with the 3rd and 7th swapping roles, since D♭7's C♭ is B. Same tension, same resolution, different bass note.
- Only dominants qualify, and the substitute always sits a semitone above the target of resolution. What it buys is chromatic bass: `Dm7 D♭7 Cmaj7` walks D – D♭ – C, which is most of the sound of neo-soul.
- It is already in pop, found by ear. "Toxic" descends iii → ii → ♭II, readable as subs of a 6-2-5; "One Note Samba" stacks two of them to make a whole turnaround chromatic.
- The V is not the only dominant on a chart. **[[JAZ-C09 — Substituting a secondary dominant, and the minimum viable form|♭II-of-ii is the commonest flat-five substitution in pop]]**, precisely because it is undramatic — it reads as a chromatic step from iii down to ii.
- And it needs far less chord than it looks: **any major triad a semitone above the target will do it.** Not an altered dominant, not even a dominant 7th. That is a two-second edit to any progression, which is the form of the rule most likely to get used.
- **[[JAZ-C10 — ♭II–I is not always a tritone sub|The same ♭II resolving to I can be two devices]]**, and the tell is how you arrive. Chromatically, with the roots stepping down a semitone at a time into it (`Dm7 → D♭7 → Cmaj7`), it is a tritone sub. From ♭VI by descending fifths, it is a borrowed Phrygian cadence.
- ♭II–I sounds complete with no dominant in it because **every note of the ♭II triad falls a semitone into a note of the tonic triad** — ♭2̂→1̂, 4̂→3̂, ♭6̂→5̂. Three semitone resolutions, no tritone, no leading tone. ♭II going to *V* instead is a third thing, the [[Neapolitan Sixth]].
- The softer arrival: **[[JAZ-C11 — The backdoor: ♭VII7 in place of V7|Fm7 – B♭7 – Cmaj7]]** in place of `Dm7 G7 Cmaj7`. Both chords are borrowed from C minor, and B♭7's A♭ — the ♭6 — falls to G instead of a leading tone rising to it.
- Reach for it when V7 is too final or too bright. It is all over Rihanna's "Stay", Zero 7's "The Waiting Line" and Stevie Wonder; the shorthand version, ♭VII7 straight to I with no minor iv, carries "New York State of Mind" and "Layla".
- Two of these are extended by [[Ch12 — Tritone Substitution and Other Mystery Chords]], which is vault-only reading rather than assigned. It confirms the mechanism the library already teaches and adds only the secondary-dominant cases, the minimum viable form and the Phrygian distinction.

**Concepts**
- [[JAZ-C07 — Substitution's governing rule: function first]]
- [[JAZ-C08 — Tritone substitution: the shared tritone]]
- [[JAZ-C09 — Substituting a secondary dominant, and the minimum viable form]]
- [[JAZ-C10 — ♭II–I is not always a tritone sub]]
- [[JAZ-C11 — The backdoor: ♭VII7 in place of V7]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — take a 3-6-2-5 turnaround and tritone-sub every dominant in it, then play the bassline alone and confirm every root drops one semitone. Then re-end a loop of yours with iv7–♭VII7–I.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — in a loop in Ableton, slide a plain major triad a semitone above the ii into the gap before it. Print that clip, then print the same bar with a full altered dominant instead, and A/B them.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 31 — Lesson 3 Test]]

### Lesson 4 — Turnarounds, Rhythm Changes and Form

**Read**
- [ ] [[Turnarounds]] → *The base form*, *The standard variations*, *The two-bar and one-bar compressions*, *Using them outside jazz*
- [ ] [[Rhythm Changes]] → *The shape: AABA, 32 bars*, *Why it's worth your time*, *Common substitutions*
- [ ] [[32-Bar Song Form]] → *The 16+16 shape*, *The 8+8 (asymmetric) variant*, *Learning method: form first, chords second*
- [ ] [[The Montgomery-Ward Bridge]] → *The shape*, *Outside jazz*
- [ ] [[Memorizing Jazz Standards by Harmonic Chunking]] → *The core method: the phone-number analogy*, *Theory as language, not a dictionary*

**The material**
- **[[JAZ-C12 — A turnaround is a controlled reset|I – vi – ii – V]]**, and its only job is to end a section by pointing back at the top. The root falls a third, then rises a fourth twice, and it lands on V, which wants the next chorus's I.
- **Write the four-chord version first, then compress** — to two bars, to one bar (`| Dm7 G7 |`, the commonest ending in the repertoire), or to V alone on beats 3–4. Compressing a good turnaround gives a good short one; expanding a bare `Dm7 G7` gives you nothing.
- Every named variation — VI7, III7–VI7, tritone subs, ♭III7, backdoor, minor — is Lesson 3 applied to this one skeleton. Learn the base form and the rules and you can generate the rest.
- Loop-based music is *all* turnaround. Three moves that work in production: end on **V7 or ♭II7**; end on **the ii**, leaving the V implied so the loop never closes; or **change only the last bar between repeats** — two loops of material for one bar of writing.
- **[[JAZ-C13 — Rhythm Changes: a looped turnaround, answered|Bars 1–4 of a Rhythm Changes A section are I–vi–ii–V twice]]** and nothing else. Bars 5–8 answer them: `I → I7 → IV → iv → I → V/ii → ii → V`, with the borrowed iv as the moment the section goes somewhere.
- Four bars of a static loop do not get boring because four bars of real movement answer them. That question-and-answer shape inside one eight-bar unit is directly transferable to a verse — and it is the answer to why a loop that is fine on its own tires over sixteen bars.
- **[[JAZ-C14 — A bridge can be contrast made from harmonic rhythm alone|The bridge is `D7 D7 G7 G7 C7 C7 F7 F7`]]** in B♭ — four dominants, each resolving down a fifth, and only the last, F7, is the key's own V7. A pure circle-of-fifths run that leaves the key and walks back in.
- What makes it feel like a new section is not a key change, a tempo change or new instrumentation. **Harmonic rhythm drops from two chords per bar to one per two bars — a 4× slowdown — and that alone does it.** Contrast does not require new material.
- The off-the-shelf cousin is [[The Montgomery-Ward Bridge]], `I7 – IV7 – II7 – V7`, which Tin Pan Alley writers used when two A sections were written and eight contrasting bars were needed. Three of its four root moves are fifths; `IV → II` is the seam, and only the V7 is diatonic — I7, IV7 and II7 each bring in one note from outside the key (in C: B♭, E♭ and F♯).
- **[[JAZ-C15 — Standard forms put devices in predictable slots|The two containers are AABA and 16+16]]**, and the first half of a 16+16 must not resolve to I — it has to reach V so the second half has somewhere to launch from.
- The slots are the point. A secondary ii–V to vi early, one to IV soon after, IV → iv → I around the IV chord, V7/V in the last line of the first half, a turnaround in the final bars. A new chart becomes "which variant is this", not 32 arbitrary bars.
- That is also how to memorise one — chunk it the way a phone number is chunked. Name the form, then name the functional units: "a 2-5 to six", not "half-diminished, then dominant, then minor". Learn a chunk once and it is recognised everywhere it appears.

**Concepts**
- [[JAZ-C12 — A turnaround is a controlled reset]]
- [[JAZ-C13 — Rhythm Changes: a looped turnaround, answered]]
- [[JAZ-C14 — A bridge can be contrast made from harmonic rhythm alone]]
- [[JAZ-C15 — Standard forms put devices in predictable slots]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — write the four-chord turnaround, then compress it to two bars, one bar and half a bar. Then take a standard you do not know, mark the form before reading a single chord, and fill it in by chunk name.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — take a busy eight-bar section of yours and rewrite it with the harmonic rhythm halved, changing nothing else. Decide whether it now reads as a different section.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 31 — Lesson 4 Test]]

### Lesson 5 — Passing Chords and Reharmonization

**Read**
- [ ] [[Passing Chords]] → *1. Chromatic passing chords (dominant diminished)*, *3. Diatonic passing chords*, *4. Dominant passing chords*
- [ ] [[Reharmonization]] → *The one constraint*, *The techniques, roughly in order of cost*, *A worked example*, *When not to*

**The material**
- The distinction that decides who has to know: **[[JAZ-C16 — A passing chord connects; a substitute replaces|a passing chord connects two chords already in the progression]]**, and the bass player can keep walking the original changes underneath it. A substitution replaces a chord, so everyone plays it.
- **Put the passing chord on the weak beat and the real chord on the heavy beat.** The ear then tracks the actual harmony and hears the insert as colour rather than as a competing change. The exception is a chromatic passing chord written into the harmony itself.
- Four families: a **dominant diminished** a half step below the target (C♯dim7 → Dm7, which is a rootless A7♭9); a **shape slid a fret** into the target, where you think about the destination and never name the passing chord; **diatonic** inserts for stepwise motion; and a **dominant**, plain or tritone-subbed.
- Then the payoff of the whole strand. **[[JAZ-C17 — Reharmonization's one constraint|The melody note must be a usable tone of the new chord]]** — a chord tone or a functioning extension. Not a note the chord contradicts, like a natural 9 over a ♭9 or a natural 4 on the 3rd.
- Everything else is negotiable, which is why you work **melody-first**: write the tune out, decide what each note could be relative to a candidate chord, and choose from there. Choosing chords first and hoping the melody survives is how you get the clashes.
- The cheapest move is **[[JAZ-C18 — One melody note, many chords|recontextualising a held note]]**. A sustained C is the root of Cmaj7, the ♭3 of Am7, the 5th of Fmaj7, the ♭7 of Dm7, the 3rd of A♭maj7 or the ♭5 of F♯m7♭5 — six meanings, one note.
- The systematic version is **delayed resolution**: harmonise the expected tonic note with anything except the tonic chord and arrive home late. Cdim7, A♭maj7, Fmaj7, D♭maj7 and B♭9 all take the tonic note as a different chord tone, and they chain.
- The rest of the ladder, in order of cost: interpolate a ii in front of any dominant, tritone-sub the dominants, add secondary dominants, change only the bass, hold a pedal point, borrow from the parallel minor — or reharmonize the *rhythm* and leave every chord alone.
- And the discipline: **[[JAZ-C19 — Reharmonize the repeats, not the first statement|reharmonization competes with the melody for attention]]**. If the tune is the point, a dense rewrite pulls focus and the song gets worse.
- Let the listener learn the plain version, then change what is underneath it. It reads as development rather than confusion, and it hands you a second section for free — which for an arrangement means verse 1 plain, verse 2 reharmonised.

**Concepts**
- [[JAZ-C16 — A passing chord connects; a substitute replaces]]
- [[JAZ-C17 — Reharmonization's one constraint]]
- [[JAZ-C18 — One melody note, many chords]]
- [[JAZ-C19 — Reharmonize the repeats, not the first statement]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — fill every transition of a I–vi–ii–V with a diatonic passing chord on the weak beats, then move them onto the strong beats and hear the progression stop reading.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — take a song of yours with a repeated section. Leave the first statement alone and reharmonise only the repeat, checking every melody note is a legal tone of its new chord.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 31 — Lesson 5 Test]]

### Lesson 6 — Modal Jazz

**Read**
- [ ] [[Modal Jazz]] → *The reaction it was*, *What replaces functional harmony*, *Modal interchange is a different thing — don't confuse them*, *Why it matters for the music you actually make*, *Modal islands — treating each chord of a non-functional vamp as its own mode*

**The material**
- Modal jazz removed the changes almost entirely — *So What* is 16 bars of D Dorian, 8 of E♭ Dorian, 8 more of D Dorian. **When harmony stops moving, everything else has to carry the music**, and that constraint is the whole subject.
- **[[JAZ-C20 — A loop is modal harmony|Four things replace functional motion]]**: the mode's own character as material, quartal voicings, pedal points and drones, and rhythm, dynamics and texture marking the form.
- **It is not modal interchange.** Modal jazz holds one mode for many bars — a static approach to form. Interchange borrows a chord from a parallel mode inside functional harmony and returns. They share a word and almost nothing else.
- This is the jazz idiom closest to how loop-based music works. A four-bar loop on Fmaj7 is exactly the situation *So What* is in, so the strand's most directly useful material is here rather than in the substitution lessons.
- **[[JAZ-C21 — The characteristic note has to be sounded|Dorian's natural 6, Lydian's ♯4, Mixolydian's ♭7, Phrygian's ♭2]]** — land on it and the mode is established, avoid it and the music collapses into plain major or minor. In D Dorian the note is B.
- A loop that never plays the ♯4 is not Lydian, it is major with a Lydian label. In a vamp of several chords that are not functionally connected, treat each chord as its own **modal island** with its own scale rather than hunting for one key that explains all of them.
- **[[JAZ-C22 — Quartal voicings state a mode without naming a chord|The *So What* voicing is three fourths with a major third on top]]** — E A D G B from the bottom. A stack of thirds names a chord and so implies a function, which is what this music is avoiding.
- Fourths sound open and unassigned, which is where the hollow, floating sound comes from. They also sit beautifully under a vocal: sparse, with no strong 3rd fighting the topline. Over sixteen bars of one chord they remove the commitment that gets tiring.
- The arrangement consequence, and it is the transferable one: with no chord changes to signal a section, **one new element — a texture, a countermelody, a register shift — reads as a whole new section.**

**Concepts**
- [[JAZ-C20 — A loop is modal harmony]]
- [[JAZ-C21 — The characteristic note has to be sounded]]
- [[JAZ-C22 — Quartal voicings state a mode without naming a chord]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — loop one chord and improvise over it without ever playing the characteristic note, then again landing on it deliberately. Name the mode you actually hear in each take.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — voice a static vamp as tertian chords and then as the *So What* stack, both under a melody you have. Then write four sections over it differing only in texture.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 31 — Lesson 6 Test]]

### Lesson 7 — Bebop Vocabulary

**Read**
- [ ] [[Bebop Vocabulary]] → *1. The bebop scales — why an eighth note*, *2. Enclosures — approach a note from both sides*, *3. Approach notes*, *8. CESH — chromatic embellishment of static harmony*, *Transferring this outside jazz*
- [ ] [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]] → *The core method*, *Why this works*

**The material**
- The melodic side of the strand, and useful here mostly for writing — these devices are what make a MIDI lead sound played rather than programmed, and they transfer to horn lines, guitar fills and vocal runs.
- **[[JAZ-C23 — Bebop scales: the extra note is rhythmic|A bebop scale is a seven-note scale with one chromatic passing tone added]]**, and the reason is rhythmic. Seven notes in straight eighths and the chord tones drift onto offbeats; the eighth note puts them back on the beat every bar.
- Bebop dominant on G is G A B C D E F F♯ G. The rule generalises: the extra note goes wherever it makes chord tones fall on strong beats, and if yours already do you do not need one. This is also what makes a walking-ish bassline land.
- **[[JAZ-C24 — Enclosures and approach notes|An enclosure surrounds a target before playing it]]** — for a target of C, D♭–B–C, or D–B–C, or B–D♭–C, or the four-note D–D♭–B–C. An approach note is the one-note cousin, strongest from a half step below because it borrows the leading-tone pull.
- Put them on the note you want heard, usually a chord tone on a downbeat — **and not on every note.** Enclosures are punctuation; wall to wall, a line stops having a shape. Enclose the second note of a two-note synth hook and it stops sounding quantised from scratch.
- **[[JAZ-C25 — CESH: a chromatic line under static harmony|CESH walks one note by half step across several bars]]** while the harmony stays still. An approach note targets a single arrival; this is a sustained line, closer to a bass device borrowed into the melody.
- It is the direct answer to a vamp going static, which pairs it with Lesson 6 — when the harmony is deliberately refusing to move, this supplies motion without breaking that decision.
- The practice method underneath all of it: **[[JAZ-C26 — Keep the concept, not the lick|learn the solo by ear, chunk it, analyse each chunk against its chord, reduce it to one named concept, then improvise your own lines from the concepts]]**.
- The fourth step is the one that gets skipped. What you need from a great solo is a nudge toward somewhere, not a part to reproduce — and a per-chord concept list is a template that yields a whole session of new lines rather than one memorised chorus.
- What does not transfer is density. Bebop's note rate is a genre convention rather than a principle, and importing it wholesale into a trap beat just sounds busy.

**Concepts**
- [[JAZ-C23 — Bebop scales: the extra note is rhythmic]]
- [[JAZ-C24 — Enclosures and approach notes]]
- [[JAZ-C25 — CESH: a chromatic line under static harmony]]
- [[JAZ-C26 — Keep the concept, not the lick]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — write a one-bar eighth-note run on a seven-note scale and mark where the chord tones land, then add the passing tone and mark them again. Then enclose only the three notes that matter in a melody you have.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — hold one chord for four bars in Ableton and walk a single inner voice down chromatically underneath it. Then take eight bars of a solo you know and write one named concept per chord.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 31 — Lesson 7 Test]]
