# Phrasing Blueprints

Part of [[Music Theory]] — **four fixed rhythmic/structural forms for practicing phrasing**, distinct from [[Singing Along With Your Playing (Phrasing)]] (training the phrasing *instinct* directly, by vocalizing) and [[Solo Dynamics and Arc]] (the macro shape of a whole solo). This note is the first of the "three broad ways to work on phrasing" referenced but not yet detailed in [[Singing Along With Your Playing (Phrasing)]]: **deliberately choose a letter-form (A, A', B, C…), commit to it strictly for a fixed number of measures, and use the constraint to force yourself to react to what you just played instead of just moving on to a new idea.** All four blueprints below share the same underlying skill — repeat, react, conclude — with a different amount of freedom in what "repeat" means (exact pitches, exact rhythm only, or a fixed measure-count/rest structure).

## The core mechanic (applies to all four)

- **A = whatever you play first — you don't plan it.** You're improvising; the constraint only kicks in *after* you've played the first idea, forcing you to react to your own material rather than to a pre-picked lick.
- **A' ("A-prime") = repeat A, change only the very last note (or few final notes) to strict-at-first.** Loosen later — let the prime mean several different possible endings — but start with one changed note so the repeat is unmistakably a repeat.
- Every blueprint below ends with a genuinely contrasting idea (B or C) that "wraps it up" — the whole point is finishing off complete ideas before switching things up, not trailing off.
- **Works over any scale, key, or vocabulary** — demoed in A minor pentatonic and, for blueprint 3, plain major scale, explicitly to show the structure is genre/scale-agnostic (works equally well with blues bends, jazz vocabulary, or plain scale tones). Also usable as a songwriting-structure tool, not just an improv drill.
- Practice a single idea can span 4, 8, or 16 bars depending on how long each lettered phrase runs.

## Blueprint 1 — A A' B (pitch-strict)

Repeat the exact pitches of your first idea, changing only the ending, then restate A exactly, then close with a wholly new idea.

**Drill:** Loop 8 bars over one chord/vamp (2 bars per phrase) in a scale of your choice (start with minor pentatonic). Count off, play any 2-bar idea (A). Repeat it near-verbatim for phrase 2, changing only the final note or two (A'). Repeat A exactly, note-for-note, for phrase 3. Close with a genuinely different 2-bar idea (B) to resolve. Run several loops, varying only the scale/key each pass — the letter structure stays fixed.

## Blueprint 2 — A A A B (rhythm-strict, pitch-free)

Same shape, no prime — repeat the *rhythm* of the first idea exactly three times, letting pitches vary freely, then close with a different rhythm.

**Drill:** Same 8-bar/2-bar-per-phrase loop, one scale. Play any 2-bar rhythmic idea (A) — don't worry about which notes land on it. Repeat the identical rhythm two more times (A A), deliberately changing which pitches fall on each hit. Close phrase 4 with a different rhythm entirely (B). Useful as a standalone rhythm-only drill — pairs directly with [[Solo Dynamics and Arc]]'s §Generating phrase material vocalize-a-rhythm constraint, which trains the same "rhythm carries the idea, pitch is free" skill from the vocal side rather than the instrument side.

## Blueprint 3 — A A B A (song form, measure-strict)

Borrowed from the AABA jazz-tune song form, applied at the phrase level instead of the whole-tune level. Each idea is **exactly 2 measures**: roughly 1 measure of playing, 1 measure of resting or sustaining (some overlap is fine). B is the one exception — it plays straight through both measures with no rest, deliberately filling the gap A left empty — then the form closes by restating A verbatim.

**Drill:** 8-bar loop, 2 bars per phrase, AABA. Phrase 1 (A): play for ~1 bar, then rest or let the last note ring for ~1 bar. Phrase 2: repeat A exactly, including the rest/sustain. Phrase 3 (B): a contrasting idea that plays continuously through the full 2 bars — no rest at all, deliberately occupying the space A left open. Phrase 4: restate the original A verbatim to close the form. Called out in the source video as one of the strongest of the four to work around — the built-in rest is what makes B's "fill the gap" instruction concrete rather than abstract.

## Blueprint 4 — A A B C (open-ended close)

Like blueprint 3's setup (A, A, B) but the final phrase is a **brand-new contrasting idea (C)** instead of a return to A — you never have to remember or restate the original idea. B is also allowed to *evolve out of* A rather than contrast immediately: start B identically to A, then let it diverge partway through.

**Drill:** 8-bar loop, 2 bars per phrase. Phrase 1 (A): any idea. Phrase 2: repeat A exactly. Phrase 3 (B): open with the same opening gesture as A, then deliberately let it drift into different material partway through the phrase (evolving, not exact repetition). Phrase 4 (C): a fully new closing idea, unconnected to A or B — no obligation to circle back. Lowest-memory-load of the four blueprints, useful once AABA (blueprint 3) feels too rigid.

## Bonus / 5th exercise — let go, sing along, try octaves

The stated "culmination" once the four blueprints are internalized: stop consciously tracking a letter-form and just react by feel. Two concrete aids for getting there, both light-touch versions of the deeper method on [[Singing Along With Your Playing (Phrasing)]]:

- **Sing or hum along with your own lines as you improvise** — doesn't need to be pitch-accurate. Reported effect: more natural space (breathing forces pauses), better memory of what was just played (so repetition happens more naturally), and simpler lines overall. This is the same underlying mechanism as [[Singing Along With Your Playing (Phrasing)]]'s 4-step method, described here in its lightest, no-structured-steps form.
- **Play in octaves as a tempo/complexity brake.** A new device not in the 4-step method above: octave shapes physically slow you down and force simpler phrasing, which in turn makes singing along easier — the two aids compound rather than being alternatives.
- **Practice phrasing divorced from pitch entirely** — deliberately improvise phrasing structure over random/atonal notes, the point being that phrasing (rhythm, repetition, reaction, resolution) is what makes something sound musical *regardless* of scale or note choice, not an effect of "correct" notes.

**Drill:** Improvise over a simple vamp for 2–4 minutes while audibly singing/humming along (any pitch accuracy is fine) — no blueprint constraint, just react. Repeat the same pass playing only in octaves while still singing, and notice the tempo/complexity drop. Optionally warm up first by running blueprints 1–4 for a few minutes each, then drop the letter-tracking and just let the singing/octaves carry the phrasing instinct.
