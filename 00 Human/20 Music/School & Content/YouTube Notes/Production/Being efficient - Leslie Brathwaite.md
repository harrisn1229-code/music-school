# Being efficient - Leslie Brathwaite

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zpf6pIo3HKY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zpf6pIo3HKY)

## Synopsis

Leslie Brathwaite (mixing a Cardi B session) walks through three unrelated mix moves — a hi-hat crisping recipe, a piano EQ move driven entirely by a specific client note, and a frequency-hunting technique for notching an annoying resonance — then pivots into the video's real theme: efficiency as a professional survival skill. His central argument is that speed and "knowing what not to spend time on" is as much a part of the craft as EQ technique, illustrated by his habit of leaving a co-producer's non-ideal compressor choice alone rather than re-matching it with his own preferred plugin, purely to avoid burning a client's trust in an unnecessary round-trip.

## Techniques

- **Hi-hat "crispness" recipe: broad low-end roll-off with a visual/graphic EQ, followed by an aggressive narrow high-frequency boost on a second, more surgical EQ.** Stage 1 — a Q10-style visual EQ, low end simply rolled off broadly by eye/ear ("I like visual EQ sometimes when you're rolling things off... you can kind of see and pick your spot"). Stage 2 — a second, more precise EQ (named as a "precision EQ" made by UAD) boosting a chosen high frequency **over about 4 dB**, deliberately aggressive on that one band. Framed as a subtle, not drastic, top-end lift specifically because the hi-hat itself "doesn't have a whole lot of very top-end information" to begin with — the boost adds crispness rather than correcting an existing bright element.
- **Client-note-driven EQ: a piano brightness/aggression boost added specifically in response to a spoken client note ("make it bite"), not from the mixer's own taste.** The piano was sent completely dry with no processing in the first mix pass. A label executive (Craig Kallman, Atlantic Records) asked for the piano to feel "a little more aggressive... a little more in your face," using the word "bite" — interpreted by Brathwaite as a request for added top-end attack. He added a boosted high-frequency EQ move in response; the client approved it in one pass with no further notes ("yes, that's it, don't touch it"). Framed explicitly as a language-translation skill: "figure out their language, you learn to develop a language with them" — once that vocabulary is established with a given client, later verbal notes can be translated into a specific, correct EQ move on the first try instead of a guessing back-and-forth.
- **Frequency-hunting technique for finding and notching an annoying resonance, by ear, with a specific step-by-step method — including a hardware-safety step.** Method: (1) place a normal parametric EQ band, (2) push the gain all the way UP (a large boost, not a cut) so the resonance is exaggerated, (3) narrow the Q down to its tightest/narrowest setting, (4) **turn your monitor speakers down first** — stated as an explicit, important safety step before the next move, since sweeping a fully-boosted narrow band can be loud/harsh, (5) sweep the frequency control across the spectrum while the source plays, listening for the single most annoying/ringing frequency, (6) once found, invert the move — pull that same band DOWN instead of up, and only partially, not all the way to a deep cut. Explicit warning against over-cutting: taking one frequency out too aggressively can make the source sound hollow, so the goal is a moderate dip that "meets it halfway," not a surgical kill.
- **Leaving an existing, non-ideal processing choice untouched specifically to protect efficiency and client trust, rather than replacing it with a preferred tool.** A co-producer/engineer (referred to as "Evan") had already placed a compressor on the money/hook sample that Brathwaite says isn't his own tool of choice — he explicitly left it in place: "if it's not broke, you don't have to fix it... this is not my compressor of choice, but that doesn't mean I can't honor the fact that this is what he chose, it's doing the job." Stated reasoning for why swapping it would be counterproductive: matching a replacement plugin's settings back to an existing sound costs real time, and if the artist later notices something sounds subtly different, "we just wasted a whole lot of back-and-forth for no reason" — an unforced error against the actual goal of the session.
- **Stated efficiency philosophy as a professional-longevity argument, generalizable beyond any one plugin choice.** "Knowing what to spend time on, what not to spend time on... is what I think also adds to being a good mix engineer." Directly contrasts spending many hours on drums (he cites eight hours as an extreme, unnecessary example — especially when the source is 808-machine-based and the qualities are largely fixed already) against staying competitive with faster engineers who can match the quality in a fraction of the time: "if it takes you eight hours to do something that it only takes a brand-new engineer a half hour to do, and his sound just as good as yours, they're gonna go with the one who can do it in a half hour." His prescription: continually condense and re-examine your own process rather than defaulting to old habits just because they used to work.
