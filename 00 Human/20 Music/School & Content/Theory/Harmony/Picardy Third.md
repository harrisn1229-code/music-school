# Picardy Third

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Cadences]] / [[Modal Interchange]]. A **Picardy third** is when a passage or piece in a minor key resolves onto the MAJOR version of the tonic chord instead of the expected minor i — the mirror-image move of borrowing a minor chord into a major key ([[Modal Interchange]]/[[Borrowed Chords]]).

## Why it works

Raising the tonic's 3rd on the final chord turns an otherwise dark/unresolved minor ending into a bright, "triumphant" one — historically a Baroque-era convention (minor-key pieces of that era very often end major), but still in active use across pop and rock.

## Where it shows up

- **Whole-piece/whole-section endings** — the most common use: a piece or section stays minor throughout and only resolves major at the very end.
- **Per-line resolution** — a denser variant where every phrase (not just the final one) resolves minor-to-major before jumping back to minor to repeat.
- **Combined with a sus4** right before the resolution — the suspended, quality-ambiguous sus4 chord heightens the payoff when it resolves major instead of minor.
- **Functional use, not just color** — a Picardy-third major tonic can itself function as the V of a DIFFERENT upcoming minor key, creating a genuine perfect cadence into the next section (rather than a plain minor tonic, which wouldn't set up that pull).

## From videos

- **A genre-spanning survey, plus one clever functional use.** Whole-piece endings: "Time of the Season" (The Zombies, E minor→E major, the textbook case), "Roundabout" (Yes). **The Beatles used it repeatedly in 1964** — "And I Love Her," "Things We Said Today," and "I'll Be Back" (per-line variant: every line in the verse resolves minor→major before repeating). **Most famous use**: "A Day in the Life" — the E minor climax resolves onto a massive E major chord (three pianos) as the final chord of *Sgt. Pepper's*. **Sus4-heightened resolution**: "Exit Music (For a Film)" (Radiohead). More examples: "Hello" (Lionel Richie), "Summertime" (Janis Joplin's version), "Under the Bridge" (Red Hot Chili Peppers, outro), "Kiss From a Rose" (Seal, already alternates major/minor throughout). **Functional/cadential use**: "Killing Me Softly with His Song" (Roberta Flack) — the F-minor chorus resolves to F MAJOR specifically because F major is the V of the next verse's B♭ minor, creating a real perfect cadence into the verse that a plain F minor resolution wouldn't provide. → [[Cadences]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use the Picardy Third]]
