# Soloing With E Dorian on Bill Withers "Use Me" (Daniel Seriff)

## Synopsis
A short, focused intermediate guitar lesson from Daniel Seriff (the same teacher behind [[NO ONE is teaching Dorian LIKE THIS! (Daniel Seriff)]]) built around his own "Use Me" (Bill Withers) jam-track upload, played over the tune's E minor 7 → A7 changes. The target problem is a very common one: players learn a scale, then only ever run it straight up and down, and it "doesn't sound like music." The fix taught here is a classic broken-thirds scale sequence ("jump in thirds" — skip a note, back a note, repeat) worked out fully ascending and descending over the E Dorian shape at the 12th fret, with the pattern also given as scale-degree numbers (1 2 ♭3 4 5 6 ♭7) so it transposes to any key. The more important half of the lesson, credited to teacher **Tom Quayle**, is what to do with the sequence *after* it's under your fingers: never play it verbatim in a solo — drill it briefly, then immediately break it into small fragments and drop those into real phrasing over the backing track, because a sequence practiced only as a mechanical run-through never shows up in actual improvising. Short (10 min), single concept, no ad break, and the demonstrated musical result (Seriff soloing over his own track using fragments of the pattern) is on-camera, not just described.

## Techniques
- **The problem being solved: scale-runner syndrome.** Naming the exact failure mode — a player learns a scale shape and then plays it only straight up/down in practice, which "doesn't sound like music" no matter how clean the technique is; the whole lesson is a structured way out of that habit. (~0:20–0:55)
- **Broken-thirds ("jump in thirds") scale sequence, taught with scale-degree numbers so it transposes to any key/scale**: skip a note, step back to the skipped note, skip again — i.e. 1→2→♭3, back to 2→4, ♭3→5, 4→6, 5→♭7, 6→octave, continuing the same shape up the full scale, then mirrored descending. Full worked-out sequence + reusable practice framing written up as its own page since it's a generalizable drill, not tied to this song → [[Scale Sequences in Thirds]] (~1:00–3:40)
- **Chord/scale choice: E Dorian, played in the standard 12th-fret Dorian shape, because it's what fits the changes** — the backing vamp is **E minor 7 → A7**, a classic ii–V-flavored one-family Dorian context (minor 7 moving to the dominant built off its own 4th degree, the textbook "Dorian vamp" sound). → **[[LEAD Slot]]** soloing vocabulary; scale formula matches [[Diatonic Modes]] (1 2 ♭3 4 5 6 ♭7). (~1:10–1:25)
- **A tab is provided on-screen for the exact sequence fingering** — the spoken degree numbers move fast enough that auto-captions garble partway through, but the visual tab (not captured in the transcript) is the authoritative source for the precise fret positions; the shape (broken thirds up, then down) is unambiguous regardless. (~1:40, ~3:00)
- **The core practice-philosophy tip, credited to teacher Tom Quayle:** drilling a scale/technical exercise straight up and down builds finger facility but doesn't by itself make you a better improviser — "you can't practice learning how to improvise better if you don't actually practice musical things." A sequence is only a musical concept if you practice it *as* music (fragmented, rhythmic, in the pocket), not as a rote pattern. (~3:45–4:40)
- **Practice-room dosage: drill the isolated sequence for 1–5 minutes only** — playing it straight through for longer than that "starts to sound dull," which is expected and fine; the drilling phase is deliberately short before moving to the next step. (~5:40–5:55)
- **Application method: grab small fragments of the sequence rather than the full run, and place them rhythmically/in the groove/pocket over the backing track** — this is the specific move that turns a mechanical exercise into music; demonstrated live with Seriff soloing over the "Use Me" backing track using pulled fragments rather than the complete pattern. (~4:45–5:30)
- **Long-term integration step: after the isolated drilling, work the fragments into your existing repertoire/material** rather than treating the sequence as a standalone party piece — named as the intended endpoint (shown, per Seriff, "on the outro") though the transcript cuts before that section is elaborated. (~6:00–6:20)
- **Practice mistake to avoid, restated from his other Dorian lesson's philosophy:** never restrict yourself to playing an exercise verbatim in performance/improvisation just because you can play it fast — speed on the isolated drill is explicitly deprioritized in favor of musical use. (~4:55–5:15)
- **Source material named for further practice**: Seriff's own "Use Me" (Bill Withers) backing/jam-track video (linked from his channel, not this video directly) is the vehicle for the demo — a one-chord-family Dorian vamp, good generic practice bed for this sequence or any Dorian soloing drill.
