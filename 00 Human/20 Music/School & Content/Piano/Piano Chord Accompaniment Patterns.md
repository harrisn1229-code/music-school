# Piano Chord Accompaniment Patterns

Part of [[Music Theory]]. A catalog of figurations/patterns for turning a chord progression into an actual piano part — this is the "how do I actually play these chords" question that sits underneath [[Chord Progressions]]. Any pattern here can be applied to any progression; picking the right one is a mix/arrangement decision (how much rhythmic energy, how much space to leave for other instruments) as much as a theory one.

## The patterns

1. **Held chords** — just sustain each chord for its full duration, no rhythm added. Provides a harmonic bed without competing with the melody. *Take Me to Church* (Hozier).
2. **Quarter-note pulse** — right hand restates the chord 4×/bar, left hand plays the root once per bar. Simple, steady, supportive. *Sign of the Times* (Harry Styles).
3. **Eighth-note left hand under a quarter-note right hand** — left hand doubles to 2 hits per right-hand hit, adding drive/forward motion. *The Scientist* (Coldplay).
4. **Top-bottom pattern** — play the chord's top notes first, then the root alone, alternating. *Don't Look Back in Anger* (Oasis) — itself borrowed (same pattern, same chords) from *Imagine* (John Lennon); note-for-real: **patterns aren't owned** — reusing one with a different chord progression is standard practice, not plagiarism.
5. **Arpeggio, up-and-down** — play a chord's notes low→high→low instead of together. *Someone Like You* (Adele).
6. **Arpeggio, up only** — Beethoven's *Moonlight Sonata*.
7. **Arpeggio, down only** — *Clocks* (Coldplay); notably varies the pattern (down twice, then down only halfway) right before a chord change, showing the base idea can be interrupted/varied rather than run mechanically.
8. **Extended-voicing arpeggios** — richer patterns become available once a chord has more than 3 notes to draw from (5-note voicings etc.) — Chopin's *Prelude in C major*, built entirely around one such arpeggio figure.
9. **Alberti bass** — named for composer Domenico Alberti, popularized by Mozart: low-high-middle-high note order (a specific "broken chord" shape, distinct from a straight up/down arpeggio). Classical-coded sound, rare in pop, but used in Lennon's *Love*.
10. **Tresillo pattern** — the 3-3-2 rhythm (see [[Time Signatures & Note Values]]) applied as a comping rhythm rather than just a groove; used to make 4/4 feel more propulsive. *All of Me* (John Legend).
11. **"Dembow" pattern** — tresillo in the right hand over a steady quarter-note pulse in the left hand, creating syncopated bounce between the two. Named for the 1990 dancehall song *Dem Bow* (Shabba Ranks), the rhythm's namesake.
12. **3-over-2 polyrhythm** — right hand plays a 3-pattern (as a block chord or a 3-note arpeggio), left hand plays 2, composite rhythm cued as "nice cup of tea." *Daydreaming* (Radiohead). See also [[Syncopation and Metric Displacement]].
13. **4-over-3 polyrhythm** — right hand 4 against left hand 3, composite rhythm cued as "pass the goddamn butter." *Fake Empire* (The National).
14. **Waltz "oom-pah"** — root note alone on beat 1, chord (minus root) on beats 2 and 3, in 3/4; staccato for a light, bouncy feel.
15. **"Turkish march" oom-pah-pah-pah** — the 4/4 extension of the waltz pattern (root alone, then chord three times); named for its use in Mozart's *Rondo Alla Turca* left hand.
16. **Staccato eighth notes (right hand) over held notes (left hand)** — a light, low-density texture that leaves sonic space for a fuller band arrangement (guitar/bass/drums) to occupy. *I Need a Dollar* (Aloe Blacc).
17. **Staccato eighth-note triplets, played high on the keyboard** — a rock-and-roll-era piano comping style (Fats Domino/Little Richard-adjacent); playing high specifically to stay out of the guitar's typical mid-range and the vocal's range. *You Really Got a Hold on Me* (Smokey Robinson & the Miracles).

## Arranging principle: find your spectral space

When playing alongside other instruments (guitar, bass, vocal), think about which register each instrument is likely to occupy and choose a piano register/pattern that doesn't collide with them — bass owns the low end, guitar (especially open chords) tends to sit in the mid-range, vocals sit in their own range. Playing higher and lighter (as in pattern 17 above) is a common way for piano to carve out its own space in a dense early-rock-and-roll-style arrangement rather than competing for the same sonic real estate.
