# Day Tripper — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

Pollack's set-piece essay on **harmonic rhythm** ([[POP-C01 — Harmonic rhythm]]): the whole song runs on tension between a fast tempo and chords that refuse to move. Ten bars of intro on a single chord, a verse managing three changes in its first twelve, and a "middle eight" that is twelve bars of prolonged V.

**Form.** The intro is **five** repeats of the riff — not four, not six. Four would be square, six too long, and an odd count means the verse arrives before you've finished counting the pattern out. Verse 16 bars in four phrases; the bridge is nominally a middle eight and actually **12 bars**. Ostinato reprises sit between verses 1 and 2 and between bridge and final verse, acting as a brake on accumulated momentum. There's a **single bridge** where the period norm was to repeat it — but this one is a total climax, so a second pass would be an anti-climax.

**Harmony.** I, IV and V matched one for one by three altered chords: V-of-V, V-of-vi and major VI. Another way to see it: there isn't a single minor chord in the song. The pacing is the substance — after a ten-bar single-chord intro the verse still can't get off the ground, and the **V-of-V is held four full bars and then still not allowed to resolve** until the end of the following phrase ([[HAR-C07 — Secondary dominants]]).

**Melody.** More rap-like and jagged than tuneful, and genuinely hard to sing without the chords underneath — one reason the record reads as instrumental. Riff and vocal syncopate at **different points and mostly don't coincide** ([[RHY-C02 — Syncopation]]); writing out the composite rhythm is the exercise, because that misalignment is what the groove actually is.

**Arrangement.** The intro thickens by increments — guitar, then bass, then rhythm guitar and tambourine, then **nothing new at all on the fourth repeat**, then drums on the fifth. Holding one repeat back is what keeps the build from sounding mechanical.
