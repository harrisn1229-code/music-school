# How to ACTUALLY guitar solo over the blues in under 5 minutes

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eNtAKv-Ckfg)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eNtAKv-Ckfg)

## Synopsis

A short, no-frills "5 Minute Monday" lesson from Jesse Glick arguing that the blues isn't the pentatonic scale, isn't major/minor pentatonic mixing, and isn't a stash of licks — it's **chord tones**: root, major 3rd, 5th, and (optionally) the flat 7. Rather than teaching scale boxes, the whole lesson is a **fretboard-navigation method**: given any root note anywhere on the neck, find its major 3rd, 5th, and ♭7 using fixed string/fret offsets, with one explicit exception where the offsets change (crossing the G and B strings, where the guitar's tuning interval shifts from a 4th to a major 3rd). Worked entirely over a G blues (G7–C7–D7, plain I-IV-V, no quick change, no turnaround shown). The pedagogical arc is deliberately minimal: build the triad-plus-♭7 shapes for each of the three chords, drill highlighting just those chord tones over the changes (no scale, no licks), and only once that's comfortable, layer the pentatonic scale back on top to add "vocal, melodic character." Framed explicitly as a foundation to build before reaching for scales or memorized licks — the opposite order most beginners default to. No production, gear, or tone content; pure technique/theory for soloing.

## What's taught

- **The core claim: blues soloing is chord-tone outlining, not scale-running.** Root, major 3rd, 5th (the triad) plus the ♭7 (dominant 7th color) are named as the actual vocabulary — "just three notes per chord... maybe a four if you want extra flavor." Presented as a corrective to reaching straight for pentatonic-scale noodling or memorized licks.
- **Finding the major 3rd from any root, by fretboard offset (not by counting scale degrees):** on any string, the major 3rd of a root note sits **half a step down, on the next string over** — e.g. G on the low E string (3rd fret): the major 3rd (B) is a half step down and one string over. This offset holds on every string pair **except** crossing from the G string to the B string, where the guitar's standard tuning drops from a 4th interval to a major 3rd interval between strings — there, the major 3rd sits **on the same fret, one string over**, instead of a half step down.
- **Finding the 5th from any root, by fretboard offset:** on most string pairs, the 5th sits **two frets up, on the next string over**. Same G-to-B-string exception applies: the offset becomes **three frets up** instead of two once the phrase crosses that string pair.
- **Finding the ♭7 (dominant 7th) from any root, by fretboard offset:** the ♭7 sits **on the same fret, two strings back** from the root — again shifting slightly once the G/B string pair is crossed. Framed as optional "extra flavor and color," not one of the three essential triad tones.
- **Why this offset method beats memorizing shapes per position:** learning the *relationship* (half step down/next string, two frets up/next string, same fret/two strings back) means the same mental rule finds the chord tone from *any* root anywhere on the neck, rather than memorizing a separate fixed shape for every position — the lesson explicitly frames this as "right away just by knowing the root note you can find your major third all over the neck."
- **Applying the shapes to the blues changes:** with root-3rd-5th (and ♭7) locatable for any root, map them onto G7 (I), C7 (IV), and D7 (V) — three separate triad-plus-♭7 shapes, one per chord, moved to each chord's own root.
- **Drill order — chord tones alone before any scale:** (1) play *only* the chord tones (root-3-5[-♭7]) slowly through the I-IV-V-I changes, arpeggio-style, with no scale run and no backing track — the point is to hear the chord changes land using nothing but the chord tones themselves; (2) only once that's comfortable, add the pentatonic scale back in on top, which is what supplies the "vocal, melodic" phrasing character the plain chord tones alone don't have.
- **Stated payoff:** playing pure chord tones with no accompaniment should still let a listener *hear* the chord changes going by — proof the notes themselves (not a backing track or a memorized lick) are what states the harmony. Once the ear/hand has this locked in, it generalizes to "any chord changes going by," not just this one G blues.
- **Sequencing advice (the lesson's closing thesis):** don't start by asking "what scale, what arpeggio pattern do I need here" — start with root-3rd-5th(-♭7) as the foundation, get that comfortable, *then* layer in pentatonic vocabulary already known from prior practice. Framed as true "whether it's a major chord, a minor chord, or a dominant chord" — general-purpose, not blues-specific — though only the dominant-7 (blues I-IV-V) case is demonstrated.
- **No sound/tone selection content** — the video is purely fretboard/theory technique, no gear, amp, or tone discussion.
