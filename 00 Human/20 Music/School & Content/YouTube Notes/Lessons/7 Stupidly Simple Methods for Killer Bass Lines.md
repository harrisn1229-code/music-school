# 7 Stupidly Simple Methods for Killer Bass Lines

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lu7430hYRWY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lu7430hYRWY)

## Synopsis

BassBuzz frames "great artists steal, but they don't copy" as the whole video's thesis, then spends nineteen minutes building out seven named, escalating-in-risk methods for turning a bass line you already love into something that reads as your own — not a vague "get inspired" pep talk but a genuinely reusable ideation toolkit, each method demonstrated on 2-4 real song pairs (and one art-history pair, Van Gogh's *Starry Night* borrowing its composition from Hokusai's *The Great Wave*) so the line between "inspired by" and "lawsuit" stays concrete rather than abstract. The methods are explicitly ordered by how much of the original material survives: method one keeps a whole half of a riff verbatim; method seven only borrows a genre's vocabulary. The video closes by stacking multiple methods on a single source riff (Megadeth's "Peace Sells") to show they combine rather than compete — the most practically useful demo in the video, since it's the one move a viewer can copy directly into their own writing process. Two songwriting-law cautionary tales anchor the risk gradient: the Led Zeppelin "Stairway to Heaven" / Spirit "Taurus" lawsuit (Zeppelin won) and the Ray Parker Jr. "Ghostbusters" / Huey Lewis "I Want a New Drug" dispute (Parker settled) — both used as "here's what happens when you undercook the transformation" warnings rather than legal advice. One example (the method-four "rearranged" pop song, framed as "not meant as a diss to Rod Temperton") is deliberately left unnamed in the video itself — likely a rights-avoidance choice — so it's captured here as a named technique without inventing the specific comparison song.

## What's taught

**The premise:** you can rip off a favorite bass line and get away with it, or you can copy it and get sued/embarrassed — the difference is *how much* and *which parts* you take. The over-the-line reference point given up front: Chuck Berry's "I'm Talking About You" bass figure became the Beatles' "I Saw Her Standing There" completely unchanged, note-for-note and rhythm-for-rhythm — no transformation at all. Paul McCartney was never legally required to give Berry a formal songwriting credit for it, but it's held up as the example of taking *both* notes and rhythm with zero change, which is the one combination the video says never to do outright.

**Method 1 — Steal half.** Take a bass line's first half (notes + rhythm) verbatim, then write your own original second half — because the halfway swap is what turns "copied" into "inspired by," while the shared opening still lets a listener feel the lineage. Traced through a real three-generation chain: Kool & the Gang's "Hollywood Swinging" → Chic's "Good Times" (same big, fat low-E opening figure, own second half) → Queen's "Another One Bites the Dust" (same trick again) — all three lock up note-for-note at the start, then diverge halfway through. Further examples of the same steal: Vulfpeck's "3 on E" (a direct tribute to that same lineage), Maneskin's "Gasoline," and the Sugarhill Gang's "Rapper's Delight" (a full rip of "Good Times," not a half-steal — cited as the "don't do this" contrast). **Practical exercise:** pick a bass line you love (the video's worked example is Cream's "Sunshine of Your Love"), snip just the first half, then noodle a new second half using the same underlying scale (a blues scale, in that example) — gets you out of the blank-page freeze because the first half is already decided.
**Method 2 — Steal the rhythm, change the notes.** Keep a riff's exact rhythmic pattern but write entirely new pitches over it — the resulting line can sound completely different while still having the same "feel good" pocket, because rhythm carries more of a groove's identity than the specific notes do. Traced through a lineage the video calls genuinely hard to catch by ear: Ricky Nelson's "Summertime" → Blues Magoos' "We Ain't Got Nothin' Yet" (same riff verbatim) → Deep Purple's "Black Night" → P!nk (unnamed track) → Green Day, all sharing one rhythmic skeleton with the notes reshuffled at each step. **Practical exercise:** take any rhythm you already know cold, pick a scale (or even random notes), and drop new pitches into that exact rhythmic slot — the video's demo turns Madonna's "Express Yourself" rhythm into a completely different-feeling line just by swapping the notes.
**Method 3 — Steal the notes, change the rhythm.** The inverse of method 2, and flagged as the riskiest of the seven when under-executed, because keeping the pitch content intact is what triggers copyright disputes (this is the mechanism behind the "Stairway to Heaven" vs. "Taurus" lawsuit cited in the synopsis — Zeppelin ultimately won, but it's presented as a "you got lucky" case, not a "you're safe" one). Worked example: Led Zeppelin's "Stairway to Heaven" intro reduces to five core anchor notes (A, G, F#, F, E); Chicago's riff (unnamed track) lifts the same five notes with a different rhythm; Green Day's "Jaded"/"Brain Stew" then takes those same notes down a half step (via down-tuned guitars) and strips the rhythm even further — three generations, same note skeleton, three different rhythmic treatments. **Two safety notes:** (1) on maximally generic note sets — roots and fifths, "the bread and butter of bass playing" — this method is nearly untraceable, since songs like "Sittin' On the Dock of the Bay," "I Just Called to Say I Love You," and "Have You Ever Seen the Rain" all share root-fifth skeletons without anyone calling it theft; (2) on a *distinctive* note set (the video's example: Black Sabbath's "Iron Man" riff), keeping the same notes in the same order but changing the rhythm still yields a recognizably different riff — distinctiveness of the source material, not just note-count, determines how much rhythmic change is needed to feel transformed.
**Method 4 — Steal notes and rhythm, but rearrange them.** Keep both the pitch content and the rhythmic content of a source riff, but change the *order* the notes appear in, shift some up/down an octave, or make other structural rearrangements — described as needing real tastefulness, since a lazy rearrangement ("~75% of the rhythm stolen, same key, released ~2 years later in the same genre") reads as an obvious copy rather than a transformation. Cleaner worked example: The Killers bassist Mark Stoermer's riff (unnamed track) vs. The Smiths' "Barbarism Begins at Home" (bassist Andy Rourke) — both are documented Smiths fans (The Killers have covered Smiths songs and toured with Morrissey) — Rourke's original starts on a low root note then resolves with a "bow-bow-bow" melodic tag built from the 2nd and 3rd notes of the minor scale up an octave; Stoermer's riff also opens on a low root, but rearranges that ending tag by moving the last root note up an octave, borrows some of the Smiths' rhythmic vibe (not all of it), makes other subtle note tweaks, and — the part called out as the "artful" half of the steal — diverges into completely different chords for the remaining three bars rather than following the source further. **Practical exercise:** take a well-known riff (the video's demo: Bruno Mars' "Uptown Funk") and simply reshuffle the note order while keeping the same rhythm and roughly the same notes — yields an "inspired by" line without changing register or feel.
**Method 5 — Change the feel.** Keep a riff's anchor notes and core rhythm close to identical, but change tempo, the drum beat underneath it, the bass tone, or the key — any combination that shifts the *context* enough that a listener's ear doesn't flag the similarity, because recognizability is context-dependent, not purely note-dependent. **Cautionary "what not to do" example:** Ray Parker Jr.'s "Ghostbusters" bass line vs. Huey Lewis's "I Want a New Drug" — the notes are only slightly different, AND the bass tone matches, AND the drum beat is similar, AND the intro guitar "slidey" sound effect matches too, so nothing about the context changed — Parker settled the resulting dispute. **Clean example that avoided the same fate:** Jane's Addiction bassist Eric Avery's "Mountain Song" line vs. Iron Maiden's "Wrathchild" (bassist Steve Harris) — same key, same pounding low-E pattern, same driving plucking technique, slightly different specific notes, BUT "Mountain Song" runs more than 20 BPM slower with a completely different drum beat, which was enough context change to read as its own thing rather than a copy. **Practical exercise:** take a riff you know and just try it faster or slower — a new tempo naturally pulls a drummer toward a different beat to match it; the video's demo slows Radiohead's "The National Anthem" and dubs the tone down until it reads as a reggae feel.
**Method 6 — Steal from other instruments.** Take a rhythmic idea from a *non-bass* part — guitar, vocal melody, anything with a strong rhythmic identity — and adapt it onto the bass, rather than only ever borrowing from other basslines. Caveat given directly: this only works when the source part is genuinely rhythmic; a smooth melodic line "doesn't make much of a bass line" when ported over unchanged. Worked example: ABC's "Poison Arrow" bass line (bassist Mark Lickley) borrows from the *guitar* part (Nile Rodgers) in Chic's "Le Freak," not from Chic's own bass line — plausible since "Le Freak" predates "Poison Arrow" by about four years in the same disco/pop-funk lineage. A layered irony noted in the video: Chic's own bassist, Bernard Edwards, *also* borrows from that same Nile Rodgers guitar part later in "Le Freak" itself — so the "steal from another instrument" move happens twice, once within the source song and once again in the song that borrowed from it. **Practical exercise:** take a vocal melody with a strong rhythmic hook (the video's deliberately goofy demo: Flight of the Conchords' "Sugalumps"), drop it down an octave, and add distortion/grit — turns a vocal line into a credible rock bass riff.
**Method 7 — Steal from a different genre.** Take a riff, rhythm, or vibe from one genre and rebuild it in a completely different genre — flagged as the method that requires the *least* direct copying to still feel transformative, since the genre shift itself does most of the transformation work. Framed against the whole lineage of modern genres: "metal came from copying rock, which came from copying blues... jazz came from blues... basically everything came from blues." Two contrasting examples of how well this can be executed: a **weaker** version — Weezer borrowing largely verbatim from The Jam and Jet — is called out as the least elegant example in the video specifically *because* all three acts are already the same genre (rock), so the genre-shift transformation never actually happens; a **legendary** version — Black Sabbath's foundational riff (Tony Iommi/Geezer Butler) reportedly grew out of Geezer Butler's fondness for Gustav Holst's orchestral suite *The Planets* (specifically its "Mars" movement) — genuinely cross-genre (classical → what became heavy metal), and the video credits it as stacking three methods at once: method seven (different genre), method one (only the first half of the "Mars" theme was taken, not the whole movement), and method four (notes and rhythm were also rearranged in the transfer). A secondary reference cited in the video: Iggy Pop drummer Hunt Sales, in an interview on the Produce Like a Pro YouTube channel, describes assembling "Lust for Life"'s beat from a grab-bag of sources (a George of the Jungle cartoon rhythm, The Supremes' "You Can't Hurry Love") — offered as an example of a drummer openly admitting to a multi-source genre-blend steal rather than a clean two-song comparison.
**Grand finale — stacking every method on one source riff.** Using Megadeth's "Peace Sells" bass line as the single starting point, the video runs it through the methods live to prove they compound rather than compete: method 1 (steal just the first half), method 2 (keep the rhythm, swap the notes — "totally different vibe"), method 3 (keep the notes, change the rhythm even further from the original), method 4 (keep the same notes and rhythm, just reorder them), then methods 5 + 7 combined (change the feel *and* the genre at once) to land on a deliberately absurd end point dubbed "Peace Sells: The Sexy Funk Jam" — the point being that a single riff, run through enough of these methods in sequence or combination, can generate effectively unlimited original-feeling ideas rather than one single "inspired by" variant.
