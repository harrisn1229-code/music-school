# I Call Your Name — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

The stylistic odd-one-out among the harder rockers around it — bluesy in flavour but not in form, closer to pop or jazz. The interest comes from a small budget spent oddly: **seven chords total, three of them non-diatonic**, including a V-of-V-of-V with no precedent earlier in the catalogue. The result is continual restless motion with no modulation anywhere.

**Form.** Full two-bridge form with two verses — one instrumental — between the bridges. Every verse after the first uses a variant, so the opening pair reads as one longer couplet-like unit while the later standalone variants sound slightly truncated. Verse and bridge are both 8 bars, parsing into four 2-bar phrases.

**Harmony.** Three of the seven chords are altered or borrowed ([[HAR-C07 — Secondary dominants]], [[HAR-C08 — Borrowed chords and modal interchange]]). The verse runs three dominant sevenths in a row and ends wide open; the verse variant instead cadences **through IV rather than V**, even though the preceding V-of-V has just built the expectation of a dominant ([[HAR-C03 — Plagal cadence]]) — answering a V-of-V with IV is a device they kept using for years. The bridge avoids the tonic completely, so it floats without a clear key. Harmonic rhythm is relaxed at roughly one chord every two bars, and the non-diatonic chords carry the entire sense of motion.

**Melody.** Pentatonic underneath, with the hook phrase outlining 1–3–5–6 and the fourth degree kept out entirely ([[SCL-C03 — Pentatonic scales]]). The appoggiatura over the V-of-V gets extra weight precisely because it's the melody's first non-pentatonic note.

**Arrangement.** The guitar-solo section switches to a jazzy ska feel **at constant tempo** — the drums trade even eighths for a limping dotted rhythm and the bass trades roots for a stepwise walk — so when the original beat returns it registers as *another* change. Probably the first appearance of a trick John reused for the rest of his career.
