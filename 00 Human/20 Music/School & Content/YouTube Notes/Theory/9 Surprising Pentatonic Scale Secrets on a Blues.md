# 9 Surprising Pentatonic Scale Secrets on a Blues

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0w7l-GcT_QI)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0w7l-GcT_QI)

## Synopsis

Jens Larsen — inspired by Wes Montgomery, McCoy Tyner, and Chick Corea — demonstrates 9 pentatonic-scale superimposition approaches over an F blues, aimed at giving a more modern jazz color than the default "F minor pentatonic over everything" approach. Central organizing idea: think in terms of **which pentatonic scale to move to at each harmonic event**, chosen so the underlying pentatonic scales themselves move by a clean interval (a half step or whole step) between chords — the connecting motion, not just each individual scale-to-chord match, is the point.

## What's taught

1. **One-note pentatonic swap for a I→IV move.** F major pentatonic (same as D minor pentatonic) over F7; change one note (A→A♭) for the same shape over B♭7 — identical phrase shape, one altered note, connects the two chords cleanly.
2. **"Playing the V on the I" (Wes Montgomery device).** Use C minor pentatonic over F7 and F minor pentatonic over B♭7 (the pentatonic rooted a 5th above/on the V of each chord) — gives a sus4-flavored color, especially audible if the phrasing leans into the implied sus4.
3. **Altered-dominant pentatonic via its Lydian-dominant relative, on the I→IV turnback (bar 4).** Reading F7altered as B Lydian dominant lets you borrow B major pentatonic (= A♭ minor pentatonic) over the altered F7, then slide the same pentatonic shape down a half step to A major pentatonic (= G minor pentatonic) over B♭7 — one shape, one half-step slide, connects an altered dominant into the IV chord.
4. **Same half-step-connecting-pentatonic idea applied to a C7 altered → F cadence.** E♭ minor pentatonic (tritone-sub-derived) over C7altered, D minor pentatonic over the preceding Gm7 — pentatonics again moving by a half step, creating an ear-catching "counterpoint" against a cadence that harmonically wants to resolve downward while the pentatonic line moves upward.
5. **A chromatic ii-V chain reharmonization (a common device, e.g. Chick Corea on "Matrix"):** superimpose Bb7→Ebm7→Ab7→Dbm7→Gm7→C7→F as a descending chain of chromatic ii-Vs over a plain blues turnaround, playing each minor chord's pentatonic scale through the chain — deliberately "against" a simpler backing track for a burst of tension that resolves cleanly back into the form.
6. **A vaguer variant of #5**, substituting F minor pentatonic for the target-chord-specific pentatonics through the same chromatic ii-V chain — less precisely chord-outlining, but keeps the half-step-descending pentatonic motion as the throughline, which matters more than exact chord-tone accuracy.
7. **A minor-6 (Dorian) color on the I chord**: take F major pentatonic (=D minor pentatonic), change one note (E♭→E) to get an "F minor 6"-flavored pentatonic — an alternate one-note-swap target for the I chord, paired with the standard B♭7 pentatonic from device #1 as a second phrase within the same chorus, deliberately connecting phrases across chords rather than re-picking a scale fresh at every chord change.
8. **Tritone-substitution ii-V reading of the turnback**, generating a chain of half-step-ascending pentatonics: read the "F7 → B♭7" turnback as F♯m→B7 (tritone sub of the ii-V), yielding F♯ minor pentatonic moving up a half step into B♭ major (=G minor) pentatonic.
9. **Lydian-dominant color via a single-note pentatonic alteration.** F major pentatonic (=D minor pentatonic) with the 5th flattened (turning it into a D minor 6 pentatonic) puts the ♯11 front and center over F7 — described as a "far-out" sound reachable by changing just one note from a completely standard pentatonic.

**Meta-principle stated at the end:** think in terms of *pentatonic scale progressions* that move by clean intervals (half/whole step) alongside the chord changes, not just "which single scale fits this single chord" — the connective motion between scales is what makes lines sound intentional across a whole chorus rather than restarting at every chord.
