# 4 classic chord progressions and why they work

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9zbP0GQzUuI)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9zbP0GQzUuI)

## Synopsis

A companion piece to Bennett's other "chord progressions and why they work" videos — four more recurring progressions, this time weighted toward minor-key and modal sounds (harmonic minor, a rotated "50s" loop, the three primary chords, and Dorian) rather than straight major-key diatonic ones.

## Techniques

- **Harmonic-minor vamp — i–VI–V** (e.g. Am–F–E in A minor) — the raised leading tone that harmonic minor adds gives V a real dominant pull even inside an otherwise all-minor-key loop. Heard in "Sweet Dreams (Are Made of This)" (Eurythmics).
- **The "Viva la Vida" progression — IV–V–I–vi** — audience-suggested name for the same four chords as the I–vi–IV–V "50s"/doo-wop loop, just rotated to start on IV instead of I. The climb (IV→V resolving up to I) then vi begins the climb back down, which is what makes the loop restart smoothly rather than sounding cut off. Heard in "Viva la Vida" (Coldplay), "I Want to Hold Your Hand" (The Beatles), "Walking in Memphis" (Marc Cohn).
- **I–IV–V–IV** — built entirely from the three primary/functional chords: tonic (rest), subdominant (mild tension, not desperate to resolve), dominant (strong pull home). Climbs I→IV→V then descends back through IV instead of resolving straight to I, which is what keeps the loop going instead of sounding finished.
- **Dorian vamp — i–IV** (e.g. Em–A in E Dorian) — just two chords. The IV chord is what specifically signals Dorian: raising natural minor's 6th degree turns the otherwise-minor iv chord into a major IV, which is what gives Dorian its "minor yet bright" character. Heard in "Another Brick in the Wall, Pt. 2" (Pink Floyd).
