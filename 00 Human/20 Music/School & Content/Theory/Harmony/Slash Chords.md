# Slash Chords

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Chords]] / [[Chord Naming by Context]]. A **slash chord** as written on a jazz/fusion chart (`Bb/C`, `A/C`, `D/C`…) is almost always a **triad or seventh chord voiced over a bass note**, not a true two-key [[Upper Structure Chords|polychord]] (though the polychord reading exists and is occasionally the intent) — the upper letter is a normal chord, the lower letter is what's in the bass, and "read" it as "\<upper chord> over \<bass note>." The symbol looks mysterious mainly because it refuses to sort into a familiar category (m7, dominant 7, maj7) at a glance — it has to be decoded rather than read off a symbol chart.

## The interpretation method
1. **Take the bass note as the root**, whether or not it's actually a tone of the upper triad.
2. **Relate every note of the upper triad back to that root** as a scale degree/extension (3rd, ♭7, 9, ♯11, ♭13…).
3. **Name the resulting chord** from those degrees — this tells you whether it's functioning as a dominant, a major-7-type, a minor-7-type, or an ambiguous/diminished-adjacent color, and therefore how it wants to resolve.
4. If the bass note **is** a chord tone of the upper triad, there's no new chord at all — it's just an inversion (e.g. `Ab/C` is 1st-inversion Ab major, `F/C` is 2nd-inversion F major).
5. Chords with **no 3rd and no 7th present** (e.g. `D/C`, built from 9-♯11/♭5-13) are genuinely ambiguous between a major-7-type (lydian) and dominant-type (lydian dominant) reading — the surrounding harmony decides which; you can't tell from the voicing alone.
6. This same take-the-bass-as-root method works identically on **minor, diminished, and augmented** upper triads, not just major (only major triads are catalogued below) — and generalizes to any 4-note upper chord over a bass note too (see below).

## All twelve major triads over a C bass note
Worked chromatically from Jens Larsen's lesson — every possible major triad stacked over the same static C bass, analyzed by the method above:

| Upper triad / C bass | Notes relative to C | Reads as | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| `C/C` | 1-3-5 | **C major** | Bass note is the root — just a plain C chord, no slash needed |
| `B/C` | ♭3(enh. D♯), ♭5(enh. F♯), maj7 | **C diminished with a major 7** | Sounds like a sus4-diminished suspension device — a "2-5-1" where the diminished chord suspends the I. See [[Diminished Chords]] |
| `Bb/C` | ♭7, 9, 11 | **C7sus4(9)** | Same voicing as the classic textbook slash-chord example (B♭ triad/C bass) |
| `A/C` | maj3, ♭9, 13 | **C7(♭9, 13)** — "diminished harmony" flavor | Ambiguous ♭7-vs-maj7 chord in isolation; in practice almost always heard as dominant |
| `Ab/C` | ♭3(enh.), 5, root | **Ab major, 1st inversion** | C is already a chord tone (the 3rd of Ab) — not a new chord, just an inversion |
| `G/C` | 9, 5, maj7 | **C major-7-type, rootless (Cmaj9 no 3rd)** | No 3rd present so quality is technically ambiguous, but reads as major in most contexts — this is Jens's own go-to voicing for a Cmaj7 sound |
| `Gb/C` | ♭9, ♭5(♯11), ♭7 | **C7(♭9, ♭5)** — dissonant, wants to resolve | Ambiguous major/minor in isolation, clearly a tension chord in context |
| `F/C` | 5, root, 3rd | **F major, 2nd inversion** | C is the 5th of F — not a new chord, just an inversion |
| `E/C` | maj3, ♯5, maj7 | **Cmaj7♯5** | |
| `Eb/C` | ♭3, 5, ♭7 | **C minor 7** | Recognize this one on sight — it's just Cm7 spelled as a slash chord |
| `D/C` | 9, ♯11/♭5, 13 | **Ambiguous: Cmaj7♯11 (lydian) or C7♯11 (lydian dominant)** | No 3rd, no 7th present — context alone decides which reading applies |
| `Db/C` | ♭9, ♯4/sus4, ♭13 | **C7sus(♭9, ♭13)** — "phrygian" sound | Heard as a susb9/phrygian-dominant color |

## Worked ii–V–I examples using these voicings
All three from the same lesson, chosen to show the slash chords in real progressions — written on the chart as their **functional names** (a ii–V–I), not as slash symbols; the slash reading is just how you find the voicing:
- **ii–V–I in F:** Gm7(11) → `A/C` [reads as C7(♭9,13)] → Fmaj6/9. The A-major-triad-over-C voicing supplies the dominant's alterations.
- **Minor ii–V into a lydian I:** Dm7 → G7(♭13/♯5) → `D/C` [reads as Cmaj7♯11] used as the tonic voicing, resolving with a lydian color instead of plain maj7.
- **ii–V–I in F (2nd pass):** Gm11 → `Gb/C` [reads as C7(♭9,♭5)] → straightforward drop-2 Fmaj7. Same dissonant Gb/C from the table above used as the dominant right before resolution.

## Beyond triads, and comping without the bass note
- **Four-note chords over a bass note work the same way** but usually carry a functional meaning beyond "here's a color" — e.g. `Dm7/C` is really a bass-motion device standing in for a known chord (here, a half-diminished-adjacent sound); the slash is describing a bass line move more than a new harmony.
- **When comping (not playing the bass note yourself)**, the "extra" note that would have been the bass can instead be voiced as a color tone on TOP of the triad — e.g. taking the `Gb/C` voicing above and putting the C on top of the Gb triad instead of underneath it produces a different, unexplored color. Flagged in the video as a follow-up-lesson candidate; not worked out in detail here.
- **The takeaway framing:** don't treat slash chords as their own scary category — think of each one as *another way to voice a chord type you already know*, evaluate whether you like the sound, and use it as a voicing choice rather than trying to memorize the symbol itself.

## From videos

- **The two jobs slash-chord notation actually does, plus the polychord it's easily confused with.** **Inversion notation**: chord/bass-note is the standard chart shorthand for "play this chord, put that note on the bottom" instead of writing "1st/2nd/3rd inversion" — 1st inversion (3rd in bass) in *Use Somebody* (Kings of Leon, C/E) and *All I Want for Christmas Is You* (Mariah Carey, G/B); 2nd inversion (5th in bass) opens *God Only Knows* (Beach Boys, D/A); 3rd inversion (only possible on a 4+ note chord) opens *Ain't No Mountain High Enough* (Bm7/A). **Shorthand for a more sophisticated chord**: sometimes the bass note isn't even in the chord above it — D/G is really Gmaj9 missing the 3rd, notated as a slash chord because it reads faster than the full extension name; *Rock With You*'s (Michael Jackson) published sheet music notates its Em9-A♭11-B♭11 progression as slash chords for exactly this reason. **Line clichés**: a static upper chord over a descending chromatic bass (Am-Am/G♯-Am/G-Am/F♯-Am/F) technically produces chords with awkward "true" names (G♯aug, Fm7♭5 no-3rd, etc.) — slash notation sidesteps that and makes the one-chord-descending-bass logic visually obvious (*Ballad of a Thin Man*, Dylan). **Polychord (a different, rarer notation)**: two full chords stacked with a **horizontal** line, not a diagonal slash — D-over-C (polychord) = full D triad stacked over a full C triad, vs. D/C (slash) = D chord with just the note C in the bass. Rare outside classical/some jazz (Stravinsky's *The Rite of Spring* is a well-known example) — don't confuse the two symbols. Full breakdown → [[What are Slash Chords used for]]. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[What are Slash Chords used for]]
- **Three specific slash-chord substitutions for common diatonic chords, each swapping a plain triad for a lusher equivalent without changing the underlying function.** **IV/V** (in C: F/G, functionally a G9sus4/G11) substitutes for a plain V→I resolution — *The Long and Winding Road* (Beatles), *Don't Know Why* (Norah Jones). **V/IV** (in C: G/F) — right hand moves to V while the left hand holds IV, a floating/suspended sound; appears inside the "Royal Road" Japanese pop progression (IV-V-iii-vi) — *Peaches* (Jack Black, *Super Mario Bros. Movie*), functionally an A♭7 in third inversion. **V-triad over the 6th-degree bass** (in C: G/A, "the Tiny Dancer chord") substitutes for a plain vi (Am) — effectively Am11 missing the 3rd/5th; Elton John's *Tiny Dancer* climbs the right hand through successive inversions of G over a static A bass for a rising, hovering effect. Full breakdown → [[3 Special Chords That You Can Use]]. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[3 Special Chords That You Can Use]]
- **A riff-writing device: keep a fixed upper voicing constant while walking the bass note underneath it through the key.** Worked over a 3-note upper voicing (B-F♯-G, the top of an Emadd9-type shape) held unchanged while the bass note moves C → D → B — since all three upper notes stay diatonic to E minor throughout, the harmony never leaves the established key even though the chord's function/color shifts with each new bass note. A cheap way to generate a small progression from one memorized voicing rather than learning a new chord shape for each harmony. → [[Chord Voicings]] — BERNTH, [[How To Write AMAZING Modern Metal Riffs! - Guitar Lesson]]

- **A delayed-resolution voicing built entirely around keeping the bass note static across a chord change: D half-diminished over a held C bass (Dø7/C), resolving to plain C major with the bass note never moving.** Third-inversion voicing of a half-diminished chord reharmonizing a held tonic note (C) as its ♭7 — praised as the presenter's favorite of a ten-example delayed-resolution set specifically because "the root stays the same on the bottom" through the resolve, i.e. only the upper chord tones change while the slash bass note is the thread that ties the substitute chord to the eventual tonic. Full 10-example set → [[Reharmonization]] § *1b. Delayed resolution*. → [[Reharmonization]], [[Diminished Chords]] — Jared Borowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared), [[Delayed Resolution - A Gorgeous Reharmonization Technique (10 Examples)]]
- **Two deliberate slash-chord bass walks written into a beginner open-chord arrangement of "Georgia on My Mind" — the chord *identity* stays fixed while the bass line descends stepwise underneath it, then continues descending across two consecutive chords.** `Am/G`: the chord stays a plain A minor triad, but G is forced as the lowest note — explicitly framed as intentional ("the a but the chord stays A minor"), not an inversion of any other chord. `D/F♯` immediately follows: a normal D shape moved up the neck so the bass note (F♯) continues the SAME downward stepwise line the Am/G started, making the two slash chords one continuous two-bar bass descent rather than two unrelated voicings. Extended further with **D7/F♯** (adds the melody note, "a little more spice") and **D9/F♯** — the jazziest option, where the shape voiced doesn't even contain the chord's own root (D); the singer supplies the root, so the chord is still functionally a D9/F♯ even though the fretted shape alone wouldn't read that way. General principle stated directly: **"when you see slash chords, it's asking for an intentional movement of a bassline — you don't have to play them [as a moving bass], but it significantly enhances the harmonic motion and feel."** Companion bonus technique in the same video generalizes this into freely walking chromatic passing bass notes between ANY two chords, not just ones already written as slash chords → [[🎵 HOAGY CARMICHAEL - Georgia on My Mind]] § *Reharmonization devices*. → [[🎵 HOAGY CARMICHAEL - Georgia on My Mind]], [[Chromatic Bass Lines]], [[Reharmonization]] — Jared Borowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared), [[Georgia on My Mind Chords (Guitar Lesson) - Easiest way to play the REAL chords]]
