## What's taught

- **Pitch matching is the prerequisite skill underneath everything else in ear training**: can you sing along with a melody (roughly, any octave)? Sing back a heard note? Find a heard note on your instrument? Sing a random pitch and find it on the instrument? Play a random note and sing it back? If any of these are shaky, THIS is where to start — ear training constantly falls back on "sing it or find it on the instrument" whenever recognition fails, so without this fallback there's no way forward when something isn't immediately recognized. Trained simply: sing along with music you like, try to match pitches you hear on an instrument, repeat.
- **Roadmap to a competent ear**: several parallel, non-sequential paths that reinforce each other rather than a strict linear order — (1) tonic recognition (foundational — see below), (2) melodic scale degrees (ranked above melodic intervals in importance, though both matter), (3) melodic intervals, (4) bass lines (a bridge between melody and harmony — bass notes carry the same scale-degree feelings as melody notes, but are also the foundation of the harmony above them), (5) chord qualities (major/minor/sus/9/11/13/inversions), (6) chord positions/progressions (the number system — is a chord the 1, 4, 5, or non-diatonic like a ♭6 or ♭2). In real transcribing/jamming these all mix spontaneously with no clean separation of technique — whatever's needed in the moment is what gets used.
- **Tonic perception works like resolving a low-resolution image as more data arrives**: the ear/mind is constantly running a "simplest explanation" process on the notes it hears — every new note either reinforces or breaks the current tonic assumption, and the assumption updates continuously, becoming clearer the more notes confirm it. If all notes are within one diatonic scale with no contradicting harmonic context, the ear will settle on that scale's tonic automatically — no conscious effort required.
- **How modulation/key-change is perceived**: chords keep confirming one tonic repeatedly, then a PIVOT CHORD (common to both the old and new key) appears; from there, new chords stop conforming to the old key's assumption; once enough assumptions break, the ear gives up the old tonic and adopts the new one — that switch is the subjectively perceivable "whoosh" feeling of a key change. Borrowed/parallel-mode notes (e.g. hearing C major, then some notes from C minor/Phrygian/Dorian) generally do NOT trigger a key change on their own — the ear tends to keep the same tonic through modal mixture; a genuine key change requires enough non-conforming material, in the right combinations, that no mode of the original tonic can explain it anymore.
- **"How do I stop mistaking scale degree 4 or 5 for the tonic?"** — not a hearing error, just insufficient repetition differentiating those specific feeling states; the fix is the same as everywhere else — more focused listening time on that exact confusion pair (native-speaker-vs-language-learner analogy: recognition speed is purely a function of accumulated exposure, nothing else).
- **Transcription as a rank beginner**: start with melodies (find the melody by ear using whatever works, without artificially restricting yourself to "pure" scale-degree hearing) before attempting chords. For chords as a beginner with no tonic/chord-quality training yet, listen for the bass note and the top note of each chord first (those two carry the most usable information); inner notes, if audible, help further but are hard at this stage. When nothing in the existing vocabulary matches what's heard, the fallback (used at every skill level, not just as a beginner) is brute force: play the passage repeatedly, match individual pitches by voice or instrument one at a time, piece together the full chord/passage, name it, file it into personal vocabulary, then try writing a short progression that uses it to cement the sound for next time.
