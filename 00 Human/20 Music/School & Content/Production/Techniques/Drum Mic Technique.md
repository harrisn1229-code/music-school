# Drum Mic Technique

**The part of a live drum sound that's decided before any plugin loads.** Mic choice, position, and how the mics are checked/blended against each other — the tracking-stage counterpart to [[Vocal Mic Technique]]. This vault's own drum chains currently run entirely from samples/loops (see the source note on [[2 Drums Group|Drums Group]]) — this note captures the tracking-stage discipline as reference, for the day a real kit gets mic'd, and because the mic-position lessons (proximity effect especially) transfer directly to mic'd overdubs and sample-source recording either way.

## The core split: direct mics get the heat, ambient mics stay alone

**Kick and snare (direct/close mics) are where EQ and dynamics processing belongs; overheads and room mics mostly shouldn't be touched.** Stated as the whole philosophy in one line: "the important thing about cutting drums is the direct mics — the kick and snare — that's where you're gonna put some heat and EQ'ing... the room tracks and the [overheads] — they're so good you're not gonna EQ those, so it's easy, left for later." A good overhead/room pair already captures the kit's real balance and space; processing it risks undoing what the mic placement already got right. Spend the EQ/comp budget where the mics are closest and least representative of the whole kit (kick, snare), and leave the mics that already sound like "the record" alone. → [[EQ]], [[2 Drums Group|Drums Group]]

**"Never be shy with EQ" on the direct mics** — the flip side of the rule above: once you've decided a mic needs correcting (a snare read too dark/deep on first listen), commit to the move rather than under-correcting it. Tentative EQ on a direct mic wastes the one place in the chain where aggressive correction is actually the job. → [[EQ]]

## Checking the kit, mic by mic

**Single hits per drum, checked in order, before playing the whole kit together.** Kick, snare (top and bottom separately), overheads, each tom individually, room, hi-hat — go around the kit having the drummer hit one piece at a time so basic level and pan are confirmed before anything gets layered into a full pattern. Catch problems here (a too-loud bottom-snare mic, a bus limiter reading hot) before they're baked into a take. → [[Gain Staging]]

**The overhead is the single most important mic on the kit.** "Overhead is real important, that's the whole picture of the record" — it's the one mic capturing the kit as a listener actually hears it (balance, blend, room character all at once), not just one drum in isolation. Get the overhead right and every direct mic underneath it is filling in detail, not carrying the whole picture. → [[Depth and Space]]

**Toms: pan each one to a distinct position, but keep EQ moves consistent across the set.** Check toms one at a time with single hits, panning each to its own spot across the stereo field (e.g. one hard right, the next center, working across the kit) — but deliberately avoid tuning each tom's EQ differently from its neighbors. Distinct pan positions read as a real kit laid out in space; inconsistent tone from tom to tom reads as mismatched drums. Pan for position, EQ for cohesion. *(qualitative — no exact pan/Hz values given in the source)* → [[EQ]], [[Width and Stereo]]

**Solo the room track and just listen before deciding anything about it.** Bring it up on its own, react to what it's actually capturing, before folding it back into the blend — the room mic's job gets evaluated as its own sound first, not judged only in context.

## Room mic: no reverb, light bus compression only

**A good room mic replaces artificial reverb, it doesn't need it added on top.** "This is no reverb, just a little bus compression" — since the room mic is already capturing real room ambience, adding a reverb plugin on top would be doubling up space that's already there. The only processing reached for: light bus compression (a hardware Fairchild, in this case) to glue/control the room signal, nothing else. Distinct from [[Vocal Mic Technique]]'s headphone-mix reverb note (there, reverb helps the *singer's performance*; here, the mic is already recording literal room reverb, so adding more is redundant rather than helpful). → [[Reverb]], [[Compression]], [[Vintage Gear Worklist]]

## Fix mic problems by moving the mic, not by EQ-ing around them

**Proximity effect on a hi-hat mic — fixed by pulling the mic back a foot, not by cutting the resulting low end.** A directional mic's bass response rises as it gets closer to the source (the same mechanism documented for vocals on [[Vocal Mic Technique]]) — when the hi-hat mic was reading with too much low end from sitting too close, the fix was physically increasing the distance ("pull the hi-hat back a foot"), not reaching for a high-pass filter after the fact. The general discipline: when a mic-position problem has a mic-position fix, take it — EQ-ing around a placement problem after the take is committed is strictly worse than not having the problem in the take at all. → [[Vocal Mic Technique]]

## Overall philosophy

**Simple miking + a good drummer > an elaborate mic setup + heavy processing.** "If you keep the miking simple and you have a good drummer, it's really easy — it's all about how you blend it." The performance and the level relationships between mics (the blend) do more of the actual work than plugin choices downstream — a recurring theme this vault's [[2 Drums Group|Drums Group]] chain notes already carry from other engineers (Kenny Beats' four-close-mics-no-room-mic approach, Zach Scholz's mechanical-bleed-control-beats-gating rule). → [[Gain Staging]], [[2 Drums Group|Drums Group]]

## From videos

- **Three named stereo mic formations for capturing an ensemble/room, each filling the 90° stereo field differently.** **Spaced pair**: two identical mics, equally spaced from each other AND from the source (the equal spacing specifically minimizes phase cancellation between them), panned hard L/R after recording. **Mid/side**: one cardioid mic (stays centered, mono) + one figure-8 (bidirectional) mic, duplicated with one copy phase-inverted, both panned hard L/R — three channels total (mid, side-L, side-R); the mid/side LEVEL balance directly sets the recorded stereo width, adjustable even after tracking. **Decca tree**: a spaced pair (panned hard L/R) plus a third, un-panned center omni mic — the omni fills the gap a spaced pair alone leaves in the middle of the field, for a fuller, more continuous stereo image than a spaced pair achieves on its own. → [[Width and Stereo]] — Sage Audio, [What is Stereo Imaging?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=h1aNrMEi5XA)
- **Sennheiser 421 on kick, occasionally doubled, positioned close (a couple of inches from the head), inside near the beater — a deliberately simple, unfancy setup.** "When I would do it I was using nothing really too fancy — I would use a Sennheiser, a lot of times a Sennheiser 421, on the kick, sometimes I use two mics, usually just inside near the head, a couple of inches from the head." A minimal, two-mic-max approach that contrasts with this note's own deep multi-mic kick references (Sylvia Massy's 421+sub-mic build, JJP's D30→FET47→speaker stack) — worth reaching for when the goal is speed and simplicity over an elaborate character build. → [[Kick Design]], [[1 Drums Kick Track|Drums Kick Track]] — Mix with the Masters (Andy Wallace), [[Mix with the Masters 2011 - Andy Wallace]]
- **Fix drum mic problems by moving the mic in small increments, live, one piece at a time — a direct worked example of this note's own "fix mic problems by moving the mic" rule, applied across a whole kit rather than just a hi-hat.** Joe Chiccarelli, tracking session: hi-hat mic moved up slightly for more stick attack; rack tom aimed closer to where the drummer actually hits the head for more presence; floor tom brought in and pointed more toward the middle of the drum; kick's inside mic nudged a tiny bit closer to the shell for more bottom weight; kick's outer mic angle changed slightly to reduce beater click; room mic pair spread wider (same distance from kit) for more stereo width. Every fix physical, none reached for EQ. → [[1 Drums Kick Track|Drums Kick Track]], [[Width and Stereo]] — Mix with the Masters (Joe Chiccarelli), [[Tracking Drums - Joe Chiccarelli]]
- **A "wild card" character mic thrown into the room on purpose, distinct from the main room pair — here, a trashy Altec placed out in the middle of the room.** Named as a standing habit ("sometimes I throw some wild card mics into the equation"), confirmed sounding good once repositioned around it. Same family as this note's other character-mic entries (JJP's birdcage mic, Epworth's speaker-as-kick-mic) — a deliberately colored extra source layered under the clean main mics. → [[Sound Selection Guide]] — Mix with the Masters (Joe Chiccarelli), [[Tracking Drums - Joe Chiccarelli]]
- **A caution on this note's own room-mic bus-compression rule: a stereo compressor on far room mics adds real excitement/energy, but can tip over into too much — back it off (or bypass) rather than re-EQ.** "I like what my far room mics sound like with a compressor, but they might actually be a little over the top, so I might just back the compressor off... without that stereo compressor it's a little boring sounding." Same instrument (a room-bus stereo compressor for glue/excitement) as this note's existing Fairchild room-comp rule above, with the added nuance that it's a dial-to-taste amount, not an always-on fixed move. → [[Compression]] (effect) — Mix with the Masters (Joe Chiccarelli), [[Tracking Drums - Joe Chiccarelli]]
- **Reamp a whole kit through a flat-response amp, then capture only that amp's room with a single distant mic — an ambient-overdub technique distinct from close-mic room recording.** Andrew Scheps busses every close mic on the kit (kick, snare, toms, overhead, a second front-of-kit mic) into a keyboard amp chosen for its flat, uncolored response, then records only one distant ribbon mic (RCA KU3, positioned high) capturing the amp's room — "we're basically doing a lively amp of the drums and capturing it with one microphone." None of the close mics are actually recorded; they exist only to drive the amp. Full technique on [[Ambient Texture and Field Recording]]. → [[Ambient Texture and Field Recording]], [[TEXTURE Slot]], [[Gate]] — Mix with the Masters (Andrew Scheps), [[Overdubs - Andrew Scheps]]
- **A deep multi-mic drum-kit reference built on the "get the sound right up front" philosophy — commit at the source rather than chase it later in the mix.** Sylvia Massy, tracking The Melvins on a Neve 8048: "if I like the sound I don't want to have to try to chase that sound later — I get the right sound up front and then I come into that sound." Full kit: kick (Sennheiser 421 + homemade sub mic), snare (SM57 top + SM81 top run through a dbx 160 for snap, blended under the SM57, + a phase-reversed bottom mic), toms (AKG C12A top + phase-reversed Sennheiser 421 bottom, same setup on both toms), hat/ride (KM 54 tube mics), MS overheads on a rare AKG C24 (one capsule down for cymbals, one figure-8 for sides, side phase flipped in the patch bay), room (a pair of U67 tube mics), plus two special-character mics: a mono JZ Black Hole room mic through heavy compression, and a "crush" ribbon mic through a Teletronix LA-2A. → [[Phase Alignment]], [[Width and Stereo]], [[UAD Teletronix LA-2A]], [[Compression Staging]], [[Gain Staging]] — Sylvia Massy (Mix with the Masters), [[Analog Recording workflow - Sylvia Massy]]
- **Build a versatile, mix-and-match drum-sound "palette" at setup time, before the band arrives or the song is known, so you're ready to hit record without repatching.** Tchad Blake's explicit goal: "if the band came in and said we don't have time for anything, record without even listening to it... I'm reasonably sure that in 99% of the cases I can make this work." Method: rapidly audition combinations of mic sources and processing (a SansAmp-driven signal panned one way, compressed overheads panned the other, blended with a flat/unprocessed bass-drum mic) so the range of available colors is known ahead of time — a current favorite blend is exactly that SansAmp + compressed-overheads + flat-bass-drum combination, valued for how much character it offers without heavy processing. → [[Compression]], [[Plugin Wishlist]], [[Vintage Gear Worklist]] — Mix with the Masters (Tchad Blake), [[Drum Sounds - Tchad Blake]]
- **A passive speaker used as a THIRD snare mic (alongside a close dynamic and a condenser), specifically to fill the 200–400 Hz lower-mid pocket and add ambience/tail — not for attack.** JJP's snare build: "545, add the 64, then add a speaker — you can hear how it fills in that lower mid-range area, that two-three-four-hundred area, and actually adds a little bit of ambience to the snare, a little bit tail. So without it, it gets really tight... you want to use it to taste." The kick-drum version of this trick (an NS10 driver as a second kick mic) was already logged below from Paul Epworth — this confirms the same speaker-as-mic move generalizes to snare, aimed at a named frequency pocket rather than character alone. → [[SUB Slot]], [[BASS Slot]], [[Sound Selection Guide]], [[1 Drums Snare-Clap Track|Drums Snare-Clap Track]] — Mix with the Masters (Jack Joseph Puig), [[Tracking drums with Jack Joseph Puig]]
- **Deconstruct the full kit sound live, adding one mic (or mic pair) at a time and A/B'ing each addition, so every mic's individual job is audible before the full blend is heard.** JJP's whole teaching method for this session: kick built D30 → +FET47 → +speaker; snare built 545 → +KM64 → +speaker; then close mics, front/back mono pair, character mics, and cymbal spots each demonstrated in/out in turn. The same instinct as Nick Launay's progressive-solo parallel-drum demos below, applied here to close-mic layering rather than parallel processing. → [[Layering and Stacking]] — Mix with the Masters (Jack Joseph Puig), [[Tracking drums with Jack Joseph Puig]]
- **A front + back "mono, tight, center" support-mic pair, run through heavy compression/EQ and judged by ear only in context — soloed alone it "might not sound like it was that hard," blended with the rest of the kit it reads as genuinely punchy.** Pulling the pair's processing out entirely dropped level but also visibly changed "the EQ curve at the front mic" and lost a "silky top end." The kick's front head is separately noted as flocked (fabric/foam-damped) — the physical prep behind why this pair reads so controlled. Same discipline as this note's room-mic rule above: judge a support mic's contribution in the full blend, not solo. *(exact compression/EQ amounts not given — source unit name garbled in captions)* → [[Compression]], [[EQ]], [[Kick Design]] — Mix with the Masters (Jack Joseph Puig), [[Tracking drums with Jack Joseph Puig]]
- **Spot mics on individual cymbals, layered underneath the overheads specifically to add overtone/ring character the overhead pair alone doesn't fully resolve.** "Here's just the overheads by themselves, now let me add the spots... you're hearing all the overtones and all the ringing... it's really thick sounding." A direct extension of this note's own overhead-as-whole-picture rule above — the overhead still carries the whole kit's balance, but a dedicated cymbal spot mic recovers detail the overhead's broader pickup pattern misses. → [[Width and Stereo]] — Mix with the Masters (Jack Joseph Puig), [[Tracking drums with Jack Joseph Puig]]
- **A deliberately bad-sounding "birdcage" character mic (run through its own distortion-flavored processing) layered in purely for attitude, contrasted against a cleaner "American" mic picking up the kick/snare's front end well — switchable as a pair, kit reads "very natural" with them off and "much cooler" with them on.** Confirms the character-mic layer is a deliberate flavor pass on an already-complete kit sound, not a fix for something missing. → [[Sound Selection Guide]], [[Saturation and Distortion]] — Mix with the Masters (Jack Joseph Puig), [[Tracking drums with Jack Joseph Puig]]
- **A physically distinct second kick drum set up in front of the main kit kick and damped with blankets, purely for character — the live-tracking-stage version of a layered-kick pattern.** Paul Epworth's Adele "Rolling in the Deep" session: a 28" marching band bass drum positioned in front of the main Ludwig kit kick, with blankets thrown over the top to damp it, described only as adding "something to the vibe." Mirrors the digital "layer two kicks" pattern already documented on [[1 Drums Kick Track|Drums Kick Track]] §2, done here with two physical drums plus damping/mic'ing instead of two samples. → [[1 Drums Kick Track|Drums Kick Track]], [[Kick Design]], [[Layering and Stacking]] — Mix with the Masters (Paul Epworth), [[Paul Epworth opens up the session for Adele's "Rolling In The Deep"]]
- **A passive speaker driver (NS10 monitor) used in reverse as a second kick mic, paired with a D12 for the low end.** Epworth: the kick was mic'd with "an NS10 driver on the front of the kick, a D12" — the D12 carries the low/subby foundation, the NS10 driver contributes the "quite bright," "loose slappiness" on top — two mics on one drumhead splitting the tonal job rather than one mic covering everything. An unconventional-mic-as-character-source trick, same family as this vault's other speaker-mic/harmonica-mic entries on [[Sound Selection Guide]]. → [[Sound Selection Guide]] — Mix with the Masters (Paul Epworth), [[Paul Epworth opens up the session for Adele's "Rolling In The Deep"]]
- **A binaural head used to capture the whole kit's overall stereo image, rather than (or alongside) a standard overhead/room pair.** Described as capturing "the overall sound of the kit," giving "this sort of stereo image of the kit" heard as a whole rather than element-by-element — a distinct approach from the overhead-as-the-single-most-important-mic rule elsewhere on this page, worth trying as an alternative when the goal is one cohesive image of the kit rather than a balanced multi-mic blend. → [[Width and Stereo]] — Mix with the Masters (Paul Epworth), [[Paul Epworth opens up the session for Adele's "Rolling In The Deep"]]
- **Direct mic + a fully parallel distorted-channel duplicate, on BOTH kick and snare, plus room mics and overdub brushes stacked on top — four layers total, built up one at a time.** Nick Launay's live-drum setup for a BRMC mix: kick = a direct/close-mic signal blended under a second kick channel run through a guitar distortion pedal (SansAmp) for thickness; snare = a direct SM57 blended under a second snare channel run through a Distressor compressor driven hard, used only for the low end (EQ'd top rolled off, low boosted, for a thuddy layer). Both pairs are demonstrated by soloing progressively — direct alone, then + distorted layer, then + room mics, then + overdub brushes — so each layer's individual contribution is audible before hearing the full stack. **Two entire drum kits** (a lighter one, a heavier "more normal" one) run through this same setup and share the same physical output channels on the desk, with the kit choice per section controlled entirely in the box rather than on separate console channels.
