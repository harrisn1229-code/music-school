# Songs that have a Mixed Meter

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PWHKtkx6F00)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PWHKtkx6F00)

## Synopsis

Distinguishes **mixed meter** (a song that switches time signature repeatedly, sometimes bar-to-bar) from simply being "in" one odd time signature throughout, via a large set of worked song examples — from a single inserted bar (Blackbird, Golden Brown) up to a chorus that cycles through four different meters with no repeating pattern (Genesis, "Turn It On Again").

## Techniques

- **Mixed meter is defined by irregularity, not oddness** — a song fully in 5/8 the whole way through is just "in 5/8"; mixed meter specifically means the meter itself keeps changing, which is a different listening effect (the pulse itself becomes unpredictable, not just uneven-feeling within a stable pulse).
- **A single inserted odd bar is the mildest form and reads as a "wonky" accent rather than full metric instability**: Golden Brown (The Stranglers) — mostly 3/4, one bar of 4/4 at each line's end; Blackbird (Beatles) — mostly 4/4 with single bars of 2/4 or 3/4 dropped in.
- **Swapping ONE bar's length changes the physical feel of a groove without destabilizing danceability — worked contrast**: Hey Ya (OutKast) uses 3 bars of 4/4 + 1 bar of 2/4 + 2 bars of 4/4; the author's stated reasoning is that a 2/4 "half bar" gives the groove an off-kilter edge while keeping the underlying pulse intact enough to stay a dance-floor hit — a 3/4 bar in the same slot, changing the pulse rather than just shortening it, would likely have broken the danceability.
- **Layering a second irregularity on top of an odd base meter, rather than an even one, compounds the effect**: Four Sticks (Led Zeppelin) is mostly 5/8 but cuts to 6/8 for a couple of bars at a time — already-odd 5/8 plus a shifting second meter reads as more exotic than either alone.
- **A verse in an unstable/asymmetric meter makes the following section's plain 4/4 read as a release, independent of any harmonic resolution** — Mountains (Biffy Clyro): verse alternates 4/4 and 7/8 (effectively 15/8), and the payoff is the "wave of relief" when the next section drops into straight 4/4 — a pure rhythmic-tension device, the meter-equivalent of the harmonic tension-and-release techniques already logged elsewhere.
- **Full-song mixed-meter catalog for reference (each a distinct pattern, useful as varied worked examples for ear-training or composition)**: Force Quit (Radiohead, verse alternates 4/4/3/4); Wuthering Heights (Kate Bush, 4/4-4/4-2/4-4/4 chorus pattern); Bastard (Ben Folds, verse 6/4 with occasional 4/4 bars, pre-chorus 7/4, chorus alternates 7/4/6/4); One (Metallica, opens 4/4 with 2/4 bars before settling into a 3/4-centric song, deliberately establishing 4/4 first so the eventual 3/4 reads as unexpected); Turn It On Again (Genesis) — the most extreme example given: verse alternates 6/4 and 7/4 (transcribable as one 13/4 bar), pre-chorus in plain 4/4, chorus cycling unpredictably through 2/4, 3/4, 4/4, and 5/4 with no repeating pattern — notable as a genuine UK top-10 pop hit despite the instability.
