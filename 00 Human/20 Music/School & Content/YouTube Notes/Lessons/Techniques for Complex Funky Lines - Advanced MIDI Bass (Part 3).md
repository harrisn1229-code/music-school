## Techniques

- **Slap articulation as a distinct accent tool, not just a tone choice.** A modern electric-bass patch with slap capability is chosen specifically so loud, popped/slapped hits can mark structural moments (e.g., landing hard on the root right where the drums break) — slap and a plain note at the same pitch are treated as functionally different articulations, worth choosing deliberately rather than defaulting to one sound throughout.
- **Accentuate a chord's non-diatonic tone on a strong beat, then resolve it to the diatonic/flattened version on the next chord**, to make the harmonic shift audible — his example: playing the natural 9th's B (non-diatonic to D minor) hard on a strong beat over G9, then landing on B♭ (diatonic) as the next chord arrives, deliberately "leaping away and coming back to the flattened version" so the ear catches the change. → [[Chords by Key]], [[Chromatic Bass Lines]]
- **Use chord inversions in the bass line for smoother connections** — instead of the obvious root-then-target walk, playing a chord's ♭7 in the bass (functionally a third-inversion voicing) can connect two chords more smoothly than a straight root-to-root walk; presented as one of several equally valid walking choices, picked by ear/feel rather than a fixed rule.
- **Reinterpret an ambiguous passing chord by outlining the chord it's really leading to, not the one that's literally sounding.** When the harmony briefly implies one chord (an "A" sound) but the real function is a different chord (D) leading into the target (G), the bass can ignore the notated passing chord and outline the functional one instead, for more coherence across the phrase.
- **A bass fill as "disintegration," borrowed directly from drum-fill logic.** Just as a drum fill breaks down a steady pattern before it reassembles on the next downbeat, a bass fill can get progressively more chromatic/free right before resolving — roughly following the chord tones is the safer version, full chromatic "craziness" is the more extreme version of the same idea, both used as a way to signal "something is about to change."
- **Vary the rhythmic density between repeats/halves of a section for contrast** — a busy walking bassline in the first half of a section against a sparser root-fifth-9th-root pattern in the second half (or vice versa), rather than keeping density constant throughout.
- **When repeating an idea, vary it the second time through — same principle as melody writing.** His example: the first pass of a phrase resolves downward, the second pass stays high and emphasizes a different chord tone before dropping — deliberate range/contour variation on a repeat rather than an exact copy.
- **The "dropping ladder": stack multiple octave drops across a phrase for range/contour drama.** Walking up to a high register, playing around, dropping an octave, walking back up, dropping again, and finally resolving to the root — covering a wide range within one phrase as a distinct arrangement device, not incidental.
- **Sing/hum the contour first, work out the actual notes afterward — restated as the core writing method across all 3 videos.** Explicitly named as *not* real-time-playable-from-memory for him — he vocalizes the shape he wants, then figures out which notes deliver it against the current chord/key, "fixing them later" rather than composing note-by-note from theory alone.
- **The full stated method, verbatim as the series' conclusion:** listen first (feel/vibe) → tone (shapes how you play) → note choice (reflects the current chord and key) → groove (interfaces with the drums, draws attention to the bassline or recedes). All three videos are worked examples of this same four-step process.
- **Deliberate "bad example" as a teaching contrast:** a version of the same line playing only root notes on chord changes, ignoring the drums entirely, shown explicitly as what happens when none of the above principles are applied — useful as a sanity check for what an under-written bassline sounds like next to a fully-considered one.
