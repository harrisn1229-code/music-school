# Triad Pairs! The Sound of Modes!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0UkXvZayrsM)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0UkXvZayrsM)

## Synopsis

A short (~7 min) request-driven overview from Nathan Borton, answering a viewer question ("can you do
a video about the triad pairs concept and how to phrase it"). It's a brief, high-level primer rather
than a deep dive — the vault already has a much more thorough worked treatment of triad pairs (see
[[Triad Pairs]], built from several longer Jens Larsen videos on the same topic). What this video adds
that's genuinely new is the **derivation angle**: instead of starting from harmonizing the whole parent
scale into seven triads and picking two adjacent ones (the [[Triad Pairs]] page's method), Borton starts
from the **chord-defining tones** (3rd and 7th) and works outward to find which two triads capture them —
a simpler, chord-first way to arrive at the same triad-pair result. He also gives one worked example
from a real Grant Green solo (two augmented triads over an altered dominant) that surfaces a distinct
technical trick — exploiting the augmented triad's symmetry so one fingering shape covers all its
inversions — and closes with general pattern-variation advice and a nod toward using pairs over a
ii-V-I (no concrete voicings given for that last part — it's described but not spelled out in the
audio).

## What's taught

- **Core definition.** A triad pair = two triads played back to back that together outline a mode or
  sound over a single chord — the same "compound source of line material" concept as [[Triad Pairs]],
  stated here in its simplest form.
- **Chord-tone-first derivation method (the video's main teaching device, distinct from [[Triad Pairs]]'s scale-harmonization method).** Ask which notes actually make a chord sound like itself —
  start with the **3rd and the 7th** (the two tones that carry the chord's quality), then bring in the
  5th or upper extensions (9th, 13th) as secondary color. Once those defining tones are identified, scan
  the triads harmonized from the chord's key and find **two adjacent triads whose combined notes cover
  those defining tones**. *(This resolves to the same "two scale-adjacent triads" relationship [[Triad Pairs]] documents — Borton just arrives at it by reasoning forward from the chord tones rather than
  backward from "remove one scale note," a useful alternate mental model for the same mechanic — see
  [[Triad Pairs]] § Finding a triad pair.)*
- **Worked example: Cmaj7.** The defining tones are E (3rd) and B (7th). Scanning C major's harmonized
  triads, **G major + A minor** played back to back covers both — matching [[Triad Pairs]]'s existing
  documentation that the tonic-triad-pair default is the V and vi triads of the key (general starting
  point for a major-quality chord, complementary to [[Triad Pairs]]'s C-minor-blues worked table which
  only covers minor/dominant/altered chords, not a plain major 7).
  - Advice given alongside: once a pair is found for one key, **move the bass note around** to apply the
    same pair-finding logic in as many different keys as possible (general practice instruction).
- **Grant Green example (over a ii-V-I from his solo on "John de Fleur"/similar changes).** Two
  **augmented triads** — **D♭ augmented + E♭ augmented** — used to get an **E♭ altered** dominant sound.
  - **Rhythmic device:** Green leaves a note out of the D♭ augmented triad, then adds a note into the E♭
    augmented triad, reshaping the E♭ augmented arpeggio into a **Coltrane-pattern-style rhythmic
    figure** (described as a "1-2-3-and-5" grouping) rather than a straight arpeggio run.
  - **Symmetry trick — new device not on [[Triad Pairs]]:** because **both triads in the pair are
    augmented**, and an augmented triad's three inversions are all the *same* augmented-triad shape
    (an augmented triad divides the octave into three equal major thirds — see [[Augmented Chords]]),
    the pair's fingering shape is identical at every inversion. That means the whole D♭aug+E♭aug pattern
    can be **shifted up the neck in major thirds** and it stays the *same physical shape* the entire way
    — a guitar-specific payoff of choosing an all-augmented pair that a mixed-quality pair (major+minor,
    etc.) doesn't offer.
  - **Chromatic connector:** chromaticism can be added between the pair's inversions to connect them
    smoothly (general device, not spelled out with exact passing tones in the transcript).
- **General pattern-variation advice (applies to any triad pair, restates/extends [[Triad Pairs]]'s
  existing "four construction patterns" with a couple of new phrasings):**
  - Instead of always running the pair straight up then straight down, **flip the direction/shape** of
    the pattern.
  - Turn the line into **groups of four notes and then reverse** the four-note group (a different
    regrouping device than [[Triad Pairs]]'s existing 4+3 split, but the same underlying goal — break
    up the mechanical "straight inversions" feel).
  - **Leave some notes out** to create rhythmic interest (same omission-as-device idea [[Triad Pairs]]
    already documents from the Out of Nowhere video, confirmed independently here as a general-purpose
    move, not tune-specific).
  - Try to generate **as many different pattern variations as possible** rather than settling on one way
    of playing a given pair — stated as the overarching practice goal.
- **Application over a ii-V-1.** Named as a further use case (don't always have to play the pair
  straight up/down the neck; can add chromaticism to connect triads, leave out notes, add notes, and
  change the pattern) but the video's on-screen examples aren't captured in the audio-only transcript —
  no specific voicings extractable here (flagged as a gap; the point is general-applicability, not a new
  worked example).
