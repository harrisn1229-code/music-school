# It's All Too Much — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

George welds an Indian-style harmonic drone to a modal tune and a pop-rock backbeat, then hangs a long improvisatory intro and outro off either end. The analytical punchline: **verse and refrain are nearly indistinguishable as music** — both 8 bars, both AA' — so any formal parse comes from the words, not from the sound. The track works by sheer length and repetition rather than by content.

**Form.** The intro runs a bit over a minute in two phases: a high sustained chord and feedback, then one refrain phrase scored for organ alone with no percussion, then a whole instrumental refrain adding guitar, bass, kit and surrealistically wide-panned handclaps, then one more with the title phrase sung. A one-bar **spacer** is appended to any refrain immediately followed by a verse — punctuation for a form the harmony can't punctuate. The outro runs roughly 2:45, deliberately balanced against the intro and longer than a fair number of complete Beatles songs.

**Harmony.** There effectively isn't any, and that's the design — the bass holds one note **under the entire song** ([[POP-C03 — Pedal point]]). Chord charts for the refrain print changes on various downbeats; Pollack's firm position is that **no root motion occurs anywhere in the section**. What sounds like changes is neighbour-tone motion in the inner voices sitting on top of the pedal ([[HAR-C09 — Non-harmonic tones]]) — the drone survives and the ear still hears harmony.

**Melody.** Modal in shape: the tune puts off-kilter weight on **scale degrees 2 and 7 while avoiding 4 entirely**. Worth setting beside "Within You Without You" for the same kind of scale-degree bias.

**Arrangement.** Organ, drums and lead guitar underneath, with trumpets and bass clarinet on top. The restraint is in the scheduling: the **trumpets are held out until the second instrumental break**, then rationed across the final refrains and selected stretches of the outro. A late first entrance is worth more than an early one.
