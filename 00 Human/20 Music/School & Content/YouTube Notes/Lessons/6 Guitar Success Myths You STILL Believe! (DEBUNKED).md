## Synopsis

A career/industry mindset video, not a technique or theory lesson — BERNTH (jazz guitar/instrumental pedagogy degree, session and touring credits) debunks six common beliefs about becoming a professional guitarist. Only the talent-as-excuse point is reusable practice-mindset content; the rest is music-industry-specific and stays on this page.

## What's taught

- **Formal music education isn't a career prerequisite.** Four years studying jazz guitar/instrumental pedagogy rarely came up in real studio/touring work — being on time, prepared, professional, and a good team player mattered far more than credentials. Useful specifically if you want to teach, but graduating doesn't automatically generate paid work.
- **Endorsements are a brand relationship, not free stuff.** Biggest self-flagged mistake: accepting an endorsement for gear you don't actually believe in — it produces low-quality gear for your own work and makes you advertise it to others. Recommended approach: identify what you can offer the company, pitch with a formal, organized email and real references, and only accept when the relationship is genuinely mutual.
- **Buying social media followers wastes money and generates no real traffic** — fake followers get purged by platform algorithms and the accounts risk suspension. Real growth: consistent uploads (weekly minimum), plus understanding how the platform's algorithm actually works (cites *The YouTube Formula* by Derral Eves as a resource) — and content quality itself improves fastest just from sustained, analyzed posting.
- **"The music industry is dead / get a real job" undersells that people still constantly consume music** — the business model changed, but demand for musicians didn't disappear. Most touring work came from consistently showing up prepared and professional at studio sessions, which led to word-of-mouth referrals from producers later working with bigger acts. Career-risk takeaway: never rely on a single project/income stream — build multiple income streams around your strongest skills, since any one tour/project can end unexpectedly.
- **Music theory isn't actually optional, even for players who claim otherwise.** Players who say "I don't know theory" usually mean they don't know complex chord/scale theory or ear training specifically — they still know fundamentals. Recommended starting point: learn the fretboard's note names first, then intervals, then chord/scale theory.
- **Talent is disproportionately used as an excuse for giving up.** Full extraction and reasoning → [[Practice Mindset — Hard Truths and Realistic Expectations]].
