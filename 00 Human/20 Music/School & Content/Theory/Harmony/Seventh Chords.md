# Seventh Chords

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Chords]]. A 7th chord = triad + the 7th, making 4 notes. The 7th usually adds tension that wants to resolve — the engine of blues and jazz harmony.

## The three you'll use constantly

| Type | Symbol | Formula | C example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dominant 7th | C7 | 1 3 5 ♭7 | C E G B♭ |
| Major 7th | Cmaj7 | 1 3 5 7 | C E G B |
| Minor 7th | Cm7 | 1 ♭3 5 ♭7 | C E♭ G B♭ |

- **Dominant 7** = most common; major triad + minor 7th. THE blues chord, and the V7 in every key ([[Chords by Key]])
- **Major 7** = major scale degrees 1-3-5-7; dreamy/jazzy color (not used in blues)
- **Minor 7** = minor triad + minor 7th; the ii and vi chords in jazz progressions

## The rest of the family

| Type | Symbol | Formula | C example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Minor-major 7th | CmM7 | 1 ♭3 5 7 | C E♭ G B |
| Half-diminished (m7♭5) | Cm7♭5 / Cø | 1 ♭3 ♭5 ♭7 | C E♭ G♭ B♭ |
| Diminished 7th | Cdim7 / C°7 | 1 ♭3 ♭5 ♭♭7 | C E♭ G♭ A |
| Augmented 7th | Caug7 / C7♯5 | 1 3 ♯5 ♭7 | C E G♯ B♭ |

- **m7♭5** = minor 7th with lowered 5th; the ii chord in minor-key jazz ii–V–i ([[Chord Progressions]])
- dim7 vs m7♭5 differ only in the 7th (♭♭7 vs ♭7) — see [[Diminished Chords]]
- 7♭5, 7♯9 etc. live in [[Altered Chords]]; mM7 as the i of melodic/harmonic minor: [[Scale Modes Reference]]

## Usage
- Diatonic 7ths per key (Imaj7, ii7… pattern): [[Chords by Key]]
- Swap all triads → diatonic 7ths for an instant "jazzy version" of a progression
- A lone 7th chord in a pop song usually exists to create a stepwise melodic line (in melody, bass, or an inner voice)
- Voicing: split root (left hand) from upper structure (right hand); see [[Chord Inversions]] for slash-chord readings

## Classical usage: which 7ths, and resolving the 7th
- Building a 7th on every scale degree gives 8 theoretical seventh-chord types, but common practice only actually used **5**: major 7th, dominant/major-minor 7th, minor 7th, half-diminished (ø7), fully-diminished (°7) — the table above.
- Root movement for 7th chords follows the same up-2nd/down-3rd/down-5th pattern as triads ([[Chord Progressions]]), PLUS a 4th option unique to 7ths: **common root** (V7→V7 is fine, but V7→V as a plain triad is NOT — a 7th chord can't "downgrade" to a triad on the same root).
- The 7th of ANY seventh chord must **always resolve down by step** — approach by common tone, step, or downward leap; never approach it with an upward leap.
- **Dominant 7th's four non-standard resolution detours**: **ornamental** (decorative detour before the down-step landing), **transferred** ("hot potato" — the 7th passes to another voice in a later V-chord before finally resolving down by step), **delayed** (motion to IV holds the 7th out until V7 returns, then it resolves normally), **bass resolution** (the 7th still resolves down by step but lands in the bass via V7→I6, while the voice that had it resolves up). → [[Part-Writing]] — Toby W. Rush, [[Music Theory for Musicians and Normal People - Enrichment|Music Theory for Musicians and Normal People]], p.32–33

## From videos

- **Why a major 7 chord reads as "calm but uneasy" — the dissonant interval is diluted, not absent.** The major 7th interval itself (e.g. C to B) is genuinely dissonant in isolation, but a full major 7 chord also contains consonant intervals (perfect 5ths, major 3rds) among its other notes — those consonances soften the dissonant interval's bite without erasing it, producing a chord that reads overall as consonant but carries "a potent dash of dissonance." Demonstrated directly on Minecraft's "Subwoofer Lullaby" (C418): removing the major 7 strips the piece's uneasy/eerie quality entirely, leaving plain consonance. Same mechanism recurs via major 9 chords (which contain a major 7 inside them) on "Sweden" and "Wet Hands," both leaning Erik-Satie-adjacent for a lush-but-slightly-off mood. → [[Extended Chords]] (major 9). — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Analysing Minecraft's Music (ft. Mumbo Jumbo)]]
- **Minor-major 7 (mM7) in its two real homes: film-scoring tension and line-cliché stepping stone, plus a useful reframing.** Film scoring — dubbed "the Hitchcock chord" but more accurately "the Bernard Herrmann chord" (he used it well beyond Hitchcock): "Vertigo," "Psycho," "Taxi Driver" (his final score; both mM7 and mM9 appear). The related mM9 closes the James Bond theme's famous final chord. **Most common real use — a transitional chord inside a line cliché** (minor triad's top note stepping down chromatically naturally passes through mM7 en route to m7): "Butterflies and Hurricanes" (Muse), "I Just Called to Say I Love You" (Stevie Wonder, walks down then back up), "In a Sentimental Mood" (Ellington, built from two separate line clichés — also the source of jazz notation's △ symbol for major 7) → [[Line Cliché]]. Standalone (non-line-cliché) jazz use, one likely inspiring the other: "Nica's Dream" (Horace Silver) and "Chelsea Bridge" (Strayhorn) both open on the identical B♭m(maj7)→A♭m(maj7) move. **Reframing**: the upper three notes of any mM7 form an augmented triad, so Am(maj7) = "C augmented over A." — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use Minor Major 7th chords]]
- **A complete 7-type 7th-chord taxonomy, each built from a triad + 7th recipe, with symbols and diatonic context.** **Dominant 7** (`C7`) = major triad + ♭7, diatonic only on V, resolves V7→I — blues deliberately breaks this, putting dominant 7ths on I and IV too. **Major 7** (`Cmaj7`/`C△`) = major triad + natural 7 — serene quality (Satie's *Gymnopédie No. 1*, built on Imaj7→IVmaj7). **Minor 7** (`Cm7`/`C-7`) = minor triad + ♭7 — lush/jazzy. **Minor-major 7** (`CmMaj7`) = minor triad + natural 7, completing the 4-way {major/minor triad}×{♭7/nat.7} grid — doesn't occur diatonically in major or minor, mysterious/chromatic quality, most common as the 2nd chord of a **line cliché** (Am→AmMaj7 as the top note steps down). **Diminished 7** (`C°7`) = a full stack of four minor 3rds (returns to the start an octave up) — often confused with half-diminished; unstable, rarely lingered on. **Half-diminished 7** (`Cø7`, more commonly written `Cm7♭5`) = diminished triad + ♭7 (a semitone above the full diminished 7th) — more common because it's diatonic on the minor-key **ii**, the backbone of a minor ii-V-i (Bm7♭5–E7–Am), a jazz/soul/funk staple. Miscellaneous: **7sus4** (dominant 7 with 3rd suspended to the 4th); **dominant ♯5** (augmented triad + dominant 7, named "♯5" rather than "augmented 7" to avoid ambiguity about which note is altered). Full breakdown → [[7 types of 7th chord EXPLAINED]]. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[7 types of 7th chord EXPLAINED]]

- **Major 6th and major 7th chords off the same root, treated as fully interchangeable in practice — a working habit, not a one-off substitution.** Applied constantly through real tunes: if a chart says major 6, major 7 works and vice versa; the two carry genuinely different colors/moods, and taste develops over time for which to reach for, but neither reading is ever "wrong." Shows up repeatedly on tonic chords, IV chords, and inside rootless voicings (see [[Chord Voicings]] § *From videos* for a case where a maj9 voicing and a following ii-chord voicing turn out to be the same physical shape partly because of this interchangeability). → [[Chord Voicings]] — Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared, [[BODY AND SOUL Guitar Lesson (chords, tab, scales, melody) No. 18 of Top 25 Jazz Standards for Guitar]], [[SOMEDAY MY PRINCE WILL COME Guitar Lesson (tab, chords, scales) Top Jazz Standards for Guitar No. 17]]
- **Why each diatonic seventh-chord quality lands where it does, derived from the major scale's own half-steps rather than just stated as a memorized pattern.** Names the underlying structure explicitly — **tertiary chords**: chords built by stacking intervals of a third on top of each other (the "skip a note" method — take every other scale degree from any root — builds a triad; one more third on top makes it a 7th chord). To find whether a given stacked third is major or minor, check whether its interval span straddles one of the major scale's two built-in half-steps (between scale degrees 3–4 and 7–1): a half-step inside the span = minor 3rd, no half-step = major 3rd. Worked degree by degree off the major scale: **I (1-3-5-7)** = major-minor-major thirds → **maj7**; **ii (2-4-6-1)** and **iii (3-5-7-2)** = minor-major-minor → **m7**; **IV (4-6-1-3)** = major-minor-major → **maj7**; **V (5-7-2-4)** = major-minor-minor → **dominant 7**, a stack that can *only* occur starting from scale degree 5, which is the structural reason dominant 7 exists nowhere else in a diatonic key; **vi (6-1-3-5)** = minor-major-minor → **m7** (the third of three diatonic homes for minor 7); **vii (7-2-4-6)** = minor-minor-major → **half-diminished/m7♭5** (two minor thirds in a row = a diminished triad underneath), a stack that can only occur starting from scale degree 7. Also gives a fast mental shortcut for any chord's 7th: think one scale-degree number below the chord's own root (7th of ii = 1, 7th of V = 4, 7th of vi = 5, 7th of iii = 2). **Song-analysis payoff:** because dominant 7 only ever occurs naturally as V and half-diminished only ever occurs naturally as vii°, spotting either chord type out of context is a strong giveaway for the parent major key/scale — treat the dominant 7 as "the V of some major key" or the half-diminished as "the vii of some major key" to back-derive it; a bare maj7 is ambiguous (could be I or IV of two different keys) and a bare m7 is even more ambiguous (ii, iii, or vi of three different keys). - **A second, independent confirmation that shell voicings (root+3rd+7th, no 5th) make minor 7 and minor 7♭5 physically indistinguishable, plus a new same-shape "freebie": diminished 7 and minor 6 also share one shape.** An 8-shape sight-reading system (16 shapes total, doubled off the 6th/5th strings) claims to cover all 60+ jazz chord-quality symbols by reducing each to one of these shapes. Diminished 7 (1-♭3-♭5-𝄫7) and minor 6 (1-♭3-6, no altered 5th) differ only in the 5th — since the shell never voices a 5th, the same root-♭3-6 grip covers both, reasoned via the 𝄫7/6 enharmonic ("harmonic") equivalence. **This is a different derivation than the flat-the-7th-a-second-half-step mechanism** the companion Seriff video uses to reach its own dim7 shape — the two systems' dim7 voicings are not the same shape and shouldn't be mixed mid-progression. Also teaches a symbol-reduction rulebook for sight-reading: ignore the 5 and slash-chord bass notes always; ignore an extension number that follows a 7 (`G7♭13` → G7); but an extension number **alone with no 7 written** secretly means "this is a 7-chord" (`G9`/`G13` → read/play as G7). - **Guitar shell voicings (root+3rd+7th, no 5th) make minor 7 and minor 7♭5 physically indistinguishable — a fretboard demonstration of why the ♭5 is a secondary color, not a defining tone.** Two movable 3-note shapes (one E-string-rooted, one A-string-rooted) walk through maj7 → dominant 7 (flat the 7th) → minor 7 (also flat the 3rd) → dim7 (flat the 7th again) one half step at a time; because the shape never voices a 5th at all, the same grip that plays minor 7 also plays minor 7♭5 — there's nothing left to flat. Full fret numbers for both shapes across all five qualities on [[Chord Voicings]]. → [[Chord Voicings]] — Daniel Seriff, [[Guitar Lesson - Simple 7th Chords For Jazz And Other Styles (Shell Voicings)]]
- **Every seventh chord's own 3rd-5th-7th is itself a playable triad, with a fixed quality per chord quality** — minor 7 → major triad a minor 3rd up; dominant 7 → diminished triad a major 3rd up; major 7 → minor triad a major 3rd up; minor 7♭5 → minor triad a minor 3rd up. Worked across all seven chords of Autumn Leaves (touches all four qualities in one progression) as both a comping simplification (string the upper-structure triad in place of the full 4-note grip) and a soloing target map. Full four-rule table and fretboard-mapping prerequisite on [[Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords]]. → [[Guide Tones]], [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]] — Jack Ruch, [Upper structure triads for comping and soloing](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Cbo6tCKEZJY)
- **Quick "reduce to basic form" table for identifying which chord family a dense embellished symbol really belongs to** — before deciding how to voice, substitute, or resolve a complex-looking chord, collapse it: Cma7, C6, Cma9, C6/9 all reduce to plain **C major**; C9, C11, C13, C9♯5 all reduce to **C7**; Cm7, Cm9, Cm11, Cm7♭5 all reduce to **Cm**. Pairs directly with the chord-embellishment habit (add 6th/maj7/9th/♭3 to a major triad; add 9th/11th/13th/♭9/♯9 to a 7th chord, in that build order for voice-leading; add 6th/7th/maj7/9th to a minor triad) — embellishment and reduction are inverse operations on the same symbol. → [[Chord Progressions]], [[Diminished Chords]] — [[Joe Pass Guitar Style - Enrichment|Joe Pass Guitar Style]], p.1–3
- **Tell maj7 and dominant7 apart by counting root-to-top half-steps, and save a built scale as a reusable clip** — a maj7 chord's root-to-top interval is 11 half-steps; lowering just that top note one more half-step (to 10 half-steps root-to-top) turns the same major-triad-plus-7th shape into a dominant 7th, which reads as much more tense/"barbershop." Build every diatonic 7th chord by continuing the triad's leapfrog (root, skip, add, skip, add) one note further on each scale degree. Workflow tip: once a scale is built as its own clip (notes pushed left of the loop bracket, Fold enabled), name and drag it into the clip browser sidebar so it can be dragged into any future project instead of rebuilt from scratch. → [[Scale]], [[Chords by Key]] — You Suck at Producing, [How to write lofi hip hop chords WITHOUT samples](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=djQlIr1ZYt8)
- **m7/maj7 soften the extremes of an all-major or all-minor progression** — an all-major progression can read as "stupidly positive" and flat; slapping a minor 7th on the minor chords makes them feel less harsh without pushing toward major, while adding major 7ths on the majors too gives a fuller "housier" chord-tone feel throughout. Practical workflow: mix minors and majors, add the matching 7th to each, and listen rather than pre-calculating. Naming reminder: "m7"/"maj7" name the chord scale-wise (root–3rd–5th–7th), not by semitone count — a minor 7th is 10 semitones up, a major 7th is 11. → [[Chord Progressions]] — Synthet, [the objectively best chord..](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fcplj-FKcKY)
