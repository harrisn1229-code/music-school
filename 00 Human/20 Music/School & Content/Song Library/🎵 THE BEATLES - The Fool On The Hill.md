# The Fool On The Hill — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

**Form:** completely flat — Intro (**one measure**) · (Verse · Refrain) ×4, the last two verses instrumental, then a fade. No bridge, no interruption of the pattern; the ballad shape shared by folk and by Schubert's song cycles. The verse is an unusual **7 measures**, its final phrase truncated so it elides into the refrain; the refrain is only **4**, with one solitary extra measure tacked on to get back. Both surviving outtakes had a four-measure intro that exposed the song's gambit immediately — the released version withholds it.

One harmonic event carries the whole song: **verse in D major, refrain in D minor.** Everything in the refrain comes from the parallel minor — i, iv, ♭VI, ♭VII ([[HAR-C08 — Borrowed chords and modal interchange]]) — and **the two crossings are deliberately asymmetric**: minor arrives with no warning at all, while the return to major gets a whole measure of runway. Pollack's historical point is that the classical models overwhelmingly run minor *to* major; this one inverts the direction, and he ties that to the difference between bittersweet love and oblivious foolishness.

The verse's first four measures are harmony **suspended over a pedal**, Bach-prelude fashion ([[POP-C03 — Pedal point]]); when the pedal releases, the harmonic rhythm doubles for the rest of the section ([[POP-C01 — Harmonic rhythm]]).

Both sections are melodic arches, and **the refrain tops out on F natural while the verse goes slightly higher to F♯** — the two peaks differ by exactly the note that defines the mode. Three appoggiatura figures are named in the essay ([[HAR-C09 — Non-harmonic tones]]), including an échappée that escapes downward before resolving up.
