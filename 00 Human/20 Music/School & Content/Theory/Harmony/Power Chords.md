# Power Chords

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Chords]]. A **power chord** ("5 chord," e.g. C5) is a major/minor triad with the 3rd removed — just the root and 5th (plus, in the standard guitar voicing, the root doubled an octave up).

## Why it exists

Playing a full major or minor triad through guitar distortion produces a muddy, dissonant result — the distortion's added harmonics clash badly with the 3rd. Dropping the 3rd and keeping only root+5th is what makes an overdriven guitar sound clean and powerful; this is the actual mechanism behind the convention, not an arbitrary stylistic choice.

## Chord vs. interval

Strictly, a "chord" requires 3+ distinct notes — a power chord (2 unique pitches) technically fails that test and is really a bare interval (a perfect 5th). Whether "chord" or "interval/harmony" is the more useful label depends on context: a power chord functioning as the harmonic backbone of a song (e.g. the opening of "Yesterday") reads naturally as a chord; a power-chord-harmonized melodic line (e.g. "Seven Nation Army"'s riff, restated in parallel 5ths/octaves rather than moving through different chords) reads more like a harmonized melody than a chord progression.

## Vocal/instrumental completion

A power-chord-only guitar part is neither major nor minor on its own — but the overall song often still reads as one or the other once another instrument (typically the vocal melody) adds the missing 3rd on top. This lets an overdriven guitar stay clean and powerful while another voice supplies the major/minor color.

## Off the guitar: "bare fifth"

Outside a distorted-guitar context, "power chord" stops being the natural name — the same root+5th voicing on piano or another clean instrument is better called a **bare fifth**.

## From videos

- **The full mechanism plus real examples across all the contexts above.** Mostly-power-chord songs: "Smells Like Teen Spirit" (Nirvana), "You Really Got Me" (The Kinks), "I Bet You Look Good on the Dancefloor" (Arctic Monkeys). **Inverted voicing** (5th on the bottom, technically a perfect 4th / slash chord): "Smoke on the Water" (Deep Purple). **Vocal completes the chord quality**: "American Idiot" (Green Day) — guitar plays bare power chords throughout the chorus; the vocal melody adds the major 3rd, making the overall sound major despite the guitar part containing no 3rd at all. **Chord-vs-interval, worked both ways**: "Yesterday" (Beatles, opening F5, reads as a chord) vs. "Seven Nation Army" (White Stripes, the SAME riff-melody restated in power-chord harmony later in the song — a harmonized melodic line, not a chord progression). **Bare fifth off the guitar**: "All of Me" (John Legend, piano) and "Defying Gravity" (*Wicked*, main motif on D♭5). — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use Power Chords]]
