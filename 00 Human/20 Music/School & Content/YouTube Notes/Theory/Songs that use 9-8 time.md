# Songs that use 9/8 time

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=I7BxNEhVHhY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=I7BxNEhVHhY)

## Synopsis

Deepens the vault's existing "9/8 has two unrelated uses" note with the specific non-Western tradition behind the odd-grouping use (Turkish/Balkan aksak rhythm), Dave Brubeck's "Blue Rondo à la Turk" as the canonical crossover piece, and a fuller grouping-pattern catalog for both uses.

## Techniques

- **The Turkish/Balkan tradition behind uneven 9/8, by name: "aksak" rhythm, specifically the 2+2+2+3 grouping.** Dave Brubeck heard this grouping from street musicians in Turkey and was told directly "this rhythm is to us what the blues is to you" — the anecdote behind "Blue Rondo à la Turk" (Dave Brubeck Quartet), which explicitly alternates between the Turkish 2+2+2+3 grouping and the regular Western 3+3+3 grouping within the same piece, making it a direct A/B demonstration of the two 9/8 identities. The same 2+2+2+3 pattern underlies the Turkish zeybek and Greek zeibekiko traditional dances.
- **Why uneven 9/8 sounds "odd" specifically to Western-trained ears, stated as a general principle**: most Western music (traditional and modern) uses isochronous beat groupings (every beat the same duration); regions where non-isochronous groupings are commonplace (Turkey, Greece, the wider Balkans) don't experience the same rhythm as unusual at all — the "oddness" is a listener-culture property, not a property of the rhythm itself, comparable to how an unfamiliar language sounds odd only until you learn it.
- **Additional uneven/odd-treated 9/8 groupings, extending the existing catalog**: 5+4 ("I Hung My Head," Sting); 4+4+1 — feels like straight 4/4 with one extra eighth-note stuck on the end of every bar, described as giving a "janky, chaotic" pulse ("The Crunge," Led Zeppelin; a similar approach in "Gamma Knife," King Gizzard & the Lizard Wizard).
- **9/8 as compound triple meter (the "regular"/Western use), mechanism restated precisely: the felt pulse is the DOTTED quarter note (3 eighth-notes per beat), the same relationship 3/4 has to a triplet feel** — effectively 3/4 with a built-in triplet subdivision on every beat, which is why compound 9/8 (like 6/8, 12/8) has a swinging/waltzing character that simple meters (4/4, 3/4) don't. Compound-9/8 song examples: "The Tourist" (Radiohead); "Fool for You" (The Impressions); Irish slip jig music (a whole traditional dance-tune category built on fast compound 9/8); Debussy's "Clair de Lune" (compound 9/8 but with rubato/expressive tempo, so the usual strong waltz feel is deliberately softened — an example of tempo choice overriding a meter's "default" character).
- **A song can mix compound and odd treatment of 9/8 simultaneously, in different instrumental layers**: "Jambi" (Tool) — the kick drum keeps a strict isochronous 3+3+3 pulse (the compound reading) while the vocal and guitar lines phrase their own irregular rhythms on top, not locked to that grouping — two simultaneous readings of the same bar rather than a switch between sections.
- **A single inserted 9/8 bar within a mostly-6/8 song reads as a "half bar" extension, not a full meter change** — "Nothing Else Matters" (Metallica) intro: mostly 6/8, with one bar of 9/8 thrown in; because 6/8 and 9/8 share the same eighth-note pulse, the effect is indistinguishable from a bar of 6/8 plus a one-off bar of 3/8 tacked on — the same "half bar" logic already documented for 4/4↔2/4 insertions on [[Songs that have a Mixed Meter]], generalized to a compound-meter pair. A second example mixing 6/8 and 9/8 line-by-line: "Anyone Who Had a Heart" (Burt Bacharach).
