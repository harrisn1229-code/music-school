# ALL SHREDDERS HAVE NO FEEL - True Or False? - How To Play Better Guitar Solos!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YfDXv1ERGmA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YfDXv1ERGmA)

## Synopsis

BERNTH records three takes of the same one-bar riff (F minor, extended power chords with the 9th) and dissects why the third is the most musical, to debunk "shredders can't play with feel." The lesson is really three separate, stackable fixes: harmonize the riff by scale degree instead of blanket-scaling it, don't default to starting phrases on the root, and separate note *choice* from note *delivery* (dynamics/attack).

## What's taught

- **Take 1's mistake: playing the parent scale over the whole progression instead of the actual chords.** F minor scale "technically works" over everything in the key but ignores that the riff's power chords (root+5th+9th, no 3rd) are ambiguous — could be major or minor triads. Fix: derive the triad on every scale degree of F natural minor (Fm, A♭dim... wait, worked as Fm–G°–A♭–B♭m–Cm–D♭–E♭) and pick the *matching* major or minor arpeggio for each chord in the progression, not one blanket scale. Directly ties to [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]'s "outline the chord tones of every chord" step.
- **Don't start every arpeggio phrase on the root.** Named as one of the most common and most boring defaults ("root-third-fifth-octave, root-third-fifth-octave..."); his fix for the F minor arpeggio was starting on the "one-and" and sliding into the 5th instead of landing squarely on beat 1 with the root.
- **Delivery separated from note choice — the single biggest lesson of the video.** Take 2 used the *same* notes and rhythm as take 3 but sounded mechanical because every note got identical picking-attack dynamics. Take 3's fix: pick out the melodically important notes in each measure and hit them with more attack, while muting/de-emphasizing the connective notes around them — same pitches, same rhythm, dramatically more expressive result purely from dynamic contouring.
- **Arpeggio shapes tracked to the underlying riff, ending in a fast sweep-picked run** — shown as proof shredding technique still has a place, just placed deliberately (climax) rather than blanket-applied.
