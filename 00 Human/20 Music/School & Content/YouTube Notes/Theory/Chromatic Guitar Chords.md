# Chromatic Guitar Chords

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oIt2m57eUVk)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oIt2m57eUVk)

## Synopsis

An overview lesson introducing two chromatic-chord (non-diatonic) devices — secondary dominants and modal mixture/borrowed chords — both of which the vault already documents in much greater depth from Jens Larsen and other Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared videos ([[Secondary Dominants]], [[Borrowed Chords]], [[Diminished Chords]] § applied diminished). This video's own applied-diminished example ("Blackbird") and the general secondary-dominant naming system are already fully captured on those pages from a different video by the same channel, so this page routes only the two genuinely new pieces: a specific, previously-undocumented **secondary dominant that doesn't resolve to its own tonicized target** (Willie Nelson's "On the Road Again"), and a **chained secondary-dominant example** (Nat King Cole's "All of Me" — V/vi → V/ii → ii).

## What's taught

- **Chromatic vs. diatonic, defined plainly**: diatonic chords come straight from the 7-note scale of the key (the numbered I–vii chords); a chromatic (non-diatonic) chord includes at least one note outside that scale. Two families covered in this video: secondary/applied dominants, and modal mixture/modal interchange.
- **Secondary dominant naming system**: any diatonic chord in a key can be "tonicized" (treated as a temporary I) by preceding it with ITS OWN V chord, built from major-scale counting off that chord's own root — e.g. in the key of C, the ii chord (Dm) can be preceded by A7 (the V of Dm), notated **V/ii**. This can be done for every diatonic chord except vii (no secondary dominant target for the diminished chord). Already fully documented → [[Secondary Dominants]] — not re-extracted here.
- **New song example 1 — "On the Road Again" (Willie Nelson): a secondary dominant that does NOT resolve to the chord it tonicizes.** The chord functions and analyzes as **V/vi** (built as if resolving to the relative minor), but in the actual song it resolves somewhere else entirely — flagged explicitly as an important exception to the "secondary dominants resolve to their target" default: "you don't always have to go to the chord that it is the five of... you can go anywhere else... but still it would be considered a secondary dominant." A concrete, named counterexample to the resolve-as-expected assumption.
- **New song example 2 — "All of Me" (the classic standard): a CHAINED secondary dominant, one tonicization leading directly into a second.** Second chord of the tune is **V/vi**, which — instead of resolving straight to vi — moves to **V/ii**, which then resolves to the actual ii chord: `I → V/vi → V/ii → ii`. Demonstrates that secondary dominants can stack in sequence rather than appearing as isolated one-off insertions.
- **Applied/secondary diminished chords** (dim7 a half step below any diatonic target, tonicizing it the same way a secondary dominant does) — already fully documented, including this video's own "Blackbird" example, on [[Diminished Chords]] § *Applied/secondary diminished chord* (from a different Jared video specifically about diminished chords) — not re-extracted here.
- **Modal mixture / modal interchange**, defined: borrow any chord from the parallel key/mode (e.g. all of C minor's diatonic chords are available for use in a C major context) — already fully documented from multiple sources → [[Borrowed Chords]]. This video's own worked examples (a song using the borrowed ♭III major from the intro, "Creep" by Radiohead) substantially overlap material already on [[Borrowed Chords]] and [[Secondary Dominants]] from other videos — not re-added.
- **Closing assignment (not routed — a practice prompt, not extractable content)**: write an original chord progression using at least one secondary/applied dominant or applied-diminished chord, and at least one modal-mixture chord.
