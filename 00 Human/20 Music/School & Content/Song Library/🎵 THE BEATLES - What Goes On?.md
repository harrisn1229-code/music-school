# What Goes On? — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

**Form:** Intro (a 3-beat pickup plus 4 bars) · Refrain (**20 bars as five equal phrases, AB-AB-C**) · Verse (14, asymmetrical AABC with the last phrase **foreshortened to 2 bars**) · Refrain (solo) · Verse · Refrain · Outro. Strict folksy alternation, no development, and fast enough that 2/2 describes it better than 4/4.

The most useful essay in the batch **as a negative example** — Pollack spends most of it diagnosing why the song has no forward drive, which makes it a working checklist of drag mechanisms. A five-line stanza needs compensating momentum and gets the opposite: every refrain phrase is short and followed by nearly two bars of dead air, so nothing accumulates, and the low point is the moment the ear registers that bars 1–8 are about to repeat verbatim. His test is to delete that repeat and play the remaining twelve bars, which flow.

I, IV and V and almost nothing else, at a **slow harmonic rhythm that compounds the phrase-length problem** ([[POP-C01 — Harmonic rhythm]]). The 4–3 suspension over V at each refrain climax is the only intensification and arrives far too late; the **minor iv** is the one borrowed colour ([[HAR-C08 — Borrowed chords and modal interchange]]).

The one thing pushing forward is the verse's foreshortened final phrase — round it out to four bars and the section sags ([[ARR-C06 — Section-length conventions, and breaking them on purpose]]). The solo refrain worsens matters by entering on phrase 2, so its start reads as a new section.
