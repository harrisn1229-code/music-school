# Synthet - Music's Most Addictive Rhythm

## Synopsis

Synthet's pitch for triplets: splitting each beat into three instead of two (or four), demonstrated as a full-bar triplet feel first, then as a subtler device layered under a still-straight 8th-note groove — a triplet fill, a triplet melodic run, or triplets "sneaked in sparsely" for impact. The framing throughout is that triplets shift a song's felt tempo without changing the actual tempo, citing Stevie Wonder, Katy Perry, and a reggaeton-era example as hit songs leaning on the device.

## What's taught

- **Triplets = each beat split into three instead of two** — "1-2-3, 2-2-3, 3-2-3, 4-2-3" against the standard 8th/16th grid. Even when not every subdivision is actually played, keeping the *feel* of a triplet pocket is what matters.
- **Triplets change the felt tempo without changing the actual tempo** — described as "almost feeling like a different tempo" even at the same BPM; a strong, recognizable character used across hit songs in very different genres (cited: Stevie Wonder, Katy Perry, and a reggaeton-adjacent example).
- **Triplets don't require abandoning a straight groove** — a song can stay on straight 8th notes for the main beat while still using triplets in a melody line for a "reaching" quality, in a fill switch-up, or in the common "triplet flow" rap-cadence pattern (syllables mapped to a triplet subdivision).
- **Sparse triplets read as more impactful than constant ones** — sneaking a triplet in occasionally, rather than running the whole pattern in triplets, keeps the device from becoming wallpaper.
- **DAW implementation:** most piano-roll grids default to straight (even) subdivisions — switch the grid/snap to "a third of a beat" to place true triplets instead of approximating them on a straight grid.
