# Major Seventh Chords

Part of [[Music Theory]]. [[Seventh Chords]] covers what a maj7 **is**. This note covers **all twelve
of them relative to one key** — where each comes from, what it does, and which tensions it will take
— from Nick Clark's ~93,000-character study.

## Why the maj7 is stable — a geometric answer

Textbooks explain the maj7's stability functionally. His explanation is spatial, and it is better:

> "A major seventh chord is a perfect square of fifths and major thirds."

C–E–G–B closes a square on the [[The Harmonic Lattice|harmonic lattice]] — E is a major third above
C, B is a major third above G *and* a perfect fifth above E. Every note is doubly connected to the
others by the two lowest non-octave prime ratios. Nothing is left dangling. That is the maj7's
stability, drawn rather than asserted.

**"Real" vs "fake" maj7s** — his coinage. Of the twelve maj7 chords in a key, only **six** are
actual lattice squares in just intonation; the other six are enharmonic impostors that sound
identical in 12-TET but are differently wired. Compare [[Tuning and Temperament]].

## His method — worth adopting for any chord survey

Three rules, and the third is the one people skip:

1. **Everything is Roman-numeral relative to one tonic.** "Gmaj7" tells you nothing; "IVmaj7" tells
   you everything.
2. **Twelve roots**, one per pitch class: I, ♭II, II, ♭III, III, IV, ♯IV/♭V, V, ♭VI, VI, ♭VII, VII.
   (♯IV and ♭V share a slot: the *note* reads Lydian, but the *chord* only occurs in a mode as
   ♭Vmaj7 in Locrian. ♯IVmaj7 is in no scale at all.)
3. **Convert everything to the relative major.** [E♭maj7 G7 Cm7] is Imaj7–III7–vi7 **in E♭**, never
   ♭IIImaj7–V7–i7 in C minor. He breaks this twice and flags both times.

## The twelve

| Chord | Comes from | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Imaj7** | diatonic | home |
| **IVmaj7** | diatonic | the other diatonic one. Extended, Imaj7(9,11,13) and IVmaj7(9,♯11,13) differ *only* in 11 vs ♯11 — and the ♯11 version is the more stable |
| **♭IImaj7** | Phrygian | resolves down a semitone to Imaj7; functionally close to SubV7 |
| **IImaj7** | no common mode (needs a ♯1) | bright; almost always a constant-structure passing chord — it is only two fifths from Imaj7 |
| **♭IIImaj7** | parallel minor | the relative major of the minor tonic ([[Parallel Minor]]) |
| **IIImaj7** | distant | shares two notes with Imaj7; arriving on it usually means a modulation, and it usually becomes the new tonic |
| **♯IV / ♭Vmaj7** | Locrian (as ♭V) | the rarest. He found **no** example that justified spelling it ♯IV |
| **Vmaj7** | one non-diatonic note (its 7th is the ♯4) | Lydian brightness; mostly in sequences |
| **♭VImaj7** | minor-key diatonic | minor-subdominant substitute, and a strong modulation pivot — V7 can resolve deceptively **up** a half step into it |
| **VImaj7** | no common mode (needs ♯1 and ♯5) | commoner than it should be: it is the **parallel major of the diatonic vi7**, so it turns up as a deceptive-cadence target that then takes over as tonic |
| **♭VIImaj7** | Mixolydian **and** Dorian | so it works in major *and* minor — "probably the third most popular." Cf. [[POP-C09 — Mixolydian ♭VII]] |
| **VIImaj7 / ♭Imaj7** | — | rarest with ♭Vmaj7, and usually really ♭Imaj7 (its ♭3 and ♭6 are minor-scale notes) |

The digression on Imaj7 vs IVmaj7 is where he engages **George Russell's *Lydian Chromatic
Concept*** — the claim that Lydian, not Ionian, is the true major scale — and then declines it,
on the grounds that Ionian is the mode with a V7 to tonicize its own I.

## Constant structure — the best idea in the document

**Constant structure** = a progression where every chord is the same *type*, usually moving in a
pattern. Stable chord types work best, which is why maj7 dominates it.

**And here is the part worth memorising: each maj7 belongs to exactly two keys.** So a chain of
maj7s descending by fifths **darkens the tonality by one mode per step**:

| In D | Gmaj7 | Cmaj7 | Fmaj7 | B♭maj7 | E♭maj7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| belongs to | D major / D Mixolydian | D Mixolydian / D Dorian | D Dorian / D minor | D minor / D Phrygian | D Phrygian / D Locrian |

A continuous slide from brightest to darkest with no chord change that sounds like a modulation.
That is a real compositional handle and it is not in any note here — see [[Modal Interchange]],
[[Chord Progressions]], [[POP-C05 — Reharmonizing a loop]].

## Tensions on a maj7

His governing rule, and it explains the whole tension list:

> "The nature of major seventh chords is that all of the relationships are purely overtonal and not
> reciprocal… the notes don't go below or to the left of the tonic note. If they do, then whatever
> note is found closest to the bottom left corner tends to sound like the tonic note."

In plain terms: **everything a maj7 will take sits up and to the right of the root on the lattice.**
Add something below or left and the ear reassigns the tonic.

**Popularity order:** 9 ≫ ♯11 and 13 > ♯8 (♭9) > ♯5 > ♯9. Two are effectively unusable — **♯10**
(the ear hears it as the 11) and **♯13** (the ear hears it as ♭7). So a maj7 with all twelve notes
is constructible on paper and not audible as one.

**The genuinely useful rule almost nobody states:** *the same tension means a different thing over a
different root.* The ♯9 of ♭VIImaj7 is the key's natural 7 (very usable); the ♯9 of IVmaj7 is the
key's ♯5 (less so); the ♯9 of IIImaj7 is out of the key entirely — though its enharmonic ♭10 is the
key's V.

**Showcase:** Billy Joel, "New York State of Mind" — D♭maj7(♯8,♯11,13) → Cmaj7(9,♯11,13), where the
second chord's notes spell a **complete C Lydian scale**, and the ♯8 is on saxophone so the C/D♭/D
cluster is spread across instruments rather than stacked in one hand.

## The other maj7-family chords

- **m(maj7)** — he argues it is an augmented triad in disguise: Cm(maj7) = E♭+/C. Which explains its
  ambiguity, since augmented triads are maximally symmetrical in 12-TET ([[Augmented Chords]]).
- **ºmaj7** — almost never harmonically honest; usually a º(♭8) or an m(maj7)(♯11) with no 5.
- **+maj7** — ♭III+maj7 lives in harmonic minor.
- **maj7♭5** — **not really a chord**: it is an upper structure looking for a foreign bass note. He
  pins it as a **Stevie Wonder fingerprint** — right-hand maj7♭5 shape, left hand supplying a
  different root each time. See [[Upper Structure Chords]] and [[Slash Chords]].
- **maj7sus4** — he searched and found **no examples**, and explains why: the 4-against-7 tritone
  spoils the stability the chord exists for.
- **maj7sus2** — fine, but it would be written as something else. Cmaj7sus2 = G/C = Cmaj9 no 3.

## Where he gets his evidence

**Stevie Wonder is the centre of gravity** (a dozen-plus citations), with Mac Miller, Thundercat,
Tame Impala, MGMT, The 1975, Silk Sonic and M83 behind him.

| Chord | Where he shows it |
|---|---|
| ♭IImaj7 | Stevie Wonder, "With Each Beat of My Heart" · Tame Impala, "It Might Be Time" |
| ♭IIImaj7 | Universal Togetherness Band, "My Sentiment" (a full descending-fifths chain) |
| IIImaj7 | Stevie Wonder, "I Love You Too Much" · Mel Tormé, "The Christmas Song" |
| ♭Vmaj7 | Louis Cole, "Disappear" — his best example |
| ♭VImaj7 | Silk Sonic, "Leave the Door Open" · The 1975, "I Couldn't Be More in Love" |
| VImaj7 | Stevie Wonder, "Lately" (the song ends on the deceptive V7→VImaj7) and "You and I" |
| ♭VIImaj7 | Tame Impala & Thundercat, "No More Lies" |
| ♭Imaj7 | Thundercat, "Pozole" — the most convincing case |
| Constant structure | Robert Glasper's outro on Mac Miller's "God Is Fair, Sexy Nasty" — eight of the twelve maj7s |
| maj7♭5 as upper structure | Stevie Wonder, "Too High" and "All in Love Is Fair" |

⚠️ **One term to correct.** He calls ♭IImaj7 → Imaj7 a **"phrygian cadence."** Classically a
Phrygian cadence is iv6–V. His usage will read as an error to a trained reader — call the move a
♭II resolution or a Neapolitan-flavoured approach, and see [[Neapolitan Sixth]] and [[Cadences]].
