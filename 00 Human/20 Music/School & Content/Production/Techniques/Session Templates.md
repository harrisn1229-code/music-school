# Session Templates

Building one reusable "starting point" Live Set — instruments pre-created and pre-routed but powered off, buses wired, default device chains parked and disabled — so a new song starts from zero setup friction instead of rebuilding routing every time. Companion to [[Session View Workflow]] (drafting *within* a session) and [[Gain Staging]] (the levels discipline a template should already encode).

## Why it exists
A template isn't just a saved tempo/key — it's every decision you'd otherwise make fresh each session (which synths, how they're routed, what the buses are, what a default insert chain looks like) captured once so composing starts immediately instead of after twenty minutes of setup. The habit compounds: each session you build without a template is unpaid research for the next template revision.

## From videos

- **A concrete 9-stage mix workflow order, useful as a session checklist — and a caution against over-relying on solo when setting initial levels.** (1) Route similar instruments to color-coded buses BEFORE any processing. (2) Subtractive EQ per track (high-pass plosives/hum, bell for a broader problem, notch for one specific issue). (3) Set initial levels with FADERS, not solo — how something sounds alone isn't how it sounds in context, since phase relationships between simultaneously-playing signals change the picture; use faders for a rough in-context balance to refine later. (4) Compress anything that gets buried in some sections but cuts through in others (vocals most commonly). (5) Saturate/distort/excite for harmonic character. (6) Process at the BUS level for group cohesion (e.g. driving a tape emulation's input on a drum bus does mild compression + distortion + its own frequency shaping in one move). (7) Room + ambient reverb sends off the buses, timed to BPM. (8) Rebalance levels, since every added effect likely shifted perceived level. (9) Automate effect parameters as a final creative pass. → [[Reverb]] — Sage Audio, [How to Mix a Song Start to Finish](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UVvCfJNfSr8)
- **Start a mix by soloing just the "basic backing track" — drums, bass, and guitar — and get that trio feeling right before building everything else around it.** Nick Launay: "I'll just solo all the drums and have the bass, and in this case the guitar, so it's kind of like the basic backing track of the band playing, and I'll just try and get this to really work so that I can then balance my other instruments around it." A deliberate minimal-first mixing order rather than bringing every element in at once from the top. → — Mix with the Masters (Nick Launay), [[Mixing Nick Cave & The bad seeds - Nick Launay]]
- **Sample-rate choice as a session default, a strong stated counterpoint to the 48 kHz default below: avoid 48 kHz specifically, prefer 44.1 kHz or 96 kHz.** From a decade of sessions: **48 kHz** consistently sounds "a little strident, a little hard... not as clear, very digital" — "out of all the different choices, 48k is the most digital sounding to me, I hear it every single time," and it's the format behind sessions where the record "doesn't come together" for him. **44.1 kHz** reads "so much more musical," easiest to work with — "the analog gear likes it, the digital gear likes it." **96 kHz** is also strongly preferred ("it loves 96"). **88.2 kHz** is the weak middle option — "always sounds kind of bland, just kind of sits there." He acknowledges the technical counterargument (48 kHz is exactly half of 96 kHz, the same ratio as 44.1:88.2) and sets it aside — this is one experienced engineer's consistent ear over years of sessions, not a measured/blind-test claim, worth weighing as a real data point against the 48 kHz/32-bit default two entries below rather than as settled fact. Also feeds the mastering-import sample-rate note on [[Master Chain]]. → [[Critical Listening for Mastering]], [[Master Chain]] — Jack Joseph Puig (Mix with the Masters), [Listening to the details with Jack Joseph Puig](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=20CbTBdY8sk)
- **Mine your own recent sessions before building the template, not from a blank slate.** Before setting anything up, open a handful of your most recent finished projects and look at what you *actually* ended up using — which instruments, how many instances, what routing, what plugins on which stage. The template should encode your real habits, not a guess at what you might need; go back and check again whenever the template starts feeling stale. → [[Gain Staging]] — Max Konyi, [The Ultimate Template Creation Guide for Studio One (or any other DAW)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eFMfip3CQcI)
- **Go overkill on instrument instances, then power them off — cheap insurance against 2am routing work.** Pre-create far more instances of your core instruments than you'd use in any one song (demoed: 10 instances of a go-to synth, 2 instances of a multi-timbral workhorse instrument), fully route and default-patch every one, then power off (Ableton: the track's **Activator**/power button, off) all but the 2–3 you actually reach for constantly. The payoff isn't using all of them — it's that when a song genuinely needs a 4th or 5th instance, it's already routed and just needs the Activator flipped back on, instead of building a new track, patching MIDI routing, and assigning outputs mid-flow. In Ableton, deactivating a track via its power button both frees CPU and, for most instruments, unloads them from memory the same way Studio One's "disable" does — the direct equivalent of the video's "disable" move. → [[Gain Staging]] — Max Konyi, [The Ultimate Template Creation Guide for Studio One (or any other DAW)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eFMfip3CQcI)
- **Spread a multi-timbral/multi-output instrument's patches across multiple plugin instances instead of maxing out one instance's channel count, for CPU-core balance.** A multi-output instrument (Omnisphere in the video, 8 possible internal outputs) mostly runs its DSP on a single CPU core regardless of how many outputs are active — stacking all 8 sounds into one instance stresses that one core, while splitting the same 8 sounds across two instances of 4–5 outputs each spreads the load across two cores. Ableton equivalent: run multiple MIDI tracks into the same multi-timbral VST instance, each filtered to a different MIDI channel, with the instrument's separate audio outputs enabled (Preferences → Plug-Ins → multi-out) and routed to individual audio tracks via each track's **Audio From** dropdown pointed at the specific output pair — same principle, same reason to split heavy patches across two instances rather than one maxed-out one. → [[Gain Staging]] — Max Konyi, [The Ultimate Template Creation Guide for Studio One (or any other DAW)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eFMfip3CQcI)
- **A three-tier bus structure — a non-drum "instrument bus," a "drum bus," and a shared "mix bus" feeding the master — as a template default, so sidechaining and mix-wide processing have a home from track one.** Everything that isn't drums (synths, keys, audio tracks, and their reverb/delay sends) routes to one instrument bus; every drum instrument routes to a separate drum bus; both buses feed one shared mix bus before the master output. Reason stated directly: this is what makes a whole-instrument-group sidechain (see [[2 Inst-Synth Group|Inst-Synth Group]]'s felt-not-heard kick duck) or bus-wide processing trivial to reach for later, since the routing already exists rather than needing to be built at the moment it's needed. In Ableton this maps directly onto the existing lane structure — [[2 Inst-Synth Group]] and [[2 Drums Group]] already play the instrument-bus/drum-bus role; a template's value-add is pre-building those groups (and their sends) into every new Live Set from the start rather than reconstructing them per song. → [[2 Inst-Synth Group]], [[2 Drums Group]], [[Sidechaining]] — Max Konyi, [The Ultimate Template Creation Guide for Studio One (or any other DAW)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eFMfip3CQcI)
- **Pre-build several send reverbs and delays, disabled, already routed to the right bus — so reaching for a send mid-idea costs zero setup.** Multiple reverb sends (the video keeps 3, one with a longer/bigger character than the others) and 2 delay sends are created ahead of time, each with a workable starting preset dialed in, each disabled until needed, each pre-routed to the instrument bus (or, for drum-specific reverbs, the drum bus) — so grabbing a send while an idea is flowing means enabling a fader, not building a chain from scratch and losing momentum. In Ableton this is exactly what Return tracks are for: pre-build 2–3 Reverb returns and a Delay return or two into the template with sensible starting settings, and since Return tracks are inherently solo-safe (audible even while soloing an insert track that sends to them, matching the video's "effects channel stays audible while soloing" behavior), no extra setup is needed to replicate that part. → [[Reverb]], [[Delay]] — Max Konyi, [The Ultimate Template Creation Guide for Studio One (or any other DAW)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eFMfip3CQcI)
- **Park a disabled default EQ + compressor insert chain on every instrument track, roughly dialed but not tuned to anything specific.** A highpass-only EQ position and a compressor with a plausible-but-arbitrary starting preset (video's example: ~14 ms attack, 4:1 ratio, auto-gain off) get added to every track's insert chain, then powered off — so when mixing starts, every track already has *something* to enable and adjust rather than an empty chain to build from a blank device browser. Ableton equivalent: save a default Audio Effect Rack (or just an EQ Eight + Compressor pair) as each instrument track's starting insert chain, both devices powered off via their own on/off switches, using **Save Default Preset** or a saved template Live Set so every new instance loads with them already in place. → [[Gain Staging]], [[Compression]] — Max Konyi, [The Ultimate Template Creation Guide for Studio One (or any other DAW)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eFMfip3CQcI)
- **Pre-build a disabled sidechain-ready compressor on the instrument bus, with the kick's send already routed to its sidechain input — so a whole-group duck is one power-button click away.** On the instrument bus, a dedicated compressor set up specifically for sidechain duty is parked (disabled) with the kick track's compressor-send already pointed at that compressor's external sidechain input and the mode already set to external/pre-fader ("preader") — none of the routing has to be rebuilt the moment a song actually wants that duck; it's a single enable click. Directly the setup step behind [[2 Inst-Synth Group|Inst-Synth Group]]'s lo-fi felt-not-heard kick duck (see [[Final Mix & Master Studio One LoFi Tutorial Part 9|Part 9]] of the lo-fi series for the dialed-in settings on the same idea). → [[Sidechaining]], [[2 Inst-Synth Group]] — Max Konyi, [The Ultimate Template Creation Guide for Studio One (or any other DAW)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eFMfip3CQcI)
- **Park a rough "mix-into-it" master chain on the template, mostly disabled, with only the limiter left active as a safety net from the first note.** The video's master-bus default: EQ, compressor, and a saturator all pre-added but disabled, with only a limiter left ON (ceiling set around −1 dB) from the very start of every new session — so nothing ever clips into the speakers no matter what's happening early, while the tone-shaping stages stay off until there's actually a mix to shape. Matches [[Master Chain]]'s own "mix-into-it vs. final loudness" split — a template's job is pre-building that chain (disabled corrective/glue stages, one always-on safety limiter) into every new Live Set rather than adding it late. → [[Master Chain]] — Max Konyi, [The Ultimate Template Creation Guide for Studio One (or any other DAW)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eFMfip3CQcI)
- **Fold/hide what you're not using instead of deleting it, and keep a visible "outer" tier plus a collapsed "everything else" tier.** Only the instruments/sends actually reached for often stay visible at the top level; everything else (extra synth instances, unused output stems on a multi-out drum sampler, rarely-touched audio tracks) gets tucked into folders/groups and collapsed, not deleted — visible again with one click the moment it's needed, but out of the way otherwise so the working view stays uncluttered. Ableton has no literal per-track hide the way Studio One does, so the closest equivalent is **Group tracks** (fold a cluster of disabled/rarely-used tracks into a collapsed Group) — accept that as the substitute rather than trying to replicate true hide/show. → Max Konyi, [The Ultimate Template Creation Guide for Studio One (or any other DAW)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eFMfip3CQcI)
- **Treat the template itself as versioned and living, not a one-time build — save-as-template again (or overwrite deliberately) every time a session teaches you something the template was missing.** After actually making music on a freshly saved template, expect to find gaps ("I probably did something wrong... I have to go back") — the fix is opening the template as a new song, making the change, and saving it back over the template (an explicit "replace existing," not a silent habit) rather than losing the fix in that one song only. Keep versioned copies (e.g. "Starting Point V2") rather than always overwriting blind, so a bad revision is recoverable. Ableton equivalent: maintain a versioned template `.als` in your Live Templates location (or use **File → Save Live Set as Default Set** for the single "always loads on launch" slot), and revise it the same way — open, adjust, re-save — every time a real session exposes a missing piece. — Max Konyi, [The Ultimate Template Creation Guide for Studio One (or any other DAW)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eFMfip3CQcI)
- **Session-setup defaults (sample rate/bit depth, grid, snap behavior) are worth locking in once at the template level, separate from the instrument/routing work.** Before any instrument exists, the video sets project-wide defaults it always wants (48 kHz / 32-bit float, a default quantize grid of 16ths) and specifically calls out **relative grid/snap** — moving an off-grid event keeps its original offset from the grid rather than snapping it dead-on, preserving intentional human timing during edits. These are DAW-preference-level settings (Ableton: Live's own Preferences for default sample rate, and the grid/snap settings in Options) rather than anything stored in the Live Set itself, but the underlying habit — go through every default once, deliberately, informed by how your last few sessions actually ended up — carries over directly. — Max Konyi, [The Ultimate Template Creation Guide for Studio One (or any other DAW)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eFMfip3CQcI)
