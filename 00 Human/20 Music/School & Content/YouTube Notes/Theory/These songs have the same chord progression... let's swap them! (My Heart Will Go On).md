# These songs have the same chord progression... let's swap them! (My Heart Will Go On)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SHTPleCXZA0)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SHTPleCXZA0)

## Synopsis

Second in the RipX mashup series (four song pairs this time), and the one video in this batch to introduce and define **modal ambiguity** as a distinct concept — a genuinely new addition to the vault's theory coverage, not just another progression catalog entry.

## Techniques

- **Pair 1 — "My Heart Will Go On" (Celine Dion) / "In the Air Tonight" (Phil Collins)**: both built on the **Aeolian vamp** — vi–V–IV–V in the major key (equivalently i–♭VII–♭VI–♭VII from the relative minor's perspective), a common natural-minor-key loop.
- **Pair 2 — "Black or White" (Michael Jackson) / "I Still Haven't Found What I'm Looking For" (U2)**: both plain **12-bar blues**, cited specifically as evidence the form shows up well outside its rock/blues home genre.
- **Pair 3 — "Watermelon Sugar" (Harry Styles) / "Blinding Lights" (The Weeknd)**: both 2020-era hits sharing dm–Am–C–G (in A minor: i–v–III–VII — Bennett's route: "not a particularly common progression," making the coincidence notable), usually performed in different keys from each other. **This is the pair that motivates the modal-ambiguity discussion below.**
- **Pair 4 — "Mr. Brightside" (The Killers) / "Shut Up and Dance" (Walk the Moon)**: both in D♭ major using IV–vi–V (Bennett's shorthand "4 6 5"), both popular wedding-DJ staples; tempos had to be met in the middle since Mr. Brightside runs noticeably faster live.
- **Bonus pair — "Can't Stop" / "Zephyr Song" (both Red Hot Chili Peppers)**: same band, same progression (vi–V–III–IV in major / i–VII–V–VI from the relative-minor read), but in very different keys (E minor vs. A minor) — cited as the weakest-sounding mashup of the batch specifically *because* the keys are so far apart that both vocal pitch-shift and tempo had to move substantially to meet, degrading the illusion compared to the closer-key pairs above.
- **Modal ambiguity, defined and explained (new concept, worth its own home)**: occurs when a chord progression (and the melody riding on it) doesn't clearly establish which of two closely-related modes is the true tonic. Worked on the Watermelon Sugar/Blinding Lights progression: heard as A minor (Aeolian) OR as D Dorian (same seven notes, D as tonic) — genuinely ambiguous to the ear. **The specific mechanism causing the ambiguity**: A natural minor and D Dorian differ by exactly one note (B♭ vs. B natural) — and both songs' progressions **withhold that deciding note until the 4th chord** (G, which doesn't contain either B), so the ear has three full chords to start assuming D minor is the tonic (reinforced by the progression literally starting on D minor) before any B is heard at all to disambiguate. **General principle for spotting modal ambiguity elsewhere**: check whether a progression's chords include the one scale-degree that would distinguish between two closely-related candidate modes/keys — if that note is absent or significantly delayed, genuine ambiguity is possible, not just a stylistic choice of framing.
