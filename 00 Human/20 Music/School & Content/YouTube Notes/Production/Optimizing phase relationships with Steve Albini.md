# Optimizing phase relationships with Steve Albini

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DvvLr-Op5yc)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DvvLr-Op5yc)

## Synopsis
A short, dense Mix with the Masters interview clip of Steve Albini talking through polarity and phase around a live drum kit — the mics ringing a kit are, in his words, "hearing an essentially random phase relationship to each other." He splits the topic in two: **absolute polarity** (does the speaker cone need to push *toward* the listener when the kick fires, to match what an audience member in the room actually hears first?) and **relative polarity** (how each mic on the kit sits in phase against the others). On the absolute question he's a skeptic — he thinks the audience-perspective convention is a defensible theory but an arbitrary one (the drummer's own experience of the hit is the opposite), rarely has a preference when he auditions it, and suspects that when people *do* hear a difference it's actually the sound of an extra inverting amplifier stage being added or removed to normalize the polarity, not the polarity itself. On the relative question — mic-to-mic within one kit — he's emphatic that it matters and there's no universal rule: rack tom vs. floor tom, overheads vs. front mic, and stereo mic vs. close mics each get auditioned independently, because the "correct" relationship changes setup to setup. He closes with a practical warning: some mics (he demonstrates with a Russian mic on hand) are electronically wired opposite of the standard, especially second-hand or repaired units — worth mapping your own mic locker for this and labeling any offender so nobody mistakes it for a console fault later.

## Techniques

**On absolute polarity (whole-kit, whole-mix):**
- **The audience-perspective convention, and why he doesn't buy it as a hard rule** — some engineers insist a kick drum recording should reproduce a positive pressure wave (speaker cone moves toward the listener) because that's the first thing an audience member's ear receives when the bass drum fires. Albini calls this "a theoretical position": from the *drummer's* perspective, the first thing that happens is the air column moving *away* from him — so enforcing the audience convention actually creates "an artificial experience for the drummer, the guy that played the drums." Two valid reference frames, no objectively correct one.
- **He rarely hears (or cares about) an absolute polarity preference** — he's auditioned full mixes both ways and "very rarely" has a preference either way; explicitly says "I have never concerned myself with it."
- **The likely real explanation for when people DO hear a polarity difference: it's the extra amplifier stage, not the polarity.** In a conventional feedback amplifier, a single gain stage inverts polarity — so reversing polarity in a signal chain means adding or removing one full stage of amplification (extra circuitry either way). His hypothesis: what people are actually hearing when they "prefer" one absolute polarity is the sonic signature of that added/removed electronic stage, not a genuine perceptual difference from polarity itself. He flags this as an untested hunch (no blind A/B done) but treats it as the more likely explanation than true audible polarity sensitivity — worth remembering next time a "polarity made it sound better" claim comes up; the real variable may be gear in the signal path, not phase.

**On relative polarity (mic-to-mic within one source):**
- **Interrogate every mic pairing on the kit independently — there's no absolute right answer, only what sums best for that setup.** "I think it's important to interrogate the polarity of each of the elements of the drum kit to see if you prefer one or the other standing polarity." No universal setting works twice.
- **Rack tom vs. floor tom** — sometimes the recording is better with the two in opposite phase, sometimes better coherent (same phase). Audition per session.
- **Overheads vs. front (room) mic** — the overheads (behind the kit) and the front mic (in front, facing the opposite direction at the same cymbals/drums) are looking at every source from opposite sides; "sometimes it sounds distinctly better if I reverse the polarity of these mics relative to the microphone in front of the drum kit." Flip and listen, don't assume.
- **Stereo mic (overhead pair) vs. close mics** — a separate relationship from the one above: listening to just the close mics plus the stereo pair, reversing the stereo mic's polarity alone sometimes reads distinctly better. Test this pairing on its own too, not just close-vs-front.
- **The rule of thumb is: there is no rule of thumb.** "There's no hard and fast rule, but I have found that polarity matters within the relationship of the different microphones to each other" — treat every multi-mic setup as a fresh audition, not a fixed preset.

**Practical gear-management tip — mapping your mic locker for polarity quirks:**
- **Some mics are electronically opposite-polarity from the "standard" out of the factory.** Demonstrated live with a Russian-made mic on hand — possibly due to extra internal electronics needed to normalize polarity, possibly a different regional wiring standard; either way, summing it with a normal Western mic produces a subtractive (cancelling) effect purely from this built-in mismatch, with no user error involved.
- **Second-hand and repaired/modified mics are a second, unpredictable source of the same problem** — a mic that's been serviced or bought used can come back wired backwards from normal with no visible sign of it.
- **Actionable fix: know your mic collection, and either correct it or document it.** If a mic in the locker is out of phase with the rest, either (a) correct it by rewiring the connector, or (b) if that's not possible (fixed internal circuit design), at least tell whoever's going to use it — "you can put a sticker on the microphone or something." The payoff: without that label, a phase-flipped mic reads on a session as a phantom console/electronics fault, sending troubleshooting down the wrong path entirely.
