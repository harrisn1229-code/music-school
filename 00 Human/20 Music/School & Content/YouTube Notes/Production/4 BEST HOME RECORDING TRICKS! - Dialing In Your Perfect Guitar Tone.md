# 4 BEST HOME RECORDING TRICKS! | Dialing In Your Perfect Guitar Tone

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H8SRmgZPUOQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H8SRmgZPUOQ)

## Synopsis

BERNTH brings in producer Daniel Fellner for a 4-part home guitar-recording clinic: interface gain staging, recording a DI signal in parallel with the amp/Kemper signal, dialing in an amp-sim/Kemper tone, and basic mixing moves for clarity — including a named "mirror EQ" technique for separating lead and rhythm guitars in a mix. Concrete, actionable production content throughout, not general theory.

## What's taught

- **Gain staging on the interface (~0:54–1:38).** Two failure modes to avoid: clipping the input (audible obvious distortion, not the wanted kind) and setting gain too low so the amp/amp-sim doesn't receive enough signal to "function properly" — the amp modeling stage has an expected input level and starving it changes its behavior, not just its loudness.
- **Record a DI signal in parallel with the amp/Kemper signal, always (~1:38–3:53).** Setup: an active DI box with a pad (20 dB pad used here) between guitar and interface, with a link/thru output feeding the amp/Kemper simultaneously — two interface channels, one from the DI box's direct out, one from the Kemper. Two concrete reasons given: (1) **re-ampability** — if the chosen amp tone doesn't work out later, the clean DI can be re-amped through a different sim/pedal chain without re-tracking; (2) **timing/mistake audibility** — high-gain amp tones obscure exactly where a note's attack/accent falls rhythmically; the clean DI makes it much easier to hear whether playing is actually on the grid.
- **Dialing in an amp-sim/Kemper tone (~3:53–7:09).** Named mistakes: (1) too much gain — "just mud," obscures what's actually happening on the guitar; (2) scooping all the mids — pleasant in isolation but the mids are "where all the meat is" of a guitar tone and scooping them undermines the sound sitting in a mix; (3) extreme knob settings generally — "never go too extreme with your knobs," since an extreme setting that sounds exciting solo tends to mask playing mistakes and translate poorly in a mix. Workflow demonstrated: start gain at zero and work up until it suits the playing style, rather than starting high and pulling back. An overdrive pedal is used with its own gain at zero — purely as a pre-EQ stage to make the DI signal "more compact and direct" before the amp sim, not for added distortion. Applies the identical zero-up gain-staging approach to a Kemper profile (Peavey 6505+ through a Mesa cab) with a simulated Tube Screamer, again making only small incremental moves — "if you have to do really big moves, probably something in the chain isn't suited for the sound you're after."
- **Basic mixing for guitar clarity (~7:09–end).** (1) **Double, don't duplicate:** record the same rhythm part twice (two separate takes) rather than duplicating one recording, for a genuinely wider stereo image — a duplicated single take does not produce the same result as two independently-played takes. (2) **High-pass rhythm guitars** around ~90 Hz — that low end isn't usable for rhythm guitar tone anyway. (3) **Ear-first EQ correction:** find a boxy/spiky resonance by ear (using a narrow sweep to audition the isolated frequency, then cut a few dB) rather than trusting the visual EQ curve alone — visually alarming curves are often necessary for the actual sound. (4) **"Mirror EQ" — a named technique for separating lead and rhythm guitar in a mix:** find the frequency where lead guitar naturally "speaks" (cited as ~900 Hz–1.5 kHz, the same range human speech intelligibility lives in) and boost it slightly on the lead guitar for extra bite/cut-through; do the **opposite** (a cut) at a nearby but distinct frequency (~1.2 kHz cited) on the rhythm guitar — complementary EQ moves on the two parts so they carve space for each other instead of competing in the same band. Lead guitars are also commonly given a delay to help them sit apart from the drier rhythm parts.
