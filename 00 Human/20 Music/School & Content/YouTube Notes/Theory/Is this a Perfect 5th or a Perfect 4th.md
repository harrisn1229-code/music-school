# Is this a Perfect 5th or a Perfect 4th?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PknOTF84_WQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PknOTF84_WQ)

## Synopsis

A follow-up/clarification video responding to comments on an earlier Circle of Fifths video — resolving whether describing a chord movement (A minor→D minor) as "a perfect fifth" or "a perfect fourth" is correct, by separating melodic-interval direction from chord-to-chord motion and from chord function.

## Techniques

- **Chord-to-chord motion is rarely purely "ascending" or "descending"** — unlike a single melodic note, a chord's voices usually move in mixed directions (some up, some down, some static), so labeling the whole move by direction is usually inaccurate to begin with.
- **A 4th and a 5th are inversions of each other, so both labels are correct for the same chord move** — A minor→D minor is validly "a descending 5th" or "an ascending 4th"; neither is more correct than the other.
- **The tempting-but-wrong fix (chord function) breaks down the moment neither chord is the tonic** — labeling A→D as "a 4th" because it's i→iv in A minor only works while you're anchored to that key; the same two chords read as v→i (a "5th") in D minor — this quietly substitutes chord-function vocabulary for interval vocabulary, which share overlapping terms for genuinely different concepts.
- **A guitarist-specific bias**: open strings (except one pair) are tuned in ascending 4ths, which primes guitarists toward the ascending-4th framing for the same two notes other instrumentalists might default to hearing as a descending 5th.
