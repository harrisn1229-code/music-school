# How to Fix Your Sh*tty Bass Tone

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7b0ShkSBtcU)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7b0ShkSBtcU)

## Synopsis

BassBuzz's Josh runs a systematic, evidence-based teardown of the entire bass tone chain — 10 links from fingertip to PA system — to answer "which links actually matter and which are internet-forum obsessions?" The method is consistent link by link: record a blind A/B (or A/B/C) comparison, play it for two professional bassists (Gio and Ben) who don't know what changed, have them guess and rate the importance, then deliver a "thumbs up/down" verdict plus the practical takeaway. This isn't just a gear-nerd exercise — it's a deliberately built priority order for troubleshooting a bad tone: the video's whole thesis is that most players obsess over the wrong links (strings, pickups, cables, wood) while under-investing in the two links that test out as most important and most controllable — fingers/technique and, further down the signal path, amp/cabinet. The blind-test format is genuinely rigorous: same bass/riff/mic position held constant per test, real trained ears doing the judging, and the video is honest when a test comes back ambiguous (the electronics swap, the cable) rather than forcing a dramatic verdict either way. Closes by pointing at the room/band/PA — the one link with the biggest potential impact and zero player control — as the subject of a dedicated follow-up video.

## Techniques

The chain, tested in signal order, fingertip to PA:

- **Link 1 — Fingers (verdict: 3 thumbs up, "the alpha and the omega of bass tone").** Same bassline played 4 ways with only finger technique changed; judges immediately heard the differences and rated this the most obvious/important link tested. Concrete technique: push the string down with **the pad of the finger**, not the area near the nail — too close to the nail changes the sound but doesn't deliver a strong tone. **Digging in harder** = growly, clacky attack (rated the "good" end by the judges). **Light/wispy plucking** (not digging in) = weak, thin, deliberately called out as sounding bad ("it made my heart break"). Fingers give the plucking hand control over **muting** and the note's **edge/attack character** — described as mattering more than any downstream gear because a "garbage bass" still sounds decent in the hands of someone with good finger technique. **Meta-rule: always start here first** when troubleshooting any bad tone, before touching gear.
- **Link 2 — Strings (verdict: 3 thumbs up, with a caveat).** Test deliberately skipped gauge/brand (too many variables) and isolated the single biggest string-type variable: **round wound vs. flat wound**, plus a trick third clip of dead old round wounds to see if the judges could be fooled. Real, audible difference exists, but even trained ears found it subtler than expected in a blind test and didn't nail all three clips confidently. Verdict: string **type** (round vs. flat) is worth choosing deliberately for the tone you want (rounds = brighter/zingier/modern, flats = warmer/deader/upright-adjacent), but don't burn 20 hours agonizing over specific gauges/brands within a type — diminishing returns past picking the right type for the job.
- **Link 3 — Tone wood (verdict: 3 thumbs down for audible tone).** Interviewed two luthiers with 50+ years combined experience (Roger Sadowski, Sheldon Dingwall) who both independently affirm wood affects tone from the **player's** side — but both are equally explicit that the **listener/audience cannot hear it**. The actual mechanism: wood changes how the instrument **feels** in the player's hands, which changes touch/attack/feel, which is what (indirectly) reaches the audience — not the wood's acoustic properties themselves. The one thing tone wood genuinely affects for the player: **weight/comfort** (a heavy bass is a back problem, unrelated to tone). Practical verdict: don't fuss with tone wood to fix a tone problem; pick wood/body style for how it feels to play and how much it weighs, not for a tone payoff nobody in the room will hear.
- **Link 4 — Pickups (verdict: 3 thumbs up — but a two-part test with an important nuance).** Part A: three different basses (Stingray humbucker, Jazz bass, P Bass) blind-tested — obviously, hugely different, easily identified. Pickup **type and string position** is "literally the piece that's grabbing the sound" off the string, and moving pickup position is called "really, really, really extremely significant." **You'll never make a P-Bass sound like a Jazz or a Stingray by swapping parts — the pickups aren't sensing the same string vibrations in the first place** (different position on the string picks up different harmonic content). Part B: the more common real-world question — swapping a **pickup upgrade of the same type/position** into a working bass (stock Squier P-Bass pickups → Seymour Duncan Quarter Pounders, a popular claimed "P-Bass tone" upgrade). Blind result: **no audible difference** to either judge ("just feels like a P Bass"), despite marketing claims of "more fat and articulate." Verdict: pickup **type + position** is a massive tone decision (this is why a P/J/Stingray sound so different from each other) — but swapping to "better" parts of the *same* type in a *working* bass is a low-payoff upgrade; don't expect it to fix a tone problem.
- **Link 5 — Electronics/wiring (verdict: 3 thumbs down for parts-swapping, but the tone knob setting itself matters a lot).** First isolates the obvious: rolling the **tone knob** all the way down is instantly, massively audible — nobody argues that. The actual test was swapping the entire wiring harness (stock Squier Jazz electronics → the popular ~$20 Kaish aftermarket kit, marketed as having "breathed new life into" people's basses) — blind result: **no meaningful difference** detected by either judge. Verdict: your **tone-knob setting** is a real, important control you should actually use — but swapping the pots/wiring/caps themselves for "better" aftermarket parts is very likely placebo if the stock electronics are functioning.
- **Link 6 — Cable (verdict: ~2.5 thumbs down).** Two independent reasons cables matter less for bass than for guitar: (1) cable-induced high-frequency loss from resistance/long runs/cheap cable is real, but those are treble frequencies that aren't where bass tone lives — guitar loses more from this than bass does. (2) **You don't actually control your full cable chain regardless of what you buy** — studio signal path is bass → your cable → the studio's own DI box → the studio's own cable to the board; live signal path is bass → your cable → your amp → the sound engineer's own cable to the board. An expensive cable is only ever one link surrounded by cables you don't own or control, which caps how much leverage it can realistically have. Blind verdict: no audible difference for most listeners, though Josh notes he personally now runs (and likes) a Mogami cable while explicitly flagging that could be placebo rather than proven.
- **Link 7 — Pedals (verdict: obviously huge when used — but situational, not a default fix).** Skipped a formal blind test (comparing distortion to chorus to whatever else has no meaningful "which is better" answer) — engaging any pedal is trivially, massively audible, so the "does it matter" question isn't in doubt. The real finding is about **frequency of use**: both judges say pedals are needed on "probably never," maybe zero, average bass gigs, and one flatly states he never uses pedals live. Verdict: pedals are a powerful tool for a **specific effect** you deliberately want, not a general-purpose fix to reach for when your baseline tone is bad — treat "add a pedal" as the wrong first move for a tone problem.
- **Link 8 — Amp (verdict: 3 thumbs up, important).** Two-stage test. Stage 1: blind-test four amp heads (SVT/Ampeg, Darkglass, Bugera, TC Electronic) at flat/neutral settings, same cab — surprisingly hard to ID, even for the judges' *own* amps ("I don't recognize my amps"). Stage 2: deliberately **dial all four to sound as close to each other as possible** via EQ, re-record through the same mic'd cabinet, and re-blind-test the resulting recording — still identifiable by a trained ear (one judge correctly called all four). Takeaway: amps can be coaxed reasonably close to each other with knob-twiddling, but there's a real ceiling on how identical they can be made to sound — and beyond raw tone, the amp also sets your **clean headroom/volume ceiling** and your **starting-point tone** before you touch a single knob, which is why it still rates as genuinely important.
- **Link 9 — Speaker cabinet (verdict: 3 thumbs up, important).** Three different cabinets (SWR, Acoustic, Bergantino), same amp, same bass loop, same mic position — still sounded "incredibly different" and were correctly identified. Selection framework given, explicitly in this priority order: **(1) can you afford it, (2) what do you actually need it for [the gig's volume/headroom demands], (3) do you like the sound** — need-for-the-gig is deliberately placed ahead of personal taste, because a cabinet you love the sound of but that can't get loud enough for, say, a double-kick metal gig with a loud drummer is still the wrong cabinet for that gig regardless of tone preference.
- **Link 10 — Room, band, and PA (verdict: the biggest potential impact, and the one link with zero player control).** Once the signal leaves your gear, named uncontrollable factors piling on top of each other: the room itself (worst named example — small "barn chic" wedding venues, called out as the single worst room type for bass, repeatedly encountered), which specific electrical outlet you're plugged into, what the guitar player's amp is doing, what the keyboard player's left hand is playing, how low the kick/floor toms are tuned. Also named: an **"amp volume arms race"** dynamic where band members subconsciously creep their own volume up to compete with (or be heard over) each other rather than communicating — called out by name as endemic in metal bands specifically, from firsthand touring experience. Stated fix (teased, not detailed here — covered in the named follow-up video): bring gear you already know sounds good and **trust it** instead of compulsively cranking volume to fight a bad room, and learn to actively troubleshoot tone live based on the specific room and band situation rather than assuming a fixed "good tone" setting travels everywhere unchanged.

**Ranked summary (most → least controllable-and-worth-fixing, per the video's own verdicts):** Fingers/technique (always fix first) → Strings (pick the right type) → Pickup type/position (defines the ballpark, but don't chase upgrade swaps) → Amp → Speaker cabinet → Pedals (only when a specific effect is actually wanted) → Tone wood / pickup-of-same-type upgrades / electronics-parts swaps / cable (marginal-to-placebo when the existing gear is functional — though the tone knob *setting* itself still matters) → Room/band/PA (biggest wildcard, least control — the subject of the follow-up video).
