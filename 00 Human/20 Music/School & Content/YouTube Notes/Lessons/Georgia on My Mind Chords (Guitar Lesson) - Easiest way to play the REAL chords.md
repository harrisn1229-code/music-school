# Georgia on My Mind Chords (Guitar Lesson) | Easiest way to play the REAL chords

## Synopsis

Jared Borowski ([Sound Guitar Lessons](https://soundguitarlessons.com)) teaches a full chord-by-chord arrangement of "Georgia on My Mind" — the chords-first companion to his own [[Georgia on My Mind Fingerstyle Guitar Tutorial (With Sheet Music & Tab)|fingerstyle tutorial]] on the same tune (his recommended order: this video, then the fingerstyle arrangement) — using only **open-position chord shapes in the key of C** — explicitly designed so any guitarist with basic open chords can play it, while still capturing "the REAL chords" (the jazz-informed harmony, not a dumbed-down 3-chord version). The lesson is built around **choice**: for nearly every chord he gives a preferred shape plus one or more alternates that trade simplicity for jazzier color, and explains *why* each alternate works (passing chords, chromatic alteration, delayed resolution, melody-note-in-the-chord logic) so the viewer can build their own arrangement rather than just copy his. He frames the song in its home key (F, most common on sheet music) and Ray Charles' recorded key (G), and shows how a capo (5th fret for F, 7th fret for G) lets the same open-C shapes work in either. Two bonus segments close the video: a free "8 movable shapes" system for reading any jazz lead sheet, and a short lesson on walking chromatic bass notes between chord changes (building on the slash-chord bass motion already written into the arrangement). This is a strong "REAL chords, easy hands" teaching approach — worth mining for its reharmonization logic (delayed resolution, chord-in-front-of-a-chord insertion, melody-note substitution rule) as much as for the Georgia on My Mind chart itself.

## What's taught

**Setup / key:**
- All shapes are open-position, key of C. Capo 5 to play along with sheet music (usually written in F, the most common key for this tune). Capo 7 to play along with Ray Charles' famous 1960 recording (key of G).
- Starting reference note for singing: 2nd fret/4th string → open 3rd string (first two notes of "Geor-gia").

**Full chord-by-chord chart (A section — "Georgia, Georgia, the whole day through..."):**
1. **C** (or Cmaj7 — lift one finger off the C shape for a jazzier color) — "Georgia"
2. **E7** — preferred: open voicing with the melody note on the pinky (2nd string/3rd fret); alt: standard E7 shape (can read "muddier"). Jazz-arrangement option: insert **Bm7♭5** (half-diminished) immediately before the E7 as a passing chord — "very much what makes jazz harmony sound like jazz harmony," adding a chord in front of the expected one.
3. **Am** — "song of you"
4. **Am/G** — A minor stays the chord, G forced as the bass note; deliberate descending bassline, not optional.
5. **D/F♯** — D shape moved up so the bass note continues descending by a half step. Alt: **D7/F♯** (more spice, melody note included). Alt (jazziest): **D9/F♯** — root isn't even in the shape, but the singer supplies it.
6. **F minor 6** — Jared's favorite chord in the whole arrangement ("so gorgeous") — "the whole day through"
7. **E minor 7** (or plain Em, fully interchangeable)
8. **A7** — only option, no alternates — "old sweet song keeps"
9. **D minor 7** (or plain Dm) — "Georgia"
10. **G13** — preferred: G7 shape with open high-E ringing on top (= the 13). Alt: standard open G7 ("cowboy chord" — works but the top note stands out to his ear).
11. **E minor 7 → E minor** (triad right after) — deliberate double voicing, not a mistake.
12. **C major** — the expected cadence point, but the arrangement moves to E minor instead here — **delayed resolution**: elongating the tension by not landing on the tonic yet (references his own dedicated theory video on delayed resolution — now in the vault: [[Delayed Resolution - A Gorgeous Reharmonization Technique (10 Examples)]]).
13. "Old sweet song keeps Georgia on my mind": **Em → Em7 → C → A7 → Dm.** The Em→A7 move works like a ii–V pull toward the coming D minor.
14. **D minor** resolution chord options: Dm7 or Dm triad (simple), or his recommended jazzier pre-chord **G7♯5** (chromatic/altered — the ♯5 is outside the key, "spicy") leading into the Dm. Alt to the dominant ♯5: **G augmented (G+)** — a plain augmented triad (1‑3‑♯5, no 7th) for the same chromatic color with the altered note lower in the register.
15. A section repeats with the same chord menu.
16. Melody-note detail: prefers keeping the open high-E ringing (the 13th — a half step above the chord's flat‑7) over shapes a half step up, because it sings better.

**Bridge into the "B" pre-chorus ("through the Pines..."):**
- **F7** (leads away from the tonic) → alt **F minor 6** (reused favorite) → alt **B♭9** — described as "almost the exact same voicing" as F minor 6, just the bottom note on a different string; B♭9 → C is another delayed-resolution move.
- General rule stated here: **any chord that contains the melody note or the key's root can substitute in as a passing color chord** that leaves and returns to a static tonic, instead of just sitting on it.
- Strumming caution on the F7 shape: avoid catching the top two strings, mute them if strumming through.
- Back to **C** (or Cmaj7, interchangeable).

**"B" section ("The arms that reach out to me..."):**
- **E7** (any earlier voicing) → **Am** (or **Am7** for a "floatier, more jazz" sound).
- **E7** again OR **D minor 6** as a "more traditional harmony" alternative — both work because the melody note fits inside both chords. Stated reharmonization rule: **any chord containing the melody note is a legal substitute**, whether or not it's the "obvious" one.
- Third alternate at the same spot: **F minor 6** again (has the open D as its melody note too).
- **Am7** (not the triad) — physically the *same left-hand shape* as the E7 voicing, just moved to a different string set, so Am7→E7→Am7 plays as a repeated shape shifted across string groups — a smooth-transition trick.
- **F7** again (same tricky-strum warning).
- B section repeats ("the ey[es] smile...").
- Explicit voicing-vs-melody rule: use the **Am7 voicing (open 3rd string)** specifically when that note sits in the sung melody — extensions/7ths matter most exactly where the melody carries that tone; even if you finger a plain triad, the combined sung+played harmony is functionally the 7-chord in that moment.
- **E7** (no alternates here) — "still in peaceful dreams I see"
- **C6** — Jared's own arrangement choice (a C shape plus an added note on the 3rd string/2nd fret), not necessarily the most common voicing elsewhere; steps down a half step into the next chord for smooth bass motion.
- **B7** — "sounds so good"; many arrangements insert a chord before it (same "add a chord in front" jazz-harmony device as the earlier Bm7♭5→E7), but Jared goes straight to B7 here for simplicity. Fingering note: genuinely hard (pinky lands on the 5th string) — worth extra dedicated practice time.
- **E minor → A7** (a ii–V in the key) — very common pairing.
- **D minor 7** — deliberately the *correct* chord here even though it does **not** contain the sung melody note (E in this key) — a stated exception to his own "melody note should be in the chord" rule. Alt: plain Dm. Alt: **D minor 9** — adds the open high-E, restoring the melody note to the voicing for players who want alignment.
- **G13** (same preferred shape) or plain G7.
- A section repeats to the end.

**The turnaround** (cycling back to repeat the form, e.g. before a solo or a repeat of the verse): recommended **E♭ diminished 7** — his preferred "more jazz" color. Alt: plain **A minor** triad, more classic/traditional. Either works; use the top-row (preferred) options for the simplest path.

**The actual ending** (last time through, not cycling): land on **C major** (or Cmaj7), arrived at via one of the same "leave-and-return-to-tonic" pre-chords already covered (F7 / F minor 6 / B♭9).

**Arrangement philosophy:** Jared states this is *not* a dumbed-down starter version — it's exactly how he'd perform the song solo (singer-songwriter / single-guitar accompaniment), even though he can and does play it in other keys and go deeper elsewhere.

**Bonus 1 — "Any Jazz Chord" method** (plug for a free companion resource, `soundguitarlessons.com/jazzchord`): eight movable chord shapes that, moved around the neck, can play essentially any chord symbol found on a jazz lead sheet — a generalizable system for tackling *other* tunes' lead sheets, distinct from the open-string approach used for Georgia itself. Not concrete Georgia content — flagged here as a technique-system reference, not extracted further (external PDF/video, not pulled into this pass).

**Bonus 2 — walking bass notes between chords:** identify a note within the current chord (often its 5th) that sits near the lowest note of the *next* chord shape, and walk a passing bass note (often chromatic, a half/whole step) between them on the beat before the change. Demonstrated C→E7 (walking down through a passing tone into E7's root) and E7→Am (walking up into Am's bass note). Framed as the same "intentional bassline movement" idea already built into the Am/G and D/F♯ slash chords earlier in the chart — go look for more of these anywhere one chord's available notes sit close to the next chord's target bass note.
