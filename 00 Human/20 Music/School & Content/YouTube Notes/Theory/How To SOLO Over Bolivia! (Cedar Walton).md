# How To SOLO Over Bolivia! (Cedar Walton)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qyXynOiAnGI)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qyXynOiAnGI)

## Synopsis

Nathan Borton walks through the chord changes to Cedar Walton's "Bolivia" section by section, purely as an improvisation vehicle — melody is deliberately skipped (stated reason: YouTube copyright), so this is a chord-scale/vocabulary lesson, not a chart or arrangement. His method throughout is **reduction**: instead of treating each chord as its own harmonic event to be handled individually, he collapses the tune's four sections down to a small handful of already-known "sound buckets" — a 5-1 (dominant-to-tonic), a tritone-sub 5-1, a long stretch that's almost entirely a single static B-minor sound (including a half-diminished chord reached via melodic minor off its ♭3), and a closing run of short 2-5s. The payoff move, saved for the tune's trickiest spot (the A7-to-G7 turnback before the tune's framing G7 vamp), is recognizing that a ii chord and its V are functionally the same sound (Dm7 ≈ G7, since Dm7 is G7's related ii in the key of C) — which lets him reinterpret A7→G7 as A7→Dm7's "5-1," folding an otherwise-awkward transition into vocabulary he already has. He closes by generalizing this into a practice philosophy: sort every jazz lick/line you transcribe into one of three buckets (5-1, 2-5-1, static-minor) rather than memorizing licks tune-by-tune, so a whole tune like this one ends up being "just 5-1 lines and static minor sounds" instead of four unrelated hard sections. Short (under 9 minutes), spoken clearly, captions clean/high-confidence — the only garble worth flagging is the ASR mishearing "tritone sub" as "trionee sub" throughout, corrected in the transcript and here.

## What's taught

- **Section 1 (E♭ major → A7 → D):** the progression is a 5-1 into the key of D (A7 → Dmaj7), arrived at from a starting E♭ major tonic. Two ways to solo it, presented as options rather than a rule: (1) **ignore the 5-1 and just play the two key centers** — E♭ major ideas, then D♭ major ideas *over both* the A7 and the D (i.e. keep the D♭-major color running through the dominant rather than switching); (2) **engage the 5-1 directly** — A7 ideas resolving into D major 7 ideas. → [[The ii-V-I]], [[🎵 CEDAR WALTON - Bolivia]]
- **Practice-methodology aside (dropped mid-lesson, not tied to one chord):** categorize every lick/language idea you steal from transcriptions by *type* (5-1 ideas, tonic-major ideas, etc.) as you learn it, so you have a ready-made bucket to reach into rather than re-deriving vocabulary from scratch each time a similar chord shows up. This is the seed of the video's closing generalization (see below). → [[Bebop Vocabulary]]
- **Section 2 (D7 → G, played as A♭7 → G):** a **tritone substitution** — instead of the diatonic D7 (5 chord of G), sub in A♭7 (a tritone away). Two ways to solo it: (1) **play the actual substituted progression** — A♭7 ideas resolving to G major 7; or (2) **ignore the sub entirely and just play a normal 5-1** (D7→G) — stated to still work fine, since the tritone-sub chord shares the underlying V-to-I pull. → [[Chord Substitution]] § *2. Tritone substitution*, [[🎵 CEDAR WALTON - Bolivia]]
- **Section 3 (the long static-minor stretch):** reduced almost entirely to **B minor** as the one sound to think in, across five different chord spellings that all read as the same color: B minor 7 (played straight); the **C Lydian chord** — played with **B minor 7 pentatonic**, stated to work because "those two sounds work together very well" (B minor pentatonic sits diatonically inside a C Lydian/G major collection); B minor 7 again; **B minor 7/A** (B minor 7 with the ♭7, A, in the bass) — explicitly called "the exact same sound" as plain B minor 7, just a different bass note, so no vocabulary change needed; and **A♭ minor 7♭5** (a half-diminished chord) — soloed with **B melodic minor**, using the rule "go up to the ♭3 of the half-diminished chord and play melodic minor off that" (A♭'s ♭3 is B, enharmonically C♭). This matches the vault's own Melodic Minor mode table exactly: the vi degree of a melodic-minor scale is a half-diminished (ø, Locrian ♮2) chord — so "melodic minor off the chord's own ♭3" and "the vi-chord of that melodic minor" are the same fact stated two ways. → [[Scale Modes Reference]] § *Melodic Minor*, [[Chord Substitution]], [[🎵 CEDAR WALTON - Bolivia]]
- **Section 4 (the closing turnaround run):** a chain of short 2-5s, a tritone sub, and a final 5-1: a **2-5 into F major**, then a **tritone sub into B♭ major** (also playable as a plain, un-subbed 2-5 into B♭ — same "you can always fall back to the plain version" option as section 2), and finally a **5-1 into D minor 7**. Stated fix for all of it: keep short 2-5 licks and short minor-2-5 licks in your vocabulary bucket and drop them straight in. → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Chord Substitution]]
- **The A7→G7 turnback (the tune's trickiest single moment, right before the closing G7 vamp), solved via ii/V functional equivalence.** The tune opens and closes on a G7 vamp; the chord immediately before that final G7 is A7 — an A7→G7 move with no obvious diatonic logic. Reasoning chain given: G7 is the V chord of a 2-5-1 in the key of C; the matching ii chord of that same 2-5-1 is D minor 7; **a ii chord and its V are "the exact same sound"** (stated directly, not hedged) — so **D minor ideas work over G7, and G7 ideas work over D minor**. That equivalence lets the whole A7→G7 move be reheard as **A7 → D minor, i.e. a plain 5-1** into a chord you already have vocabulary for, rather than an unfamiliar dominant-to-dominant jump. Matches the vault's existing "dominant 7th → its related ii" shortcut (C7's related ii is Gm7, by the same bottom-note/top-note logic — G7's related ii is Dm7) already documented from a different source. → [[The ii-V-I]] § *Guitar shortcut: the power-chord finder*, [[Chord Substitution]] § *From videos* (the "2 equals 5, 5 equals 2" Grant Green bullet), [[🎵 CEDAR WALTON - Bolivia]]
- **Closing generalization — the whole-tune payoff of the reduction method above, stated as a portable practice philosophy.** Categorize every piece of jazz language you're building as one of three buckets: **static minor sounds**, **2-5-1s**, or **5-1s** — "puzzle pieces you put in different spaces" rather than tune-specific licks to be memorized fresh each time. Applied retroactively to the whole tune just covered: "Bolivia" reduces from four separately-scary sections down to just 5-1 lines and static-minor sounds once you stop treating each chord change as a unique event. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]], [[🎵 CEDAR WALTON - Bolivia]]
