## Synopsis
Sixth and (for this batch) final lesson in the "You Suck at Drums" course, delivered as a "jazz studies professor" comedic bit. Applies the by-now-familiar 4-clip energy-tier method (Intro/A/B/Outro) to a live-feel jazz/rock/funk drum library instead of a straight electronic kit, and centers on the realism payoff: multi-sampled acoustic kits (velocity triggers genuinely different samples, not just volume) plus a bounded velocity-randomization range so a looping pattern never plays back identically twice.

## Techniques
- **Multi-sampled drum kits swap the actual sample, not just the volume, based on velocity** — a multi-sampled kit (here, Ableton's Drum Booth "rock" kit, with Kontakt/Superior Drummer as third-party alternatives) triggers a genuinely different recorded hit depending on how hard the note's velocity is, rather than the same sample played louder or quieter — lowering a kick's velocity visibly and audibly swaps in a different sample layer. → [[Rhythm and Groove]] (already routed)
- **Randomize velocity within a bounded range so a looping clip never repeats identically** — Ableton's velocity lane lets you drag a range above each note instead of setting one fixed value; the hit then plays back at a random velocity within that band every time the clip loops. Keep the range narrow for subtle variation — too wide reads as inconsistent playing rather than natural playing — and this pays off more on a multi-sampled kit specifically, since a wider velocity range also swaps which sample gets triggered, not just the volume. → [[Rhythm and Groove]] (already routed)
- **The same Intro→A→B→Outro energy-tier method, applied to a live-feel kit** — builds four clips at rising/falling energy exactly as in episodes 2–4 (kick/snare/hi-hat placement, then adding snare rolls, opening up hi-hats to rides, layering tambourine, adding a tom fill), confirming the method generalizes past straight electronic drum programming into acoustic-feel genres. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]] (already routed)
- **Slow the tempo down when auditioning a live-feel kit** — dropping BPM (here to ~85) is called out as part of getting the jazz/rock feel to sit right before programming starts, rather than fixing tempo after the pattern is written.
- **A tambourine/shaker layer benefits from the same bounded-velocity-range randomization as the kick** — the same "drag a range above the note, not a fixed value" technique applied to a secondary percussion layer, not just the kit's core drums.
