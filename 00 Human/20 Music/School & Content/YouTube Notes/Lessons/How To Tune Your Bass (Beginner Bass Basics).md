## What's taught

**Part 1 — The musical alphabet, framed for tuning (not just theory for its own sake).**
- 12 notes total in music; the 13th note away from any starting note is the same note name, one octave higher.
- 7 of the 12 are plain letters (A B C D E F G); the other 5 are filled in by sharps and flats.
- Piano-keyboard framing: moving right = ascending/higher pitch and forward through the alphabet (A→G); moving left = descending/lower pitch and backward through the alphabet (G→A).
- **♯ (sharp) raises a note one step; ♭ (flat) lowers a note one step** — the same physical black key has two valid names depending on which direction you approached it from (raising A → A♯; lowering B → B♭ — same key, same pitch, different name/context).
- **The two "no black key" gaps: B→C and E→F have no sharp/flat between them** — these are the two spots in the 12-note cycle where adjacent letters are already only a half-step apart.
- **The practical payoff, stated directly: reading a tuner's wrong-note display tells you which way to turn.** If your G string's tuner reads F, F is behind G in the alphabet (lower), so turn toward higher pitch (forward through the alphabet, through F♯, to G). If your A string reads B♭, B is ahead of A (higher), so turn toward lower pitch (backward through the alphabet) to reach A.

**Part 2 — Three ways to tune, explicitly ranked, with the tradeoffs stated.**
1. **Plug-in tuner — the host's own favorite and top recommendation.** Instrument cable from the bass into the tuner; bass volume knob must be up or it won't register. Most accurate of the three, **especially on the low strings**.
2. **Clip-on tuner — second-best, chosen for this video's demo anyway.** Clips onto the headstock, reads string vibration through the wood — no cable needed, which is why a lot of people default to it. Named tradeoff: **less accurate than a plug-in**, and specifically prone to **misreading which string you're on** (reports a different letter/octave than what's actually being played) — happens twice in this video's own demo (see Part 3).
3. **Tuning apps (phone or computer, mic-based) — ranked last.** Relies on a microphone rather than a direct cable/vibration signal, so it's the least accurate of the three — most people don't have a way to plug a bass directly into a phone or computer. Framed as **a stand-in until you own a real tuner**, not a long-term primary method.
- **Tuning order, stated as a clean ranking: plug-in best → clip-on second → app as a stopgap.**
- **Ear tuning (with a reference pitch, or harmonics) is possible but explicitly discouraged for beginners** — without a developed ear, the odds of landing on a wrong pitch (including a full octave off) are much higher than just reading a tuner's direct output.

**Part 3 — The actual tuning session (clip-on tuner, worked in real time, including the misreads).**
- **Turn the tuning peg that actually matches the string you're adjusting** — stated as a deliberate check, not an assumption.
- **Tuner display reading:** centered + green = in tune; drifts left = flat/too low; drifts right = sharp/too high.
- **Pluck firmly** for a clean, stable reading — called out specifically as important for getting a reliable clip-on read.
- **G string:** tuned cleanly first, confirmed centered/green — used to demonstrate what drifting flat vs. sharp looks like on the display (including deliberately overshooting to A♭/G♯ to show the wraparound).
- **D string, misread #1 (clip-on octave/string confusion):** the tuner briefly reads "A" instead of D even though it's definitely the D string being played. **Fix demonstrated: pluck the 12th-fret harmonic instead of the open string** when a clip-on gives an ambiguous or clearly-wrong read — a harmonic produces a cleaner overtone for the tuner to lock onto, avoiding the misread.
- **D string, worked alphabet-navigation example:** tuner reads B on the D string → B to D is two letters forward in the alphabet → turn toward higher pitch. Mid-turn readings pass through B → C → D♭ before landing on plain D — called out explicitly as normal (you'll pass through "correct-looking" intermediate notes like C being in tune on its own, that aren't the target) rather than a sign something's wrong.
- **A string, worked "which direction is closer" example:** tuner reads a flat-leaning B (not B♭, just B read slightly flat) → two valid paths to A (forward around the full 12-note loop, or backward/down directly) → **rule of thumb: take whichever direction is closer** — down to A from B is a short hop, up and around is the long way.
- **The critical safety rule — always err toward flat/loose when uncertain, never sharp/tight.** Explicit reasoning: an over-loose string is just floppy (annoying, safe); an over-tight string can actually **break**, which is a real cost, not just a wrong sound. So when direction is genuinely unclear, deliberately detune low first, then creep the pitch up slowly rather than guessing a starting point that might already be too sharp — demonstrated live on the A string (intentionally dropped to a floppy F, then crept upward to A).
- **E string:** tuned last, only slightly sharp — small correction. Called out again as a spot where the **clip-on is specifically "finicky" on the low E** — a second, separate accuracy strike against clip-ons beyond the D-string misread, reinforcing the plug-in-tuner recommendation from Part 2.
- Tuning order demonstrated: **G → D → A → E** (not the canonical thickest-to-thinnest E-A-D-G naming order used elsewhere in this vault, but the same four strings/pitches either way).
