# Simandl vs. One Finger Per Fret — Choosing a Bass Fretting Technique

BassBuzz's "versus battle" for the **fretting hand** — the direct mirror of [[String Crossing and Plucking Patterns#The math hack — pick technique by note-grouping parity, before you even try both|the raking-vs-alternating showdown]] on the plucking-hand side, same channel, same head-to-head-on-three-real-basslines format, same closing myth-bust. Where that video settles which *plucking* pattern to use per string crossing, this one settles which *fretting-hand fingering system* to use per bassline: **Simandl** (index-middle-pinky, a 3-fret span) vs. **One Finger Per Fret** (OFPF — one finger per fret across the position, up to a 4-fret span). The video runs both through three real basslines, awards a point per round, and lands on a genuine 2-2 tie — which is the point: **the myth it busts is that you have to pick one technique to learn.** Both are tools for different jobs, like a hammer and a screwdriver, and the real skill is knowing which one a given passage calls for.

## The two techniques, defined

**One Finger Per Fret (OFPF / 1FPF):** each finger is assigned to one fret within a position — e.g. frets 5-6-7-8 get index-middle-ring-pinky respectively. Doesn't mean staying rigidly stretched static in the position — the hand can still **microshift** within an OFPF fingering (small in-position creep, not a full relocation), same mechanism as [[Fretting-Hand Strength, Stretch, Stamina, and Finger Control#Exercise 2 — Stretch|Exercise 2 — Stretch]] and [[One-Finger-Per-Fret 5-Level Daily Drill]]'s five-fret-position framing.

**Simandl:** named for Franz Simandl's 1881 upright-bass method book; on electric bass the term has narrowed to mean fretting with **index-middle-pinky covering a 3-fret span**, with the **ring finger acting as a support** for the pinky rather than fretting independently (the ring isn't used as its own note-fretting finger the way it is in OFPF).

The core structural difference: Simandl covers **3 frets** with 3 active fingers (index-middle-pinky); OFPF covers **4 frets** with 4 active fingers (index-middle-ring-pinky). That one-fret difference in reach is what every round below actually turns on.

## Round 1 — "Doing It to Death" a.k.a. "Gonna Have a Funky Good Time" (James Brown, bassist Fred Thomas): Simandl wins

**The spot:** the bassline's last four notes, low on the neck, using only **frets 1 and 3** (fret 2 and fret 4 are never touched).

- **OFPF fingering:** index=fret 1, middle=fret 2 (unused), ring=fret 3 → the line plays **ring-index-ring-index**. Because the pinky/fret-4 is never needed, the index-to-ring stretch buys nothing — it's a stretch spent for no reach gained.
- **Simandl fingering:** index=fret 1, middle=fret 2 (unused), pinky=fret 3 → the line plays **pinky-index-pinky-index**. A more neutral, natural hand position for the same two frets.
- **Named failure mode of forcing the OFPF stretch here, low on the neck:** carelessly reaching index-to-ring on these wide, low-fret spacings risks **slanting the fingers**, which pushes the **palm toward the neck** and the **thumb over the top** — bad fretting-hand mechanics, not just wasted effort.
- **Verdict and why:** Simandl — it covers the same two frets (1 and 3) without unnecessary stretching tension. **General rule extracted:** don't stretch to a finger you don't structurally need; a 2-fret-apart target (frets 1 and 3, skipping 2) needs a 3-fret span, not a 4-fret reach, and Simandl's index-middle-pinky is a closer match than OFPF's ring reach.

## Round 2 — "Time Bomb" (Rancid, bassist Matt Freeman): One Finger Per Fret wins

**The spot:** a fast passage covering a full **4-fret span, frets 4-5-6-7**.

- **Simandl fingering:** index=fret 4, then **shift** the hand (no stretch) to bring the pinky to fret 7. Because the span is a full 4 frets and Simandl only reaches 3, this means **repeated shifting back and forth (4→7→4→7…)** every time the line revisits both ends of the span — tiring at this song's tempo.
- **OFPF fingering:** same index-pinky fingering at the two ends, but the hand **stretches to cover the whole 4-fret position** instead of shifting — more held tension in the hand, but no frantic back-and-forth relocation.
- **Verdict and why:** OFPF — a more **stable hand position across the whole bassline**, trading (an acceptable amount of) held stretch tension for eliminated shifting motion. **General rule extracted:** on a bassline that genuinely spans 4 frets at speed, repeated Simandl shifting costs more than the OFPF stretch does — and shifting also has a **noise/precision cost** (each shift is a chance to mis-land), not just a fatigue one.

## Round 3 — "Plush" (Stone Temple Pilots, bassist Robert DeLeo): a split decision, one point to each

Two spotlights judged separately in the same bassline — the clearest proof in the video that the decision is per-passage, not per-song.

- **Spotlight A — bottom of the neck, frets 1-2-3 (walking up to fret 3 on the E string, then no further):** Simandl plays it index-middle-pinky; OFPF plays it index-middle-ring. Same logic as Round 1 — the walk only returns to fret 3, never reaching fret 4, so OFPF's ring-finger stretch is wasted energy. **Simandl wins this spotlight** — saves stretching energy for a job that doesn't need the extra reach.
- **Spotlight B — a 4-fret span, frets 2 to 5, four notes needing to land on four frets:** Simandl (3 fingers) needs a shift somewhere in the walk-up — workable here because there's a full 8th-note gap between the fret-4 and fret-5 notes to shift inside, so it doesn't feel rushed. But OFPF is the cleaner call: **just assign one finger per note** — index-middle-ring-pinky straight across frets 2-3-4-5, no shift needed at all.
- **Verdict:** a genuine tie within one bassline — Simandl for Spotlight A, OFPF for Spotlight B — because the line moves in and out of a single position and each technique has the advantage at a different point in it. **General rule extracted:** don't judge a whole bassline with one verdict; a line that both stays inside 3 frets *and* stretches to 4 at different moments calls for switching technique mid-phrase, the same way the video's own demo does (Simandl for most of the line, switching to OFPF specifically for the 2-3-4-5 walk-up).

## Final scoreboard: 2-2, a tie — and that tie is the whole point

Simandl: Round 1 + half of Round 3. OFPF: Round 2 + half of Round 3. **The myth this video sets out to bust: that a bassist has to choose ONE fretting technique to learn and use.** The tool analogy the video makes directly: arguing Simandl vs. OFPF as if one must categorically beat the other is like arguing a hammer beats a screwdriver — it depends on whether you're dealing with a nail or a screw. Working bassists use both, chosen per passage, same "no universally correct technique, most pros use both depending on the line" conclusion [[String Crossing and Plucking Patterns#The math hack — pick technique by note-grouping parity, before you even try both|the plucking-hand showdown video]] reaches about raking vs. alternating — this is the fretting-hand version of the same underlying lesson.

## Strengths and weaknesses, summarized

**Simandl:**
- Saves stretching energy — the "least work for the result" option when a passage doesn't need more than 3 frets of reach.
- A real advantage for **small hands**, especially low on the neck where fret spacing is widest.
- Worth defaulting to for **sustained grooves** — holding a low-tension hand shape for 5–10 minutes straight (as in the James Brown groove) matters more than raw reach.
- **Weakness:** only a 3-fret span, so any bassline spanning more than 3 frets forces repeated shifting — less efficient once speed is a factor, since every shift is both a fatigue cost and a mis-land risk.

**One Finger Per Fret:**
- Better efficiency on basslines spanning **more than 3 frets** — crucial as tempo rises, since it trades shifting motion for held stretch.
- Can reduce transition noise and shifting effort **even at slow/medium tempo** (the Time Bomb case) — not purely a speed-tempo tool.
- **Weakness:** costs more held tension in the hand to maintain the stretch, especially on the wider fret spacing low on the neck (the same tension risk Round 1 flags as a slanted-finger/bad-mechanics trap when the stretch isn't actually needed).

## Decision framework — how to actually choose, in the moment

**1. Practice mode vs. performance mode — different biases, opposite reasons.**
- **Practice mode** (scales, exercises, skill-building) → bias toward **OFPF**. The stated reasoning is squarely about skill development, not efficiency: as a beginner, covering a 4-fret span and building **finger independence** both take deliberate work, and OFPF puts productive pressure on exactly those two skills. Stop short of pain — this is [[Interleaved Practice, Chunking, and Deliberate-Practice Drills#Practice at the edge of your ability ("deep practice")|edge-of-ability practice]], not a demand for a permanent full stretch.
- **Performance mode** (prepping a bassline to actually play on stage) → bias toward **Simandl**, because the goal flips to **least work for the max result**, and most basslines don't actually require more than a 3-fret reach.

**2. Two guiding principles for a specific bassline, beyond the practice/performance split:**
- If the passage is **relatively slow**, default to Simandl regardless of fret span — there's plenty of time to shift, so the shifting cost that hurts at speed doesn't apply.
- If the passage is **fast AND spans more than 3 frets**, OFPF is the best bet — this is the Time Bomb condition exactly.

**3. Beyond those two principles, it's judgment, not a formula.** Hand size and personal taste are named as real variables the video doesn't try to reduce to a rule — the stated path to good instincts is early guidance from a teacher or course (a plug for BassBuzz's paid course, noted here for context, not as content) rather than a lookup table. **Practical takeaway for this vault:** treat the two guiding principles above as the fast heuristic for a new bassline, and treat Round 3's split verdict as license to **switch technique mid-phrase** when a line genuinely calls for it, rather than forcing one fingering system through an entire song.
