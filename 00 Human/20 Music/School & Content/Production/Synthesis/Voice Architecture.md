# Voice Architecture

How a synth allocates notes — and how many copies of the [[Signal Path]] run at once. This is where thickness comes from, and where CPU goes.

## Unison and detune

The placement (SYN-09) had detune and not unison. They only work together, and unison is the one doing the work.

- **Unison** stacks multiple copies of the same oscillator on the same note.
- **Detune** spreads those copies slightly apart in pitch.

**Unison without detune does almost nothing** — identical copies just sum to the same waveform, louder. The thickening comes entirely from the copies being *slightly out of tune with each other*, so they drift in and out of phase and produce slow beating. That beating is what the ear reads as "big."

| Control | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Voices** | How many copies. 3–5 for warmth; 7–16 for the supersaw wall |
| **Detune** | How far apart. Small = warm; large = seasick |
| **Blend / stack** | Balance between the centre voice and the outer ones |
| **Spread / width** | Pans the copies across the stereo field |

**The supersaw** is exactly this: many detuned saws, spread wide. [[DUNE 3]]'s `Leads/Supersaw` banks have it pre-engineered; [[Serum 2]] builds it from unison voices on a saw.

> [!warning] Two costs
> **Mono compatibility.** Detuned voices spread wide can partially cancel in mono. Always check — see [[Width and Stereo]].
>
> **Bass.** Detune below about 150 Hz produces beating that reads as wobbly, unstable low end. **Keep subs and 808s in mono with no detune.** If you want a wide bass, layer: clean mono sub + detuned wide upper layer. See [[Bass Design]].

## Polyphony modes

| Mode | Behaviour | Use for |
|---|---|---|
| **Poly** | Many notes at once, each its own voice | Pads, chords, keys |
| **Mono** | One note at a time, retriggers envelopes | Basses, leads |
| **Legato** | One note; overlapping notes *don't* retrigger envelopes | Expressive leads, 808 glides |

**Legato is the one worth understanding.** In mono, every new note restarts the envelopes — a fresh attack each time. In legato, if you hold one note and play another before releasing, the pitch changes but the envelope keeps running. The result is a smooth, connected phrase.

Combined with **glide/portamento** — the time taken to slide between pitches — legato is how you get the 808 slide that defines trap low end. Glide with retrigger off, short glide time, and the note bends into the next rather than restarting.

## Voice count and CPU

Each voice is a full signal path. A 16-voice unison patch played as a four-note chord is **64 simultaneous oscillator-filter-amp chains**, before effects.

This is the main CPU cost in most projects. Practical responses:

- Drop unison voices on parts that don't need the width — a bass rarely does.
- **Freeze or resample.** Printing the part to audio removes the cost entirely and forces a commitment, which is usually good anyway. See [[Committing and Printing]].
- Watch **voice stealing** — when a synth hits its voice limit it kills the oldest note, which on a pad with a long release is audible as tails cutting off abruptly.

## Voice-level randomization

The subtle thing that makes hardware sound alive: on a real analogue synth, every voice is a slightly different physical circuit, so no two notes are identical.

Digital synths emulate this deliberately:

- [[Drift]]'s **drift** control randomizes pitch and filter slightly per voice — the reason it sounds analogue.
- [[Analog]] and [[Model 84 Polyphonic Synthesizer|Model 84]] apply small per-voice variation by design.
- In [[Serum 2]] or [[Wavetable]], route **random-per-note** to pitch at a tiny depth for the same effect.

Without it, repeated notes are byte-identical and the ear notices — the machine-gun effect. This is the synth equivalent of the humanization discussion in [[Rhythm and Groove]].

## Drill

On [[Serum 2]] or [[DUNE 3]]: take a saw lead, set unison to 1 voice, then walk it up to 7 with detune at zero — note that nothing much happens. Now bring detune up. That's the whole mechanism. Then check the result in mono.

## From videos

- **On a multi-voice poly synth, give different voices genuinely different filter TYPES (not just different tunings/pans) for real dimension — an Oberheim Four-Voice worked example.** One voice's output panned hard right with a highpass filter, another panned hard left with a lowpass filter (plus 1-2 more voices at different octaves/simplicity levels) — demonstrated A/B against the same patch with the highpass voice removed, where it reads as "a normal synth" without it. A different move than assigning identical-filter voices to different physical units for humanized drift (documented elsewhere): here the filter TYPE itself differs per voice, deliberately. Same source's hardware-performance workflow: print a part BOTH wet (through the full pedal/effects chain) AND dry simultaneously, specifically to preserve the option to change the effects treatment later despite there being no MIDI/recall on a hardware-only setup — the closest a fully-hardware, print-live workflow gets to a DAW's non-destructive flexibility. → [[Parallel Processing]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[My Synth Studio Revealed - Studio Tour 2025]]

- **Unison mode + a limited voice count creates a real performance gotcha on pad chords: connecting/sustaining between chords drops voices, so every chord has to be re-articulated separately.** Because unison mode allocates multiple voices per note (e.g. a 16-voice-polyphony synth in unison splits into 4 voices per note on a 4-note chord), holding a note over into the next chord (via legato playing or a sustain pedal) doesn't carry that voice cleanly — it can silently drop out, thinning the chord. Fix demonstrated live: play WITHOUT the sustain pedal and re-strike every chord cleanly ("as if it's all multiple-trigger") rather than connecting them. **Chord density interacts with voice allocation too**: a 4-note chord divides voices evenly, but adding a 5th note (demonstrated on the same patch) unbalances the per-note voice count and measurably weakens the sound — worth knowing unison-mode pads often have a genuine "sweet spot" chord size, not an arbitrary preference. → [[Extended Chords]] — Anthony Marinelli (feat. Greg Phillinganes), [[Greg Phillinganes Breaks Down Pads Behind Michael Jacksons Thriller]]

- **A subtle two-oscillator detune (audible slow beating, not full unison spread) acts as a built-in vibrato/chorusing on a simple lead — and a Decay on/off button can be toggled live for real-time articulation changes.** Dr. Dre's "G Thang" Minimoog lead: two oscillators (square + sawtooth) detuned just enough to beat gently against each other reads as expressive/"singing" where dead-in-tune reads flat and static — treat a small amount of deliberate mistuning as a default option on any simple melodic lead, not just on pads/unison stacks. Separately: pushing the VCA envelope's Decay button on/off mid-performance switches the same patch between a decaying/plucked articulation and a normal sustained one without touching any other setting — a fast performance-time articulation switch worth knowing exists on hardware/plugins that expose it. → [[Bassline Writing]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[Nuthin but a G Thang Minimoog G-funk Lead]]

- **Why modulating only ONE of two oscillators' pitch (not both) is what makes a two-oscillator patch sound like real strings.** Two sawtooth oscillators, no resonance; route a triangle-wave LFO (via the mod wheel) to only oscillator A's pitch — the two oscillators drift in and out of phase/pitch relative to each other, which the ear reads as a natural chorus/ensemble effect. Vibrato on BOTH oscillators together (the typical factory-preset wiring) instead gives a uniform, generic "synthy" wide vibrato — not strings — because there's no relative drift for the ear to latch onto. Find a fixed, moderate modulation depth by ear rather than maximum: too much stops sounding like strings. → [[LFOs and Modulation]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[Get MORE EXPRESSION Without Spending Money]]
- **Two hardware units of the same synth, detuned against each other, for a thicker unison** — Steve Porcaro ran two CS-80s detuned against each other for extra thickness, distinct from a single instrument's own internal oscillator-detune. → Anthony Marinelli & Steve Porcaro, [[Secrets to Steve Porcaros Sound - The CS-80 and More]]
- **A chord-transition trick on an Oberheim: play a chord in unison mode, then take the patch OUT of unison mid-note so the stacked unison voices "peel off" and slide up into their individual, separated notes.** A transition device rather than a static patch — the move from unison to split IS the effect, not a settings choice you'd otherwise leave fixed. → Anthony Marinelli & Steve Porcaro, [[Steve Porcaro In-Studio - Writing Human Nature and Unreleased MJ Songs Part 1]]
- **Single-trigger vs. multi-trigger, reframed via a woodwind-tonguing analogy — and dropping sustain slightly to expose the effect.** Multi-trigger = tonguing every note (identical hard attack each time); single-trigger = tonguing only the first note of a phrase and letting the rest connect ("du-d" instead of "d-du"), which is why classic Stevie Wonder-style synth-bass lines read as vocal/connected rather than mechanically articulated. Dropping the envelope's sustain slightly (not to zero) makes the single-trigger phrasing more audible without changing the patch's core tone — a quick diagnostic move whenever a bass part isn't "finessing" the way it should. → [[Bassline Writing]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[Stevie Wonder and MJ Keyboard Secrets by Greg Phillinganes]]
- **A velocity + pressure-sensitive keybed (CS-80-style) is a deliberate source of natural, note-to-note variation — not noise to eliminate.** Because the CS-80 tracks both strike velocity and post-strike pressure, no two attacks on the "Billie Jean" chord-stab patch come out quite the same, contributing to why it reads as organic. Recreating the same patch on a synth with a simple on/off gate keybed (demonstrated on a Prophet-5) gets close tonally but loses that irregularity — release/attack become perfectly consistent every time. Two easy-to-miss settings that silently break a Prophet-5 recreation of a dual-oscillator CS-80 patch: the RELEASE button must be engaged (otherwise zero release time regardless of the envelope knob) and the mod WHEEL must be up (otherwise zero pulse-width modulation, losing the "chewy" richness). → [[Filters and Resonance]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[The Billie Jean Chord Stack - It's 4 Sounds!]]
- **Correct oscillator detune — not pulse-width modulation — is what actually gives many classic "Oberheim" leads/stabs their character.** Van Halen's "Jump" intro: two sawtooth oscillators at equal level, no resonance on the filter, filter envelope amount at maximum with only a modest base cutoff (a snappy attack/quick-decay/low-sustain filter envelope for "bite," paired with a near-instant-attack/full-sustain VCA envelope). Demonstrated in/out of tune, too-detuned, and correct — getting the detune amount exactly right does all the character work; there's no PWM in the patch at all, despite that being the commonly-assumed source of "the Oberheim sound." → [[Filters and Resonance]], [[Sound Selection Guide]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[Van Halen Jump Intro Breakdown]]
- **Build a multi-oscillator tuning chain off ONE reference oscillator, never a daisy-chain.** Tune oscillator 1 to an external pitch reference (zero beating), then tune oscillator 2 against oscillator 1, then oscillator 3 ALSO against oscillator 1 — never oscillator 3 against oscillator 2 — so drift never compounds across the chain. To deliberately detune afterward for color: detune one oscillator and pull its level down in the mix, keeping a clear in-tune primary underneath rather than detuning everything at once. → [[Synth Patching and Editing Method]], [[Filters and Resonance]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[5 Mistakes People Make With Synths]]
- **Skip the VCA entirely — patch the filter straight to the mixer and let the filter envelope alone shape amplitude.** On a modular/semi-modular synth (demonstrated on an ARP 2600), the "funky" layer of MJ's Billie Jean bass patches filter output directly to the mixer output with no VCA stage in the chain at all; the note is triggered purely via keyboard control-voltage gate + trigger routed straight into the oscillator and filter, so decay/sustain/release all come from the filter envelope rather than a separate amplitude envelope. A useful non-default patching option whenever a filter's own envelope shape (rather than a clean, filter-independent amplitude curve) is exactly the character wanted. → [[Layering and Stacking]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[MJ's Billie Jean Bass - It's 4 Instruments!]]

- **Voice a harmony interval at LOWER volume than the lead — orchestration-style doubling logic applied inside a synth patch — and use "Wyoming tuning" (asymmetric sharp/flat spread around true pitch) rather than single-direction detune when deliberately adding beating/dissonance.** From a 3-oscillator ARP 2600 recreation of Roy Ayers' "Everybody Loves the Sunshine": oscillator 1 (sawtooth) carries the lead; oscillator 2 (pulse, tuned a fourth below) mixed audibly quieter, the same logic as doubling a lead line with extra strings/brass rather than balancing voices equally. A third, high-octave oscillator is tuned deliberately sharp and mixed low for subtle beating/dissonance ("almost like a jazz element") — framed as a deliberate exception to the usual advice of keeping oscillators in tune, since synths drift out on their own without help. → [[Filters and Resonance]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[Cosmic Synth Slide - Everybody Loves the Sunshine by Roy Ayers]]
- **Additive/wavetable octave-stacking as an alternative to subtractive patching — the Thriller opening's Synclavier organ layer.** Three separate voices, each built from stacked sine waves at the fundamental plus up to four octaves above it (drawbar-organ logic, but every "drawbar" is a pure octave rather than mixing in thirds/fifths), with small amounts of FM layered on top for brightness/buzz and one voice detuned ~8 cents (438 Hz vs. 440 Hz) for width. Demonstrates that a big, "powerful" layered sound doesn't require any single layer to be big alone — this organ patch sits under a Prophet-5, a Jupiter-8, Fender Rhodes, and live brass, each contributing a modest slice. → [[Vintage Gear Worklist]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[How I Programmed The Synth Opening On Michael Jackson's Thriller The Synclavier Pt.3]]
- **The OB-X/OB-Xa "1999" riff patch: an ear-first diagnosis method plus the full two-oscillator recipe.** Listen in this order before touching a knob — part/pitch relationship (here: octave doubling), the *type* of "wiggle" (vibrato = pitch modulation vs. pulse-width modulation, tell them apart by whether the movement sounds sine-smooth vs. sawtooth-jagged), filter character, then envelope shape on filter and amp separately. The patch itself: two oscillators an octave apart, both at full volume, both starting on a pulse wave (not sawtooth — only a pulse wave's width can be modulated) so both PWM and vibrato (sine-shaped, medium rate) can drive them **together, in sync** — the key choice that makes this a tight lead rather than a string patch, where the same two modulation types are instead sent to only one oscillator (or the oscillators are detuned) specifically to produce chorusing/beating. → [[LFOs and Modulation]], [[Filters and Resonance]], [[Sound Selection Guide]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[How To Program The Synth Riff From Prince's 1999]]
- **Single-trigger (legato, retrigger-off) vs. multi-trigger (retrigger-on) is a phrasing choice, not just a technical setting — and pair it with a deliberate long-note/short-note pattern for bass personality.** Confirms this page's own Polyphony-modes distinction from the hardware side: in single-trigger/legato, playing a new note without releasing the first *doesn't* re-attack the envelope — the line glides/connects; in multi-trigger, every note re-attacks even under a legato phrase. Some hardware (Minimoog) is hard-wired single-trigger with no option to change it — know your instrument's default before assuming you can pick. Separately: classic synth-bass parts (the Thriller bassline cited as the worked example) read as musical specifically because they mix **long held notes and short/muted ones**, not a uniform gate length — an all-long or all-short bass reads flat/mechanical by comparison; check MIDI gate lengths aren't defaulting to one uniform value ("machine-gunning") when programming rather than playing live. → [[Bassline Writing]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[10 Tips For Synth Bass]]
- **Retune multi-oscillator detune deliberately rather than by drift — and when you DO want a wide unison texture on a low part, weight the voices unevenly (an acoustic-piano "Wyoming" stretch-tuning move), not centered/symmetric.** Baseline discipline: retune oscillators before every session — analog oscillators drift, and an out-of-tune bass reads as "wrong" even when everything else is right. When intentionally running 3 detuned voices for width (rather than 1 clean voice), don't spread them symmetric-and-equal around the center — favor one voice as the true pitch anchor (louder/centered) with the other two detuned slightly below and above it, mirroring how an acoustic piano's tripled unison strings get tuned (one true, one flat, one sharp) for a "fat" sound that still reads as one pitch rather than a chorus-y smear. Ties into this page's own mono/detune-below-150-Hz warning — this is the deliberate, controlled version of that risk, not a contradiction of it. → [[Bass Design]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[10 Tips For Synth Bass]]
- **Two-oscillator bass phase cancellation ("some notes go thin, some go fat" as you play up the keyboard) is fixed by favoring one oscillator's level over the other, not by re-tuning.** When two oscillators sum on a bass patch, their relative phase varies by note/frequency, so some notes reinforce (fat) and others partially cancel (thin) — an inconsistency that reads as an uneven, unreliable bass part. The fix isn't perfect tuning (they're already in tune) — it's an intentional level imbalance: raise one oscillator's volume over the other (e.g. keep osc 1 dominant, osc 2 as a lower-level octave-down layer) so the "thin" notes never fully cancel, since the dominant oscillator's own energy carries through regardless of the phase relationship. Directly useful whenever a 2-oscillator bass patch, real or [[Voice Architecture|unison]]-based, sounds inconsistent note-to-note. → [[Bass Design]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[10 Tips For Synth Bass]]
- **Why voice-level randomization sounds "alive" — a repair-tech's framing, not just a design choice.** A vintage-synth restorer's take, contrasting a modern synth's factory filter-calibration routine (which tunes every voice/note range to be perfectly identical) against true analog voice variance (each voice slightly different — "it might be just slightly darker on one voice in the bottom than on the top"): the imperfection IS the character, not a flaw to eliminate. Direct theoretical backing for this section's [[Drift]]/per-voice-randomization guidance above — deliberately reintroducing that variance on a perfectly-calibrated digital instrument is what [[Drift]]'s drift control and Serum/Wavetable's random-per-note routing are for. A useful gut-check when a patch sounds "too perfect": that's the digital-precision problem this section exists to fix, not a setting to leave alone. → [[Drift]] — Rob Rosen, [[Rob Rosen Reveals His Ultimate Synth Collection]]
- **A hardware joystick's two axes independently driving two different parameters at once — a compact, physically-performable multi-parameter mod source — plus a non-obvious fact: a ring modulator is a VCA when DC-coupled.** Worked on a modular rig missing velocity/aftertouch entirely: the X axis sends control voltage straight to the filter cutoff (brightness), the Y axis sends voltage into a ring modulator being used AS a VCA (an uncommon but functional use — ring mods are VCAs once DC-coupled) gating a low-frequency sine oscillator, producing vibrato depth. Net result: moving the joystick right adds brightness with no vibrato, moving it up adds vibrato with no brightness, and diagonal movement blends both — full expressive control on an instrument with no velocity/aftertouch to draw on. **"Bite" on the VCA specifically comes from the envelope's exponential input plus a touch of decay time** — a linear input or zero decay reads audibly flatter/duller; a short attack/release on the AR envelope avoids pops at the start/end of the gate. **Ableton translation**: a physical XY controller isn't required to get the same idea — map two [[Expression Control]] instances to the same MIDI CC/incoming controller axis, one targeting filter cutoff and the other targeting an LFO's amount routed to pitch, to reproduce a two-axis expressive mod source without hardware. → [[Expression Control]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[My Favorite Eurorack Module]]
- **"Variance" — randomize panning and ADSR shape per note played, not just pitch/velocity, as a built-in humanizer.** "It gives sounds subtle changes with each note that's pressed… panning adds width, and ADSR changes [the shape]" — a purpose-built plugin feature that re-rolls both the stereo position and the envelope shape on every note-on, so a held chord or a repeated phrase never sounds mechanically identical twice. Reusable on owned synths: map a Random-source [[Expression Control]] (Remote mode, small range) to Pan and to Attack/Decay simultaneously per voice — see the per-voice randomization pattern already used for the [[Randomized Rack Design|"Sketch Kit"]]'s per-sample micro-variation macros. → [[Randomized Rack Design]] — loner, [I Created The 'Perfect' Plugin For Producers](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kI2f9JNImGE)
