# Blues Scales and Blue Notes

**The melodic half of the blues** — what to play, and why notes that should clash don't.

Companion to [[Blues Form and Harmony]], which covers the form and the chords.

## Blue notes

**The ♭3, ♭5 and ♭7, played against a major or dominant background.**

That's the definition (BLU-05, where you answered "tritone" — the ♭5 is *part* of a tritone with the root, but blue notes are the three flattened degrees, not an interval).

They're "blue" because they sit *against* the harmony rather than inside it. The ♭3 rubs against the chord's major 3rd, the ♭7 against the major 7th you'd expect in a major key, the ♭5 against nothing at all — it's pure passing colour.

## The blues scale

**Minor pentatonic plus the ♭5.**

In A: **A C D E♭ E G** — six notes.

Built from A minor pentatonic (A C D E G) with the E♭ added as a chromatic passing note between the 4th and 5th.

**The ♭5 is a passing tone, not a resting note.** Sit on it and it sounds wrong; pass through it between D and E and it's the most characteristic sound in the vocabulary.

## Why the ♭3 isn't just wrong

The question the whole strand hinges on (BLU-14).

The blues scale has a **♭3** (C in A). The I7 chord has a **major 3rd** (C♯ in A7). Played together those are a semitone apart — in functional harmony, an error.

**Three reasons it isn't:**

1. **The friction is the sound.** The grind between ♭3 and ♮3 *is* what "bluesy" means. Remove it and you have a major-key melody over dominant chords, which is something else entirely.
2. **Blues isn't functional major/minor.** As [[Blues Form and Harmony]] covers, the dominant 7th is the home sound here, not a tension. The scale is imposed over the chords rather than derived from them, so "belonging to the key" isn't the test.
3. **The note is a region, not a pitch.** Players bend the ♭3 *up* toward the natural 3rd and sit between them. On a guitar that's a quarter-tone bend; on a voice it's just where the note lands. What's notated as a ♭3 is played as somewhere between the two.

That third point is the one that resolves the apparent contradiction. **The blues has microtonality built in**, and notation can only approximate it — which is also why tuning software struggles with blues vocals (see [[Vocal Tuning]]).

## What to play over the I7

**Mixolydian, the blues scale, or a blend — usually the blend.**

- **Mixolydian** (major with a ♭7) matches the chord exactly. It has the major 3rd, so it sounds "correct" and sweet.
- **The blues scale** has the ♭3 and the ♭5, so it grinds.
- **The blend** is what most players actually do: blues scale for the grit, Mixolydian's major 3rd and 6th for the sweetness, moving between them.

**Why minor pentatonic works over dominants at all** (SCL-14, also missed): besides the blue-note friction, it **avoids the natural 4th** — which genuinely clashes with the chord's major 3rd and is the one note that sounds like a mistake rather than a choice.

That's the underrated half. The minor pentatonic isn't "wrong notes that happen to work" — it's a set that keeps the productive friction and removes the destructive one.

## Kansas City blues scale

A named, guitaristic **five-note distillation of the blues scale**, sourced from a Nathan Borton breakdown of Grant Green's playing — captured here as a distinct, named blues-vocabulary variant even though the exact interval formula wasn't given in the free source material (gated to the creator's paid lesson).

- **Five notes, not six** — trims the six-note blues scale (minor pentatonic + ♭5) down further, described as distilling "the sound of the blues scale."
- **Emphasizes the 6th degree** specifically — called out as the note the scale "really focuses on," distinguishing it from a plain minor-pentatonic-plus-♭5 reading.
- **Movable, guitaristic pattern** — designed to be repositioned around the fretboard rather than tied to one box shape, used for riffing and generating melodic ideas rather than outlining a fixed lick.
- **Flag:** exact scale-degree spelling not confirmed from the source captions — treat as a named device to revisit if a fuller breakdown surfaces, not a verified formula. → [[🎵 GRANT GREEN - Cool Blues]]

## Practical

- **One scale over all three chords works.** A blues scale in A covers A7, D7 and E7. That's unusual and it's why blues is a good first improvising vocabulary.
- **Following the changes sounds different.** Switching scale with each chord (A Mixolydian over A7, D Mixolydian over D7) is more "correct" and less bluesy. Both are valid; know which you're doing.
- **Target the chord tones on the strong beats**, pass through the blue notes. The same principle as [[Non-Harmonic Tones]] — the colour notes live between the structural ones.
- **The ♭7 is in the chord**, so it's not really a blue note against a dominant — it only reads as blue against a major context.
