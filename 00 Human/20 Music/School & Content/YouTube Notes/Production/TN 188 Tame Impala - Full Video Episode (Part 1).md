## Synopsis

John (Tape Notes) interviews Kevin Parker (Tame Impala) about writing, recording, and producing *Deadbeat* (Oct 2025). Part 1 covers two tracks in stems-level detail: "My Old Ways" (a piano-and-drums loop track built around a narrow, deliberately-repeated drum sonic signature) and "Loser" (largely tracked live to tape at Diamond Mine Recording Co. in NYC with engineer Lauren, one-man-band style, almost no comping). Parker talks at length about his demo process (voice memos are literally the demo — no separate demoing stage), a deliberate palette-narrowing philosophy across the whole album (one dominant drum sound, one dominant synth), his vocal chain and doubling strategy, and closes with reflections on lyric-writing discipline vs. performed casualness, and general advice ("think less and do more").

## Techniques

**Demo / songwriting workflow**
- **The voice memo IS the demo — no separate demoing stage.** Parker doesn't build demos in a DAW first; he sings/plays an idea directly into his phone (before that, a handheld dictaphone) the moment it occurs, specifically to avoid losing the idea while learning to play it: "as soon as I start singing it, it starts affecting the memory of it." The phone recording is the reference he later re-records properly from.
- **Re-record the demo take in the same physical space**, not just the same performance — for "My Old Ways" he went back to the exact living room (with the piano) where the original voice-memo idea was captured, specifically to preserve the room's character, then upgraded only the recording method (mono phone → stereo field recorder) rather than the performance.
- **Reference-track calibration in-session.** Keeps a reference track (e.g. "Levitating" by Dua Lipa) at the tail end of the Ableton session and plays it periodically while working, specifically to "get my mind back into the real world" — a deliberate check against losing perspective on what's actually a well-balanced, listenable mix while deep in a solo session.
- **"Think less and do more."** States his most creatively fruitful periods are when he isn't second-guessing ideas before trying them — write more songs rather than polish/deliberate over fewer. Explicitly: don't pre-judge a melody as bad before recording it; the judgment is frequently wrong in both directions.
- **Album-level sonic-personality strategy via deliberate constraint.** States he wanted *Deadbeat* to be recognizable "a block away" the way the first Strokes album is — achieved not through a new sound per song but by picking a small, consistent sonic palette (one dominant drum signature, one dominant synth — see below) and reusing it across the album, as a deliberate reaction against his prior album (*The Slow Rush*)'s more varied drum-sound approach.

**Piano recording ("My Old Ways")**
- Re-recorded the piano with a **stereo field recorder using an XY stereo mic pair**, placed close to the piano in the room (not spot-miked/close-miked for isolation) — deliberately chasing a "roomy, standing-next-to-the-piano" sound rather than a pristine, isolated piano tone.

**Drum sound design — "the Deadbeat sound"**
- **Signature layered drum sound used across most of the album:** a Roland TR-808 kick/drum sample **run through a guitar amp** (for distortion/grit), layered/synced with a **Vermona DRM1** analog drum synth. The two are synced together and "blasted in the studio," recorded in multiple passes/ways, then layered — the combination (not either alone) is the recurring signature texture. *(Vermona DRM1 not yet in [[Vintage Gear Index]] — added to worklist.)*
- **Cabasa on the off-beat as a techno-derived rhythmic detail** — cites it as a recurring element in techno he loves, used here even though the track (110 BPM) is slower than typical techno tempo; framed as importing a specific rhythmic signature from another genre rather than a tempo-matched borrow.
- **Personality-through-micro-layering:** describes building electronic drum-beat texture and "evolution" (so a repetitive beat doesn't feel static) by layering many small, quiet auxiliary hits/blips ("tiny small little blips in the right places") rather than macro arrangement changes — explicitly framed as the way to make a repetitive electronic pattern feel like it's "going somewhere" without a human drummer's natural fill-based variation.
- **Deliberately "unlearning" acoustic-drummer instincts when programming electronic beats** — Parker is a trained drummer and says the habit of "the hi-hat always plays," specifically constant 8th-note hi-hats, is a rule to actively break when programming; electronic beats don't need to obey acoustic-kit conventions.

**Arrangement / density discipline**
- **Deliberate minimalism as a production choice, not a limitation.** States it would be "easy" for him to layer dense synth parts quickly, but chose restraint on this album so the track reads as just drums + piano riff (with synths present but subordinate) — ties this explicitly to a "brushstrokes" aesthetic (cites Frank Ocean, Serge Gainsbourg) where the idea is felt rather than over-polished/over-thought.

**Vocal recording & chain**
- **Mic choice driven by performability, not just tone.** Tried a U47 clone (described as sounding "incredible") but couldn't hold it while performing, which mattered more than he expected — settled back on a **Shure SM7(B)**, held in hand while singing, specifically because being able to physically hold and move with the mic changes the performance.
- **Tracking compressor: a dbx 165** (vintage analog compressor, used on drums on his first two albums, now also used while tracking vocals) — recorded *through* it live, not just applied after. *(dbx 165 not yet in [[Vintage Gear Index]] — added to worklist.)*
- **Post-tracking vocal processing kept deliberately plain** — "just compression and EQ, and the compression is quite stock standard"; explicitly resists over-processing the vocal itself, keeping the character work in the separate effects bus (below) instead.
- **Vocal doubling as a per-song creative decision, not a fixed default.** He "usually" double-tracks all vocals, but on "My Old Ways" deliberately kept the **main hook single/undoubled** so it would read like a sampled vocal "plucked from an old record"; the verses ARE double-tracked as usual.
- **Hard-panned "whisper" vocal layer for width/texture** — a distinct technique from doubling: records an additional whispery pass of the vocal, pans it hard left and hard right (so 4 total vocal elements on "My Old Ways": 2 center doubles + 2 hard-panned whisper layers), and heavily filters it (very little bass or treble — mostly midrange only) plus heavy compression, aiming for a subtle "ASMR-y," barely-audible textural width element rather than an audible harmony layer.
- **Effects run as a separate bus/stage**, not per-track inserts, for the vocal reverb/delay treatment (see Effects below).

**Vocal effects**
- Started using **Soundtoys EchoBoy** heavily on this album (prior albums *Currents*/*The Slow Rush* mostly used stock Ableton delay/echo) — cites EchoBoy's distinct stereo image as the reason for the switch, without being able to name the exact mechanism ("it's just the way you want your vocal to sound").
- **Valhalla Room** plus Ableton's stock **convolution "Spring" reverb device** used on most tracks for vocal space — deliberately kept vocal treatment "not too experimental," aiming for clarity over heavy processing.
- **Soundtoys Decapitator** used on vocals on some songs (named example: "Dracula") purely "for some grit" — a light-touch saturation pass, not a defining effect.

**Tracking to tape ("Loser")**
- Tracked at **Diamond Mine Recording Co. (NYC)** with engineer Lauren, largely live/one-take, recording direct to **tape**, with almost no editing/comping afterward — Parker calls this "almost completely… as we recorded it," rare for his usual heavily-multitracked-alone process.
- **Deliberately used the "record drums to nothing, then overdub on top" method** (his phrase: "the Stevie Wonder thing") instead of building around a click/loop — records the full drum performance first with no other material playing, then layers the rest of the song on top of that take, song-length, non-looped.
- **Tape's non-punch-in constraint as a deliberate creative pressure ("red light fever")** — because a mistake mid-song on tape can't be quietly punched in and fixed, Parker frames the resulting performance tension/anxiety as audibly present in the take and as a contributor to the song's character, not just a limitation to route around; explicitly keeps small performance imperfections (e.g. audible stick clicks) rather than editing them out.
- Notes the closing-track ambience/room tone of tape-to-tape studio takes (people talking at the end of a take) as incidental texture he likes.

**Synth choice**
- **Sequential Prophet-5** used for roughly 70% of the synth sounds across the whole album (named specifically for "Dracula" and generally across *Deadbeat*) — a deliberate single-synth-palette choice ("only a handful of synths… that one just got, it was the one next to me the whole time"), reinforcing the album's overall narrow-palette strategy.
- **Roland SH-1** (a simple single-oscillator mono synth — distinct from the more common SH-101) used for a solo part on "My Old Ways," chosen specifically for its "primitive" character; Parker frames it as an intentional callback to 60s/analog-organ-style noodling solos (cites The Doors' "Light My Fire" as the reference feel) largely absent from modern, tightly-sequenced electronic music. *(Roland SH-1 — the 1978 monosynth, not the SH-101 — not yet in [[Vintage Gear Index]]; added to worklist.)*
