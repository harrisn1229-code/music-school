# How To Solo Over Grant's Tune! (Grant Green)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=c345PMX-p2o)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=c345PMX-p2o)

## Synopsis

Nathan Borton breaks down how to solo over "Grant's Tune," a Grant Green original off the album *Solid*, picked by audience poll (tied with a promised follow-up blues video). The whole lesson is a reduction exercise, same method as his "Bolivia" breakdown: the tune *looks* tricky at a glance but collapses to a small number of already-known sound-buckets once the harmony is renamed correctly. The A section's two-chord vamp (Cm7–D♭7) is really just a **1-5-1 in disguise** — D♭7 is the tritone sub of G7 — so every option for soloing over a plain minor-to-its-dominant applies directly, and he lays out four parallel systems (C blues scale, C Dorian + A♭ melodic minor, C "minor diminished," or just parking on G7♭9 the whole time) rather than picking one as correct. The A-section tag (E♭ major) and the B section (a set of slash chords over an E♭ pedal that turn out to be ordinary E♭ major/minor7/major/sus9 shapes once fingered with E♭ in the bass) get the same treatment — play them modally chord-by-chord, or blend E♭ blues with a major/minor 3rd swap keyed to each chord's quality. He closes by naming the tune's real lesson: the ability to sit on one sound over a long static stretch (the vamp) AND the ability to move one sound fluently across many different chords (the B section) are two different skills, and this tune is built to drill both in the same eight bars. Short (8:27), spoken clearly, captions clean/high-confidence — the only garble worth flagging is the ASR mishearing "tritone sub" as "trionee sub," corrected here and in the transcript, and a couple of spots where the spoken example licks aren't transcribable from captions alone (no words, just playing).

## What's taught

- **The A-section vamp is a 1-5-1 wearing a disguise.** Cm7 → D♭7, repeated, reads at a glance like two unrelated minor/dominant chords a half step apart — but D♭7 is the **tritone substitute for G7** (§2 tritone sub), so the whole vamp is functionally **Cm7 → G7 → Cm7**, i.e. a minor 1-5-1 looped. Naming it this way is what unlocks every option below — none of them would be obvious moves over "Cm7 to D♭7" read literally. → [[Chord Substitution]] § *2. Tritone substitution*
- **Four parallel soloing systems over the vamp, presented as interchangeable options, not a ranked list — pick one and stay consistent with its partner sound on the dominant:**
  1. **C blues scale over the minor, then hit E♭, F, or G specifically when the dominant lands** — those three notes are chord tones/color tones of the D♭7/G7 dominant that also live inside the C blues scale (C E♭ F G♭/F♯ G B♭), so the blues-scale line doesn't have to change shape, just needs to resolve through those three notes as the harmony turns over. → [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]]
  2. **C Dorian over the minor (natural 6, ♭7) paired with A♭ melodic minor over the dominant** — A♭ melodic minor is the standard altered-scale derivation for G7 (melodic minor a half step above the dominant root = the altered scale), so this system reduces the whole vamp to exactly **two key-center sounds**, C minor and A♭ minor, rather than four separate scales. → [[Diatonic Modes]], [[Altered Chords]], [[Scale Modes Reference]] § Melodic Minor
  3. **C "minor diminished" (the whole-half diminished scale built off C) playable over BOTH chords** — unusual, since most dim-scale/chord matchups are chord-specific; the caveat given is that the scale's **A natural and D natural sound a little weird against the D♭7/G7**, so it's not a clean fit everywhere. **Practical fix: start the scale on E♭ or A and walk straight up** — both entry points work over either chord, making it a good **transition device between the two chords** or extra "scalier language" dropped into an otherwise blues/Dorian line rather than a scale to run start-to-finish. → [[Diminished Chords]]
  4. **Ignore the Cm7 entirely and just play G7♭9 the whole vamp** — the simplest option: treat the two-chord loop as one static dominant sound and solo it as a straight-ahead altered/♭9 dominant, matching the "reduce to one sound over a long stretch" skill the video closes on. → [[Altered Chords]]
- **5-to-1 motifs to open, close, or connect lines across the vamp** — stock guide-tone/target-note phrases built around the dominant-to-tonic motion (see [[Chord Substitution]] § *From videos* — the "2 equals 5, 5 equals 2" Grant Green precedent — and [[Guide Tones]] for the general mechanism); used here specifically as connective tissue between the D♭7/G7 and the return to Cm7 rather than as a whole-phrase device. → [[Turnarounds]]
- **A-section tag: a plain E♭ major resolution, played as tonic language.** At the end of each A section Grant lands on E♭ major — the advice is simply to play E♭ major tonic vocabulary or enclosed chord tones of E♭maj7, no substitution or reharmonization involved. → [[Guide Tones]]
- **B section: four slash chords over a held E♭ bass that resolve to ordinary shapes once fingered correctly.** Written as E♭ | G♭/E♭ | E♭ | D♭/E♭, but voicing the upper triads with E♭ in the bass reveals familiar guitar shapes underneath — the chords are actually **E♭ major → E♭ minor7 → E♭ major → E♭sus9**, all sharing the same root. Two ways to solo it: **(a) modally** — treat each chord as its own brief key center and play the matching sound over each (major over the maj chords, minor over the m7); or **(b) blend E♭ blues throughout, swapping only the 3rd** — flat 3rd over the E♭m7 spot, major 3rd everywhere else — keeping the rest of the blues line unchanged. Stated payoff: **combine both approaches** rather than picking one, since fluency over these changes comes from moving between the modal and blues readings depending on the phrase. → [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]], [[Modal Jazz]]
- **Closing generalization — the tune's actual two-skill payoff.** "Grant's Tune" is built to drill **(1) sitting on one sound over an extended static stretch** (the A-section vamp, once renamed as a 1-5-1) and **(2) moving one sound fluently across several different chords** (the B section's four E♭-rooted qualities) — described as two genuinely different soloing skills, not the same muscle. Stated practice range: you can get through the whole tune on nothing but the blues, or hit every single chord change precisely — "as easy or as hard as you want to."

## Steal this

- **Rename a two-chord vamp before you try to solo it.** "Minor chord to an unrelated-looking dominant a half step away" is scary; "1-5-1 with a tritone sub" is a shape you already have vocabulary for — the rename is the whole unlock.
- **When a scale isn't a clean fit for both chords in a two-chord loop, use it as a transition device instead of a full-time solution** — the C minor-diminished walk-up from E♭ or A works specifically *between* the chords, not as the main vocabulary for either one.
- **Reduce a two-chord vamp to one sound (G7♭9 straight through) as the "easy mode" fallback**, then layer in the chord-specific systems once that's comfortable.
- **On a set of slash chords over one held bass note, finger the upper triads with the bass note underneath before assuming the progression is exotic** — it may just be a familiar chord quality wearing an unfamiliar slash-chord spelling.
