# One-Box I-IV-V Triads Inside a Pentatonic Scale

Part of [[Guitar Positions]]. **Opposite move from [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]]:** that page takes **one** triad and relocates it to three different neck zones; this page takes **one fixed four-fret zone** (a single pentatonic box) and finds **three different triads** — the I, IV and V chords of the key — all sitting inside it without moving the hand out of position. The pitch of the source video is explicitly that "a skilled guitar player is someone who could take the span of four frets and play everything they'd ever need" — this is the mechanism for that claim over a diatonic major-key progression, the major-key counterpart to [[Diagonal Pentatonic Blues Soloing]]'s one-shape-plus-one-note-edit system for a dominant-7 blues.

## Setup: one pentatonic box, one I-IV-V loop
Worked in the key of **C major**, using **C major pentatonic** (identical pitch content to A minor pentatonic, same shape) fingered in the box starting at **fret 8 on the low E string** (the "second box" shape, not the open-position box). The progression is the key's own **I-IV-V** — C, F, G — looped as a backing vamp. *(General starting point, not stated as a formula in the source but implied by the method: this generalizes to any major key — find that key's I chord on the low E or A string, box the pentatonic scale around it, and the IV and V triads will fall inside the same box the same way, since I-IV-V in any major key shares this same pentatonic-box relationship.)*

## Finding each triad without leaving the box
- **C (I) triad:** built from the C major pentatonic box itself, landing on **fret 8, fret 7, fret 5** (root-3rd-5th, exact string assignments lost to a garbled stretch of the auto-transcript — re-derive by ear/against the source video if the precise strings matter). ✅ *(garbled captions — frets confirmed, strings not confirmed)*
- **F (IV) triad:** the **identical fret-8/fret-7/fret-5 shape**, relocated only across the strings — "the exact same thing, starting on the A string on the F note." Because the IV chord's triad shares the same physical shape as the I chord's, one shape covers both; the hand doesn't have to learn two shapes, only two starting strings.
- **G (V) triad:** also findable inside the same four frets, but the **naive fingering is not voice-led** — grabbing G's triad in the box the same casual way as C and F works technically ("for proof, inside of these four frets, I have G") but produces awkward voice leading between chords. **A second, voice-led fingering for G exists in the same box** and is the one actually used in the C-F-G loop — the video doesn't tab it precisely, but flags voice leading as the deciding factor for *which* in-box G shape to grab, not whether one exists.

## Practice step 1 — just touch the triads on the loop
Before soloing, drill **finding the root note and grabbing the triad** while the C-F-G loop plays, purely as a targeting exercise: while C is sounding, put fingers on the C triad; when the chord changes to F, move to the F triad shape (same shape, different string); on G, the triad (voice-led shape). The goal at this stage isn't music, it's proving the hand can find and land on the correct triad, in-box, on every chord change without hesitation — the prerequisite for the soloing move below.

## Practice step 2 — triad up, pentatonic scale down (per chord)
Once the triads are locked in, layer them onto the pentatonic scale: **on each chord change, start the phrase by arpeggiating that chord's triad (ascending), then continue by coming back down through the pentatonic scale** — not switching to a different pentatonic scale per chord, but always the **one key-level C major pentatonic scale** the whole way through, regardless of which chord (C, F, or G) is currently sounding. Explicitly corrected in the video: over F, you go up the **F triad** but still come down the **C pentatonic scale**, "because that's the scale for the key" — not an F pentatonic scale. This is the throughline that makes the technique work: **one scale for the whole progression, a different triad grabbed inside it as each chord passes**, matching [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]'s Step 1 principle of treating a fast tonic-centered progression as one scale with chord-tone accents layered on top, here demonstrated specifically as *triad-arpeggio-then-scale-descent* rather than free targeting.
- **Variation:** reverse the order — pentatonic scale first, then come down through the triad — as an alternate phrase shape once the basic up-triad/down-scale move is comfortable.

## The actual hard part: making the seam disappear
Named directly as the difficult skill in the lesson: a plain triad-then-pentatonic phrase "sounds a little robotic," like two separate ideas stapled together rather than one line. **The fix is phrasing, not note choice** — the notes (triad + scale) don't change; how you connect them does:
- **Slide into the triad instead of picking it straight.** Demonstrated concretely: playing the same three triad notes but **sliding into the first one** rather than attacking each note cleanly reads as smoother/more connected, immediately less "robotic" than the plain picked arpeggio version.
- **General instinct to carry forward:** deliberately listen for "what sounds good after the triad and what doesn't" — the seam between triad and scale is a phrasing decision to audition by ear, the same "judge it by playing it back" instinct as the connective-phrasing work on [[Open Voice Triads for Soloing]] and [[Connecting Licks Into Longer Lines]], applied here specifically to the triad→scale handoff rather than lick-to-lick transitions.

## Why this differs from the vault's other in-box triad-soloing device
[[Diagonal Pentatonic Blues Soloing]] also keeps one continuous pentatonic shape running across an I-IV-V progression and edits only what has to change — but that page's I-IV-V is a **blues** progression (all three chords dominant 7ths), tracked with a **single altered note** (swap the 3rd for the ♭7) plus a **whole-step slide** for the V chord, no triads named. This page's I-IV-V is a **diatonic major-key** progression (C major, F major, G major — all plain major triads, no dominant 7ths), and instead of editing single notes, it explicitly **names and grabs a full triad shape** per chord, always inside the same fixed box rather than sliding the whole shape up the neck for the V chord. Two different "stay in one zone across a I-IV-V" systems for two different harmonic contexts (blues dominant-7 vs. diatonic major).
