> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **How a phrase ends — the punctuation of harmony**
> It's the difference between a phrase that stops and one that lands.
>
> **Q2.** **V–I — maximum resolution**
> The dominant carries the most tension available in the key and the tonic answers all of it, which is why it's the default ending of most Western phrases.
>
> **Q3.** **IV–I — it settles because IV has no leading tone**
> Nothing in IV pulls up by a semitone into the tonic, so the arrival happens without tension being discharged. It relaxes into home rather than being pulled there.
>
> **Q4.** **V–vi — believable because vi shares two of its three notes with I**
> The ear is set up for I, gets a chord that's mostly I, and accepts the near-miss. Swerving to something with nothing in common would just sound wrong.
>
> **Q5.** **…–V — it ends *on* the dominant instead of resolving to it**
> The tension is left standing rather than discharged.
>
> **Q6.** **Because it makes an antecedent phrase demand an answer**
> That's what builds 8- and 16-bar sections out of 4-bar ones: the first phrase asks, the second answers.
>
> **Q7.** **B and F**
> *Also accepted:* F and B
> The 3rd and the ♭7 of G7.
>
> **Q8.** **B rises to C and F falls to E — each by a half step**
> Both notes sit a semitone from a tonic chord tone, and both resolve inward to consonance.
>
> **Q9.** **Because the tritone is maximally unstable and both its notes are a semitone from a tonic chord tone**
> *Rubric (5):* names the tritone inside V7 (1) · says it's the most unstable interval, so it demands resolution (2) · says each note resolves by half step into a tonic chord tone (2)
> A tritone is the most unstable interval in the system, so a chord containing one is heard as needing to go somewhere. What makes V–I feel inevitable rather than merely likely is that the resolution is maximally efficient: B is a semitone below C and F is a semitone above E, so both voices move the smallest possible distance and land on chord tones of I. The ear isn't following a convention — it's following the shortest path out of instability. That same fact is what makes tritone substitution work later.
>
> **Q10.** **Gospel and rock — the settling character suits an arrival that isn't dramatic**
> *Also accepted:* the "amen" cadence
> Both idioms want a resolution that feels warm and conclusive rather than tense and discharged.
>
> **Q11.** **Two — in C major, vi is Am, sharing C and E with the C major triad**
> Only the outer note differs, which is why the substitution reads as a shade rather than a change of direction.
>
> **Q12.** **Authentic lands · plagal settles · deceptive surprises · half asks, and the half demands more**
> *Rubric (5):* characterises all four endings distinctly (2) · identifies the half cadence as the one requiring a continuation (2) · notes that the deceptive also creates continuation pressure, but by a different route (1)
> The authentic cadence closes the door: the phrase is complete and could end the song. The plagal arrives without tension, so it's conclusive but soft. The deceptive sets up an authentic ending and lands elsewhere, so the phrase feels both surprising and unfinished. The half cadence is the one that structurally demands an answer — it stops on unresolved tension, so the ear treats the phrase as the first half of something. Deceptive creates pressure too, but through broken expectation rather than through leaving the tension standing.
