# The Simple Soloing Concept All the Pros Know (But Nobody Teaches)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vda_HUsfG2I)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vda_HUsfG2I)

## Synopsis

A jazz-guitar lesson from Nathan Borton built around a single concept — **digital patterns** — pulled
from Jerry Coker's book *Elements of Jazz Improvisation* (tracing back to David Baker, taught to Borton
by Steve Kabulk). The pitch is deliberately narrow: solos that sound "noodly and unfocused" usually
aren't missing licks, they're missing the connective material *between* licks, and a digital pattern —
a short melodic phrase expressed as scale-degree numbers rather than absolute pitches — is offered as
that connective tissue. Because the pattern is stored numerically, the same shape transfers instantly
across scales (major → Dorian, just by changing the underlying scale under the same numbers) and across
keys, which is what makes it useful for three distinct things: generating new melodic ideas from
scratch, bridging into and out of memorized language (worked over a Honeysuckle Rose phrase inside an
F7-based 2-5-1), and — the most valuable part of the lesson — reaching more advanced sounds (upper-
structure minor/major, altered-dominant scales, tritone substitution) through the same simple numeric
shapes rather than memorizing each substitution as a separate vocabulary. Closes with a Coltrane/Giant
Steps namecheck (fast-key-center navigation via the same portable cells) and a four-phase practice
progression (foundation → connection → integration → substitution). Short, single-topic, and
example-driven rather than exhaustive — three named patterns (1-2-3-5, 1-2-3-4-5-3-2-1,
1-2-3-4-5-7-6-5) stand in for what Coker's book documents at much greater length.

## What's taught

- **Definition.** Number every note of a scale 1–8 (C major: C=1 … C=8). A digital pattern is a short
  (4- or 8-note) phrase written and memorized as that numeric sequence rather than as fixed pitches —
  e.g. **1-2-3-5**, playable forward or backward.
- **Transferability is the whole point.** The identical numeric shape moves to any scale (apply it to
  C Dorian instead of C major and the pattern becomes minor automatically, no new fingering needed) and
  to any key — described as "musical Legos" that stack over any chord or reproduce the sound of any
  scale on their own.
- **A second named pattern, eight notes:** 1-2-3-4-5-3-2-1 (ascend then descend).
- **Use 1 — generate new ideas from scratch.** Play a digital pattern directly as a melodic idea over
  its matching chord/scale — useful standalone, no other vocabulary required.
- **Use 2 — connect existing licks/language.** Worked example: a Honeysuckle Rose phrase over F7,
  inside a 2-5-1 (Gm7–C7–F). Play **1-2-3-4-5-7-6-5** over the ii chord to bridge melodically into the
  F7 language that follows ("the perfect melodic bridge"). Reversed order also works: play the F7
  language first, then a digital pattern to exit — the worked phrase closes with **1-2-3-5** over the I
  chord (B♭maj7).
  - Recommended combination: mix in **enclosures** leading into a digital pattern's first note, and use
    the underlying chord's **arpeggio** as further connective material — the full worked phrase chains
    C-minor arpeggio → digital pattern → language → enclosure into the next pattern's first note (B♭) →
    the eight-note pattern ascending → closing arpeggio.
- **Use 3 — access advanced sounds via chord-scale substitution** (numeric shapes stay the same; only
  the underlying scale changes):
  - **Minor 9 sound over a minor chord:** play a minor chord/scale built off the **5th degree** — over
    Cm, use G minor instead of C minor (G minor's notes = Cm's 5th, ♭7, 9th, 11th). Apply digital
    patterns to the G-minor scale, or alternate C-minor- and G-minor-based patterns in the same phrase.
  - **Same device over a major chord, off the 5th degree:** over B♭maj7, play digital patterns off an
    F-major scale/arpeggio — "we're gonna play major over major."
  - **Altered-dominant sound:** play digital patterns off the **minor scale a half step above the
    dominant root**, specifically that key's **melodic minor**, for the true altered-dominant color —
    for F7, use G♭ minor → G♭ melodic minor (same pitch collection as the F altered scale).
  - **Full worked 2-5-1 pathway** stated directly: over Gm7–F7–B♭maj7, "G minor for the two chord, G♭
    going down a half step for the five chord," resolving into I with the 5th-degree-major idea (an
    F-major-based pattern) from the previous point.
  - **Tritone substitution:** F7's tritone sub is B7 (a dominant chord a tritone/three-whole-steps
    away, temporarily substituting for the original V7). Applied to the digital-pattern pathway, the
    same 2-5-1 simplifies to a three-stop shape: [G] minor → B7 → B♭. *(Caption note: the transcript
    names the first stop "C minor" here, inconsistent with "G minor for the two chord" stated moments
    earlier for the same progression — likely an auto-caption mishearing; flagged rather than guessed.)*
  - **Coltrane/Giant Steps namecheck:** the classic advanced example of digital patterns navigating
    hard, fast-moving changes — because the patterns are portable numeric shapes, the same handful can
    be re-applied across Giant Steps' fast key centers. Also usable within one static sound to link
    bebop ideas together, not just across changing harmony.
- **Four-phase practice progression:**
  1. **Foundation** — play a couple of digital patterns through an entire tune, forward and backward,
     over whatever harmony passes, before combining with anything else.
  2. **Connection** — mix in simple arpeggios, connecting into the patterns drilled in phase 1.
  3. **Integration** — add in language/licks currently being worked on (e.g. the Honeysuckle Rose lick
     from this lesson).
  4. **Substitution** — use digital patterns to outline substitute harmonies (upper-structure
     minor/major, altered-dominant minor, tritone sub) and connect them with vocabulary already
     internalized in phases 1–3.
  - Stated explicitly: work on only a couple of patterns at a time; internalization takes a while.
- **Source material pointer:** Jerry Coker, *Elements of Jazz Improvisation* (linked in the video
  description) is named as the deeper reference — the video demonstrates a small sample of the book's
  full pattern catalog. Borton also mentions a Patreon etude applying these patterns to "All the Things
  You Are."
