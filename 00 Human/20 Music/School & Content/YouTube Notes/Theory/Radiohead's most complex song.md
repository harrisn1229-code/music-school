# Radiohead's most complex song

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s7yDKE_ZvI0)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s7yDKE_ZvI0)

## Synopsis

A full-song analysis of "Sail to the Moon" (Hail to the Thief, 2003), argued as Radiohead's most complex song: roughly 30 time-signature changes in four minutes, a deliberately countdown-structured intro, A major/A minor modal mixture blended with Lydian coloring through the verses, and a chromatic-mediant chord run that dissolves the key entirely in the outro.

## Techniques

- **A deliberately countdown-structured meter intro**: the intro's repeating phrase is a bar of 7/4, a bar of 2/4, then another bar matching the first (7/4 first time). On each repetition the outer bars shrink by one beat — 7/4→2/4→7/4, then 6/4→2/4→6/4, then 5/4→2/4→5/4 — a numeric countdown (7, 6, 5...) that eventually settles into a stable 4/4 as the first verse begins. Thom Yorke's own account: the band had to count through it explicitly in rehearsal ("this one's seven, this one's six, this one's five") specifically so the final performance would sound effortless rather than counted.
- **Modal mixture as a recurring key-color device, not a one-off borrow**: the verses hover between A major and A minor — chords keep resolving to A major even while the surrounding harmony implies A minor — and later add a raised 4th (D♯) suggesting Lydian on top of that major/minor blend, so three modal colors (major, minor, Lydian) are layered rather than a single clean borrowed chord.
- **A refrain that changes its own internal meter each time it repeats**, standing in for a chorus the song otherwise doesn't have: first appearance built from 2/4 + 3/4 + return to 4/4; second appearance instead built from two bars of 3/4 — the refrain's own meter shifts are a second, independent layer of the song's metric instability on top of the verse/intro changes.
- **A 5/4 instrumental interlude reprising the intro's modal mixture**, this time with the Lydian raised 4th made explicit in the guitar part.
- **Beat-splicing inside an otherwise-normal verse**: the final verse restates verse one's material but has random beats spliced out mid-bar, turning several 4/4 bars into one-off 3/4 bars — an irregular, non-repeating instability distinct from the song's other, more patterned meter changes.
- **Chromatic mediants used to dissolve the key at the very end**: on the refrain's final repetition, an E major 7 chord appears where an E minor chord had been all song, followed by an A♭ major 7 — neither expected in the key of A. Together with the C major chord that precedes them, these three chords are chromatic mediants (root relationships by a third, not functioning as a normal progression) — the same device covered on [[Chromatic Mediants and Pop Modulation]]. The song's closing seconds are a string of these unrelated chords, instrumentation peeling away until only a solo piano remains, deliberately floating without a resolved key.
