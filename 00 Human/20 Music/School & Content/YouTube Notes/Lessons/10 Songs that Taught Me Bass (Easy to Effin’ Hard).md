## What's taught

- **Song 1 — a Ray Charles bassline (per the description's "How Ray Charles helped me get started"; exact title not stated, and this vault's two existing Ray Charles library notes — [[🎵 RAY CHARLES - Hard Times]] and [[🎵 RAY CHARLES - You Don't Know Me]] — are both from an unrelated guitar-soloing lesson series, not a match for this beginner-bass context, so no library link is made here).** Josh's dad taught him this at age 13, his first-ever song on bass. Only a few notes, but nailing the right note at the right time was still genuinely hard as a total beginner, and he admits to probably plucking the wrong string half the time. **The lesson: minimal-plucking beginner songs are a legitimate, deliberate starting point, not a compromise** — because the line doesn't demand plucking speed, it frees a brand-new player to focus entirely on basic fretting/plucking coordination without also fighting a speed ceiling. → [[Goal-Tiered Practice and the Self-Taught Bass Commandments]] (Commandment 2 — a real-world case study for "songs early," specifically at the minimal-technique-demand end of the spectrum).

- **Song 2 — a Ramones song (description: "what it took to learn my first Ramones song"; not named — a fast, blazingly-quick three-or-few-note line, consistent with the same Ramones songs the [[10 Songs That Taught Me Punk (Easy to Effin’ Hard)|Punk]] video names — "Blitzkrieg Bop"/"I Want to Be Sedated" — but not confirmed here independently).** Way faster than song 1 — keeping up with the plucks felt "impossibly hard" on first attempt. Josh loved the Ramones enough to take it as a direct challenge rather than skip it: **the fix was pure deliberate practice on alternating plucking** — start really, really slow, then gradually work the tempo up. **Second thread on this same song: it's written for a pick, but Josh learned it entirely fingerstyle** as a beginner, which he frames in hindsight as good for his overall growth even though it wasn't the "right" tone for a punk tune. → [[Goal-Tiered Practice and the Self-Taught Bass Commandments]] (Commandment 3's start-slow-then-speed-up checklist — a second early-career case study alongside the Ramones/Rancid pair the Punk video already contributes) and [[Pick vs. Fingers on Bass — Tone and Technique Tradeoffs]] (a real-world instance of that note's own sequencing advice: get solid on fingerstyle first, even on material written for a pick).

- **Song 3 — an AC/DC song, bassist Cliff Williams (name transcribed correctly by the auto-captions; song title not stated).** Not as fast as song 2, but introduced more complex, offbeat rhythms — good prep for the funkier songs later in the list. **The headline lesson is about restraint, not rhythm:** Williams doesn't come in on bass at all until right before the chorus, which gives the song's arrival an exciting energy boost purely from the bass's absence up to that point. Josh's own summary: **"sometimes being a good bass player means knowing when to shut up."** → [[Slap Bass Dynamics — Contrast, Force, and Restraint]] tool #4 (Marcus Miller's ensemble-restraint principle) — a non-slap, hard-rock worked example of the exact same "the absence itself is the arrangement move" mechanism, new "From videos" bullet alongside song 6 below.

- **Song 4 — "Chameleon" (Herbie Hancock & The Headhunters); bassist Paul Jackson, though Josh flags directly that the line as usually played on bass is actually a synth part in the original recording, not Jackson's bass part — it only becomes "the bassline" in a live band where the keys player isn't covering it.** Learned in a Motown/soul cover band with his mom — his first funk fundamentals. **The technique: a walk-up pattern that starts three frets below the target root and walks up chromatically into it**, instead of just hitting the root cold. Josh calls this pattern a genuine "soap killer" — directly usable across funk, disco, gospel, and slap, not a one-song trick — and points to a dedicated BassBuzz "walk-ups" video (now in this vault, see routing below) for more of the same family. **This is the exact three-fret-below chromatic-walk device [[Jamerson's Root-Fifth-Octave Exercise]] already documents from a different BassBuzz source** — new worked example there, and a second confirmation of that note's own "Beyond Motown — the formula in other genres" cross-genre claim. → [[Jamerson's Root-Fifth-Octave Exercise]], [[Bass Walkup and Walkdown Fills]] (the simpler two-fret sibling device, named directly in this video as "my walk-ups video").

- **Song 5 — likely "I'll Take You There" (The Staple Singers) — inferred from this video's own later cross-reference ("after learning songs like Something and I'll Take You There"), which places it as the other song learned in the same early cover-band period as song 6; not independently confirmed by name in this song's own section, so treated as a strong but unconfirmed match.** Another cover-band-era tune, and Josh calls it one of his favorite soul basslines to this day — the first time he encountered **a classic soul bass-fill shape he names "the major triangle,"** which he says is taught fully in a separate BassBuzz "killer bass fills" video (**not yet in this vault** — logged here as a named pointer only, not independently actionable, since this video only names the shape rather than diagramming it). The tune also has a bass solo that doubled as **great shifting practice** — moving the fretting hand between positions mid-solo. **The concrete, transferable lesson: bass solos/melodic lines benefit from real held/long notes, not reflexive staccato** — Josh names this directly as a common bass-player habit worth correcting ("us bass players tend to play way too staccato sometimes"), and credits the long notes in this solo specifically with sounding more melodic as a result. → [[Connected vs. Disconnected Bass Phrasing]] (a third worked example of that note's connected/held-note-vs-staccato choice, this time framed as a genre-spanning default correction rather than a single-song match-the-source call) and [[Look-Ahead Position Shifting]] (a brief, non-mechanical reinforcement — "great shifting practice" — that a bass solo with real position changes is valuable shifting material in its own right, alongside that note's Seven Nation Army fingering-level detail).

- **Song 6 — "Something" (The Beatles); bassist Paul McCartney, played on a Hofner in the original, faked here on a P-bass for lack of a Hofner (confirmed by name directly in this video's narration).** Josh calls it one of Paul's coolest basslines ever — "which is really saying something," his own pun. **The lesson: how to play with a singer**, named as a skill every bassist needs. McCartney's line is active throughout, but **he's most active specifically when the vocal is resting** — the mechanism that creates a genuine call-and-response effect between voice and bass, rather than the bass just running constantly underneath the vocal regardless of what it's doing. → [[Slap Bass Dynamics — Contrast, Force, and Restraint]] tool #4 (paired with song 3 above — same ensemble-restraint mechanism, applied here to a vocal melody instead of a horn/other instrument) and [[🎵 THE BEATLES - Something]] (new "Learning" link, plus the call-and-response nuance layered onto that note's existing "bass as counter-melody" framing — it already credits McCartney's line as one of his most celebrated, but hadn't yet named *why* it reads as a conversation rather than just a busy counter-line).

- **Song 7 — bassist "Nikolai" (name transcribed correctly by the auto-captions; song and band not stated — the name most plausibly points to Nikolai Fraiture of The Strokes, given the early-2000s indie-rock "active melodic bass developing into a counter-melody, kept in check by strict rhythmic repetition" description, but this is not confirmed anywhere in the video or its description, so treat it as a named-but-unidentified artist rather than a confirmed song ID).** Follows directly on from songs 5–6 in Josh's own playing chronology: having developed a taste for active, melodic basslines from "Something" and "I'll Take You There," his teenage playing "degenerated into extreme overplaying." This song taught the fix: **repetition as a discipline, not a limitation** — the first two beats of every bar repeat the identical rhythm and note-relationships every time, even across a chord change, while only the last two beats vary; even the fixed portion of bars 1 and 3 share the same actual notes. The payoff: the line still functions as a real counter-melody supporting the song, but reads as *supportive* rather than as a distracting bass solo, purely because of that repeated skeleton. → [[Production/Techniques/Bassline Writing|Bassline Writing]] (a new worked example for that note's existing "record busy, mute down" / "don't get in the way" restraint mechanism — a second concrete instance, alongside its existing Thundercat "After Last Night" case study, of using deliberate repetition specifically to keep an otherwise-melodic bassline from reading as overplaying).

- **Song 8 — a funk-jam standard from Josh's home turf, Sonoma County, California (song not stated — described only as a tune "you've definitely heard... maybe too many times" at funk jams).** The technical challenge: **swung sixteenth notes**, played with a deliberately uneven, "lumpy" feel rather than mathematically even subdivisions — counted "one-e-and-a, two-e-and-a, three-and-a" instead of a perfectly even "one-e-and-a" every beat. The video plays the same line both ways as an A/B: straight sixteenths read as "different — a little more robotic," while the swung version adds what Josh calls "a little bit more grease." → [[Rhythm and Groove]] (Subdivision section — a live-playing/performance-technique confirmation of the same swing-feel mechanism that page already documents from the production/programming side, new "From videos" bullet).
