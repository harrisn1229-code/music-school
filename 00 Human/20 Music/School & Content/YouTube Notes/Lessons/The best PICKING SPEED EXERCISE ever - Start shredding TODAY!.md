## Synopsis

Reframes picking-speed training around note-spacing accuracy rather than raw tempo: two takes that are equally hand-synchronized and equally clean can still sound completely different if the notes aren't evenly spaced on the rhythmic grid — fixed with an odd 7-note (6 sixteenths + dotted-eighth-rest-shaped grouping) chromatic burst pattern practiced against a double-bass drum backing track specifically for its constant, unambiguous timing reference.

## What's taught

- **The core distinction: speed ≠ control/precision/note-spacing.** Two picking takes can both be well hand-synchronized (every stroke aligned to the correct fretted note) and still differ hugely in perceived quality if one has notes landing exactly on the 16th-note grid and the other doesn't — spacing accuracy, not synchronization alone, is what actually separates a good-sounding fast take from a bad one.
- **The exercise: a 7-accent chromatic burst (6 sixteenth notes + 1 eighth note + 1 eighth rest) repeated as a constant pattern.** Because 7 is an odd grouping against a 4/4 grid, the rhythmic accent constantly shifts position measure to measure — deliberately harder to feel than an even grouping, which is the point (forces active spacing awareness rather than muscle-memory autopilot).
- **Why a double-bass drum backing track specifically, not just a metronome click:** the constant 16th-note drum accents give continuous feedback on whether a burst lands early, late, or exactly on the grid — richer timing information than a bare click, especially useful for catching a fast burst that's rushed or dragged relative to the beat.
- **Picking-angle detail for this exercise:** the pick needs to escape the "dead zone" between strings in the *same direction* on every single transition for the pattern to stay clean at speed — named as angle "number one" vs. an alternative angle that doesn't escape cleanly; small gear note attached — prefers small Jazz III-style picks for this kind of fast picking work.
- **Progression stated:** demonstrated at a simplified/slowed arrangement first (to hear the shifting accent clearly), then at practice tempo, then pushed to 220 BPM as the advanced target — same relaxed-hand, no-bigger-motion-at-speed discipline emphasized throughout (consistent with this batch's other speed-burst videos).
