## Synopsis

A comprehensive speed-building lesson: the fast-vs-slow tempo-building methodology debate (already documented from an earlier video), concrete alternate-picking and legato speed exercises, and — the most substantial new content — two picking-mechanics rules (inside/outside picking, pick slanting) that materially affect achievable speed and aren't yet documented anywhere in the vault, plus a stamina-building protocol.

## Techniques

- **Fast vs. slow tempo-building methodology: same resolution as Debate 3 in [[Resolving Seven Common Guitar-Practice Debates]] — use both, sequentially.** Restated with a concrete example: find your max clean 16th-note tempo (test: play a 1-2-3-4 exercise for a full minute straight — the fastest tempo you can sustain cleanly for that minute is your limit), then use the "fast methodology" to push to a higher target tempo despite initial sloppiness, clean it up over several sessions, then repeat the cycle at the next tempo target. Context given for why two well-known teachers (Jamie Robinson, Bog) disagree publicly: Robinson teaches in-person beginners (needs the simpler slow approach), while the other creator's audience has already tried slow-and-steady without breaking through (needs the fast-methodology unlock) — same technique, different starting audience.
- **Inside vs. outside picking — a picking-direction rule for 2-notes-per-string lines (e.g. pentatonic scales).** **Inside picking** = each pick stroke moves toward the next string you're about to cross to; **outside picking** = each stroke moves away from it. For an **ascending** 2-notes-per-string line, starting with an **upstroke** puts you on inside picking (the following downstroke naturally lands moving toward the next higher string) — starting with a downstroke instead puts you on outside picking, which is slower because each string change requires an extra "hop" over the string just left. For a **descending** 2-notes-per-string line, the rule flips: start with a **downstroke** to stay on inside picking. **General rule: pick-stroke direction relative to string-crossing direction, not just alternation, determines achievable speed** — this is a mechanical fact independent of raw hand speed.
- **Pick slanting — a physical pick-angle technique for even note-groupings per string.** For an **even** number of notes per string (e.g. groups of 4 or 6), angling the pick in a consistent **downward slant** lets the pick clear the next string without a separate "hop" motion, and the slant angle never has to change since the picking direction pattern repeats identically on every string. For an **odd** number of notes per string (e.g. 3 notes per string), the required slant angle flips every time you cross a string (downward slant becomes insufficient, an **upward slant** is needed on the alternating string), which is inherently less efficient than the even-grouping case — flagged as the source of his own mixed feelings about the technique: genuinely faster, but only cleanly applicable to even note-groupings without constant slant-switching.
- **Picking-direction is a deliberate compositional choice, not a fixed rule to always alternate-pick.** Worked personal example: a fast line that resisted full alternate picking for 7 weeks of practice was solved not by more practice but by switching part of it to a **sweep-picked** passage instead — the lesson generalized: when a fast line isn't working, actively ask whether a different picking approach (sweep, economy, etc.) fits the line better, rather than assuming more repetition of the same picking pattern will eventually work.
- **Legato speed exercise, worked in F minor Dorian:** a scale run in groups of 5, picking only the first and last note of each group of 5 (everything in between is hammer-ons/pull-offs), with the picked notes alternating stroke direction group to group (upstroke-start on one group, downstroke-start on the next) rather than resetting to the same stroke every time.
- **Stamina-building protocol: play continuously, in time, without stopping, for 1 to 3 minutes** at a tempo deliberately set **below** your absolute max speed (since sustaining cleanly for minutes at a time is a different demand than a short accurate burst). The hardest version of this is the **chain exercise** — continuous improvisation in one key without stopping — which requires strong key/scale-position knowledge; a simpler fixed-pattern alternative is offered for players not ready for the freely-improvised version. *(Same underlying stamina principle as the "chain exercise" capstone in [[Harmonic Minor Scale Practice Ladder]], generalized here beyond harmonic minor to any key/scale.)*
