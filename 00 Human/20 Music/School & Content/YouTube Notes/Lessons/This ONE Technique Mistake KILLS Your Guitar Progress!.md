# This ONE Technique Mistake KILLS Your Guitar Progress!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=S-O2p6uDi6g)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=S-O2p6uDi6g)

## Synopsis

Three fretting-hand independence problems, framed as deeper than the usual flying-pinky/legato-technique advice: over-anchoring the index finger, always leading licks with the index finger, and gripping the neck far harder than actually needed.

## Techniques

1. **Don't over-press the index finger when it isn't actively needed.** A common unconscious habit: pressing the index finger down hard as an "anchor" for the whole hand, even mid-legato-phrase when it isn't the finger currently doing work — this measurably reduces feel and independent control of the other fingers, gripping the neck harder overall and enlarging the whole hand's motion. Fix: lift the index finger slightly the instant it's not needed, same as any other finger.
   - **Drill: incorporate open strings into finger workouts.** An open string forces every fretting finger to actually lift off between notes (there's no fret to rest on), which trains the lift-when-not-needed habit directly. Run this specifically **without wrist assistance** — isolate the motion to the fingers alone, not a wrist rock helping them along.
2. **Don't always anchor/lead licks with the index finger.** Constantly using the index finger as a fixed anchor point limits phrasing vocabulary, especially when improvising. Drill: practice a lick where the index finger deliberately plays a minor, non-anchoring role and can't stay planted on one string — forces the other fingers to work independently without index-finger support, described as feeling "crazy" the first time but opening up noticeably more creative lines once comfortable.
3. **The "death grip of doom" — gripping far harder than actually needed, out of fear a note won't ring.** A specific over-gripping habit traced to early live-performance nerves (worrying notes would fail to sound). Drill: a variation of the open-string chromatic workout above, this time deliberately **decreasing** grip intensity rep by rep until notes start to die out — the goal is finding the real minimum-pressure sweet spot experientially, by pushing past it and backing off, rather than guessing. Complements the existing minimum-pressure test (increase pressure until clean, stop there) with the inverse direction: start too light on purpose and increase, so both ends of the range get calibrated by feel.
