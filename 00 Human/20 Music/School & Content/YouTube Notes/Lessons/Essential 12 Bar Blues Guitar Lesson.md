# Essential 12 Bar Blues Guitar Lesson

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J48j_SfWhEI)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J48j_SfWhEI)

## Synopsis

A beginner/intermediate-focused Daniel Seriff lesson teaching **the two most common 12-bar blues chord progressions**, both worked in the key of A: the **dominant blues** (I7-IV7-V7, with the "quick change" variant) and the **minor blues** (i7-iv7 with a ♭VI7-V7 turnaround). For each progression Seriff names a well-known reference song (Sweet Home Chicago for the dominant form, BB King's "Thrill Is Gone" for the minor form), walks the bar-by-bar chord sequence, plays each progression in the context of a full band backing track, and gives the standard soloing starting point (A minor pentatonic box shape 1 over the dominant form; the same scale, plus a pointer to his own diagonal-pentatonic video, over the minor form). It's a compact, foundational lesson rather than a deep-dive — most valuable as a clean, plainly-stated restatement of material the vault already has in more depth on [[Blues Form and Harmony]] and [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]], but it independently confirms two things worth having a second source for: the quick-change dominant blues laid out beat-by-beat exactly as documented, and — new detail — the minor blues turnaround worked explicitly in the key of A (F7-E7-Am7-A7) rather than only the C-minor instance already in the vault, plus the direct assertion that both forms are freely transposable to any key. Free backing tracks and scale-shape diagrams are offered via a link in the video description (not fetched here — external resource, not transcript content).

## What's taught

- **The A dominant 12-bar blues, full bar-by-bar layout.** I7 = A7, IV7 = D7, V7 = E7, all dominant seventh chords, 4/4 time, four beats per chord per bar. Reference song: **Sweet Home Chicago**. *(~1:30–3:00)* → [[Blues Form and Harmony]]
- **The "quick change" named and demonstrated as its own concept**, distinct from the plain form: bar 2 moves to the IV (D7) instead of staying on the I, then back to I for bar 3-4. Full 12-bar sequence given explicitly: **A7 / D7 / A7 / A7 / D7 / D7 / A7 / A7 / D7(V-implied)... A7 / D7 / A7 (turnaround, often closing with a "little B7 or some variation")**. This matches the quick-change variant already documented on [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *The variants worth knowing* bar-for-bar. *(~3:00–4:15)*
- **Movable (barre) vs. open chord shapes flagged as a real choice** — open A7/D7/E7 works fine for accompanying a singer, but movable/barre dominant-7 shapes (referencing a prior video on the CAGED system) are worth learning for transposability and mobility on the neck. *(~2:00–2:40)*
- **A minor pentatonic (box shape 1) as the default soloing scale over the entire A dominant blues** — stated as the instinctive first move most guitarists make: run A minor pentatonic across all three chords (A7, D7, E7) without changing scale per chord. Explicitly calls out the theoretical oddity — playing a **minor** scale over **major/dominant** chords — as the mechanism that produces "the sound of the blues," directly restating [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]] § *Why the ♭3 isn't just wrong*. *(~4:20–5:15)* → [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]]
- **Named as the more advanced/authentic approach: blending minor pentatonic and major pentatonic** rather than staying in one scale — "most of the great blues guitar players actually blend between the minor pentatonic or the major pentatonic," stated as a direct pointer without worked fingerings in this video. Same blend concept already documented in depth on [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]] § *What to play over the I7*. *(~5:15–5:40)*
- **The A minor 12-bar blues, full bar-by-bar layout.** i7 = Am7, iv7 = Dm7 (explicitly flagged as minor 7, not dominant D7 — a common mix-up), no quick change (unlike the dominant form): **Am7 ×4 / Dm7 ×2 / Am7 ×2 / turnaround**. Reference song: **"Thrill Is Gone" (BB King)**, noting BB played it in multiple keys over his career (C minor, B minor) and that the form transposes freely to any key. *(~6:00–7:30)* → [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *Minor blues*
- **The minor blues turnaround, named and spelled out in A: ♭VI7 → V7 → i7 → I7 — "flat six to five back to [one]."** Concretely: **F7 (♭VI, F dominant 7) down a half step to E7 (V) back to Am7 (i), then A7.** Framed as the key structural difference from the dominant blues' plain V-IV-I turnaround, and explicitly tied to the "flat 6, 5" sound of "Thrill Is Gone." This is a second, independent worked instance of the ♭VI-V turnaround already documented (in C minor) on [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *From videos* — confirms the same shape holds a fourth up, in A. *(~7:30–8:15)* → [[Blues Form and Harmony]], [[Turnarounds]]
- **A minor pentatonic recommended again as the default soloing scale over the minor blues** (same A minor pentatonic works for both progressions here, since both are centered on A), with a direct pointer to Seriff's earlier video on a "diagonal pentatonic scale across the entire guitar neck" for players who want to move beyond one box shape. *(~9:00–9:40)* → [[Diagonal Pentatonic Blues Soloing]] *(the diagonal-shape technique note already built from that referenced video)*
- **Full-band demonstrations of both progressions** — the dominant blues played with a straightforward backing groove, the minor blues played over a "funky backbeat" groove explicitly likened to the feel of "Thrill Is Gone," used to illustrate that the minor form reads as "more chill, more cool sounding" than the brighter dominant form. *(~5:45–6:00, ~8:15–8:45)*
- **Free resources offered but not extracted:** backing tracks for both progressions plus printable scale-shape neck diagrams, available via a link in the video description — flagged for context only, not transcript content. *(~9:40–10:15)*
- **No plugin, effect, mixing, or production content** — a pure guitar instrument/theory lesson, so production-side routing (Steps 4/6/7 of the enrichment workflow) has nothing to do here.
- **Both reference songs (Sweet Home Chicago, Thrill Is Gone) are name-dropped as listening examples only** — no chords/voicings/arrangement detail is taught for either song in this video, so neither gets a `Library` entry from this pass; "Thrill Is Gone" already has its exact minor-blues turnaround documented in more depth (in C minor, from a different source video) on [[Blues Form and Harmony]].
