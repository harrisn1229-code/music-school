# Songs that use 10/4 time

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=T6IA2d54NFs)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=T6IA2d54NFs)

## Synopsis

Introduces a distinct category from true odd meter: **composite meter** — a time signature whose top number CAN divide evenly by 2 or 3 (so it fails the strict "odd meter" test) but still feels unusual because in practice it's built from two different smaller meters glued together, worked via 10/4 examples.

## Techniques

- **Composite meter, precisely defined and distinguished from true odd meter.** True odd meters (5/4, 7/4, 11/4) feel unusual because the beat count itself can't split evenly into groups of 2 or 3 — an inherently uneven pulse. 10/4 and 6/4 both fail that test (10 divides evenly into 2+2+2+2+2 or similar) yet still sound unusual in practice — because real songs in 10/4 aren't using an even 10-beat pulse, they're alternating between two DIFFERENT smaller meters bar-to-bar or beat-group-to-beat-group (e.g. 4/4 + 2/4, or 4/4 + 12/8) rather than a genuinely uniform 10-beat feel. The tell: if a song's "10 beats" divided cleanly into uniform groups, you'd just call it two bars of 5/4, not one bar of 10/4 — the composite label only makes sense when the internal groupings are UNEVEN sizes (4+4+2, not 5+5).
- **Two distinct 10/4 grouping patterns, worked via three songs**: **4+4+2** — "Everything In Its Right Place" (Radiohead): reads as two regular 4/4 bars plus one 2/4 bar; the song also occasionally drops to a one-off 9/4 bar (one beat missing from the pulse), an instability the video reads as thematically ironic against the title. **4-fast-beats + 4-slow-beats (quarter notes then dotted quarter notes)** — "Go To Sleep" (Radiohead) and "Just Like You Imagined" (Nine Inch Nails): four regular quarter notes followed by four dotted quarter notes (each 1.5 beats) — functionally equivalent to alternating a bar of 4/4 with a bar of 12/8, and the video argues that's actually the more useful way to notate/communicate the rhythm to a performer rather than a single unbroken 10/4 bar.
- **General principle for classifying an "odd-feeling" meter that technically isn't odd**: if a time signature divides evenly on paper but still feels unusual, check whether it's actually a composite of two DIFFERENT smaller meters rather than one uniform pulse — that's the actual source of the odd feeling, distinct from the uneven-subdivision mechanism behind true odd meters.
