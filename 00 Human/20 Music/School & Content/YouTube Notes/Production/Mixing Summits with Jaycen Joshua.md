## Techniques

- **Gain-structure discipline, drums-first.** Route the drum bus through an analog-console-emulation stage (Waves NLS, unowned — see [[Plugin Wishlist]]) into a mastering-style bus compressor (Shadow Hills, run via the UAD plugin specifically — "the UAD one to me is the best one by far, but you can choose the one you love best"), and set gain so the drum bus **hits that compressor at roughly −1 dB** (a little over/under is fine). Since drums are deliberately the loudest thing in the record, hitting the compressor at the right level there guarantees headroom for everything layered in afterward. Check the master bus level next: sweet spot is **−5 to −6 dB** (roughly −5, −4 to −6 acceptable) on the 2-bus meter, leaving ~5 dB of headroom before 0 so vocals/instruments can be added without clipping risk. → [[Gain Staging]], [[Master Chain]]
- **Multiple-kick cleanup, a reggaetón-specific habit.** Reggaetón sessions routinely arrive with 2–3 kick drums stacked by different producers, often with no clean "main" kick isolated. Find the actual main kick (cross-check with the producer/engineer if needed), mute the rest, and A/B: if the mix sounds just as good without the extra kicks, cut them — "sometimes producers just add stuff because they like to add stuff," then hear their own kick made bigger by the *mix*, not their sample. → [[Kick Design]], [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **The core low-end move: duplicate the kick (with every plugin already on it), duck the bass under it by hand, then let [[Soothe2]] mop up whatever still pokes through.** Duplicate the kick track complete with its full processing chain (not a dry copy — the *exact* triggering kick matters for timing), bring the duplicate down to sit right under the bass, then manually "dip" the bass around each kick hit (a hand-drawn duck, not a compressor reacting live) before adding [[Soothe2]] on top, triggered by the kick, to catch any transient/resonant energy that still leaks through after the manual dip. Net result: kick and bass/808 read as if they're not fighting for room, with **no audible bass-dip** and **no voltage added** even after the bass comes in — a straight 4 dB of headroom recovered vs. not doing this at all. Emphasized as his technique across "pretty much every mix," genre-agnostic ("sidechain compression works for every genre that's out there") — the only variable is *how much* of it you use (EDM can push it obvious/pumping; most genres want it invisible). → [[Soothe2]], [[Sidechaining]], [[1 Digital Bass Track|Digital Bass Track]], [[3 Drums Bus|Drums Bus]]
- **Run the analog-console-emulation stage (NLS) as a SEPARATE instance per low-end source, not one shared instance across the whole drum bus.** His stated house rule: never lump the kick, snare, and 808/bass through one shared compressor/console-emulation stage the way a typical drum-bus chain does — "I treat the 808 different, I treat the bass different, and I treat the drums and the kick different." Running two-plus NLS instances (one per source) rather than one across everything means each source picks up its **own** distinct analog-console harmonics/inflections instead of sharing one blended color — described as making the whole record read "bigger, thicker, deeper." Unowned (Waves NLS) — see [[Plugin Wishlist]]; owned-lane stand-in for the per-source console-color idea is a dedicated [[UAD Century Tube Channel Strip]] or [[Decapitator]] instance per bus rather than one shared saturator across the group. → [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[2 Drums Group|Drums Group]], [[2 Bass Group|Bass Group]]
- **Front-to-back "panner" via reverb early reflections only — the reverb tail itself stays off.** On [[Pro-R 2]]/[[Reverb]] (Ableton)-equivalent thinking (source used Waves True Verb, unowned — see [[Plugin Wishlist]]): the reverb's three signal paths are dry (direct), early reflections, and the reverb tail proper — turn the **tail off entirely** and rely only on the **early-reflections** balance to place a source psychoacoustically closer to or farther from the listener, "tricking your ears" into hearing the direct source as nearer or more distant purely from how present/timed the early reflections read, with zero added wash. A genuinely different reverb use-case than the existing wet/dry-mix depth entries on this page — depth from early-reflection *timing/balance*, not decay length. → [[Reverb]], [[Depth and Space]]
- **"Make it longer, not louder" — the ear judges perceived size by duration, not just amplitude, and it's a cheat code for a kick that can't get any louder against the compressor.** When a kick needs to "hit a little harder" but it's already driving the compressor as hard as it safely can, don't reach for more gain — **extend the kick's sustain/tail** (a longer release/decay on the same hit) instead. Same peak level, same processing, but the ear perceives the longer-sustaining hit as bigger because it hears the sound for longer, not because it's actually louder. → [[Kick Design]], [[Compression Staging]]
- **Reframe "Release" as "Weight" — the fix for a band you notched out but now want partially back.** Once a frequency has been cut (a static EQ notch, or ducked out via a compressor's active band), the way to reintroduce presence in that band isn't to raise the gain back up — it's to **add release** on whatever's compressing that band. Longer release lets more of the pre-cut content back through on the decay/tail side of each hit, giving weight back with more control than a gain move would. Practical habit: mentally rename the Release knob "Weight" for any band-specific compression — "if you want it to be heavier in that band, add the release." → [[Compression]] (effect), [[Compression Staging]]
- **Stem-level near-mastering before the 2-bus, via an adaptive limiter that doesn't chase a fixed threshold.** Every section of the mix (drums, hats, music/instrumental, vocals) gets processed essentially as its own mini-master — "every section has its own mix... they've all been mixed and basically mastered individually" — so each stem barely nudges the 2-bus when it arrives. The tool doing this per-stem limiting (the "God Particle," unowned — see [[Plugin Wishlist]]) is framed as an **adaptive limiter**: it doesn't hunt for a fixed dB threshold the way a normal limiter does — "it adapts to whatever energy you're sending it and then it corrects it, gives it what it feels that it needs" — so if it's engaging too hard, the fix is pulling back the *input* and raising the limiter's own makeup, not chasing a threshold number. The stated philosophy behind letting original transients through rather than fighting to preserve them consciously: "if you don't let your original transients through, you're never going to have an open and beautiful exciting mix." → [[Limiting]] (effect), [[Master Chain]]
- **"Pushpull" EQ — parametric bands placed BEFORE the compressor stage, used to nudge toward a target indicator rather than as ordinary boost/cut tone-shaping.** Distinct from a normal EQ mental model: these bands sit ahead of the compressor and exist to "push" or "pull" a signal's level into a target range on a per-band indicator (demoed pushing more energy into the mid-range indicator) rather than shaping tone for its own sake — closer to a pre-compression gain-staging EQ than a corrective/tonal one. A **Big Knee** mode is available for a broader, gentler curve when a narrower push/pull reads as too aggressive. → [[EQ]] (effect), [[Pro-Q 4]]
- **Artist-matched reference EQ presets for vocal tone-matching, applied per section of the frequency spectrum.** A match/reference-EQ workflow with presets captured from specific artists' vocal tonal signatures — Whitney Houston and Rihanna presets for the main vocal-EQ stage, Michael Jackson and Prince presets for a later "air"-stage EQ — load the preset, and it applies a matching curve toward that artist's captured tonal balance rather than dialing bands by ear from scratch. Owned-lane equivalent for the mechanism (reference-track spectral matching, not artist-specific presets out of the box): [[Ozone 9 Match EQ]]. → [[Ozone 9 Match EQ]], [[EQ]] (effect)
- **Remote client mix-review software as a session-time saver, not just a nice-to-have.** Audio Movers (unowned — see [[Plugin Wishlist]]) named directly as "the best thing for mixing if you can't have the client come to your studio" — affordable, saves "endless amounts of time" versus bouncing/sending files back and forth for approval. → [[Plugin Wishlist]], [[Mix Handoff]]
- **Client-management protocol for a genuinely weak vocal take — never tell the artist their take is bad.** When handed a rough vocal with little to work with: go to the *engineer* privately for a direct, respectful conversation about raising quality upstream — not the artist, who typically believes their take sounds great ("they're all in this together"). At the mix stage itself, do the best possible job, then **walk away** rather than endlessly chasing an idealized version of the vocal in your head — every mixer knows what a "great" version would sound like and can burn unlimited time chasing it; recognize the point of diminishing returns and stop. Never volunteer that a vocal is weak — flagging it just invites a re-sing request nobody actually wants ("don't wise up a fool").
- **Three-skills career advice for engineers, in order:** (1) **patience** — flagged directly against people who quit an apprenticeship/assist role after only a month wanting a raise; (2) **make the process fun and be present in the journey**, not fixated on the destination/outcome; (3) **study your mentors' influences, not just their finished output** — "if you want to work like Jay, don't just listen to Jay's work, go listen to what Jay listened to," then blend that influence with your own voice rather than only imitating the end product.
- **Recurring philosophy, stated twice across the session:** "Always think to yourself — what can I do for my client that other people aren't doing for me? You might fall on your face, but I'd rather try." Framed as the differentiator between a technically-competent mix and one a client remembers.
