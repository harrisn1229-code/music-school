# How Radiohead use Key Changes

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LORrqu1gadw)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LORrqu1gadw)

## Synopsis

Three Radiohead songs across three albums, chosen to show a band's key-change technique maturing over time: "Sulk" (1995, a plain truck-driver key change), "Knives Out" (2001, chromatic pivot-chord modulation), and "Present Tense" (2016, deliberately ambiguous, sliding tonal centers inside a 9-bar loop).

## Techniques

- **Gluing chromatic (out-of-key) chords together with shared common tones, so a jump doesn't feel jarring**: in "Present Tense," a D♯ major chord moves not to its expected resolution (G♯ minor) but to C minor instead — a chord that doesn't belong in G♯ minor at all. It works because D♯ major and C minor share two of three notes (two-thirds overlap), and the vocal melody sustains a G natural across both chords, reinforcing the shared tone even further. The next move (C minor → B major) is glued the same way — all three chords in the sequence share a common D♯.
- **Deliberate tonal ambiguity via shared-scale enharmonic overlap**: after "Present Tense"'s common-tone chain lands on B major → E major (a textbook perfect cadence, which would normally establish E as the new tonic), the B chord doesn't *feel* like a dominant wanting to resolve — it feels like a tonic at rest. Since B Mixolydian and E major share the exact same seven notes, the passage is genuinely ambiguous between "E major with a IV-I-ish motion" and "B Mixolydian" — which scale you're actually in depends entirely on which note your ear picks as the root, not on the notes themselves. Same underlying mechanism as [[Diatonic Modes]]'s Bowie "Let's Dance" pivot (see [[How David Bowie uses Modes]]) — one fixed set of notes, disputed tonic.
- **An unusually short (9-bar, not 8 or 16) loop as a structural signal that something modulatory is happening**, even before analyzing the chords — worth listening for irregular loop lengths as a tell.
- **A chromatic chord that's "chromatically right next door" to the diatonic chord it replaces, used as connective tissue between two otherwise-unrelated keys**: in "Knives Out," the verse (C minor) moves to E minor 6 — a chord that doesn't belong in C minor. It works because 3 of 4 of its notes sit a semitone away from the diatonic F6 chord it effectively substitutes for. The real payoff: those same 3 of 4 notes ALSO fit the chorus's key (A minor) — so the "foreign" chord in the verse is secretly already preparing the ear for the chorus's key change, functioning as a genuine pivot chord between two keys with almost no notes in common (C minor → A minor is normally a bold, disconnected leap).
- **The "truck driver key change" (an abrupt, un-prepared whole-step-up modulation, usually saved for the last third of a song for an energy lift) — named, and explicitly flagged as a well-worn pop cliché** ("nowadays... a cheap trick... associated with very commercial pop music"). "Sulk" uses exactly this: three chords in D major for the first 2.5 minutes, then a flat modulation up to E major with no pivot chord or common-tone preparation at all. Included here specifically as the contrast case against the other two songs' much more elaborate techniques — evidence of a band's harmonic sophistication increasing across their catalog (1995 → 2001 → 2016).
