# Slow Dancing in a Burning Room — John Mayer

> *Continuum* (2006). This note is a stub focused on one small, recurring arrangement detail — the IV→I (A→E) triad move and John Mayer's hammer-on ornament over it — as broken down by [[Slow Dancing in a Burning Room Chords - Unique John Mayer Guitar Lesson|Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared]], not a full chord chart for the song. Jared picked this exact fragment because it's the recurring question from his students: "what is he doing when he does this thing?"

## Chords
Key: **E major**. This gives the whole-song chart the A→E fragment above sits inside.

**Verse:** C♯m7 – A – E, looping → **vi7 – IV – I**

**Chorus:** B11 – C♯m7 – A – B11 – C♯m7 – F♯m7 – C♯m7 – A – E → **V11 – vi7 – IV – V11 – vi7 – ii7 – vi7 – IV – I**

→ The chorus's tail end (C♯m7–A–E, i.e. vi–IV–I) is the same core cell as the verse, just arrived at from the dominant (B11) instead of repeated cold, and the section touches all four of the "axis" chords (I, V, vi, IV) at some point. It doesn't cleanly rotate the same four chords in a fixed loop the way a true axis-progression song does, though — the inserted ii7 (F♯m7) and the verse's omission of the V chord keep it a looser, more idiosyncratic phrase than a clean rotation, so it's left untagged below rather than forced into the axis family.

**The recurring A→E (IV→I) fragment** broken down below — with its voice-leading and signature hammer-on ornament — is the harmonic backbone both the verse and the chorus's ending keep landing on.

## The move: A→E plagal cadence with hammer-on

**Harmony:** key of **E major**. The chord move is **A → E**, a **plagal cadence (IV → I)** — the "amen cadence," softer than a V→I authentic cadence because there's no leading tone pulling into the tonic. → [[Cadences]]

**Core voicing (strings D-G-B / 4-3-2), 5th position:**
| Chord | D string | G string | B string | Chord tones |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A** (root position) | fret 7 (A) | fret 6 (C♯) | fret 5 (E) | 1-3-5 |
| **E** (2nd inversion) | fret 6 (G♯) | fret 4 (B) | fret 5 (E) | 3-5-1 |

**Voice leading between them** (why it sounds so smooth): the B-string note (E) **stays exactly the same**, the D-string note moves **A→G♯ (half step down)**, the G-string note moves **C♯→B (whole step down)** — one voice held, one half-step move, one whole-step move: the smallest possible per-voice motion between the two triads.

**The hammer-on (the "unique John Mayer thing"):** just before resolving A→E, Mayer hammers on to the chord's **3rd (C♯)** from the **major 2nd (B)** a whole step below it, on the triad's middle voice — in this voicing, the G string, **fret 4 → fret 6** — then continues into the voice-led E chord. Described in the lesson as "2 hammering onto 3." This ornament, layered on top of the plain voice-led A→E move, is what gives the phrase its identity.

**Bass-note mute (critical, easy to miss):** playing the triads with their open bass strings underneath (open low A under the A triad), the right-hand **thumb must hop over and mute the open A string** the instant you move to E — otherwise the low A rings against the E harmony and sounds muddy. Mute by hopping the thumb clear or laying its side across the string.

**The same move recurs in at least three neck positions**, found each time by voice-leading to the nearest E triad and locating the 3rd→2 hammer-on spot on whichever string carries the 3rd in that voicing:
- **Strings 4-3-2** — the 5th-position voicing tabbed above.
- **Strings 5-4-3** — a second A→E pairing; here **E is kept as a constant pedal bass note under both chords** (E is already the 5th of A and the root of E, so one bass note works under both harmonies) — the "neighbor IV chord" variant of the same plagal cadence.
- **Strings 3-2-1** (top three strings) — a third A→E pairing at the top of the neck, same voice-leading-then-hammer-on method.

**Telecaster vibrato aside (general technique, not specific to this song's own gear):** a slight pull on the guitar's neck (bending the neck, not a string) gives a subtle vibrato that "breathes life" into sustaining notes — called out as common on Telecaster.

## Steal this
- **Find the closest triad voicing between two chords before anything else** — voice-lead the plain chord change first (smallest per-voice motion, using inversions), THEN add ornamentation on top; ornamenting an un-voice-led move loses the smoothness that makes it sound intentional. → [[Chord Inversions]]
- **The "2 hammering onto 3" embellishment is portable** — any major triad's 3rd can be preceded by a hammer-on from the major 2nd a whole step below, on whichever string carries that voice; not specific to A or E, works on any triad shape once you can identify the 3rd.
- **Mute the outgoing bass note on a IV→I move that keeps open strings** — the classic "muddy plagal cadence" failure mode is an un-muted IV bass note ringing into the I chord; thumb-mute it, don't just trust the new chord to mask it.
- **Package a small discovery into a timed, in-tempo, ordered exercise** — chord, then embellished chord, repeated up each string set (5-4-3 → 4-3-2 → 3-2-1) — turns a one-off lick into a testable milestone ("can I do this in time, cleanly, through every position") rather than open-ended noodling.
