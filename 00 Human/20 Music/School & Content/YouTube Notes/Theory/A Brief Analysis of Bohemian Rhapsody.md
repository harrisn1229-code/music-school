# A Brief Analysis of Bohemian Rhapsody

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XQb827N6oww)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XQb827N6oww)

## Synopsis

A section-by-section harmonic analysis of "Bohemian Rhapsody" drawn from the original multitrack, covering all five sections (a cappella intro, ballad, guitar solo, operatic, rock, coda). Central thesis: the song's harmonic core is actually very functional (heavy use of perfect cadences and secondary dominants), and the famous chromatic oddities land as hard as they do specifically BECAUSE they're set against that functional backdrop, not because the whole song is chromatically unmoored.

## Techniques

- **The "Bohemian motif" — a named, recurring tension device: trap the note the ear expects to hear as a resolution between two chromatic (non-diatonic) chords on either side, so the target note is implied but never directly landed on.** First appears in the intro: an F7 secondary dominant almost resolves to B♭ (the expected root), but the B♭ itself never sounds cleanly — it's "encapsulated" between chromatic neighbors, delaying the resolution and forcing the ear deeper into the song. Recurs in the operatic section (immediately after the staccato A major chord that opens it) and returns one final time in the coda — but on its last appearance, the trapped note finally "breaks free," resolving up to the true root instead of staying caught in the semitone pendulum. Functions as a structural throughline tying together sections that otherwise sound completely different from each other.
- **Deliberately withholding an expected chord to avoid a predictable resolution.** After a line cliché that sets up an expected return to E♭ major, Freddie substitutes F minor instead of the "obvious" F major (which would have completed a clean perfect cadence F→B♭) — described as a deliberate choice against predictability, creating a more jarring "jolt" back to B♭ instead of a smooth landing.
- **A deceptive modulation setup used to control audience attention at a structural boundary.** At the end of the guitar solo, D♭ and B♭m chords (foreign to the current key of E♭) strongly imply an upcoming modulation to A♭ major — confirmed by an isolated edit demonstrating the "expected" A♭ resolution sounds pleasant and natural. Freddie instead jumps a semitone higher, to A major — deliberately breaking the setup, combined with a tempo and dynamic change, specifically to catch the listener off guard right as the operatic section begins.
- **Two distinct secondary-dominant types used in different sections, distinguished by which scale degree they're built on**: the intro/ballad sections use secondary dominants built on the 2nd scale degree (the standard V/V-family shape); the operatic section instead uses a secondary dominant built on the 3rd degree, reused again in the coda — flagged explicitly as a different, less common secondary-dominant type from the same harmonic toolkit. See [[Secondary Dominants]] for the general device.
- **"Cascading chords" as a modulation tool**: a C minor 7 chord "cascades" downward specifically because it shares two notes (E♭ and B♭) with the destination key of E♭ major, making it an effective bridge chord into that key — reused later via a G♭7 cascading chord during the operatic section's rapid-fire cadence run.
- **A minor plagal cadence (iv-minor → I) used for a more melancholic alternative to a perfect cadence** — the borrowed minor iv chord (A♭m in the key of E♭) resolving to the tonic, contrasted directly against the sharper, more "final" perfect cadence used elsewhere in the song.
- **Key ambiguity between closely related keys exploited deliberately, not resolved by the song itself.** A passage using only notes valid in BOTH B♭ major and E♭ major (which differ by a single note, A natural vs A♭) is analyzed as establishing E♭ as the new root via an internal perfect cadence — but the video is explicit that the ambiguity is real and arguable, not a definitive claim.
- **A metrically-prepared feel change: the shuffle/triplet groove is set up gradually before the section that "owns" it arrives.** The drums begin shifting from a straight feel toward a triplet-based shuffle DURING the tail end of the operatic section, ahead of the rock section's tempo — isolating the drum stem makes the transition point audible. The payoff: the rock section's shuffle feel doesn't read as a hard cut, because the groove was already arriving before the section technically started.
- **A circle-of-fifths modulation chain used to make a distant key change feel smooth**: E♭ major → briefly through B♭ major → lands on F major for the coda's final resolution — moving through the intermediate related key (B♭, one step around the circle from both E♭ and F) is what makes the E♭-to-F shift read as natural rather than abrupt, rather than jumping there directly.
