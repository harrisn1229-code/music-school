# Sherman Filter in a High-End Mixer Should Be ILLEGAL!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oFEoeKfc_Wg)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oFEoeKfc_Wg)

## Synopsis

Tom De Ridder (Rodec) demos a brand-new, about-to-launch product: the Rodec MX, a modular hybrid analog console with API 500-series slots, a DJ-style stem crossfader, and a new "Mini Styler" module — a compact successor to the Sherman/Rodec "Restyler," a 1990s DJ-focused filter processor. Genuinely rich in both hardware-design philosophy (why the console sounds the way it does) and performance-workflow technique (how to actually use a hybrid analog rig creatively rather than just as a mixer).

## Techniques

- **The Mini Styler was deliberately designed to be foolproof for live use**: unlike the more versatile-but-complex Sherman Filter Bank (which can be misconfigured into producing no audible signal), every knob position on the Mini Styler is guaranteed to pass sound — critical for live performance, where you can't afford to hunt for where the signal went mid-set.
- **Parallel wet/dry bus routing**: insert the Mini Styler on a bus while simultaneously running the same channels straight to master in parallel, blending the processed/wet signal against the original dry signal to keep transient punch while adding character.
- **Treat an improvised hardware performance as raw material, not a finished take**: record ~30 minutes of hands-on playing into separate DAW stems (including wet effect signals on their own tracks), then switch into an editing mindset afterward to find and condense the best ~5 minutes — explicitly two separate creative modes (performing vs. editing) that shouldn't run simultaneously, since self-editing mid-performance disrupts creative flow.
- **A DJ crossfader repurposed for stems, not full tracks**: assign each channel to the X side, Y side, or "always through," turning a physical crossfader into a compositional tool for one arrangement's own elements.
- **Post-fader vs. pre-fader recording**: post-fader bakes live fader/crossfader moves directly into the recorded audio (capturing a human feel no manually-drawn automation reliably replicates); pre-fader captures the unprocessed signal when that's what's actually needed.
- **Standing at physical hardware vs. sitting at a screen is treated as a real performance-feel factor**, not just ergonomics — likened to why an electric guitar is played standing.
- **Parametric midrange EQ as a deliberate design choice**: "the mid is where the action happens in music" — modern mixes are described as commonly low-end/high-end heavy with a real dip in the middle; the console's fully parametric mid band is built specifically to make restoring (or deliberately scooping) that midrange easy.
- **Subtle saturation as mix glue, distinct from audible distortion**: a "+30dB, past 11" boost stage on some preamp modules, pushed gently (well short of obvious distortion), adds harmonics that blend a mix together without necessarily reading as saturation to the listener.
- **Console architecture behind the character**: discrete hand-soldered op-amps (a "70s-style" saturating design for the first gain stage, a clean high-slew-rate design to properly drive the output transformer) paired with Lundahl transformers and +26 dBu of headroom, voiced by ear over 3 years of listening sessions rather than a purely spec-driven process.
- **History**: Rodec (founded 1958 by Roger De Clercq, originally building bar amplifiers) built one of the earliest crossfaders (to seamlessly switch a bar's audio between radio and jukebox) and was the first to put 3-band EQ on DJ mixers in the 1980s — this console's EQ circuit is literally still based on the 1986 Rodec design.
