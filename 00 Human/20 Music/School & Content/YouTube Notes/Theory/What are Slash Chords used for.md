# What are Slash Chords used for?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LWNSkemOeBE)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LWNSkemOeBE)

## Synopsis

Explains the two distinct jobs a slash-chord symbol does in notation — marking a chord inversion, or acting as shorthand for a more complex extended chord — plus a related-but-different notation, the polychord.

## What's taught

- **Use 1 — inversions.** A slash chord (`chord/bass note`) is the standard way chord charts notate an inversion without writing "1st inversion"/"2nd inversion": the player just reads "play this chord, put that note on the bottom." **1st inversion** (3rd in the bass): C/E — *Use Somebody* by Kings of Leon (C → C/E → F progression); *All I Want for Christmas Is You* by Mariah Carey (G → G/B). **2nd inversion** (5th in the bass): C/G — *God Only Knows* by The Beach Boys (opens D/A). **3rd inversion** (only possible on a 4+ note chord, e.g. a 7th chord): Bm7 with A in the bass — the opening chord of *Ain't No Mountain High Enough*.
- **Use 2 — shorthand for a more sophisticated chord.** Sometimes the bass note isn't even in the chord above it — e.g. D/G is really a G major 9 missing the 3rd, notated as a slash chord because it's easier for a player to read as "D chord, G bass" than to parse "Gmaj9 (no 3rd)." Real sheet-music examples: L'Age d'Or ("Never Too Much," as referenced) opens on a run of slash-notated 9sus4/11 chords; *Rock With You* by Michael Jackson's published sheet music notates its E♭m9-Ab11-Bb11 progression as slash chords (G♭/A♭, A♭/B♭) rather than spelling out the extension names.
- **Line clichés as a slash-chord use case**: a descending chromatic bass line under a static upper chord (e.g. Am → Am/G♯ → Am/G → Am/F♯ → Am/F) technically produces a sequence of chords with awkward "true" names (G♯ augmented, C major 2nd inversion, F half-diminished missing the 3rd, F major 7 missing the 3rd) — slash-chord notation sidesteps that complexity and makes the pattern's logic (one chord, descending bass) visually obvious. Example: Bob Dylan's *Ballad of a Thin Man* (Bm-F♯aug/A♯-D/A-E7/G♯-G), more legibly read as Bm-Bm/A♯-Bm/A-Bm/G♯-Bm/G.
- **Polychords (a related but distinct notation)**: two full chords stacked, one over the other, written with a **horizontal** line (not a diagonal slash) — e.g. a D chord over a C chord. Rare in practice (mostly classical/some jazz, almost never on a pop chart); a well-known example is in Stravinsky's *The Rite of Spring*. Visually similar to a slash chord but conceptually different: D/C (slash) = D chord with just the note C in the bass; D-over-C (polychord, horizontal line) = full D chord stacked over a full C chord.
