# A Simple Step To Level Up Your Rhythm Guitar Playing

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NGTMiuI-Zuw)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NGTMiuI-Zuw)

## Synopsis

Jack Ruch's core pitch here is deliberately small-scale: rhythm-guitar parts built from **diads (2-note chord fragments) and triads (3-note fragments)** rather than full bar chords, because full chords crowd out a bass player, keyboard player, or second guitarist in a band mix. He works the whole lesson over one backing track — a 12-bar blues shuffle in A (A7-D7-E7) — and builds up a single comping part in layers, from bare guide-tone diads to an embellished, drum-mimicking two-register part, ending with a second diad vocabulary built from a different triad's outer notes. It's a leaner, more foundational cousin of his other rhythm-guitar lessons (already covered on this channel): where [[9th and 13th Chord Voicings for Blues Rhythm]] and [[Rootless 9th Chords and Chromatic Movement in Blues Rhythm Guitar]] work from bigger 4-note rootless extended-chord grips, this lesson starts from the smallest possible unit — just the two notes (3rd and ♭7) that actually define a dominant chord's quality — and treats everything else as embellishment layered on top of that core shape.

## What's taught

**1. The guide-tone diad: just the 3rd and ♭7 (~0:30-3:00)**
A dominant 7 chord's four notes are root-3rd-5th-♭7, but only the **3rd and ♭7** actually determine major/minor/dominant quality — Ruch states this explicitly (change the 3rd to minor and it's a minor chord; change the ♭7 to a major 7 and it's a maj7 sound). Since the backing track's bassist covers the root, the guitar part only needs those two notes.
- **A7 grip:** worked as the two notes just outside a bar-chord A7 shape — **♭7 (G) and major 3rd (C♯)**.
- **D7 (the IV chord):** the *identical two-note shape* slides **down a half step** from the A7 grip.
- **E7 (the V chord):** the *identical shape* slides **up a half step** from the A7 grip.
- The whole I-IV-V cycle in the key of A is covered by **one movable 2-note shape**, relocated by half steps: A7 → (down ½ step) → D7 → (back up) → A7 → (up ½ step) → E7 → (back down) → D7 → A7.

**2. Embellishment 1 — sus4 slide-and-raise (~3:00-5:00)**
Layered on top of the same guide-tone diad: **slide into the diad rhythmically**, then **raise the 3rd up a half step to the 4th** (a sus4 color), landing briefly on the suspension before it's implied to resolve back. Applied across all three chords with the same shape (the 3rd's physical position moves — e.g. on D7 the 3rd sits on the *bottom* of the grip, so the raise-to-4th move has to be adapted to that string). On the A chord specifically, an alternative to the plain sus4 hit is grabbing a **full D-triad fragment** and **hammering on** from a lower note up to the 3rd — one more embellishment option layered on the same base shape.

**3. Embellishment 2 — high/low "kick and snare" register alternation (~5:00-9:30)**
Ruch names this explicitly as a technique he uses a lot: alternate **low bass-register notes** (fretted with the thumb wrapped over the neck, or a bar-chord shape) against **high chord stabs on the top two strings**, mimicking a kick drum (low) and snare (high) rhythmically.
- **Low note:** the guide-tone diad in its low-register position (root/3rd-♭7 area).
- **High stab:** just the **top two strings of the bar-chord shape** — concretely, **5th fret of the high E and B strings** = the **root and 5th** of the A chord.
- Build order demonstrated: slide into the low A7 diad → jump up to the high root/5th stab → back down (thumb-wrapped bass notes) → repeat the D-triad hammer-on move from embellishment 1 → another high chord jab.
- **Over the IV chord (D):** instead of the plain diad, Ruch substitutes a **full D9 chord** — described as the same dominant-7 "C-shape" grip (barred/moved up a whole step) **with the 9th added on top** — explicitly called out as "a super common chord to use in blues rhythm stuff." The raised-4th (sus4-style) embellishment from technique 2 layers on top of this D9 grip too.
- The whole two-register part is then played straight through against the backing track to hear it land with the drums and groove.

**4. A second diad vocabulary — outer notes of a different triad, up the neck (~9:30-13:00)**
A distinct closed voicing, deliberately moved out of the low-neck position used for everything above: based on the **A major triad up around the 9th/10th fret**, playing only the **two outside notes — the 5th and major 3rd** (skipping the middle/root note of that triad shape). Same rhythmic slide-in treatment as the earlier diads.
- **A chord:** 5th + major 3rd of A, in this higher position.
- **D chord:** the *identical interval relationship* moved **down one string set** — 5th + major 3rd of D.
- **E chord:** the *identical shape* moved **up a whole step** from the A position — 5th + major 3rd of E.
- Same underlying principle as technique 1 (one small movable shape relocated by scale steps across I-IV-V), but a second, higher-position vocabulary drawn from a different pair of chord tones (5-3 instead of 3-♭7) — gives a genuinely different sound/register option for the same progression rather than just a variation on the first shape.

**5. Stated general philosophy (closing, ~13:00-end)**
Explicitly generalizes beyond this one 12-bar blues example: these concepts (small chordal fragments, guide-tone-driven diads, register alternation, sliding/chromatic embellishment) transfer to "any genre of music, any type of chord progression." The core instruction for applying it on your own: **listen to the track and the other musicians you're playing with, and find the rhythmic spaces** where a small interesting chord fragment fits, rather than following one fixed arrangement.

## Drill (video's own build order — practice it exactly this way)

1. **Bare guide-tone diads only** — play just the 3rd+♭7 shape through the full 12-bar A7-D7-E7 form, moving it by half steps (down to D7, up to E7, back to A7) with no embellishment. Lock this in first.
2. **Add the sus4 slide-and-raise** on top of step 1 — slide into each diad, then raise the 3rd to the 4th, across all three chords (remembering the 3rd's position flips between chords). Optionally add the D-triad-hammer-on variant on the A chord.
3. **Add the high/low register alternation** — layer in the top-two-string root/5th chord stab against the low guide-tone diad (thumb-wrapped bass notes), mimicking kick/snare alternation. Swap in the full D9 grip (with raised-4th embellishment) over the IV chord.
4. **Play the full assembled part** against the backing track through the whole form, listening for how it locks with the drums.
5. **Learn the second (5th + major 3rd) diad vocabulary separately**, up around the 9th/10th fret — same I-IV-V relocation logic (down a string set for IV, up a whole step for V) — as an alternate, higher-register part.
6. **Free-application stage:** apply the same guide-tone-diad-plus-embellishment approach to other progressions/genres, using the stated method of listening to the track and other players to find where a small chord fragment fits, rather than reusing this exact arrangement note-for-note.
