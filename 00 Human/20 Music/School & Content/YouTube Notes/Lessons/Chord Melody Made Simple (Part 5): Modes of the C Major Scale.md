# Chord Melody Made Simple (Part 5): Modes of the C Major Scale

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vt5rQcsYRZU)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vt5rQcsYRZU)

## Synopsis
Part 5 of Jared's bite-size "Chord Melody Made Simple" series (each part = a specific homework assignment building toward arranging anything as a chord-melody / solo-guitar piece in the key of C, open position). This part is about modes — not as abstract theory to memorize, but as the practical tool for chord-melody arranging: knowing a melody note's relationship to the root of the chord currently sounding underneath it (not just its relationship to the parent key) is what tells an arranger which memorized rootless chord shape to reach for. He opens with a real Let It Be-style example (a shape moved across G→Am→F→C→G→F), defines a mode plainly ("a scale whose root is a chord in the key, but not the tonic"), then walks all 7 modes of the C major scale — formulas match the vault's existing [[Diatonic Modes]] table exactly — before giving the actual homework: an up-and-down spoken-number drill for each mode, plus a couple of practice-strategy tips (a baby-step ear-training variant, a "lock onto the closest root" fretboard trick, and a mindset reframe around perfectionism in drilling). Most valuable part for the vault is the *application*, not the mode formulas themselves (already documented) — a full reusable technique note was created from it, see Routed to.

## What's taught
- **Why modes matter for chord melody specifically** — arranging a melody with (often rootless) chord shapes underneath it requires knowing each melody note's relationship to the CURRENT chord's root, not just its degree in the parent scale — that's literally what a mode is (the same 7 notes, renumbered off a different chord tone). Knowing modes this way is not the same as "playing modally" (a style/genre choice, e.g. modal jazz, modal jam tunes, modal psych-rock) — it's a functional-analysis tool usable in any kind of harmony, tonal or modal.
- **Worked Let It Be example (0:00–1:30ish)** — a passage moving G → Am → F → C → G → F. Over the G chord, a melody move he calls "4-5 of G" (notes C→D) — not "1-2 of the C scale," which they also literally are, but renumbering off G's own root is what reveals the real relationship in that moment. The next note is "4 of A minor," which triggers one of his **memorized go-to rootless minor-7 shapes** (melody on the 4th, no root voiced, though the open A string is available as an optional root) — a shape he reaches for systematically any time a minor-7 chord's 4th needs to be on that particular string. From there the same "renumber off the new root" logic continues: "6-5-5 of F," "4-3-4-3 of C," "6-5-6-5 of G," "6-5-6-5 of F" — same physical shapes reused, only the mental root anchor changes chord to chord. This whole reusable pattern (not the specific Let It Be arrangement) is now documented as its own technique — see Routed to.
- **A mode, defined plainly** — a scale where the root is a chord that belongs to the key but isn't the tonic (the "one" chord). Lowest priority: memorizing the Greek names (Ionian, Dorian, etc.) — those are nice-to-know, not the point. The actual point: know there's a two-chord (etc.), and if you renumber the SAME notes of the parent scale off that chord's root instead of off the tonic, you get that chord's mode.
- **All 7 modes of C major, walked with formulas + the up/down homework drill.** Every formula below matches [[Diatonic Modes]]'s existing table exactly (cross-verified, not new info theory-wise) — what IS new is the drill itself: say the scale-degree numbers out loud while physically playing the C-major-scale shape in open position, renumbered relative to each chord in turn.
  - **Ionian, off C (I chord)** — 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 1, back down 1 7 6 5 4 3 2 1. ("We've already done this before" — carried over from an earlier part in the series.)
  - **Dorian, off D (ii chord, Dm)** — 1 2 ♭3 4 5 6 ♭7 1, back down 1 ♭7 6 5 4 ♭3 2 1. Demonstrated with the baby-step note→root variant first (play the melody note, then the D root, to hear the interval before naming it) — noted as sounding "very much... modal" once isolated that way.
  - **Phrygian, off E (iii chord, Em)** — 1 ♭2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7 1, back down 1 ♭7 ♭6 5 4 ♭3 ♭2 1. Called "the hardest one" of the set to say/track.
  - **Lydian, off F (IV chord)** — 1 2 3 ♯4 5 6 7 1, back down 1 7 6 5 ♯4 3 2 1. Flagged as easy to say because it's otherwise straight numeric order except for the ♯4.
  - **Mixolydian, off G (V chord)** — 1 2 3 4 5 6 ♭7 1, back down 1 ♭7 6 5 4 3 2 1. Framed via "how is this different from the major scale" (answer: only the ♭7) rather than memorized cold — the general method for approaching any mode ("what's altered vs. major") is stated explicitly here.
  - **Aeolian, off A (vi chord, Am)** — 1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7 1, back down 1 ♭7 ♭6 5 4 ♭3 2 1. Same natural-minor formula as [[Diatonic Modes]].
  - **Locrian, off B (vii° chord, B diminished)** — 1 ♭2 ♭3 4 ♭5 ♭6 ♭7 1, back down 1 ♭7 ♭6 ♭5 4 ♭3 ♭2 1. Called out as flat on every degree except the 4th, and explicitly the hardest of the seven to play/say descending.
- **Fretboard strategy: lock onto the closest root, not always the lowest one.** When computing a melody note's scale degree relative to a chord, mentally re-anchor to whichever instance of that chord's root sits physically closest on the neck (not necessarily the lowest open-string root) — makes the interval math faster and works regardless of which octave/string the phrase is in. Demonstrated finding 4, ♭3, 2, 1, ♭7, 6, 5 around a mid-neck D by treating that specific D as "home."
- **Two valid entry points for learning to hear/say degrees, and it's fine to mix them** — fretboard-first (you already recognize the shape/interval, e.g. "that's a 4th," and only then attach the number) or name-first (drill the spoken sequence until it's automatic, and the fretboard association builds from repetition). Which is faster depends on what intervals you already know cold; use whichever ladder you already have.
- **Homework/mastery bar and practice-mindset reframe.** Drill: play up and down the C major scale in open position for each of the 7 chords/modes in turn, saying the renumbered scale degrees out loud while playing. No metronome required to start — timing can be added once the numbers come without struggle. Get each one **correct three times in a row** before moving on. The actual advice: reframe "I have to play it perfectly" (which breeds anxiety and avoidance) into "I'm going to practice it perfectly" — full focus, following the drill's own rules — so that an imperfect take is still a completely fine outcome as long as the practice itself was focused. Explicitly names that this kind of drilling can feel unpleasant while you're still weak at it, and that discomfort is normal, not a signal to quit.
