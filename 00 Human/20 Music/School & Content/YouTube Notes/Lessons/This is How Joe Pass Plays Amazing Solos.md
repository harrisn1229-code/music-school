Jens Larsen transcribes and breaks down the opening of Joe Pass's chord-melody arrangement of **"Little Darlin'"** from Pass's *Intercontinental* album (Larsen: "probably one of his best albums if not his best"), working through roughly the first 16 bars bar-by-bar. ## What's taught

### Block-chord melody harmonization — the foundational approach (~0:40–1:00)
- **A chord under every melody note ("block chords"), not selective harmonization.** Pass's core technique for the whole arrangement: rather than harmonizing only strong-beat notes, he puts a full chord voicing under essentially every note of the melody, moving the voicing as the tune's harmony and the melody note both change. → [[Chord Melody Arranging]], [[Chord Voicings]]
- **Song structure sets up where fills go:** each 4-bar phrase breaks down as a 2-bar melodic statement followed by a **sustained note** — and it's specifically that sustained-note space where Pass inserts the connective fills this video spends most of its time on. The takeaway generalizes past this tune: look for the held/sustained notes in *any* melody as the natural slot to add a chord-voicing-derived fill. → [[Chord Melody Arranging]]

### Bars 1–2 (D7 → G7 → Cmaj7): opening harmonization, rootless upper-structure relationship (~1:00–2:00)
- **The tune is in C major but opens on the "dominant of the dominant"** — D7 (V7/V) rather than the tonic — resolving D7 → G7 → Cmaj7, a ii-V-I read a step removed from the tune's actual key center at the very top of the arrangement. *(General starting point: opening a chord-melody arrangement on V7/V instead of I is a cheap way to delay the tonic landing and create forward motion right out of the gate.)* → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Secondary Dominants]]
- **Rootless-voicing same-shape-different-identity move: the D7 bar is voiced as a rootless D9, which — with the root left out — is the identical shape as an F♯ half-diminished (F♯m7♭5) voicing.** Same notes, two valid readings depending on which root you assume underneath; Larsen frames the whole bar as "different variations of this same shape" against the melody's A. Direct instance of the rootless-voicing principle already catalogued on [[Chord Voicings]] (Em7 grip = rootless Cmaj9, same logic one scale degree over). → [[Chord Voicings]], [[Guide Tones]]
- **On the G7 bar, the same one-shape-multiple-melody-notes idea reused:** the melody note (a C in the recording) is harmonized with essentially the same G7 shape as the bar before it, just without the previous bar's top note — Pass moves the melody note without abandoning the underlying voicing, then resolves back to a C on the way into Cmaj7.
- **A flat-9 passing color arrives right before the I-chord landing:** approaching the Cmaj7 resolution, Pass adds a flatted color tone to the G7 — read as **G7♭9** — immediately before resolving up to Cmaj7, the classic altered-dominant-into-major-7 cadential color. *(General starting point: a ♭9 on the V chord immediately before a major-7 I landing is a reliable "make the resolution sound more considered" move — cheap and always available on a dominant chord a half-step from I.)* → [[Altered Chords]], [[Guide Tones]]

### Bars 3–4: the first fill, turning a diatonic chord into a dominant (~2:00–4:00)
- **The first fill is explicitly framed as material generated by moving through the *same* underlying voicing, not a new scale run** — Pass takes us back to D7 by restating essentially the same melodic idea twice in a row: Cmaj7 → F7 → E7 → A7 → back to D7. This is the earliest instance in the video of the "turn a diatonic chord into a dominant" trademark below (E7 stands in for the diatonic iii, Em).
- **Trademark reharmonization move, named directly by Larsen and called out as recurring "at random" throughout Pass's playing (not tune-specific):** Pass frequently reharmonizes a diatonic minor chord — most often the **iii** (Em → E7 in C) — as its own dominant seventh instead of leaving it minor, "because he really loves that pull forward." Larsen states this shows up **inside blues turnarounds too**, landing on the third degree as a dominant rather than resolving plainly back to the tonic. **New home for this device:** [[Secondary Dominants]] § *From videos* below — it's the "borrow V7/vi and just leave it there as a color substitute for the diatonic iii, don't require it to resolve to vi" reading of a secondary dominant, distinct from the already-documented ii-V-to-every-degree drill on that same page. → [[Secondary Dominants]], [[Turnarounds]]
- **A fill built from re-fingering one voicing across the top three strings, not a fresh shape per chord:** Pass reaches a colored extension (read as the 13th) on top of a chord, then simply **moves the top three strings** of that same shape to generate the next few notes of the fill — economical, one-hand-position fill construction rather than jumping shapes.
- **An "ear-surprising" altered move on the E7:** the E7 in this fill carries a ♭9 that Pass then slides up to a sharp color (read as ♯9) — flagged by Larsen as genuinely unexpected because a listener's ear doesn't anticipate an altered E7 with that particular color at this point in the tune. *(General starting point: a ♭9 → ♯9 slide on the same dominant chord is a quick way to add "surprise" tension without changing the underlying harmony — same chord, shifting altered color.)* → [[Altered Chords]]
- **A9♭13 relaxing to a natural extension to keep motion going:** on the A7 that follows, Pass moves from a ♭13 color to a more open extension purely to sustain forward motion into the next chord (F♯ leading into D7) rather than to make a harmonic statement — described as connective, not a "big" reharmonization choice.

### Second phrase, harmonized differently — third-interval voice leading and a two-fret shape shift (~4:00–6:00)
- **A near-identical melodic phrase gets a *different* harmonization the second time through** — same tune, same melody shape, deliberately re-harmonized rather than repeated verbatim; a chord-melody arranging habit worth stealing on its own (don't feel locked into harmonizing a repeated phrase the same way twice). → [[Chord Melody Arranging]]
- **A rootless D7 voicing, moved by a 3rd-interval voice-leading step**, is used to carry the melody down smoothly into the next chord tone — Larsen calls out that this specific interval choice is what makes the line "move nicely in the scale" rather than jumping.
- **Same physical shape, moved two frets, converts F major triad material directly into C7 material** — a concrete "one shape, new root, new chord" move (same underlying logic as the rootless-voicing/upper-structure entries already on [[Chord Voicings]]): a flat-9 color is reintroduced as part of the same shift. → [[Chord Voicings]]
- **A short G7 fill reduced to just two functional notes — root, then the tritone — resolving into a rootless C7 voicing**, described as "quite neat" for how little material it takes to imply the full harmonic motion; Larsen offers a concrete fingering for turning that rootless C7 into a **C7♭9** by adding one note (the color tone) with the pinky, calling the resulting fingering genuinely playable rather than a theoretical curiosity. *(A ghost note in the recording that Larsen believes was intended as a G is flagged directly rather than treated as intentional vocabulary — worth knowing this kind of "almost landed" note happens even in a released, canonical recording.)*

### Bars 9–12 (IV-chord passage): 4ths-interval voicing, and "arpeggiate the voicing instead of a scale run" (~7:30–9:30)
- **Basic diatonic harmony under a slightly fuller melodic statement:** the melody moves to the IV chord and back to I — F6 (with the 6th in the melody, "typical for that period," per Larsen) → a leading B♭7 → resolving down to C6. → [[Guide Tones]]
- **The F6→C6 move is voiced using stacked 4th intervals** rather than tertian (3rd-stacked) voicings — a genuinely different color choice from the shell/drop-2/drop-3/rootless vocabulary already catalogued on [[Chord Voicings]]; worth a look alongside [[Quartal Chord Voicings]] even though this isn't a full quartal system, just a 4ths-flavored passing move. → [[Chord Voicings]], [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]
- **The video's central reusable concept, stated directly by Larsen and explicitly tied back to a chord-melody-fills video he'd published the previous week: turn a *complete chord voicing* into a fill by simply playing (arpeggiating) it, instead of harmonizing a run of individual notes one at a time.** Framed as a genuinely powerful habit for three different contexts at once — chord melody, playing themes, and single-note chord-tone soloing — because it reuses a shape you already know as melodic material "without really doing anything" extra and without needing to harmonize a large number of separate notes by hand. This is the exact "physical execution" idea already documented on [[Chord Melody Arranging]] § *Physical execution: don't harmonize every note* (choosing when to strike a full voicing vs. treat it as a melodic source) — this video gives it a second, independent worked example (an F7-flavored fill built the same way) reinforcing it as a real recurring Pass device, not a one-off. → [[Chord Melody Arranging]]

### Bars 13–16 (repeat, reharmonized): the trademark dominant substitution again, plus a bossa-adjacent reharmonization (~9:30–11:30)
- **The trademark "diatonic chord → dominant" move recurs, this time landing on an E7♯9 that Larsen flags as possibly Pass's own addition rather than something in the original tune** — he says directly he isn't sure whether it's in the "standard" version of the song or something Pass adds himself, which fits the "at random, wherever he wants that pull" framing given earlier in the video. Pass plays this one with tremolo. → [[Secondary Dominants]]
- **A named reharmonization move Larsen explicitly associates with bossa nova even though it shows up here in a swing standard: moving a phrase down onto an A7, where the "expected" ♭13 color is set aside in favor of a natural/parallel extension (read as A7(13)) before resolving to the more expected altered A7 and on to D7.** Framed as a smooth way to connect a "surprising"-sounding A7 to the "expected" one — worth trying as a connective device in other tunes/genres, not just bossa. *(General starting point: when two colorings of the same dominant chord — one altered, one natural — both want to appear back to back, use the natural color as the bridge into the altered one rather than the reverse.)* → [[Altered Chords]]

### Bars 17–20 (approach to the bridge): ii-V harmonization density increases, arpeggiated ii-11 voicing fill (~11:30–13:30)
- **The opening melodic statement returns a third time, now harmonized more densely** because the tune has moved to the middle of the form — instead of the "five of five" (V7/V) reading used at the top, it's now harmonized as a straight **ii-V** (2-7 chords) throughout, landing the melody's 5th, 9th, and root in turn over the D7, then a faster G7 run harmonized the same 2-7 way (3rd, 7th, 9th of the melody in sequence) into Dm7.
- **Arpeggiating-voicings device gets its most concrete worked example: a Dm11 (ii-7/11) voicing is not strummed but arpeggiated as a triplet** — the C, E, and G of the shape played in sequence landing on the third beat, with the rest of the chord filled in afterward, then resolving into G7. Direct, fully-worked instance of the "turn the whole voicing into a fill via arpeggiation" idea from the IV-chord section above — this is the clearest single example in the video of that principle in action and is worth treating as the reference worked example when applying the device elsewhere. → [[Chord Melody Arranging]], [[Chord Voicings]]
- **Another E7-family alteration reading, with an alternate bass reading offered directly by Larsen:** the melody moves up into another altered E7-type shape that Larsen reads as playable/nameable either as an E7-with-altered-color voicing *or*, given the low B♭ actually present, as a **B♭7** instead — an explicit "same notes, two valid chord-symbol readings depending on what you call the bass note" moment, the same ambiguity already flagged on the D9/F♯ø7 bar at the top of the tune. → [[Chord Voicings]]
- **"Circling the tritone" of the A7 — named directly as another recurring, genuinely typical Pass device (not unique to this tune)** — a short melodic figure built around the tritone of the dominant chord before landing; the exact fretwork isn't recoverable from the garbled transcript, but the naming of it as a repeated personal habit is clear and worth flagging as a "listen for this elsewhere in Pass's playing" pointer. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]]
- **The phrase's final fill note is repurposed as the harmonic pivot into the next section:** the last note of the closing melodic fill is simply reinterpreted as a D7, setting up the second half of the tune's form — a cheap, no-extra-material way to launch the next section directly out of whatever the fill was already doing melodically.
