> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **A sustained bass note held while the chords above it change — the tonic or the dominant**
> The held note disagrees with whichever chords don't contain it, and comes back into agreement when they do.
>
> **Q2.** **It's a dominant pedal — sitting on V and refusing to resolve, so the drop is the resolution**
> The technique predates the genre by about four centuries. Naming it is what lets you build one deliberately instead of by feel.
>
> **Q3.** **Tonic pedal for stability under moving harmony · dominant pedal for mounting tension**
> *Rubric (5):* says a tonic pedal holds things stable under moving chords, often under intros and outros (1) · says a dominant pedal builds tension by refusing to resolve (2) · explains that the fixed note creates tension against the chords that don't contain it and releases when they agree again (2)
> The pedal is motion above a fixed anchor, which is exactly the shape of the loop-based problem. Nothing in the bass changes, so nothing has to be rewritten, and yet the relationship between the held note and the chords above it is different in every bar. A tonic pedal makes that relationship read as stability — the ground is never in doubt. A dominant pedal makes the same device read as pressure, because the one thing the ear wants is for that V to move and it doesn't.
>
> **Q4.** **The chords stay put and the bass walks through inversions underneath — `C – G/B – Am – F`**
> The progression is still plain I–V–vi–IV. Only the bottom voice moves.
>
> **Q5.** **Because the ear takes the lowest note as the root**
> *Rubric (5):* says the ear hears the lowest note as the harmonic root (2) · says changing the bass therefore changes the perceived chord even when the notes above are static (2) · notes it's the cheapest way to make four chords feel like eight (1)
> Harmony is not heard as a neutral set of pitches. The bottom voice is privileged: it's what the ear uses to decide what chord it's hearing, so an inversion registers as a new harmonic event even though the pitch content is unchanged. That's why writing a descending bass under four static chords costs one MIDI part and returns something close to the impression of eight chords. It also pairs with the inverse move — hold the bass and change the chords — and the two together cover most of what a loop needs.
>
> **Q6.** **The bass descends C–B–A–F while the chords stay I–V–vi–IV**
> All the movement is in the bottom voice; nothing above it has been rewritten.
>
> **Q7.** **Reharmonize the last bar · insert a secondary dominant · move the bass under static harmony**
> Also available: swap a chord for its parallel major or minor, or open the voicings into sus and add9 shapes.
>
> **Q8.** **Reharmonizing the last bar — the loop resolves differently on every pass**
> Changing where the loop lands changes the whole cycle's shape, which is why one bar rewritten beats three bars fiddled with.
>
> **Q9.** **E7 — the V of Am, so the loop arrives with a leading tone and a tritone pulling into it**
> *Also accepted:* E7 → Am · E major · the secondary dominant V/vi
>
> **Q10.** **They remove or blur the 3rd — the note that decides major or minor**
> With the 3rd out of the way the pad stops colliding with the topline, and the same voicing survives being heard dozens of times.
>
> **Q11.** **Colour without a change of function — the 9th isn't in the triad, so it floats**
> It reads as open and unresolved rather than settled, which is why toplines on the 9th feel more emotive and less final than ones on the root or 3rd.
>
> **Q12.** **Pull the 3rd out of the pad — voice it sus2 or add9 and let the vocal be the only 3rd in the arrangement**
> *Rubric (5):* names sus2, sus4 or add9 as the voicing to reach for (1) · identifies the 3rd as the note being removed or blurred (2) · says the vocal then supplies the major or minor identity, so nothing contradicts it (2)
> Two parts fighting usually means two parts both asserting the same thing. The pad is stating the quality of the chord with its 3rd, and the topline is stating it again a few octaves up, often on a different note of the scale and a fraction behind the beat. Taking the 3rd out of the pad doesn't weaken the harmony — it hands the decision to the vocal, which is the part the listener is following anyway. In practice that's the difference between a bed you stop noticing and one you keep wanting to turn down.
