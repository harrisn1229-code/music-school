# Chords

Part of [[Music Theory]]. Construction hub for every chord family. A chord = several pitches sounded together; its notes are named **root, 3rd, 5th** (+ 7th, 9th… for bigger chords). All formulas below are in half-steps from the root (see [[Notes & Pitches]], [[Intervals]]).

## Triads (3 notes)

| Type | Symbol | Scale formula | Half-steps | C example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Major | C | 1 3 5 | 4 + 3 | C E G |
| Minor | Cm | 1 ♭3 5 | 3 + 4 | C E♭ G |
| Diminished | C° / Cdim | 1 ♭3 ♭5 | 3 + 3 | C E♭ G♭ |
| Augmented | C+ / Caug | 1 3 ♯5 | 4 + 4 | C E G♯ |

- Major = degrees 1-3-5 of the major [[Scales|scale]]; minor lowers the 3rd a half-step
- The 3rd decides major vs minor; the 5th decides dim/aug — details: [[Diminished Chords]], [[Augmented Chords]], [[Unlimited Minor Chords]]

## Beyond thirds: non-tertial harmony
Everything above stacks 3rds ("tertial" harmony — the common-practice default). Three other named families, by stack interval: **secundal** (stacked 2nds → tone clusters, timbral rather than harmonic), **quartal** (stacked 4ths — an early-1900s sound, used deliberately in [[20th-Century Harmony|Stravinsky's neoclassicism]]; guitar fretboard shapes + the *So What* voicing on [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]), **quintal** (stacked 5ths — respells as the same notes as quartal chords, so it doesn't form a separate system). — Toby W. Rush, [[Music Theory for Musicians and Normal People - Enrichment|Music Theory for Musicians and Normal People]], p.18

## From videos

- **A complete named-triad taxonomy — 10 types under a broad "3-note chord" definition, 4 under the strict tertiary (stacked-3rds) definition.** Strict four: major, minor, diminished (minor 3rd + minor 3rd), augmented (major 3rd + major 3rd — symmetric, like diminished). Sus chords: sus4, sus2 (both "suspend" the 3rd away from its resolved position). Three **modal triads**, each named for the mode whose defining color-tone it contains, and each occurring **diatonically on the scale degree matching that mode's position in the diatonic-modes sequence**: **Lydian triad** (sus♯4) on the **4th** degree; **Phrygian triad** (sus♭2) on the **3rd** degree; **Locrian triad** (diminished + ♭2) on the **7th** degree. Odd one out: **major♭5** (mirror of the augmented's raised-5th construction; carries a Lydian-adjacent color since ♭5 = ♯4 respelled). **What falls outside the definition**: a no-3rd chord voicing is conventionally read as an incomplete 7th chord, not a new triad type; a minor triad with a raised 5th is just an inversion of an existing major triad, not a distinct type. **Chord vs. cluster**: "chord" implicitly requires some 3rd-and-5th relationship (even heavily altered) — a stack that's really just a 3rd + a 2nd with no 5th-like relationship is better called a **cluster**; chords are a human labeling *construct*, not a natural category — harmony exists regardless of whether a stack has an agreed name. **Quartal harmony** (triads stacked in 4ths instead of 3rds) doesn't fit the conventional tertiary-based naming system at all. Full breakdown → [[Every Triad chord EXPLAINED]]. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Every Triad chord EXPLAINED]]
- **Why bassists mostly play arpeggios instead of full chords, plus the two triad shapes that cover most bass playing.** Playing several chord notes together on bass "makes a bunch of mud that doesn't work in the average song" — the job is normally left to guitar/keys, and bass instead plays the same chord tones **one at a time** (an **arpeggio**), choosing from the chord's notes to build a line that fits. Exceptions named explicitly: Peter Hook and Marcus Miller do play chords on bass, but that's the exception, not standard practice. **Two triad shapes cover most of it**: the major triad shape (the 1-3-5 of the major scale shape) works over any major chord, the minor triad shape (1-3-5 of the minor scale shape) works over any minor chord — find the chord's root via the musical alphabet ([[Notes & Pitches]]), then lay the matching shape over it. Matches this note's own 1-3-5-skip triad-construction rule above. → [[Notes & Pitches]] (root-finding step), [[Scales]] (source shape the triad is pulled from) — BassBuzz, [[Music Theory for Bass]]
- **Widen a chord voicing by moving only the MIDDLE note of each triad up an octave, leaving root and top note where they were** — a from-scratch gospel/choir sample build used this on a simple vi–I–iii / vii–I–iii–V progression: the resulting chords "look a lot more complex," but the harmony hasn't changed, only the spacing — an open, spread voicing built from one cheap move rather than adding real extensions. Cheapest possible route to a "wider, more emotional" sound on plain triads. → [[Chord Progressions]] — loner, [making a soulful/emotional gospel sample for lil durk & lil baby (turning my vocals into a choir)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nHBEE-uvq4M)
- **Build a triad by half-step counting instead of scale degrees, and flip its quality with one note** — root, up 3 half-steps for the 2nd note, up 4 more half-steps from there for the top note, gives a minor triad (matches the 3+4 formula above); raising ONLY the middle note a half-step flips the whole chord to major (4+3) — the fastest way to toggle a chord's quality without rebuilding it. Practical workflow: find an existing bassline's repeated root note first, then build the chord starting on that exact pitch so chord and bass agree; alternating the same root's minor/major triad across a loop (e.g. minor for bar 1, major for bar 2) gives an instant mood swing with no rhythm change. → [[Chord Progressions]], [[Bassline Writing]] — You Suck at Producing, [You Suck at Producing: Creating Chords](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=F4ykt0tRy0Y)
