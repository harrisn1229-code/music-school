# How to Get the PERFECT Bass Tone

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-77UU4ZzG4c)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-77UU4ZzG4c)

## Synopsis

BassBuzz's Josh (20+ years gigging ska/jazz/funk/prog-Celtic-rock) reframes bass EQ around a sound-engineer in-joke he saw on a T-shirt at a gig: "The 7 Bad System Dwarves" of the frequency spectrum. Five of the seven map onto bass-relevant territory, and the video is built as a myth-busting, no-new-gear-required tour of all five — Tubby (~80 Hz), Muddy (~250 Hz), Honky (~500 Hz), Bitey (~1.5 kHz), and Edgy (~3 kHz+) — each demonstrated as an isolated "bad" sound, then as a problem to fix (which knob/slider to reach for on a basic 3–4-knob amp EQ vs. a graphic EQ like the Darkglass Microtubes 900 V2), then flipped into a *feature*: boosting that same zone (while taming the others) is shown to be most of the recipe for nailing a specific famous bassist's tone — James Jamerson (Motown/"What's Goin' On"), John McVie (Fleetwood Mac/"Rhiannon"), Steve Harris (Iron Maiden/"Power Slave"), Geddy Lee (Rush/"Closer to the Heart"), Tim Commerford (Rage Against the Machine/"Bombtrack"), and a nod toward Nolly/Periphery's modern metal edge. The throughline, stated explicitly near the end, is that **tone is a chain — fingers → strings → pickups → amp settings → EQ** — and EQ has outsized power specifically because it comes *last*: even a perfect chain up to that point can be ruined by bad EQ at the end, and conversely EQ alone (dialing in the right dwarf zones) can get a mediocre setup surprisingly close to an iconic reference tone. Explicitly not sponsored by Darkglass (used throughout as the graphic-EQ demo unit) — the actual pitch at the end is for BassBuzz's own Beginner to Badass course. This is the video teased as the dedicated follow-up at the end of [[How to Fix Your Sh*tty Bass Tone]]'s Link 10 (room/band/PA) — but its actual content turned out to be this frequency-dwarves EQ framework, not the room/trust-your-gear material that was promised there; that specific follow-up content is still unresolved/not covered by this video.

## Techniques

The "Seven Bad System Dwarves," the five relevant to bass, in ascending frequency order — each has a **problem symptom**, a **fix** (both on a basic amp EQ and on a graphic EQ), and a **positive use** (boost it deliberately to chase a specific tone):

- **Tubby (~80 Hz) — rumble/boom.** Symptom: flick the amp on, pluck one note, get a "godawful rumble shaking the stage" that drowns out the kick drum. Fix: **cut the bass knob** on a basic 3–4-knob amp EQ — counterintuitive (it's *called* the bass knob on a *bass* amp) but cutting it removes rumble and clears space for the kick without gutting the low end; on a graphic EQ (Darkglass Microtubes 900 V2), pull the **leftmost slider** down. Positive use — the Jamerson lane: check Marvin Gaye's "What's Goin' On" and the 80–120 Hz range dominates the tone. Recipe: roll the bass's passive **tone knob** all the way down, **boost the bass knob** on the amp, reduce everything else to taste — gets close to that girthy Motown tone with no special gear. Explicit warning: that same super-Tubby setting on a Tool song gets you "a headlock" from an aggressive frontman — it's a deliberate genre choice, not a universal "good" setting. → [[BASS Slot]] (60–250 Hz, this sits at the bottom of it, bordering [[SUB Slot]])
- **Muddy (~250 Hz) — boomy mush, lost note definition.** Symptom: the low end is already tamed (Tubby's handled) but you *still* can't tell what notes are being played — "a big boomy mess of bassy-somethingness," notes mushing together on an intricate line. Fix: roll down whatever knob/slider sits around 250 Hz — "the listed number is just the center frequency, but it affects the whole mud zone," so any nearby control works; **if there's no dedicated low-mid control, the bass knob used for Tubby also helps with Muddy in a pinch** (they're adjacent enough to overlap). Reverse case: a tone that feels thin/lacking fullness can take *more* mud added deliberately for a fuller-feeling bassline — this is a two-way control, not just a cut. Positive use — the McVie lane: John McVie's tone on Fleetwood Mac's "Rhiannon" lives on a knife-edge here — too much 250 Hz and the whole song turns muddy; pull too much out and the bass sounds "weirdly hollow," like it's not filling the space the bass is supposed to fill in the arrangement. Every good bass tone has to find its own balance point with Muddy, not eliminate it. → [[BASS Slot]] (top edge, ~250 Hz) / [[BED Slot]] (bottom edge, 250–800 Hz) — this is the exact boundary between the two slots
- **Honky (~500 Hz) — nasal, "blowing your nose."** Symptom: low end is fine, no rumble, but the tone has an unpleasant nasal quality no matter what — described as "the business end of a pissed off goose." Fix: push the ~500 Hz slider/knob down (Darkglass: the 500 Hz slider). **When there's no dedicated 500 Hz control**, use whatever's closest as a workaround rather than assuming you need new gear — demonstrated live on a bass whose only nearby controls are a low-mid knob at 280 Hz (Muddy's territory, not quite right) and a high-mid at 1200 Hz (Bitey's territory, too high): the actual fix used was the bass's built-in **contour switch**, which gives a cut around **670 Hz** plus a little low-mid cut — "not a perfect fix, but enough to probably de-honk you in whatever room or situation you're dealing with." Explicit myth-bust: forum chatter claims sub-$800 basses commonly honk and implies you need to buy up — the video's stated position is you can get a professional tone out of "basically any gear" once you know how to tame the dwarves; **almost never a real reason to buy new bass gear** to fix a tone problem. Positive use — Honky's alter-ego is **Growly**: harnessed deliberately, this zone gives tasty/snarly/growly tone. Steve Harris (Iron Maiden) on "Power Slave" shows heavy activity right around 500 Hz; recipe = **boost ~500 Hz** + **switch to the neck pickup** (his original P-Bass only had one pickup — the neck; on a multi-pickup bass, solo the neck pickup to get the same effect) — the neck-pickup switch alone visibly dials up energy in the 500 Hz range on a spectrum view, and combined with the EQ boost gets close to Harris's tone (acknowledged as still not identical — "otherwise I'd be out on the road with Maiden"). → [[BED Slot]] (250–800 Hz)
- **Bitey (~1.5 kHz) — harsh, sharp-edged bite.** Symptom: band wants a mellow slow number, but an overly aggressive bass tone "bites horrifically" through the song when it enters. Fix: the **high-mids knob** is the right tool — on a Fender Rumble it's centered at 1.2 kHz, "close enough" to 1.5 kHz to help — pull it down; on a Darkglass, there's a slider right at 1.5 kHz. **No dedicated control** → roll the passive **tone knob** down "maybe like 20%" as a fallback. Caution given explicitly: don't overcorrect while soloing the bass in isolation — a tone that reads as too bitey alone can sit completely fine once the rest of the band is in the mix; judge bite in context, not solo. Positive use — the Geddy lane: Rush's "Closer to the Heart" shows heavy activity around 1.5 kHz compared to the same riff played flat; recipe = **fresh strings** (not dead ones — string age directly affects how much high-frequency content is available to boost) + **the right clacky plucking technique** + **boost Bitey**, i.e. EQ alone isn't enough without the upstream chain (strings, technique) also being right — a direct callback to the "tone is a chain" framing. → [[MIDS Slot]] (800 Hz–2.5 kHz)
- **Edgy (~3 kHz, extending up through ~5 kHz+) — unlistenable top end, clank/hiss.** Symptom: every other dwarf is tamed but the band still cringes — an "unlistenable top end" the video separates into two distinct sub-problems at two different points in this range: **clankiness around 3 kHz**, and **hiss/sizzle at 5 kHz and above**. Fix: the amp's stock treble knob is often the wrong tool — on the Fender Rumble it's centered at **10 kHz**, "kind of ridiculously high for a bass EQ knob" — it'll reduce hiss on top but doesn't touch the clank. A graphic EQ with dedicated sliders does both jobs separately: pull the **3 kHz slider** down to fix clankiness (attack improves, but hissy/clicky top end can remain), then pull the **5 kHz+ slider** down separately to kill the remaining hiss — treated as two separate moves, not one. **No graphic EQ** → the passive tone knob can get a similar effect ("make that less nasty"). Positive use, two lanes: (1) Tim Commerford (Rage Against the Machine) on "Bombtrack" — heavy activity in the 3–5 kHz range gives his brutal, clean, "metallic" cut-through-the-mix sound; recipe = **fresh strings** (max high-end content) + **a nudge to the high-mids** (Bitey territory, right below Edgy) + **boost Edgy**. (2) Modern metal / Nolly (Periphery) territory goes further: **play with a pick** instead of fingers (a pick puts noticeably more energy in the bitey/edgy range than fingers do) plus **distortion** (Darkglass named directly as the go-to) to push all the way into that edgy-metal tone. → [[LEAD Slot]] (2.5–6 kHz)

**The overarching frame (not dwarf-specific):** tone is a **chain** — fingers, strings, pickups, amp settings, then EQ last. EQ's power comes specifically from being the *last* link: it can take a genuinely great upstream chain (cited example: Steve Harris's "brutally clacky P-Bass that cuts through Maiden's mix like a knife," with great fingers/strings/pickups/amp settings) and turn it into "a muffled blob you'd never be able to hear in the song" with bad EQ at the end — and conversely, dialing the dwarf zones correctly is shown across every artist example above to get a mediocre setup surprisingly close to an iconic reference tone.
