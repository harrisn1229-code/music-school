# Songs that use the Circle of Fifths progression

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-DQJmicTFGQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-DQJmicTFGQ)

## Synopsis

Explains why chord progressions that move by the interval of a fifth sound inherently satisfying (the physics/consonance of the perfect fifth), then works through both directions around the circle — anticlockwise (the "circular fifth chord progression" proper) and clockwise — with real compositional case studies, most notably Ray Manzarek's account of writing the Doors' "Light My Fire" intro deliberately around the circle.

## Techniques

- **Why fifth-related chords sound satisfying**: the perfect fifth is (beyond the octave) the most consonant, universally satisfying interval — true whether you're talking melodic intervals, key relationships, or chord-to-chord root motion. Chords a fifth apart are "closely related" and their consecutive use sounds "meant to be."
- **The circle as an analysis tool**: arranging all 12 pitches around a circle so each neighbor is a perfect fifth away gives an at-a-glance map of key/chord relatedness — neighbors on the circle (e.g., C next to F and G) share all but one note and modulate smoothly; opposite sides of the circle (e.g., C and F♯) are maximally unrelated and a jump between them sounds stark/disjointed (not necessarily bad — sometimes stark is the goal).
- **Anticlockwise root motion** ("the" circular-fifths progression) is the default direction people mean by the term.
- **Clockwise root motion** works just as well and is used to bridge otherwise-distant chords smoothly: "Here Comes the Sun" (The Beatles) and "Hey Joe" (Jimi Hendrix) both travel clockwise; in "Hey Joe" specifically, C major and E major are harmonically distant (a direct jump would sound abrupt), but walking clockwise around the circle glides between them "frictionlessly."
- **Deliberate compositional use, not just intuition**: most songwriters land on circle-of-fifths progressions by ear without thinking about the circle explicitly, but some use it as a conscious tool. Ray Manzarek (The Doors) has described, in his own words (quoted from an interview), consciously building the "Light My Fire" keyboard intro to travel around the circle of fifths in imitation of Bach — moving clockwise for the first two chord changes, then anticlockwise from the F chord onward, before a deliberately stark final jump to A minor: *"it was a box circle of fifths as it's called... I knew I was going to end up on an A minor but we had to get to that A minor, so I went from A, G, D-flat, A, and then modulated to the A minor... it just came out of 15, 20 years of music... of practice."*
- **General writing tool**: from any chord, moving up or down a fifth is close to "foolproof" — you can chain several fifth-moves in a row even while straying outside the starting key, and each individual step still sounds consonant; the further you wander this way, the more deliberate you need to be about the fifth-move back home to resolve cleanly.
