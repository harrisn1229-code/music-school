## Synopsis
Jens Larsen breaks down how Joe Pass comps behind a soloist — specifically Ella Fitzgerald, across several of their duo albums together — arguing that Pass "demonstrates pretty much everything you want to know about comping" in that setting and that some online teachers wrongly tell students *not* to copy this vocabulary. The video is built almost entirely around transcribed, timestamped clips from four tunes (**I've Got the World on a String**, **Love for Sale**, **Gee Baby, Ain't I Good to You**, and a brief mention of a jazz-blues chord-solo album) plus a couple of short clips of Pass himself explaining his approach in his own words. Larsen's throughline is **practicality over density**: Pass's basslines, chords, and fills are simple and playable individually — the sophistication is in *when* he reaches for each element, not in any one element being complicated. Four comping devices get worked in detail — (1) a straight quarter-note pulse under simple shell/drop voicings, occasionally splitting the bass note and chord apart for rhythm; (2) a Charleston-rhythm groove for setting up a tune's feel; (3) "chords and walking bass," where Pass plays full chords while also carrying a moving, largely stepwise bass line underneath; and (4) fills, which Pass only plays when the vocalist rests, and which explicitly function as *connective tissue to the next phrase or section*, not decoration. Pass's own stated philosophy, quoted directly, is that the four elements (bass note, chord, melody, fill) are never literally simultaneous — "it's an illusion... you're playing a bass note sometimes, a chord sometimes, the melody most of the time, and you're filling" — and that everything is "done by doing something with motion." Larsen closes with three concrete rules for applying that philosophy (below). Transcript is the channel's own manual English captions (not auto-generated), so text is clean throughout; the handful of sung lyric fragments (marked ♪ in the source) are not chord-accurate and are treated as color, not data.

## Techniques

### Groove and quarter-note comping foundation (~1:00–3:40, over "I've Got the World on a String")
- **Rhythm is the priority, not the chords.** Larsen states it directly: "the most important part of jazz is rhythm," and in a duo behind a soloist the guitarist's first job is a clear, swinging groove — the harmonic content is almost secondary to that. → [[BED Slot]] (comping occupies the harmonic mid layer under a vocalist/soloist)
- **"I've Got the World on a String" is played at a real, difficult "jazz tempo" (slow-medium) using mostly basic shell voicings** — a drop-3 voicing, a shell "bass-one" voicing, and a single drop-2 voicing cover almost the whole tune. Even just the shell voicings alone, played on a **solid quarter-note pulse, chords mostly landing on the beat**, is called "a great take on the song" and a solid foundation to build comping from. → [[Chord Voicings]] §Comping, practically, [[Jazz Comping — Rhythmic Motif and Walking Bassline]]
- **Splitting the bass note and the chord apart is the first rhythmic embellishment on top of the plain quarter-note pulse** — instead of striking bass+chord together, play the bass note, then the chord shape separately, for rhythmic interest without changing the harmony. Demonstrated on an **E♭7** and again on a **Cm** in the same tune. *(General starting point: try this split on any chord that's starting to feel static under a steady quarter-note comp.)* → [[Jazz Comping — Rhythmic Motif and Walking Bassline]]
- **Practice progression suggested by this section:** start with shell voicings alone on a steady quarter-note pulse through a whole tune before adding the bass/chord split, and before adding fills (below) — "don't be afraid to just play some clear quarter notes and use that as a foundation to build on."

### Charleston-rhythm groove (~3:40–4:40, over "Love for Sale")
- **A simple Charleston rhythm (long-short syncopated hit, roughly a dotted-quarter-then-eighth feel across the bar) is used to lay down and set up the groove for the whole tune**, described as "really simple, really basic, but it definitely works" — a second concrete rhythmic template alongside the plain quarter-note pulse above, for when the tune calls for more forward lean. *(General starting point: try both the plain quarter-note comp and a Charleston-style hit on the same tune and pick by feel — the video frames them as two legitimate, contrasting default grooves, not a beginner/advanced pair.)*
- **The first chord of the progression is a B♭7 voiced with no 3rd** — deliberately incomplete-sounding on its own; the missing 3rd shows up one bar later, reused as a **bass note leading into a rootless F7♭13**. Framed as an example of Pass building harmonic movement across bar lines rather than completing every chord in isolation. → [[Chord Voicings]]
- **Turnaround move: B7♯11, functioning as a chromatic upper-neighbor/tritone-flavored lead-back into B♭7** — see [[Turnarounds]] §From videos for the full harmonic breakdown of this exact move.

### "Chords and walking bass" (~4:40–8:00, over "Love for Sale," A section)
🎬 Full technique writeup and cross-linking on [[Jazz Comping — Rhythmic Motif and Walking Bassline]].
- **The technique itself, named directly by Larsen as "not everybody approves of":** play full chords on the beat *and* carry a continuously moving, largely stepwise bass line underneath, rather than choosing one or the other. Explicitly framed as sitting alongside the quarter-note-pulse and Charleston grooves above as a third comping option, not a replacement for them.
- **Construction, worked bar by bar over the A section:** start by playing the **full chord**, then move that same chord shape **up the neck in steps** as the bass line advances; add a color tone (the **♭3rd of the next chord, e.g. D♭**, as **an inversion**) as a connecting note en route to the next chord (**E♭7**); the bass line reads as **"a simple stepwise melody moving from one chord to the next,"** not a set of independent arpeggio choices.
- **Practical exception — skip rather than walk when a full stepwise line isn't worth it:** on the **A♭maj7** bar, Pass plays the A♭maj7 then **skips all the way down to a leading note (A natural)** to catch the following **A♭7♭13**, rather than walking every scale step down. On the following **D♭7** bar he plays the chord, then just **drops to the 5th** as "the most simple bass change you can make" and it "still works as a walking bass." Both are stated as the same underlying rule: **practicality beats intricacy** — "if you can't play it in time, if it doesn't sound good, it's not worth anything."
- **Repeated-note handling:** when a chord repeats or the harmony is only moving by a half step, Pass often **just repeats the bass note** rather than inventing new movement, because the chord change itself (or the half-step motion, e.g. **F down to E down to E♭** on a D♭ chord in this passage) is already "something happening" — repetition here reads as intentional, not lazy.

### Fills — concept and construction (~8:00–12:30, over "Gee Baby, Ain't I Good to You")
🎬 Full technique writeup on the new note [[Comping Fills]].
- **Fills only happen when the vocalist/soloist is resting** — Pass never fills over the top of Ella singing; this is call-and-response spacing, not decoration layered on top of the melody.
- **A fill's job is to connect to the next phrase, not just decorate the current chord** — quoted directly from Pass himself (captured on a separate clip Larsen inserts): "it's gotta go... with the melody... to the next bar." A fill that doesn't lead somewhere is, by Pass's own framing, doing it wrong.
- **Short vs. long fills chosen by how much space is actually open in the form** — a first example is a very short fill (a few notes at the end of a bar, a melodic fragment on the A7 built from **13 down to ♭13 down to the 9th on the D7**); a second example, where there's more open room in the form, is a longer fill that deliberately mixes blues and jazz vocabulary and doubles as a turnaround.
- **Chords are built bottom-up, then colored, to keep momentum without overcomplicating a static harmony:** on a **D7♭9** chord, Pass initially plays only the **lower part of the voicing**, then **adds the high E later** just to keep the sound moving — described explicitly as "a way of staying practical." He does the same move on a **G7**.
- **A passing chord extends a fill into real harmonic motion, not just melodic decoration:** the first fill moves from **C6 to C7** by adding a **G7 passing chord**, resolving up to a "stretchy" **C7 voicing with a high C in the melody**, then via a bass-note melody to a **rootless C7♭9,13**, into the bridge.
- **Bridge passing-diminished move (four-chord form: IV → ♯IV° → I):** in this tune's bridge, Pass moves **F6 → F♯ diminished → Cmaj7**, played as open, spacious voicings behind Ella — see [[Diminished Chords]] §From videos for the fully cross-linked harmonic writeup of this exact move.
- **A longer fill built the same way as the shorter chord technique above — simple base line first, color added after:** the fill starts as "a really simple major blues melody," then color is layered on afterward in a way Larsen calls "quite surprising" — first a plain major 7th (approached by a slide), then a **D♭7 → C7** move, then a run of **C7 chords down to a rootless F7** so the phrase can continue into the next section. Framed as evidence you "could probably spend a lifetime harmonizing blues licks like this."

### Pass's own stated philosophy on doing it all "at once"
- **The four comping elements (bass note, chord, melody, fill) are never actually simultaneous — direct quote:** "A lot of people think you're playing bass notes at the same time you're playing chords at the same time you're playing melody, and it's not like that — it's an illusion. You're playing a bass note sometimes, you're playing a chord sometimes, you're playing the melody most of the time, and you're filling — it's all done by doing something with motion."
- **Three practical rules Larsen distills from that quote and the examples above, for deciding what to leave out at any given moment:**
  1. **If there's a lot happening in the chords, don't add a lot of fills or bass movement.** Worked over the turnaround from "I've Got the World on a String" (**B♭m → E♭7 (repeated) → passing A♭maj7 → landing A♭maj7 → short fill → D♭7 → Cm7 → B7 → B♭sus → A7 → bass melody into A♭7**): once the chords themselves are doing the work — mostly simple 3- and 4-note shell voicings and one drop-3 shape, moving fairly densely — everything else (bassline, fills) is kept deliberately minimal.
  2. **When the bass is moving (walking bass, as in "Love for Sale"), fills and complicated chords get "in the way."** Movement itself is the point of that texture; adding more on top competes with it rather than adding to it.
  3. **Basslines are pragmatic, not maximally melodic** — Pass prioritizes movement and playability over crafting an intricate independent bass melody, per the repeated-note and skip-down-to-a-leading-note examples above. *(General starting point: when building a walking-bass-plus-chords comp, default to the simplest bass motion that still moves — a repeated note or a single leading tone — before reaching for a fully composed bass line.)*
