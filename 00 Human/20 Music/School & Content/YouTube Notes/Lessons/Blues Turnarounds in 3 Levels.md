# Blues Turnarounds in 3 Levels

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fjPyk_11ybo)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fjPyk_11ybo)

## Synopsis

A short, tightly-focused lesson from Jack Ruch on **one classic blues turnaround lick, built up in three levels of density** — beginner, intermediate, advanced — rather than three unrelated licks. Worked entirely in the key of C over a standard I7-IV7-V7 blues (C7-F7-G7), landing where a turnaround always sits: the last two bars (measure 11) of a 12-bar form, resolving toward the V chord that pulls back to the top. Level 1 is a bare two-note lick — a held pedal tone on top against a chromatically descending line underneath. Level 2 layers in a second, ascending line moving in contrary motion against the first (Ruch's own use of the word "counterpoint" — loose, not the formal species-counterpoint sense), turning the lick into a three-note block. Level 3 is the most valuable idea in the video: reinterpret those same three-note clusters as pieces of full chord voicings already sitting under the fingers, which turns the lick into a real chord-melody progression (**C9 - F7 - A♭7 - G**). The video is short and demonstration-heavy (notated tab is paywalled behind Ruch's Patreon, not available here), but the underlying idea — that a single-note lick, a two-voice counterpoint line, and a full chord progression are the same skeleton at increasing density — is clearly and concisely stated and is the real takeaway, more than the specific C-key fingerings.

## What's taught

- **Turnaround placement:** the last two bars of a 12-bar blues (measure 11 into 12), the section that leads to the V chord in bar 12 and pulls the form back to the top. Framed as something worth having "a little vocabulary" of different licks for, so a player doesn't lean on the same one every time.
- **Key/form used throughout:** C7 blues — I = C7, IV = F7/F9, V = G7/G9.
- **Level 1 (beginner):** a held pedal tone **C** (8th fret, high E string — the root of C7) against a **chromatically descending line** on the D string: **B♭ (8th fret, the ♭7 of C7) → A → A♭ → G**, one fret at a time. Picking options given: alternate the two notes, or a pick-plus-finger "claw" that grabs both together. Several unspecified rhythmic-phrasing variations mentioned as available once the shape is clean.
- **Level 2 (intermediate) — counterpoint:** combine the Level 1 descending line with a **second, ascending line** moving the opposite direction — **E (the 3rd of C) walking up to G (the 5th of C / root of G7)** — for genuine contrary motion (one voice down, one voice up, simultaneously). Picking: the pick grabs the lowest note, the middle and ring fingers grab the B string and high E string together, producing a piano-like block-chord hit rather than a scalar run.
- **Level 3 (advanced) — full chords:** the stated big idea — melody, bass lines, and chords are all the same thing, connected through harmony; "if I play a solo it's really just an extension of what's happening underneath in the chords." Reinterpreting the Level 2 three-note clusters as parts of larger chord shapes produces: **C9** (♭7-9-major 3rd-root voicing) → **F7** (the top part of an F7 barre-chord-family voicing, root removed, 5th on top) → **A♭7** (a D-shape-family voicing transposed to A♭, i.e. the ♭VI7) → **G major** (the V chord, resolves the turnaround). Explicitly likened to Joe Pass-style chord-melody playing — described as "dipping your toe in the water" of that approach.
- **Closing note:** Ruch mentions a bonus alternate turnaround (built off the same basic idea) posted separately for Patreon supporters, along with notation/tab for the examples in this video — neither is available from the free video/captions.

Full worked-out drill sequence (loop Level 1, layer in Level 2 as block hits, convert to Level 3's chord progression, transpose to other keys, drop into a real 12-bar form) and the harmonic explanation for why C9→F7→A♭7→G works (♭VI7→V7 half-step pull, the same device as the minor-blues turnaround already in the vault) are written up on the technique page below rather than repeated here.
