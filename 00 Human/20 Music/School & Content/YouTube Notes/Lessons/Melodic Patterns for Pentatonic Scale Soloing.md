# Melodic Patterns for Pentatonic Scale Soloing

📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aTMoxAhqgG8)

## Synopsis
Jared Borkowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared) lays out his top three **melodic patterns** — playing scale notes out of order but in a logical sequence, also called melodic or tonal sequences — for the most common pentatonic scale shape on guitar (the fifth-position box, viewable as either A minor pentatonic or C major pentatonic on the same shape). The lesson opens with the "why" before the "how": scales played strictly up-and-down make improvisation "sound like a scale" rather than a melody, and strict in-order playing only ever teaches you the *next* note in a shape (his **maze view**) rather than the whole shape's layout (his **map view**). This is a short, focused lesson (7:13) with clean spoken captions throughout (no garbling) — three concrete patterns demonstrated on-camera, plus the practice bar he recommends (at least 3 patterns per scale/scale-form, both directions). ## What's taught

**Why melodic patterns matter (0:00–2:30):**
- **Reason 1 — sound.** Playing a scale perfectly up and down and then trying to improvise tends to produce something that "just sounds like a scale." Melodic patterns are one of the main tools improvisers and composers use to make a scale application sound genuinely melodic instead of scalar.
- **Reason 2 — fretboard awareness.** Running a scale strictly in order only ever shows you the *next* note — he calls this the **maze view** (same term reused in his harmonic-minor video, already captured in [[Root-to-Root Scale-Form Mapping Method]]). Breaking the scale into patterns forces bigger jumps, building a **map view** — an overhead sense of every note's location in the shape so you can jump to any of them on demand, not just step to the adjacent one.
- Framed as directly actionable: practicing scales *with* melodic patterns is "exactly how we achieve both of those things" (sounding melodic + fretboard awareness).

**Scale form (2:30–3:00):** the most popular pentatonic box shape on guitar, played in the fifth position — same shape reads as either A minor pentatonic or C major pentatonic depending on which note you treat as root (relative major/minor pair). Explicitly says not to worry about which note is the root while drilling the patterns — get the sequence itself right first.

**Melodic pattern 1 — every other note (3:00–3:40):** skip every other scale note rather than running the shape stepwise. Called a pattern that should generalize to **every single scale** you ever learn, not just pentatonic.

**Melodic pattern 2 — back-neighbor pattern (3:40–4:50):** play a scale note, step back one note, replay the first note — one 3-note unit — then advance to the next scale degree and repeat the unit. Because the very first unit needs a note below it to step back to, start the whole pattern on the **second-lowest note** of the scale form. Also called a pattern to be able to apply to every scale learned, not pentatonic-specific.

**Melodic pattern 3 — five-note pentatonic pattern (4:50–6:30):** explicitly **pentatonic-specific** — "does not need to be applied to other types of scales," unlike patterns 1 and 2. Most commonly used as a descending lick: play 5 notes in one direction (one unit), jump back up the same direction skipping a note, repeat the 5-note unit from there, and continue down the shape; when you run out of room, stop even if the last repetition doesn't complete all five notes. Notes there are two equivalent ways to count/start the pattern that land on the same run. Also often played starting on the second string, and works ascending as well as descending.

**Closing guidance (6:30–7:13):** you don't need to master every possible pattern on every scale ("that would be impossible"), but for any scale/scale-form you actually want to use effectively, aim to play it with **at least 3 different melodic patterns, in both directions** — the explicit "top 3" bar this video sets.
