# Stand By Me: The Sounds I made For The Film

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XcWZJCQGPVE)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XcWZJCQGPVE)

## Synopsis

A second Jack Nitzsche film-scoring story (after Starman), this one about Rob Reiner's *Stand By Me* — and a real "the whole score changed at the last minute" production story: an original score (for the film's working title, *The Body*, after the Stephen King novella) was fully completed and entirely resynthesized-FM on the Synclavier, then scrapped when Reiner decided mid-production to rename the film after its title song and rebuild the score around that theme, in about a week.

## Techniques

- **Frame count scales with a sound's structural complexity, as a practical resynthesis budgeting rule**: the attack portion of a sound typically needs only 3-4 frames, the sustain portion 7-8, and the release a handful — a worked flute resynthesis used 13 total frames across attack/sustain/release. More structurally complex sections (the sustain, where the ear has time to notice detail) get proportionally more frames than the transient attack/release.
- **Resynthesized wine glasses as a melodic texture**: real wine glasses (finger-rubbed rim, tuned by water level, "glass harmonica" style) were sampled and then resynthesized into an FM instrument, giving playable, sustained glass tones used as background chords under the melody.
- **Resynthesized upright bass and flute** (not exact replicas — deliberately "a little unusual") rounded out the re-scored cue's instrumentation, all played live against picture from a simple ~4-track Synclavier sequence.
- **Real-time, director-in-the-room scoring workflow, enabled specifically by having no tape-machine punch-in delay**: the director could sit in and give live notes ("just sustains there," "elongate the melody here," "let it run as a bed for this montage"), and the composer could respond instantly — start/stop the sequencer, solo a single track, transpose a part up or down an octave on the fly — all before MIDI or DAWs existed in film scoring, when this kind of iteration speed wasn't otherwise available.
- **Working without a click track**: this particular cue was scored in free time, watching picture and manually lining up sequencer start points to the action, rather than locking to a fixed tempo grid.
