# Chord Melody Arranging is Miserable (Until You Do It This Way)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vwpRSw5HP1Y)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vwpRSw5HP1Y)

## Synopsis

Jared Borkowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared) walks through the full arranging process he teaches inside his paid course, Chord Melody Magic, building a real chord-melody arrangement of the jazz standard "What Is This Thing Called Love" from its lead sheet, chord by chord, on screen. Where [[Chord Melody Made Simple (Part 5): Modes of the C Major Scale]] (already in the vault) teaches the *live, real-time* method — renumbering the melody note against whichever chord/mode is currently sounding and reaching for a memorized go-to shape — this video is the complementary *slow, deliberate, working-it-out-on-paper* method: a repeatable four-step process (root of the chord → letter name of the melody note → chord-tone number of the melody note → look up/build a voicing with that note on top) applied lead-sheet-line by lead-sheet-line, plus a chord-tone dictionary organized by which guitar string the melody note falls on. The video closes with what's genuinely the most useful transferable idea for playing (not just arranging) chord melody: you don't have to harmonize every note with a full chord grab — deliberately choosing when to play a single melody note versus a full voicing is both easier to execute and sounds better, because it creates space instead of a wall of chords. Several concrete jazz-guitar voicing moves come out of the worked example: a rootless diminished-seventh shape standing in for a dominant 7♭9, a one-note-moves-a-half-step voice-leading link between a half-diminished ii chord and its rootless V7♭9 (a "piano left-hand comping" effect), and rootless altered-dominant (G7alt) shapes built from guide tones plus color tones.

## What's taught

- **Four-step chord-melody analysis process, repeated for every melody note in the lead sheet:** (1) determine the root of the chord from its letter name (G half-diminished → root G; C7♭9 → root C; Fm6 → root F); (2) determine the letter name of the melody note using the "FACE in the spaces" staff-reading shortcut (spaces on a treble-clef staff spell F-A-C-E bottom to top, everything else falls alphabetically between/around them); (3) determine the chord-tone number of that melody note relative to the chord's root — the hard step; (4) look up or build a voicing that has that melody note as the top note. ~0:50–2:00.
- **Two ways to get the chord-tone number in step 3:** a visual interval-number chart anchored on the current root (numbers relationships of every fret relative to that root — he uses [guitarcientist.com](https://guitarcientist.com) to generate these), or counting by ear/hand using the major scale formula from the root (whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half) — "1 2 3 4 5 6 7 1 / 1 7 6 5 4 3 2 1" sung against the fretboard. Counting straight up gets you close (e.g. "some kind of 3") but doesn't tell you major vs. flat — you still need the actual note name to disambiguate. ~2:00–6:30.
- **Chord-tone dictionary organized by melody string, not just by chord type** — for a given chord quality (e.g. half-diminished, dominant 7♭9), the course's voicing dictionary is laid out with separate pages/rows per string the melody note lands on (string 1 / string 2 / string 3), each with 3-note and 4-note versions and a 2-note "melody note isn't a chord tone" fallback. Practical takeaway independent of owning the course: build your own voicing reference the same way — indexed by "which string is the melody on," not just "what's the chord" — because that's the actual lookup you need mid-arrangement. ~6:30–9:30.
- **Guide tones (3rd, 7th) are non-optional; root and the altered/extension tone are interchangeable.** Demonstrated live on the C7♭9 voicing choice: "these two are totally interchangeable, play the root or the flat-9, whatever the melody calls for" vs. the 3rd and 7th, which you can drop only if you're deliberately going for ambiguity — otherwise you lose the chord's quality (major/minor from the 3rd, dominant/major-7 from the 7th). Matches [[Guide Tones]] exactly; this video supplies a fresh worked, in-context example rather than an abstract rule. ~10:30–11:30.
- **Rootless diminished-7 shape used as a C7♭9 voicing** — the most common way to grip a diminished-seventh chord on guitar, deployed here with zero root in the shape, but functioning entirely as a rootless C dominant 7♭9 (all the "important stuff" — 3rd, ♭7, ♭9 — is present). Confirms/extends the existing "a 7♭9 chord is itself a diminished chord with an added root a major 3rd below" relationship already on [[Diminished Chords]] — this is the guitar-shape version of that relationship, chosen specifically because it puts the melody's 5th on top. ~10:00–11:00.
- **One-note half-step voice-leading link between a half-diminished ii and a rootless V7♭9** — moving from the Gø7 shape into the rootless C7♭9 shape, only ONE fretted note moves, down a half step; everything else stays put or is re-struck identically. Produces a "minor 2-5" sound that shifts with a single physical motion and is described as sounding like a piano player's left-hand comping (block chord, one voice moves). Practical notation habit shown: mark a held/sustained note with a square symbol so you know NOT to re-strike it when the rest of the voicing changes underneath it. ~11:00–12:30.
- **Rootless altered-dominant (G7alt) voicings built from guide tones + one color tone at a time**, two variants shown: (root, ♭7, 3, ♯5) and a denser (♭7, 3, ♯5, ♯9) — both G7-altered, chosen by which top note the melody needs. Complements the existing altered-scale-extension framing on [[Altered Chords]] with a concrete comping-shape pair rather than a scale-degree derivation. ~14:30–15:30, ~26:00–29:00.
- **Fretboard-only alternative to the chart lookups (no external resources needed):** memorize the natural half-steps (E–F, B–C; every other adjacent natural note is a whole step), walk the alphabet from any known note to find any other note by ear/hand; then apply the major-scale interval formula from the root to get the chord-tone number without a chart. You still need to know each chord type's own spelling (e.g. half-diminished = root, ♭3, ♭5, ♭7) to build the actual voicing once you know the target chord-tone number. ~15:30–20:30.
- **Deriving inversions by hand, without a chart:** starting from a known voicing, for each note find "the next closest chord tone on the same string, moving down" — do that across all four notes simultaneously and you get the next inversion shape down the neck; repeat again for the inversion below that. A mechanical, repeatable move once you know a chord's spelling. ~20:30–21:30.
- **Melody-first, fill-after technique** — for many notes in the worked arrangement, land the melody note first with a single finger (typically the 4th), THEN add the rest of the chord's notes underneath/around it. Two stated reasons, not just one: it's physically easier to execute (you reach one note, then add fingers, rather than grabbing a whole shape at once), and it sounds better because it leaves a beat of space before the harmony fills in rather than everything landing simultaneously as a block. ~21:30–23:30.
- **The core physical-execution "bonus tip" (the video's headline promise): you do NOT need to harmonize every melody note with a full chord shape.** Deliberately alternate single melody notes and full chord grabs through a phrase — demonstrated as single-note → chord → single-note → chord and other patterns through the arrangement — which is both drastically easier to play (fewer full-hand jumps between shapes) and sounds better (creates space/phrasing instead of a wall of block chords). Framed explicitly as a per-note artistic choice, re-decidable every time you play the piece, not a fixed rule — "you are the artist." Works even solo/unaccompanied: playing mostly single melody notes with occasional chord shapes still reads as fully harmonized. ~21:30 onward, reiterated through the full walkthrough.
- **A concrete "glide" execution move for repeated/sustained melody notes across a chord change:** play the first chord shape, glide the 4th finger down two frets while the melody note holds, sound that note alone with the 4th finger, then land on the next full chord shape with the same finger now in position. Makes a chord-shape-jump physically smoother because the finger doing the gliding is already positioned for the next chord's top note. ~4:30–5:00.
- **Explicit easy-vs-hard tradeoff decision, stated out loud as a modeling example for arrangers:** a C7 with a ♭9 voiced by barring plus reaching the pinky up is flagged as "physically quite difficult," with an easier alternative (same chord, no ♭9) shown right alongside it — and the deliberate choice to keep the harder version in the arrangement anyway for a fast passage, reasoning "the more you know, the better" (know the fuller-color option exists) balanced against "who's going to notice" for a very quick moment. Framed as the kind of judgment call arrangers make constantly. ~29:30–30:30.
- **Ending-chord vocabulary:** the arrangement resolves through a rootless G7-altered voicing (3, ♭7, ♭9, ♯5) into a 6-9 chord (6th, 9th, 5th, root — no 3rd or 7th) as the final tonic, described as "very much an ending chord," followed by a 5-1 cadence tag. ~30:30–31:30.
