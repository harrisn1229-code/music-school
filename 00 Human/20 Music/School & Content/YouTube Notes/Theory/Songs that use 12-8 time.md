# Songs that use 12/8 time

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WlU9z0xt768)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WlU9z0xt768)

## Synopsis

12/8 (compound quadruple, "sounds like a complex/strange time signature but is actually very common") is explained as the source of the "shuffle" feel in a wide range of pop/rock, and distinguished carefully from plain 4/4, from 6/8 at slow tempos, and used as a natural home for 6-against-4 polyrhythm.

## Techniques

- **12/8 = 4 strong beats/bar with each dividing into 3 (12 eighth notes total) — equivalent to 4/4 with triplets on every beat.** The shuffle sound comes specifically from the 4×3 grouping, not the raw note count. "bury a friend" (Billie Eilish), "The Way You Make Me Feel" (Michael Jackson), Led Zeppelin's "Dazed and Confused" (slower shuffle). A/B demonstration: playing "bury a friend" straight in 4/4 instead of its native 12/8 audibly loses the shuffle entirely.
- **Density of played 8th notes controls how intense the shuffle feels**: Tame Impala's "Lost in Yesterday" articulates all 12 possible 8th notes per bar for a full-tilt density; Stevie Wonder's "Master Blaster" leaves some out for a more laid-back groove — same meter, different feel purely from note density.
- **The "Purdie shuffle," named and traced through its lineage**: Bernard Purdie's half-time 12/8 shuffle groove (Steely Dan's "Home at Last") directly influenced 70s/80s rock drummers — John Bonham on "Fool in the Rain," Jeff Porcaro (Toto) elsewhere.
- **12/8 vs. 6/8 becomes genuinely ambiguous at slow tempos**: "Oh! Darling" (Beatles) still has side-to-side swing but loses forward momentum at its slower tempo; Lou Reed's "Perfect Day" is transcribed by different people as either 6/8 or 12/8 with no meaningful audible difference — evidence that meter labeling can be a notation-preference choice, not always an objective fact about the music.
- **12/8 as a natural home for a 6-against-4 polyrhythm**: since 12 divides evenly by both 3 and 4, a groove can emphasize 6 beats/bar (hi-hat) against 4 beats/bar (bass) simultaneously without fighting the meter — Tears for Fears' "Everybody Wants to Rule the World."
- **Bohemian Rhapsody's mid-song transition audibly demonstrates the 4/4-to-12/8 shift**: the Opera section (4/4) moves into the Rock section (12/8), and the drums make the shift from straight to shuffled feel audible right at the transition point.
