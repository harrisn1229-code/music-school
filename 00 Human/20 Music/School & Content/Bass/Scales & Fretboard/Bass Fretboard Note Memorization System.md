# Bass Fretboard Note Memorization System

BassBuzz's five-step system for learning every note name on the bass neck without brute-force memorizing all 84 (or more) frets at once. The core idea: memorize a small, high-value **anchor set** first (the "money notes" — the plain letter names most basslines actually use), then layer on three tools that let you **derive** any other note from the nearest anchor instead of memorizing it directly — the musical-alphabet walk (Say and Play), two movable relocation shapes (octave and unison), and a mnemonic-driven map of the dot-marker frets. A fifth step (Bass GPS) is the test/drill that proves the map actually works under recall pressure. Framed throughout with a "lost in the forest" metaphor: money notes = the safe home base, the other tools = ways to navigate out from it and back.

## Step 1 — Money notes (the anchor set)

The **money notes** are the plain letter-name (no sharp/flat) notes within the **first four frets of each string** — the low, most-played register that "supports the band down low," as opposed to the higher, more solo-oriented reaches of the neck. On standard 4-string bass (E A D G):

| String | Open | Fret 1 | Fret 2 | Fret 3 | Fret 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E | E | F | — | G | — |
| A | A | — | B | C | — |
| D | D | — | E | F | — |
| G | G | — | A | — | B |

Read across the strings, the money notes form one continuous alphabetical run with **no gaps**: E F G A B C D E F G A B. **Practice method:** don't try to absorb all four strings at once — take it one string at a time (fully learn open E / F / G on the low string before moving to A), reviewing for just a couple minutes each time you pick up the bass. This small anchor set alone is enough to "trick people into thinking you've memorized the fretboard" long before the rest of the neck is solid, because it's the register most basslines actually live in.

### When to leave the money-note register (the Peter Hook counterpoint)

Money notes are the right register to *anchor* on, not a ceiling to stay under forever. A "rules of bass" breakdown states the standard version of this rule directly — staying down on the low, first-few-frets register is common guidance not just from teachers but from studio sessions, producers, and bandleaders, which is exactly why building this step's anchor set matters so much early on. The break: Peter Hook (Joy Division/New Order) treats bass as a **lead melodic voice**, playing well up the neck past the money-note register — "Love Will Tear Us Apart" is cited as unimaginable without its high-register hook line. **The trick for keeping low-end presence while playing melodically high, in the right key:** weave in **low open strings** underneath the higher line, so the band doesn't lose bottom end even while the bass is voiced way up the neck. **Verdict:** the low-register default is right for supporting a band — it's the register most basslines actually live in, which is the whole reason Step 1 anchors there — but it's a starting point, not a hard ceiling; a strong melodic bass voice can go as high as the part calls for, especially paired with the open-string low-end trick. → BassBuzz, [[The 10 Rules of Bass Playing (That Pros Break)]]

## Step 2 — Say and Play (the musical-alphabet walk)

The bridge between "a few memorized money notes" and "any note on the neck." **Not a memorization drill itself** — it's a *navigation* drill that teaches you to move along the chromatic alphabet on the neck so you can step off a memorized anchor and find a nearby unknown note.

**How to do it:** on one string, play each fret in turn from open to the 12th fret, saying the note name out loud as you look at that fret. **Use sharp names ascending, flat names descending** (the "least clunky" version, per BassBuzz) — e.g. up the E string: E, F, F♯, G, G♯, A, A♯, B, C, C♯, D, D♯, E (12th fret = the octave — same note name, an octave higher, which BassBuzz notes effectively "cuts your load of notes to learn in half" since the fretboard's note layout repeats past the 12th fret). Coming back down: E, E♭, D, D♭, C, B, B♭, A, A♭, G, G♭, F, E.

**Applying it to find an unknown note:** locate the nearest money note, then walk the Say and Play alphabet from there to the target fret. Worked examples from the video: the 3rd fret of the E string is the money note G, so G→G♭ or G→G♯ is one step either direction; the 4th fret of the A string has no money-note anchor of its own, so find the nearest money note (3rd fret A string = C) and walk one more chromatic step to reach C♯.

**Session shape:** Say and Play doesn't need to be run on all four strings every time — one string is enough in a short practice window, paired with a money-notes review.

## Step 3 — Octave and unison shapes (relocating a known note)

Two movable shapes that let you take a note you already know and find the *same* note (or its octave) elsewhere on the neck, without re-deriving it from scratch. Unlike [[Octave and Unison Shapes for Fretboard Navigation]] (the guitar version of this same idea), bass's standard tuning (E A D G, all perfect 4ths, no major-3rd interval anywhere in the string set) means these two shapes are **uniform across every adjacent string pair** — there's no G→B-style exception to watch for the way there is on 6-string guitar.

**Octave shape** — same note name, one octave higher/lower: **up two strings, up two frets** (or the mirror: down two strings, down two frets). Worked examples: 8th fret of the E string (C) → up 2 strings, up 2 frets → 10th fret of the D string (also C). 7th fret of the D string (A) → down 2 strings, down 2 frets → 5th fret of the E string (also A).

**Unison shape** — the *identical* pitch, same octave, on a neighboring string: **up one string, down five frets** (or the mirror: down one string, up five frets). Worked examples: 6th fret of the E string (B♭) → up to the A string, down 5 frets (6 − 5 = 1) → 1st fret of the A string (also B♭). 3rd fret of the D string (F) → down to the A string, up 5 frets (3 + 5 = 8) → 8th fret of the A string (also F).

**Why it matters:** rather than treating the fretboard as an undifferentiated "data dump graphic," these shapes turn a handful of memorized notes into a much larger set of derivable notes — the same relocation logic [[Octave and Unison Shapes for Fretboard Navigation]] documents for guitar's Closest Octave Exercise and cross-string unison finding, just simpler here because bass has no irregular-interval string pair.

## Step 4 — The dot markers as a skeleton map

The fretboard's inlay dots aren't decorative — BassBuzz frames them as a "skeleton" of known landmarks so that, once memorized, **you're never more than one fret away from a note you already know**. Learn one dot fret at a time, using a mnemonic per fret (first letters = the four open strings' note names read in string order E-A-D-G):

| Fret | Notes (E-A-D-G order) | Mnemonic |
|---|---|---|
| Open | E A D G | "Eat and drink grapes" |
| 3rd | G C F B♭ | "Guitarists can frequently be flat" |
| 5th | A D G C | "And drink grapes, captain" (continues the open-string mnemonic) — or "all dogs get cats" |
| 7th | B E A D | "Bead" (spells the word — "in 7th grade you made bead bracelets at camp") |
| 9th | C♯ F♯ B E | "If you C♯, you'll F♯, and then be" (BassBuzz's own acknowledged weakest mnemonic — no clean payoff word) |
| 12th | E A D G | Repeats the open strings — the octave point, same as Say and Play's 12th-fret wraparound |
| 15th+ | mirrors the lower dots | 15th fret = same shape as 3rd fret, 17th = same as 5th, etc. — the dot pattern repeats past the octave the same way note names do |

**Expect this step to take weeks**, not one session — internalize one dot fret at a time rather than trying to hold all five at once.

## Step 5 — Bass GPS (the recall drill / test)

The verification step: prove the map from Steps 1–4 actually works under real recall pressure, not just recognition pressure.

**How to run it:**
1. Look away from the bass.
2. Fret a random note (don't look).
3. Look back at the fretted note and say its name **as fast as you can**.

**Progression:** start restricted to the money-note zone (frets 0–4) only, then expand to the whole neck as accuracy holds up. **Gamify with a metronome:** 4 beats to look away and fret a note, 4 beats to look back and say the name — increase the metronome tempo over time. Treat wrong/slow answers as diagnostic (they flag exactly which zone of the map is still weak) rather than as failures, and track progress by watching the miss rate shrink at a given tempo — same edge-of-ability tempo-pushing logic as [[Interleaved Practice, Chunking, and Deliberate-Practice Drills]] (that page's guitar-side "100%-then-10%-miss" threshold framing applies directly here, though BassBuzz didn't state an explicit miss-rate target for this drill).

## Practice cadence

BassBuzz's suggested rhythm across all five steps: **a couple of minutes reviewing note names every time you pick up the bass**, one string (or one dot fret) at a time rather than the whole neck at once — small, frequent reps over one long cramming session. Once the map is solid, apply it *while playing actual songs* (calling out note names as you play basslines) rather than running memorization drills in isolation forever — BassBuzz explicitly warns against "get[ting] stuck doing only fretboard memorization exercises."

## From videos

- **Why the money-note anchor set stops at three naturals per string, not four — the reasoning behind Step 1's table, spelled out explicitly.** The would-be 4th natural on each string (one more letter past the money zone) always falls on the 5th fret, and the 5th fret of any string is always the same pitch as the open string one string up: 5th fret E (A) = open A; 5th fret A (D) = open D; 5th fret D (G) = open G. You've already got that note "in your bag" from the next string's open note, so there's no separate fret position to memorize for it — Step 1's three-per-string cutoff is a deliberate no-redundancy design, not an arbitrary stopping point. Paired with a live jam application of the money notes: given a guitarist calling out a C–F–G chord progression, root-find each chord inside the money zone (C → 3rd fret A string, the only option; F → two options, 1st fret E string or 3rd fret D string; G → two options, 3rd fret E string or open G string) and, **when more than one fret has the same note, default to the lower-pitched option** — "that's always the safest choice" for a beginner following a jam by ear. → BassBuzz, [[Learn Your Bass Fretboard Notes (Easy Starter Method)]]
