# Harmonic Ear Training

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Intervals]]. The harmonic counterpart to [[Scale Degree Ear Training]] — where that note trains hearing *sequential*, tonally-contextualized notes (scale degrees relative to a tonic), this one trains hearing *simultaneous* notes: pulling one note out of a chord, or separating two-or-more notes sounding at once. Jared (Sound Guitar Lessons) draws the line explicitly: **harmonic ear training** = identifying a specific sound among many happening at once; **melodic ear training** = hearing the structure/distance of single notes happening one after another. Both matter and are trained differently.

## Why guitar specifically underdevelops this

Guitar's frets let you find/verify any note by looking at your hand instead of listening for it — you tune once, then navigate by sight for the rest of the session. Fretless/bowed string players (violin, cello, upright bass) have no such shortcut: from the first lesson, every note has to be intonated by ear, so the skill is forced rather than optional. This is a structural fact about the instrument, not a talent gap — a guitarist who also plays a fretless instrument (or switches to one) typically reports a sudden, forced jump in harmonic-ear ability, because for the first time listening isn't optional. — Jared, [[Important Ear Training Exercise for Guitarists (we need this!)]]

## Exercise 1 — Chord-tone isolation test

An unstructured, "every so often" gut-check rather than a scheduled drill:

1. **Strum a chord quickly** — not slowly/broken-out. Hearing notes ring individually as you strum defeats the point; the test is separating a genuinely simultaneous sound.
2. **Silently choose a target note before playing** (e.g. "the note on the 3rd string"), so you're testing deliberate isolation, not just noticing whatever pops out.
3. Strum the chord, isolate the target note by ear, then **sing it**.
4. **Check** by playing that string alone right after singing.
5. **Total failure at first is the correct, expected result** — it means the brain is being forced to listen in a way it hasn't had to before, not that the ear is "bad."
6. **If you can't isolate it at all, give yourself the answer**: play the target note alone, then go back into the full chord and listen for it now that you know what you're hunting for. Repeat (target → chord → target) until it starts popping out.
7. **Middle notes are the hardest** — top and bottom notes of a voicing are comparatively easy; buried inner voices take the most repetition. Train outer notes first, then hunt middle notes specifically.
8. **A note "not being heard" ≠ inaudible** — it means attention hasn't yet been trained to grab onto it. One note (often the loudest-feeling, not necessarily loudest in volume) dominates attention by default; the skill is consciously pushing past the dominant note to isolate a different, chosen one. Jared describes the moment it clicks as feeling like "a whole other dimension" — genuinely different subjective access to the same physical sound.
9. Extend to isolating notes low-to-high, high-to-low, or any specific note anywhere in a voicing, and apply the same test live inside a song you're playing: pause and ask "can I hear the individual notes in this chord?"

**Motivating case for why this matters, not just as an ear drill**: Jared discovered — by accident, arpeggiating a barre chord, not by ear in the moment — that a D7-shaped barre voicing he'd been playing was missing its own supposed-to-be-there color tone (the 3rd-string note he was meant to be barring) and had never noticed. Barre-chord shapes get trusted blindly; deliberately checking a voicing's chord tones by ear (rather than assuming the shape delivers them) catches this class of silent failure. — [[Important Ear Training Exercise for Guitarists (we need this!)]]

## Exercise 2 — The two-note interval ladder (structured, milestone-gated)

A scheduled ~5 min/session drill, methodical by design (contrast Exercise 1's casual gut-check framing):

1. Work through **every interval type in ascending order**: half-step (minor 2nd) → whole step (major 2nd) → minor 3rd → major 3rd → perfect 4th → tritone → perfect 5th → minor 6th → major 6th → minor 7th → major 7th → octave — the same physical shapes and order as [[Interval Ladder]] (half-step = 5 frets total on a string pair, one fret smaller crossing the B string, per that drill's B-string-break rule).
2. For each interval: **drill the shape until comfortable**, then **play it in random neck positions**, isolate and **sing both notes** (low, then high), then **check each against the fretted note**.
3. **Octave-transposition trick**: if the two notes sit outside your comfortable vocal range, sing the same pitch class down an octave rather than skipping — still counts, still trains recognition.
4. **Milestone gate: 10 correct in a row before advancing to the next interval.** A miss resets the streak to zero. This is the same milestone-gate philosophy [[The Practice Priority Pyramid]] later names explicitly (Milestones/Rotation/Variation) — this video predates that framework by name but already applies it.
5. **Framed explicitly as slow, months-long progress** — compared to physical fitness ("your ears getting in shape... it takes months"), not a quick fix. 5 minutes a day/session is enough if consistent; this is not a race.
6. **Two side benefits**: (a) the physical drilling doubles as fretboard/interval-shape practice — same shapes [[Interval Ladder]] trains for spatial recall, here with an ear-training/singing layer stacked on top; (b) real-world listening starts to feel "more three-dimensional" as individual notes inside recorded music start popping out, even before any formal interval-naming knowledge (major 3rd, etc.) is consciously applied — this drill is about *separating* two simultaneous notes, not quizzing interval-name recognition.
7. **Extending past two notes**: once the two-note ladder is solid, apply the same isolate-and-sing method to **three-note structures** — any 7th-chord shape, or a plain 1-3-5 triad — broken apart note by note. Flagged by Jared as a likely future video, not covered in depth here.
