# What makes a song sound like Christmas?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=siZiLymWW4A)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=siZiLymWW4A)

## Synopsis

Traces sleigh bells' history (originally a functional warning device on horse-drawn sleighs, not an inherently "festive" sound) to argue that genre-signaling instrumentation works through pure **association via repetition and exclusivity**, not any inherent musical quality of the sound itself — a genre-coding thesis with broader application than just Christmas music.

## Techniques

- **The core thesis: a "genre sound" is created by associative conditioning (repetition + seasonal/contextual exclusivity), not an inherent sonic property.** Sleigh bells appear in plenty of non-Christmas pieces (cited: Prokofiev's "Troika" from *Lieutenant Kijé*, Mahler's "Schlittenfahrt," and — per the video's argument — "Air Bag" and "God Only Knows," both featuring sleigh bells without reading as remotely Christmassy) and only became genre-coded because listeners hear them almost exclusively during a narrow yearly window. General extractable principle: a sound reads as "belonging" to a genre/mood mainly because of WHEN and HOW OFTEN a listener has heard it in that context, not because of an inherent acoustic property — the same instrument can be genre-neutral or strongly coded depending purely on listening history.
- **A quantified trend in how deliberately songwriters lean into existing genre signifiers**: sample of 139 popular Christmas songs — sleigh bells present in 38% pre-1970, 67% post-1970, 77% in the last 20 years — read as evidence that producers increasingly and deliberately copy recognizable instrumentation from the existing genre canon specifically to make a new song read as "fitting in," rather than the association happening organically each time.
- **A related, more personal mechanism for how any specific song (not just an instrument) becomes genre-coded**: a songwriter's own example — a novelty toy that only played Manfred Mann's "Do Wah Diddy" every Christmas at his grandparents' house as a child permanently linked that specific unrelated song to Christmas for him — the same "narrow contextual exclusivity" mechanism as the sleigh-bells argument, just applied to an entire song rather than one instrument.
- **A practical consequence worth remembering as a production/release-timing consideration**: playing genre-coded material outside its expected exclusive window (Christmas music in late November) is argued to actively weaken the association/effect — "it starts to break that spell" — suggesting genre-coded material's impact is partly protected BY its scarcity/timing, not independent of it.
