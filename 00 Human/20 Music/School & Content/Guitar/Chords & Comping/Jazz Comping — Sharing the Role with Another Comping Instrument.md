# Jazz Comping — Sharing the Role with Another Comping Instrument

Part of [[Jazz Harmony]] — the **ensemble-etiquette** companion to [[Jazz Comping — Rhythmic Motif and Walking Bassline]] (which is about *what* to play once it's your turn to comp). This note is about *whose turn it is* — how two harmony instruments (typically guitar and piano, but the same logic covers vibes or a second guitar) share the comping role in a small group without colliding. Two comping instruments playing fully independently almost always reads as clutter to the listener, so the two players need an explicit or implicit division of labor.

## Assign roles out loud, before playing
The single most reliable fix: **call it at the top of the tune**, the same way a bandleader calls a form. A concrete script: *"you comp the head, I'll comp the first solo, you take the second solo, I'll comp the head out, and you can add fills."* This removes the guesswork entirely rather than leaving it to be negotiated in real time. *(General starting point — assign by section: head / solo 1 / solo 2 / head out, alternating or splitting however the tune calls for.)*
- **Default hierarchy in an unrehearsed jam session:** if there's no time to negotiate, **piano is the default primary comping instrument** and the guitarist defaults to functioning like a horn player — solo when cued, otherwise lay out rather than comp — until/unless the guitarist gets an explicit cue to comp. This isn't a rule about instrument hierarchy in general, just a practical default for a jam session with no rehearsal time.
- With musicians you know well, the explicit call can shrink to a nonverbal cue: one player just starts comping (plays one or two chords), the other player catches on within a bar or two and drops out/switches roles — same negotiation, compressed to real time because the trust is already there.

## Comp in the holes, not on top
When the soloist establishes a **repeating, predictable rhythmic motif**, don't double it — place your comping hits in the *gaps* the motif leaves open. A predictable, repeated idea from the soloist is exactly what makes this possible: by the second or third repetition you can hear where the holes will be and land there instead of on top of the line. This is also framed as the more generally useful comping instinct even outside a two-comper situation: **playing something repetitive and a little predictable gives the soloist a stable surface to build on and interact with** — it reads as more supportive than constantly varying what you play, and a stable comping pattern is also what lets a drummer catch a hit and double it (see below). This pairs directly with the "one repeatable motif, not constant variety" principle already on [[Jazz Comping — Rhythmic Motif and Walking Bassline]] — here it's a *listening* skill (finding the other player's holes) rather than a *producing* one (holding your own motif).
- **Big-band horn kicks as the mental model** for these comping hits/fills — think of the rhythms a horn section would punch in behind a soloist, not a constant harmonic wash. Named explicitly as where these rhythmic ideas come from.
- **The interaction runs both ways through the whole rhythm section, not just soloist↔comper.** A comping hit can be picked up and doubled by the drummer (or vice versa — the drummer originates a hit and the comper catches it), and a bass player or a second comping instrument (vibes was named as an example) can join the same call-and-response. This is why an *unplanned* second comping instrument is described as "a disaster" — it turns a two-way conversation the whole rhythm section can plug into into an uncoordinated crowd.

## Stay out of each other's register ("canceling each other out")
When there's a soloist plus a single comping instrument plus rhythm section, actively track the soloist's register and move opposite it: **soloist goes up → comp lower; soloist goes down → comp higher.** Concretely: watch the neck/hands, and react. This is much easier when a bass player is covering the low end and the comper's only job is comping (not also supplying a bass line) — trying to do both (full harmony + implied bass + register-tracking) at once, e.g. solo guitar comping without a bassist, is harder and gets deprioritized first.
- Going *higher* than the soloist is flagged as the more delicate move of the two — it risks visibly pulling listener attention off the soloist, so it's used more sparingly than dropping lower.
- **Extension awareness is part of the same "stay out of the way" skill, but harmonic rather than registral:** if the other comping voice is playing a specific alteration (e.g. a natural 13), don't simultaneously play a clashing alteration of the same extension (e.g. a ♭13) — track what alteration the other harmony instrument has committed to and don't step on it.

## It's always OK to not play
**Laying out is a legitimate, valued choice, not a cop-out** — including laying out for an entire tune once roles are assigned at the top (e.g. sitting out completely through another player's solo chorus). Framed explicitly through a conversation analogy: in a group conversation, staying quiet for a while while others talk is a sign of confidence, not insecurity; comping works the same way. Not playing also **buys thinking time** — leaving room lets the soloist define what's actually needed moment to moment, which makes it easier to come back in with something that actually fits, rather than reflexively filling every gap. Named reference points for comping-optional/no-piano playing: McCoy Tyner-less late Coltrane, and Jim Hall laying out extensively in duo playing (the Jim Hall / Bill Evans duo album is cited as the model for two harmony players making constant room for each other rather than both filling space).
- **This is a practiced skill, not just a preference — comping habitually makes laying out feel effortful.** A player who mostly performs *without* a second harmony instrument (piano-less trio, for example) gets so used to filling all the harmonic space alone that deliberately laying out in a different context (with a comper present) can feel unnatural at first — the adjustment is staying locked to the form (still tracking where you are) while consciously not filling it.

## Fills as the "other" role
When one player is assigned to comp, the other player's job in the gaps is often **fills**, not silence and not full comping — think of it as the horn-section fill role again (see the big-band mental model above), placed in the space the comping player leaves open.
- **Octaves are a solid default texture for guitar fills** when you're not comping full chords underneath — a simple, clean way to state a melodic fill line without it reading as competing harmony.

## The exception: a rhythm role can run under someone else's comping
Two ways two harmony instruments can sound at the same time without colliding, both because one part stops behaving like "comping" and starts behaving like a rhythm-section pulse instead:

- **Freddie Green–style straight-quarter-note guitar rhythm.** All-quarter-note strummed guitar (the Freddie Green big-band rhythm-guitar role) is low-profile and metronomically predictable enough that it functions like a **second drummer laying down the pulse**, not a harmonically assertive comping voice — so the piano is free to comp on top of it without stepping on anything. The style is sometimes looked down on as "just sitting there strumming," and in a big band it's often nearly inaudible in the mix, but it locks the rhythm section together and is described as "the best thing in the world" when it's working. Because the shapes involved are simple, almost any chord fits, and the rhythmic role is completely predictable — the same "very defined, predictable role" logic that makes comping-in-the-holes possible above.
- **Pre-arranged unison hits instead of improvised comping.** Write the hit ahead of time so both instruments land it together — an arrangement decision, not a real-time comping negotiation. Diana Krall's band is cited as using arranged hits regularly; besides solving the two-comper collision problem, it's also just a general way to make an arrangement feel tight.

## Groove-carrying instrument depends on the style — don't assume guitar always sits back
For a **straight-eighth-note tune (bossa nova given as the example, e.g. "Corcovado"/"Quiet Nights")**, the convention flips from a swing tune: the **guitar carries the recognizable rhythmic groove pattern**, and the piano deliberately stays out of that pulse-carrying role, instead laying down sparser harmony and fills that *answer* the guitar rather than doubling its rhythm (mirrors what's already documented on the production side for this genre — see [[Bossa Nova (João Gilberto)]]'s "Keys — Jobim's answers" pillar: piano/keys comping in the same syncopation as the guitar's stabs, answering rather than doubling). Only one instrument should carry the groove ostinato at a time — if the guitar is playing the pattern, the piano shouldn't also try to play it.
- Contrast explicitly drawn with swing/Freddie Green tunes, where the *rhythm-carrying* role (predictable quarter notes) more often falls to guitar while piano comps more freely on top — same principle (one instrument owns the predictable pulse, the other is free to comp), different genre convention for which instrument owns which role.
