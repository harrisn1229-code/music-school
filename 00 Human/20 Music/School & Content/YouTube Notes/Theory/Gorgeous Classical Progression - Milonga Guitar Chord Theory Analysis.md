# Gorgeous Classical Progression | Milonga Guitar Chord Theory Analysis

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YH6qnXR7LvI)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YH6qnXR7LvI)

## Synopsis
Jared Borkowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared) does a full chord-by-chord and Roman-numeral analysis of the opening progression of **Milonga by Jorge Cardozo**, a classical guitar repertoire piece he'd previously used to teach a left-hand technique (referenced but not covered here). The video has three layers, done in order on purpose: (1) name every chord physically and harmonically, including the extensions created by the piece's constant open-E drone string; (2) trace the voice leading — how little each individual note actually moves between chords; (3) step back and show the underlying Roman-numeral skeleton is a plain **vi–ii–V–i** (6-2-5-1) turnaround, the exact same four-chord shape that opens "Fly Me to the Moon," "All The Things You Are," and "I Will Survive," played live on guitar as proof. The throughline and stated teaching philosophy: naming chords/Roman numerals is something you do *after* the fact to build vocabulary and speed up learning/remembering — the actual music is just notes moving to intentional places, and it's worth being able to do both (name it AND let go of naming it). Strongest material for Nate: the open-string-as-a-free-extension-tone technique (every chord in this progression gets its color from one held open E string, not from adding fingers), the physical-inversion-choice logic for smooth voice leading (each chord voiced so the notes moving are moving the shortest distance), and a clean, concrete worked example of the same vi–ii–V–i skeleton recurring across wildly different genres/eras with completely different surface sound.

## What's taught
- **The four-chord progression, named chord-by-chord (song in D minor):** [~1:20]
  1. **D minor add9** — a D minor triad with the open high E ringing as the 9th. Not a 7th chord (no 7th present) — genuinely an *add* chord, distinct from an extended chord. → [[Add Chords]]
  2. **G minor6, 1st inversion** — physically resembles an E♭ major 7 shape (flat-3 in the bass could read as a familiar shape at a glance), but it's actually G minor triad in 1st inversion with the same held open E functioning as a **major 6th over a minor triad** — i.e. a minor 6 chord, not a major 7. Called out explicitly as a "don't trust the shape, check the function" moment. → [[Chord Inversions]]
  3. **C dominant 9** — a standard root-position C7 guitar shape with the 3rd (E) on the string that carries the open string, letting that same open E double as the chord's 9th instead of its 3rd — same physical family as the standard sixth-string-rooted dominant-7 shape, one note substitution. On the progression's second pass through, this same slot **alters to C dominant 7♭9** (the 9 drops a half step) for extra tension before resolving. → [[Extended Chords]], [[Altered Chords]]
  4. **F major9** (F major triad + add9 shape, but with the 7th present via the open E it's a true maj9, not an add9) resolving to plain **F major7** (same shape, open E drops out of the "add9" reading once the 7th itself is functioning) — back to D minor add9.
  - On the progression's repeat, slot 2 becomes **G minor7, 1st inversion**, and with the same open E ringing on top that reads as **G minor13** (the 6th degree present again, now over a m7 chord instead of a triad, so it's the 13 rather than the 6). → [[Extended Chords]]
  - The turnaround resolves through **A7♭13** (not written A7♯5 — the plain 5th, E, is already present in the voicing from the open string, so the color tone has to be named as the 13 rather than an altered 5th) back to the D minor tonic. → [[Altered Chords]]
- **One open string, reused as a different extension on every chord — the piece's actual "trick."** [~1:20]–[~5:00] The held/ringing open high E string never changes pitch across the whole progression but reads as a *different* functional extension depending on what chord is underneath it: 9th on the i chord, major 6th on the iv chord (making it eerie/ethereal over a minor triad), 9th (then ♭9) on the C7, and the 7th/9th color on the F major chords. Generalizable guitar-voicing technique: **a static open string can supply a chord's color extension "for free"** without adding a fretting finger — the color comes from context (what chord is sounding under it), not from the string itself. → [[Chord Voicings]]
- **Voice leading — physically the smallest possible move between each chord shape.** [~7:40]–[~10:30] Walked chord-to-chord: D minor → G minor (1st inversion chosen specifically because it's the closest physical shape to the D minor voicing) moves one note up a half step, one note stays exactly the same, and the top note moves up a whole step — no note moves further than a whole step. Repeated down the whole progression: from G minor13 into A7♭13, the two notes doing the moving both move down by a half step; between repeats of the D minor → G minor13 move, the top two notes are held as common tones and don't move at all. Stated principle: **every note's destination is intentional — pick the inversion of the next chord that minimizes movement**, and the "gorgeous"/"eerie" quality of the piece comes directly from how little motion each voice actually makes, not from the chord names. → [[Chord Inversions]]
- **Roman numeral reveal: this is just vi–ii–V–i.** [~11:00]–[~12:00] Reframed the same four chords in the relative major (key of F) as vi–ii–V–i, then re-grounded them in the piece's actual home key (D minor) as **i–iv–VII(as V7)... ** — explicitly: D minor is the i (tonic), G minor is the iv, and A7 is the V (the dominant 7 that resolves back to i is what defines the key as minor rather than modal). Framed as the standard textbook explanation of what makes a "minor key" minor: a raised-leading-tone dominant 7 (A7, not A minor) pulling back to the i. → [[Turnarounds]], [[Keys]]
- **The same vi–ii–V–i skeleton, played live as proof, across three very different songs.** [~12:00]–[~13:00] Demonstrated the identical four-chord shape (transposed to fit) underneath: **"Fly Me to the Moon"** (Bart Howard/Sinatra) — melody "Fly me to the moon, let me play among the stars"; **"All The Things You Are"** (jazz standard) — "you are the promised kiss of springtime"; **"I Will Survive"** (Gloria Gaynor) — "at first I was afraid, I was petrified." All three use the exact same underlying progression in the same key relationship even though they sound completely different on the surface — offered as the concrete payoff of learning to hear/name progressions: recognizing the same skeleton under wildly different genres and surface treatments. → [[Turnarounds]]
- **The rest of the piece (not analyzed chord-by-chord) mostly alternates i and V**, returning to the full vi–ii–V–i turnaround "a few times" as a structural anchor — general form observation, not a full-piece breakdown. [~14:00]
- **Stated practice/analysis habit: label the underlying harmony of every piece you learn, on purpose, as a memory/speed tool** — "if I call D minor six, it's going to do that six-two-five-one thing again... labels and categories help us learn music faster and remember it way longer" than relying on muscle memory or remembered sound alone — paired with an explicit caveat that he also deliberately lets go of the labeling and plays from feel, treating it as one tool among several rather than a requirement.
