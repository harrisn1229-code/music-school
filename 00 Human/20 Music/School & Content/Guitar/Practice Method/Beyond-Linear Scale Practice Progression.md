# Beyond-Linear Scale Practice Progression

Seven ways to practice a scale beyond running it straight up and down ("the traditional/linear way"), ordered easiest to hardest. The linear method isn't wrong — it's the correct way to *memorize* a new scale shape in every key — but continuing to drill it after memorization wastes practice time; these seven exercises are what to move to once a shape is solid. Complements [[Scale Sequences in Thirds]] (a related but distinct interval-skipping device) and [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]] / [[Hard, Yet Effective Way To Learn The fretboard]] (round 7 below, already documented in full elsewhere).

## Round 1 — practice over the scale's own chords

Loop the diatonic chords of the scale's key (a loop pedal, or a friend comping) and solo the scale over them, listening for how each scale degree colors each chord — the same ear-focused goal as [[Chord-Tone Soloing]], approached from the scale side rather than the arpeggio side.

## Round 2 — interpolate the root between every scale tone

Credited to Joe Satriani. Play the scale ascending and descending, but insert the root note between every single scale tone (degree → root → next degree → root → …) instead of running the notes in a straight sequential line. Breaks reliance on pure muscle memory — sequential scale runs can be played correctly on autopilot; interrupting the sequence with a note that isn't next in line exposes whether the scale shape is actually internalized or just physically memorized.

## Round 3 — practice in intervals (thirds, fourths, fifths, sixths…)

Run the scale in skipped-interval patterns rather than stepwise — see [[Scale Sequences in Thirds]] for the fully worked-out thirds version of this same idea. Forces explicit recognition of each scale degree's interval quality (e.g. which scale tones form a minor third vs. a major third with their neighbor) in addition to building finger dexterity.

## Round 4 — say the note names aloud while playing

Play the scale while speaking each note's letter name out loud in time. Deceptively hard — trivial in a key with few accidentals (e.g. G), noticeably harder in a key stacked with sharps/flats (worked example: B major, all seven notes carrying accidentals). Framed as one of the most effective fretboard-memorization drills precisely because it forces real-time note-name recall rather than shape recall.

## Round 5 — chord scales (harmonize the scale into moving chord voicings)

Instead of single notes, play the diatonic chords built on each scale degree as you move through the scale — demonstrated as four separate passes: triads, then 7th chords in first inversion, second inversion, and third inversion, each pass climbing the scale. Root movement isn't fixed to the tonic every time — voicings are chosen for how they lay out on the fretboard (starting a shape on a non-tonic root as needed to avoid running out of neck), which is itself a mild fretboard-navigation lesson. Builds chord-voicing vocabulary and stretches finger independence beyond what single-note scale runs train.

## Round 6 — sweep the scale as a chain of arpeggios

Move through a scale by **sweep-picking its diatonic 7th chords** in sequence rather than picking single notes — effectively chaining [[Sweep Picking Arpeggio Shapes]]-style arpeggios up the scale instead of running the scale note by note. Second-inversion and root-position three-string sweep shapes are the preferred/faster-feeling voicings for this; first-inversion three-string sweeps are avoidable if they feel harder to execute cleanly. Mixing up the rhythmic subdivision while sweeping adds further variety.

## Round 7 — diatonic triads climbing the full fretboard (hardest)

**Already fully documented** — see [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]] § *From videos* and [[Hard, Yet Effective Way To Learn The fretboard]]. Ranked hardest here not because it's technically the most demanding exercise on this list (that's round 6's sweeping, by the source's own admission) but because it took the longest to master in every key. Worth noting how key choice changes the drill's difficulty: G is comparatively easy because its diatonic triads resolve cleanly up the neck, while a key like D is harder because the lowest playable triad without open strings turns out to be built on the IV of the key rather than the I, which changes how the whole climb lays out physically.

## Practice sequencing

Work through one round at a time, in every key, before moving to the next round — the whole point is escaping autopilot, and stopping at "good enough in one key" defeats that for any of these seven.
