# 20th-Century Harmony (Impressionism & Neoclassicism)

Part of [[Music Theory]]. Two early-20th-century movements that broke from common-practice harmony in opposite directions — impressionism toward color/mood over function, neoclassicism toward a deliberate return to Classical-era order (filtered through very different techniques depending on the composer).

## Impressionism
Debussy (with Ravel, Satie, Griffes, Respighi, Sibelius, Hanson) drew a deliberate parallel to visual Impressionist painting (Monet) — broad strokes and mood/light over fine detail.

| Technique | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Planing** | Parallel 5ths/octaves moving as whole chord-blocks — a common-practice no-no, used deliberately here |
| **Whole-tone scale** | All whole steps, no half-steps — avoids traditional dissonance entirely |
| **Non-functional harmony** | Chords juxtaposed chromatically, defying Roman-numeral analysis |
| **Augmented triads** | Less tonally "loaded" than diminished triads — used for color, not tension |
| **Chords with added 2nds/4ths/6ths** | Color without harsh dissonance |
| **Uneven harmonic rhythm** | Chords change unpredictably rather than on a regular grid |
| **Pentatonic scale** | Specifically the anhemitonic (no-half-step) 5-note pentatonic |
| **Extended harmonies** | 7ths/9ths/13ths used for color regardless of function — this vocabulary fed heavily into early jazz |

## Neoclassicism
A return to Classical-era restraint after Romantic-era intensity — but the two poster composers demonstrate opposite favored techniques:

**Hindemith's toolkit:**
- **Pantriadicism** — juxtapose major/minor triads with zero traditional functional logic between them.
- **20th-century counterpoint** — dissonance treated freely; melodic/rhythmic independence prioritized over strict Palestrina-style rules (contrast [[Species Counterpoint]]'s much stricter dissonance handling).
- **Pandiatonicism** — combine diatonic melodic lines in multiple voices with no regard for the resulting vertical harmony.

**Stravinsky's toolkit:**
- **Polytonality** — genuinely different keys sounding simultaneously (not just borrowed color — actual key conflict).
- **Quartal harmony** — chords stacked in 4ths/5ths instead of 3rds (see [[Chords]]'s tertial-vs-quartal-vs-quintal-vs-secundal breakdown; fretboard shapes and the canonical *So What* example on [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]).
- **Rhythmic primitivism** — intense, unpredictable accents; complex meters (see [[Time Signatures & Note Values]]); even polymeters (conflicting simultaneous meters).

- **Why 20th-century composers turned to quartal stacking, stated directly and tied to the same tonal-center-avoidance goal already documented above for Stravinsky.** Tertiary/functional harmony dominated Western classical music through the 19th century; in the 20th century, Stravinsky, Ravel, Debussy, Schoenberg and others deliberately began stacking voices in 4ths instead of 3rds specifically to escape the sense of a traditional key center — the goal was more open, ambiguous, dissonant sound, not incidental to the technique. Guitar-fretboard application (shapes, the Miles Davis "So What"/modal-jazz real-world example, and a reharmonization trick using quartal shapes over ordinary tertian chords) fully worked on the new note [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]. → [[Quartal Chord Voicings]], [[Modal Jazz]] — Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared, [[Quartal Harmony - all about quartal chords on guitar]]
- **Stack two full triads (not a slash chord) with unrelated roots, linked only by shared common tones, for a genuinely unsettling poly chord** — from John Williams' Star Wars scoring: two complete triads (e.g. C♯ major spread underneath, E augmented on top) sound together as one bizarre, deliberately non-consonant sonority — NOT the same thing as a slash chord (C/B♭), which is one triad over a foreign bass note. The chords don't need to "mesh" — the goal is dissonant strangeness, and augmented triads on top intensify that (see Augmented, above). What keeps it from reading as pure random noise: **the two triads share 1–2 common tones** (here, C and E appear in all three examples used across the cue) — that shared anchor gives the ear something coherent to hold onto even while everything else clashes. Practical workflow: pick the shared tone(s) first, then freely try triads above/below by ear until something feels "wrong" in the right way — no need to derive it from theory. → [[Chords]] (tertial-vs-quartal-vs-quintal for other non-standard stacks), **Polytonality** above (Stravinsky's genuinely-different-simultaneous-keys technique this is a close relative of) — Max Konyi, [[Star Wars Harmony: 3 Hidden Techniques in John Williams' Music]]
- **Withhold the tonic in root position through an entire walking-chord passage, then land it exactly when the payoff arrives** — building anticipation into a diatonic, functional progression (contrast the poly-chord example above): state the tonic once at the very start, then walk through a long series of chords (hitting vi, iii, and others) that never return to a root-position I, ending on a dominant-function chord sitting on a **pedal 5 in the bass** right before the goal. A few genuinely non-diatonic/chromatic notes creeping in right before that arrival function like a drum fill destabilizing a steady groove — signaling "something's about to happen" without yet resolving. The tonic's return (finally, in root position, at the start of the next big section) reads as a release precisely because it was withheld the whole time. → [[Non-Harmonic Tones]] (pedal tone), [[Cadences]] — Max Konyi, [[Star Wars Harmony: 3 Hidden Techniques in John Williams' Music]]
