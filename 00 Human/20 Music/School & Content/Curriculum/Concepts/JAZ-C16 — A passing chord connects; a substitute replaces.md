# JAZ-C16 — A passing chord connects; a substitute replaces

**Q.** What separates a passing chord from a substitution, and what is the placement rule that keeps the real progression audible?

> [!question]- Answer
> A passing chord **connects two chords that are already in the progression**, and is usually optional decoration — the bass player can keep walking the original changes underneath it and it still works. A substitution **replaces** a chord of the chart, so everyone has to play it.
>
> The rule: **put the passing chord on the weak beat and keep the real chord on the heavy beat.** The ear then tracks the actual harmony and hears the passing chord as colour rather than as a competing chord change.
>
> The exception is a chromatic passing chord written into the harmony itself, which changes the bass motion and does need the whole band.

## Why it matters

It settles the question of who has to know. Decoration you can add alone while comping; a substitution has to be agreed, or the bass and the chords will disagree about what the tune is.

The four families are worth having by name: a **dominant diminished** a half step below the target (C♯dim7 → Dm7, which is a rootless A7♭9); a **chord shape slid a fret** into the target, where you think about the target and not about naming the passing chord; **diatonic** chords inserted for stepwise motion (Dm7 → Em7 → Fmaj7 → G7); and a **dominant**, either a plain secondary dominant or its tritone sub.

## Drill

Take a I–vi–ii–V and fill every transition with a diatonic passing chord, keeping the real chords on the heavy beats. Then play the same passage with the passing chords on the strong beats instead and hear the progression stop reading.
