# Bootsy's Funk Formula — On the One and the Funk Box Shape

BassBuzz's distillation of a formula it credits directly to **Bootsy Collins** (James Brown's JBs, Parliament-Funkadelic, Bootsy's Rubber Band) for making a beginner sound reliably funky with no theory required: **lock a big, loud root note to beat one, then fill the rest of the bar with whatever feels good.** The video is explicit that this isn't the only way to sound funky — it's "a reliable formula to nail that James Brown Parliament sound," a beginner-safe on-ramp rather than a law of funk. Two BassBuzz notes already in the vault ([[Locking In With the Drummer]], [[The 10 Rules of Bass Playing (That Pros Break)]]) both cite "BassBuzz's dedicated Bootsy Collins/'the one' video" without being able to link it — this note (and its source video page) is that video, filling the gap and closing both loops.

## "Everything is on the one" — the anchor concept

- **Bootsy's own framing, quoted directly in the video:** "everything is on the one" — "it's the rhythm of life," "you got to count 1 2 3 bang." The "bang" is the beat-one hit; whatever happens between beats is negotiable, but the return to beat one is not.
- **The felt demonstration the video builds the whole lesson around:** play a single root note (D, 5th fret of the A string) on beat one against a drum groove, then noodle freely in between — fills, random notes, "weird stuff." As long as beat one keeps landing, the groove holds together and "it all resolved and felt good." Stop hitting the one, and the groove immediately reads as unstable, even though nothing else changed — direct, ear-testable proof that beat one is the load-bearing element, not whatever fills the space around it.
- **The physical gesture behind it:** in the classic James Brown/Parliament sound, the drummer hits a big crash on beat one and the bass player hits a big, loud root note in the same instant — sometimes only **every two bars** rather than every single bar, but always landing exactly together.
- **Why this matters more than note choice for a beginner:** the formula gives a fixed, reliable anchor that makes almost any in-between playing "resolve" — freeing a beginner to experiment with fills and passing notes without needing to know *why* a given note will or won't work, because the one is doing the harmonic and rhythmic work of holding the phrase together.
- **Relates directly to [[Locking In With the Drummer]]'s own thesis** that beat one alone isn't proof of being locked in (everyone nails beat one; the nuance is staying locked *through* the bar) — this video is the earlier, more basic half of that argument: before worrying about drift after beat one, first make sure beat one itself is a hard, unmissed anchor. → also the direct source of that note's and [[The 10 Rules of Bass Playing (That Pros Break)]]'s Rule 10 ("play on the one, for God's sake, unless you want to sound like Sting") — both now link back here for the full case.

## The funk box shape — root, 5th, ♭7, octave

A movable four-note fretboard shape for filling in the space around the one, given directly as usable "almost all the time whether it's a major chord or a minor chord" — no need to know the chord's quality first, the same quality-blind promise [[Root-Fifth Bass Lines and the 3-Step Style Rhythm Formula]] makes for the plain two-note root-fifth shape.

**Geometry, relative to a starting root note:**
1. **Root** — the starting note itself.
2. **5th** — up one string (toward higher pitch), up 2 frets from the root's fret.
3. **♭7th** — up two strings from the root, same fret as the root.
4. **Octave** — up two strings from the root, up 2 frets from the root's fret (i.e., 2 frets past the ♭7th, on the same string).

**Worked example in D** (the key used for the video's own groove): root = D, 5th fret of the A string.
- **5th (A)** — D string, 7th fret.
- **♭7th (C)** — G string, 5th fret.
- **Octave (D)** — G string, 7th fret.

**To use it on any song:** find the root by ear or by asking a bandmate, hit that root on the one, then fill the rest of the bar with any note(s) from the funk box built on that root, placed wherever it feels good rhythmically — same "no wrong answer, only does-it-feel-good" permission as the felt demonstration above.

- **How this relates to the vault's existing shape vocabulary:** [[Bass Shapes — The Chunking Principle]] documents five starter shapes (root-fifth, octave, major triad, minor triad, and "supershapes" layering) built on the same fretboard-uniformity logic bass gets from being tuned in perfect fourths string-to-string — this funk box is effectively **Shape 1 (root-fifth) and Shape 2 (octave) fused into one four-note run**, with a ♭7th added as the connective note between them. The quality-blind promise ("works over major or minor") is the same property that note's root-fifth shape has, extended across a bigger four-note shape.
- **Compare to [[Jamerson's Root-Fifth-Octave Exercise]]:** that note's root-fifth-octave formula is the harmonic skeleton of this same shape minus the ♭7 — this video adds the ♭7 as a funk-specific connective tone (a bluesy, dominant-leaning color) that the plainer Motown-style root-fifth-octave formula doesn't include.

## Fingering and plucking pattern

- **Fretting:** because the root and ♭7 share the same fret (two strings apart) and the 5th/octave share the fret two higher (also two strings apart), the natural fretting-hand assignment is **index finger for the root and ♭7, pinky for the 5th and octave** — an index-pinky-index-pinky pattern that mirrors the shape's own fret geometry rather than a standard one-finger-per-fret box.
- **Plucking:** the video plucks this same shape **index-pinky-index-pinky**, using the pinky on the plucking hand too rather than the usual index-middle default — distinct from the standard alternating vocabulary documented on [[String Crossing and Plucking Patterns]] (that note's four approaches are all index/middle-based). Treat this as shape-specific: reach for index-pinky plucking specifically when playing this funk box run, not as a general-purpose replacement for index-middle alternating elsewhere.
- **The underlying groove rhythm** (before the funk box notes are added, played on the root alone) is a syncopated, "and"-count-heavy 16th-note-adjacent pattern plucked with standard alternating index-middle, with the plucking hand realigning back to the index finger at the top of each phrase — the transcript's spoken counting-and-fingering callouts here are dense and partially garbled (auto-caption confidence issue, not a content issue — this is a fast-spoken, music-under-talk passage), so treat the exact hit-by-hit sequence as approximate; the reliable takeaway is "alternate index-middle through the syncopated groove, reset to index at the top of the phrase" rather than a specific counted-out sequence. *(general starting point pending a cleaner source)*

## Practical application — build your own funk groove

1. **Find the root** — by ear, or by asking a bandmate what key/chord you're on.
2. **Hit the root hard on beat one** — every bar, or every two bars for the fuller Bootsy-style crash-and-hit.
3. **Fill the rest of the bar with funk box notes** (root, 5th, ♭7, octave, built on that same root) wherever it feels good rhythmically — no fixed rhythm or note order required, and it doesn't need to line up with any other instrument's part.
4. **The one fixed rule: always resolve back to the one.** Everything else in the bar is negotiable; the downbeat isn't.

This is presented in the video as literally beginner-safe: "as long as you hit that one, you can do whatever you want" in between — the funk box just gives a beginner a pre-approved note set to improvise from instead of guessing blind.
