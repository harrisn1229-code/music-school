# How to recognise Minor key chord progressions by ear

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eXsXPzWyVuk)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eXsXPzWyVuk)

## Synopsis

The minor-key "how it feels" companion to [[How to recognise chord progressions by ear]] — same method, minor-key functions, including several that can appear as EITHER their diatonic-minor OR a chromatically-altered major/minor variant (the five and four chords especially), each with its own distinct character. Closes with a 5-progression graded quiz, all in A minor.

## Techniques

- **Notation convention restated**: like the major-key video, functions are named relative to the major scale even in minor (so the diatonic minor 6th is "♭VI," not bare "VI") — chosen for consistency when a piece blurs between major and minor, even though a pure-minor-scale-relative system also exists and is common elsewhere.
- **i (tonic)** — fully at rest, same test as major I, but darker/more "serious, solemn" in color. Example: "Don't Speak" (No Doubt) opens sitting on i.
- **v (minor, diatonic default)** — wants to resolve home like major V, but "more subdued," less urgent. Example: "Losing My Religion" (R.E.M.), i→v.
- **V (major, chromatically raised — the far more common choice in practice)** — raises the diatonic 7th (borrowed from harmonic minor) specifically to restore V's strong pull. Example: "Für Elise" (Beethoven), i→V(major)→i.
- **iv (minor, diatonic default)** — same patient, eventually-resolving character as major IV. Example: "People Are Strange" (The Doors), oscillates i↔iv.
- **IV (major, chromatically raised, less common than the V case)** — "a sudden ray of sunshine," "sun breaking through the clouds." Example: "Mad World" chorus.
- **♭VI (major, diatonic default)** — a chord with forward "sense of movement," a good place to travel from before returning home. Example: "Californication" (RHCP), i→♭VI.
- **vi (minor, chromatically lowered from ♭VI — rare)** — "spooky, haunted house," "intimidating, dark, disconcerting." Example: The Imperial March ("Darth Vader's Theme," *Star Wars*).
- **ii° (diminished, diatonic default)** — "serious, sinister," typically classical-sounding. Example: "Because" (Beatles), i→ii°.
- **ii (minor, chromatically raised from diminished — borrows Dorian color)** — "whimsical," "curious." Examples: "Light My Fire" (The Doors) solo vamp, i↔ii; "505" (Arctic Monkeys), oscillates i↔ii throughout.
- **II (major, chromatically raised further — rare, "classical")**: "Sing for Absolution" (Muse), i→♭VI→II.
- **♭VII** — "a softer, more folky" alternative resolution to the usual V→i. Example: "Stairway to Heaven" (Led Zeppelin) verse cadence, ♭VII→i.
- **♭III (diatonic default, "an alternative tonic/relative major")** — a secondary resting point, though less final than i itself. Example: "House of the Rising Sun," i→♭III.
- **♭II (Phrygian-colored, "exotic, Arabesque, Middle Eastern")** — Example: "Clint Eastwood" (Gorillaz), largely vamps i↔♭II.
- **♭V/♯IV (one of the rarest possible minor-key chords, "spooky, otherworldly")** — Example: "The Union Forever" (The White Stripes), largely a constant i↔♭V vamp.
- **iii (minor, chromatically lowered from ♭III — very rare, a chromatic mediant)** — a deliberately "these chords don't belong together" clash sound. Example: "Enjoy the Silence" (Depeche Mode), i→iii(minor).
- **I (major tonic in an otherwise-minor piece — "Picardy third")** — resolving a minor piece's ending onto its parallel MAJOR tonic instead, common in Baroque music, still used in modern songs. Example: "Exit Music (For a Film)" (Radiohead) ends on B major despite being in B minor throughout.
- **Closing ear-training quiz (5 graded progressions, all in A minor)**: (1) i–iv–♭VI–V (same progression as Amy Winehouse's "Back to Black"). (2) i–VII7–iv–i. (3) i–ii°–♭III–iv–V (climbs straight up the scale). (4) iv–V–i–♭VII–iv–V–i (doesn't start on i). (5) ♭VI–i–♭VII–♭II–♭VI–i (hardest — doesn't start on i, includes the rare ♭II "Clint Eastwood" chord).
