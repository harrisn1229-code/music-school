> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **The 3rd — replaced by the 2nd (sus2) or the 4th (sus4)**
> With no 3rd the chord is neither major nor minor, which is the whole colour.
>
> **Q2.** **Cadd9 = C E G D · C9 = C E G B♭ D — the add chord has no 7th**
> An add chord is a triad plus one note. A 9 chord stacks through the 7th to get there.
>
> **Q3.** **Nothing is sharpened or flattened — one removes a chord tone, the other adds one**
> Alteration moves a tone a half step. Sus and add leave every tone where it was.
>
> **Q4.** **A chord tone shifted a half step — on a dominant: ♭5, ♯5, ♭9, ♯9, ♯11**
> The movable tones are the 5th and the 9th, sometimes the 11th.
>
> **Q5.** **Do not alter a note the melody is sitting on**
> The alteration and the melody land a half step apart, and the clash reads as a mistake.
>
> **Q6.** **The symbol names a scale, not a fixed note collection, and both point at the same one**
> A dominant symbol says "V chord of a minor key"; that key's dominant scale carries both.
>
> **Q7.** **A major or minor triad plus the 6th degree, no 7th — milder colour than a 7th**
> The 6th adds shade without the tension a 7th brings.
>
> **Q8.** **B D F♯ G♯ — the added 6th is always the major 6th, even on a minor triad**
> "Minor" describes the triad, not the added note. A minor triad with the actual ♭6 is m♭6.
>
> **Q9.** **Am7 — with no bass to anchor a root, both names are defensible**
> Any 6th chord is its relative minor 7th re-sorted. An unaccompanied vocal chord is the real case.
>
> **Q10.** **dim7 is 3+3+3 with four names · augmented is 4+4 with three**
> The interval pattern repeats, so choosing a different note as the root gives the same pitches.
>
> **Q11.** **Buys transposability · costs an audible root, a true inversion, and any function of its own**
> Slide a dim7 shape three frets and it is the same chord — which is exactly why the ear can't find its root.
>
> **Q12.** **Is any note the leading note of what follows? No is a passing chord, yes a dominant substitute.**
> *Rubric (5):* states the test as the leading note of the following chord (2) · no means a passing chord, yes means a dominant substitute that will resolve (1) · gives ♯iv° going to I as the passing case (1) · gives the same ♯iv° going to V as the functional case, because ♯4̂ is the leading note of 5̂ (1)
> The reference material teaches only the passing half, and the passing half is the less useful one. A diminished chord has no intrinsic function, because symmetry took its root away — so the job it is doing has to be read off the chord after it, not off the chord itself. ♯iv° on the way back to I contains no leading note of I, so it is decoration: it walks 4̂ up to 5̂ and drives nothing. The identical chord on the way to V does contain 5̂'s leading note, so it is a functioning dominant substitute standing in for II7, and it will resolve. One shape, two jobs, and the only thing that changed was what came next.
