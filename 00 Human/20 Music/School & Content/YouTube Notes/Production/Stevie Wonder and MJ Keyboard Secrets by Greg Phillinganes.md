# Stevie Wonder & MJ Keyboard Secrets by Greg Phillinganes | NAMM 2024

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=R54QYlk53TM)

## Synopsis

A NAMM-booth demo/interview with Greg Phillinganes (Stevie Wonder's longtime keyboardist, who also played on Michael Jackson's Thriller) walking through the live performance techniques behind the Thriller bassline recreation Anthony Marinelli programmed, then a set of Stevie Wonder-taught CS-80 articulation tricks Phillinganes says he "learned at Stevie Wonder University." Light on settings numbers, heavy on the physical, hands-on performance craft that makes a programmed patch actually sound like a person playing it.

## Techniques

- **Coarse-then-fine tuning method**: dial the pitch roughly into place first ("get it close"), then dial in fine tuning last to land exactly on pitch — a two-pass workflow rather than trying to nail the pitch in one motion.
- **Single-trigger mode used deliberately for phrasing, demonstrated A/B against multi-trigger**: in multi-trigger mode, the filter envelope re-fires on every note in a fast lick, so each note gets an identical hard attack; in single-trigger mode the filter only triggers once per phrase, so subsequent notes in a run keep more of the filter's decayed/settled state — described as the difference between a phrase sounding "too" articulated versus having "finesse" and natural feel. Dropping sustain slightly (not to zero) makes the effect more audible.
- **Woodwind-tonguing analogy for phrasing**: multi-trigger = tonguing every note (each one re-articulated identically); single-trigger = tonguing only the first note of a phrase and letting the rest flow (du-d instead of d-du), which is why classic Stevie Wonder synth-bass parts have that vocal, connected quality.
- **CS-80 ribbon-strip vibrato simulation** (Stevie Wonder's technique, demonstrated live): sustain a held note with one finger on the keybed, and simultaneously use a separate finger to manipulate the CS-80's pitch-bend ribbon strip to simulate vibrato — an unorthodox two-hand-on-one-note move Phillinganes says he'd never seen anyone else think to do. The ribbon is a continuous (not stepped/quantized) pitch-bend strip, which is what makes the simulated vibrato sound natural rather than mechanical.
- **A related one-note "trill" articulation**: hold a sustained note and rapidly re-articulate a lower note against it — framed as the classical-music equivalent of a trill, used as ornamentation rather than vibrato, and heard throughout older Stevie Wonder recordings (cited: "Maybe Your Baby" from *Talking Book*).
