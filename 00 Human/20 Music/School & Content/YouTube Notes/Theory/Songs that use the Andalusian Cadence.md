# Songs that use the Andalusian Cadence

## Synopsis

The dedicated deep-dive on the Andalusian Cadence (i–♭VII–♭VI–V, descending), massively expanding on the brief mention already documented from an earlier Axis-family survey video in this batch — covers harmonic-rhythm variation, the major-vs-minor-V ambiguity, power-chord ambiguity, and the dual major/Phrygian conceptualization used in flamenco.

## Techniques

- **The progression**: i–♭VII–♭VI–V in the minor key, descending stepwise through the first four notes of the scale in the bass/roots — a strong, clear directional "countdown" sound. Wide song range demonstrated across the intro montage (auto-transcript garbles most individual titles beyond recognition here, so not itemized by name from that segment).
- **Harmonic rhythm changes the character of an identical progression**: "Look What You Made Me Do" (Taylor Swift) — one chord per bar (slow, weighty). "Hit the Road Jack" (Ray Charles) — two chords per bar (twice the pace, more driving). "Sultans of Swing" (Dire Straits) — deliberately uneven: rushes through the first three chords, then lingers/rests on the final V chord — creating anticipation before resolution rather than a steady countdown.
- **Major vs. minor V — the "is it really Andalusian" question**: the textbook definition specifies a **major** V chord (borrowed from harmonic minor's raised 7th, since natural minor's diatonic v is minor) — songs using a plain minor v instead are progression-adjacent but debatably not "true" Andalusian cadences. Two songs demonstrate BOTH within the same track: "A Hazy Shade of Winter" (Simon & Garfunkel) — verse uses the minor v, later in the song shifts to the major V for a proper Andalusian cadence. "Genie in a Bottle" (Christina Aguilera) — chorus repeats the progression twice, minor v the first time, major V (with the raised 7th audible in the vocal melody too) the second time.
- **Power-chord ambiguity**: "Citizen Erased" (Muse) — the guitar riff alone uses bare power chords (no third), so it tracks the Andalusian cadence's root contour without technically being major or minor yet; only once Matt Bellamy's vocal enters with the chord thirds does it become a genuine (confirmed minor-key) Andalusian cadence. "The 2nd Law: Isolated System"/"The Globalist" (Muse) — Bennett notes the band has explicitly called "The Globalist" a sequel to "Citizen Erased," and their shared use of this same progression in the same key is read as a deliberate compositional callback linking the two songs. Muse's cover of "The Man with a Harmonica" (Ennio Morricone, *Once Upon a Time in the West*) also uses it, live-show-linked to introduce their song "Knights of Cydonia."
- **Flamenco origin and the dual Phrygian/minor conceptualization**: the "Andalusian" name comes from the cadence's strong association with flamenco music from Spain's Andalusia region. Flamenco musicians sometimes conceptualize the SAME four chords differently than the "i as tonic" framing used throughout this video — treating the *final* chord (the major V, e.g. E in an A-minor-framed version) as the true tonic instead, which reframes the whole passage into the **Phrygian mode** rather than natural/harmonic minor. Same notes, same chords, two valid harmonic interpretations depending on which chord is heard as "home." Example: "Innuendo" (Queen) — the middle section descends through the Andalusian cadence while Steve Howe (Yes) plays a flamenco-styled guitar solo over it, explicitly evoking this dual-tradition sound.
- **Roman-numeral notation note**: same convention discussion as raised in the "Running Up That Hill" video in this batch — how you notate a minor-key progression (with or without explicit flats) depends on whether the ambient key context is already clearly established.
