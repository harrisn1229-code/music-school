# Interval Ladder

Every interval from minor 2nd to the octave, in order, played two ways: **along one string, then across adjacent strings.** Called the "interval hip-hop exercise" where the source learned it.

The **second drill in the library that carries theory content** — the other being [[Full Scale Workout]].

## The ladder

Start on **A, 5th fret of the low E string.** For each interval, play it on one string first, then on a string pair:

| Interval | Semitones | String pair |
|---|---|---|
| minor 2nd | 1 | E + A |
| major 2nd | 2 | E + A |
| minor 3rd | 3 | E + A |
| major 3rd | 4 | E + A |
| perfect 4th | 5 | E + A |
| tritone | 6 | E + D |
| perfect 5th | 7 | E + D |
| minor 6th | 8 | E + D |
| major 6th | 9 | E + D |
| minor 7th | 10 | E + D |
| major 7th | 11 | E + G |
| octave | 12 | E + G |

The string pair widens as the interval does. Semitone counts are the verification — if the fret math doesn't match the column, the shape is wrong.

## How to run it

- **Say the interval name out loud as you play it.** The point is connecting three things — the name, the semitone count, and the shape — and the name is the one that falls off if you don't voice it.
- **Do it in a second key.** Starting from the 5th fret of the low E is comfortable and therefore memorisable as a picture rather than a system. Move it to the A string and the B-string offset changes everything, which is the real lesson.
- **The B string breaks the pattern.** Guitar is tuned in fourths except G→B, which is a major 3rd. Every cross-string interval shape shifts by one fret once the B string is involved. Running the ladder in order is what makes that a known quantity instead of a recurring surprise. → [[Guitar Positions]]
- Five minutes. This is a warm-up, not a workout.

## Why it's different from other interval practice

Most interval work is **aural** (name what you hear → [[Scale Degree Ear Training]]) or **theoretical** (count semitones → [[Intervals]]). This is **spatial** — what an interval looks and feels like under the hand.

Guitarists who skip it end up able to name intervals on paper and unable to find them on the neck, which is the gap that makes transposition and improvisation slow.
