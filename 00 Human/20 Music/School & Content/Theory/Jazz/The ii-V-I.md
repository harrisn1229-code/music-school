# The ii–V–I

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Jazz Harmony]]. The sentence jazz is written in. Learn to see it and most lead sheets stop looking arbitrary.


## The major version

In C major: **Dm7 → G7 → Cmaj7.**

- **Dm7** = D F A C — subdominant function, the setup
- **G7** = G B D F — dominant function, the tension
- **Cmaj7** = C E G B — tonic, the resolution

Why these three and not others: it's the **circle of fifths in miniature**. D → G → C, each root falling a fifth. Root motion down a fifth is the strongest progression in tonal music, and ii–V–I stacks two of them back to back. → [[Circle of Fifths]]

The voice leading is the other half of the reason. Between Dm7 and G7, **two notes stay put and two move by a step.** Between G7 and Cmaj7, the tritone resolves: G7's B and F are a tritone apart, B pulls up to C and F pulls down to E. That's [[Guide Tones]], and it's why the progression feels inevitable rather than merely conventional.

## The minor version

In C minor: **Dm7♭5 → G7♭9 → Cm7** (or Cm6, or CmMaj7).

Three differences from the major, all forced by the minor scale:

- **ii becomes half-diminished.** Dm7♭5 = D F A♭ C. The ♭5 is simply the 6th degree of C natural minor.
- **V keeps its major 3rd but usually gains a ♭9.** G7♭9 borrows from harmonic minor — the raised leading tone is what makes it a functioning dominant at all, and the ♭9 (A♭) is the minor 6th of the key.
- **The tonic is ambiguous on purpose.** Cm7, Cm6 and CmMaj7 are all in use and pick different flavours. Cm7 is the modal/pop choice; CmMaj7 is the classical-sounding one.

## Chains — the thing that makes it worth learning

ii–V–I units chain, and standards are largely built by chaining them through different keys. The most common device: **make the I of one unit into the ii of the next**, or drop a ii–V in front of any chord you want to arrive at.

- **Descending in whole steps:** `| Dm7 G7 | Cmaj7 | Cm7 F7 | B♭maj7 |` — a ii–V–I in C, then one in B♭.
- **Turnaround chains:** `| Cmaj7 A7 | Dm7 G7 |` — the A7 is a secondary dominant setting up Dm7. → [[Secondary Dominants]] · [[Turnarounds]]

**Reading tip that pays for itself:** scan a lead sheet for any m7 chord followed by a dominant 7th a fourth above it. That's a ii–V, and whatever follows is the intended I — even when the I never actually arrives.

## When the I doesn't arrive

Two very common cases, both worth recognising rather than mis-analysing:

- **The deceptive move** — ii–V goes somewhere other than I, usually vi or ♭VI. `Dm7 G7 Am7` instead of `Dm7 G7 Cmaj7`.
- **The unresolved ii–V** — a ii–V that just moves to the next ii–V without resolving at all. Extremely common in bebop heads and the reason a chart can look like it changes key every two bars when it doesn't.

## Guitar shortcut: the power-chord finder

A fast, no-math way to find both halves of a ii–V using nothing but a two-note power-chord shape on the low two strings — the same physical shape used both directions:

- **Chord → its V (or secondary dominant):** fret the power chord with the **bottom note on the root of the chord you're starting from**. The **top note of that shape is its V.** Example: bottom note A (for Am, the vi of C) → top note E, so **E7 is V7/vi** in C. → [[Secondary Dominants]]
- **Dominant 7th → its related ii:** same shape, read the other way. **Bottom note = the dominant chord's root**, **top note = the root of its related ii.** Example: bottom note C (for C7) → top note G, so **C7's related ii is Gm7** (the pair resolves to Fmaj7: `Gm7 C7 Fmaj7`).
- Same trick derives a **backdoor** ii from its dominant: bottom note B♭ (for B♭7, the backdoor dominant of C) → top note F, so **B♭7's related ii is Fm7** — the backdoor 2–5 `Fm7 B♭7 Cmaj7`. → [[Turnarounds]] § *Backdoor*

## Practical use outside jazz

The ii–V–I is why so much neo-soul and jazzy hip hop sounds the way it does. Three ways it shows up in modern writing:

- **As the whole loop.** `Dm7 G7 Cmaj7 Cmaj7` over four bars is a complete, endlessly loopable progression.
- **As the turnaround only.** Straight-ahead pop verse, then a ii–V at the end of the section to push back to the top.
- **Stripped to guide tones.** Play only the 3rds and 7ths and the progression still reads — which is how it survives in arrangements with almost no chordal instrument. → [[Guide Tones]]

- **A major ii–V–I used as a "secondary ii-V" targeting a diatonic non-tonic chord, plus a general "any target chord is a 251 landing spot" reharmonization tool.** Worked in the key of C: **Gm7 → C7 → Fmaj7** is a textbook major ii-V-I, except its "I" (F major 7) is actually the diatonic **IV** of the home key — the whole three-chord unit is dropped in front of F purely to give it more pull and color than landing on it directly. Generalizes to an explicit compositional rule stated in the lesson: given any plain progression (worked example: C → Am), treat the *target* chord as a landing spot and ask "what 251 gets me there?" — for a **major** target, use a major ii-V built a whole step above it (targeting F: Gm7-C7); for a **minor** target, use the minor version (see next bullet) a whole step above it. C13 offered as a free, "prettier" substitute for the plain C7 in the V slot. → [[Secondary Dominants]], [[Chord Progressions]] — Jack Ruch, [How To Break Down Complex Songs and Play Over Changes](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XHNgYFoZMW4)
- **The minor version applied as a "landing spot" device targeting A minor: Bm7♭5 → E7 → Am7** — the same ii°7–V7–i shape this page documents in C minor (Dm7♭5→G7♭9→Cm7), transposed and reframed as a reharmonization tool rather than a fixed key's cadence: given a plain C→Am move, insert a minor 251 a whole step above the Am target (Bm7♭5-E7) to add tension/color before the resolution — "how do I spice this up... seeing these chords as a potential landing spot for a secondary dominant or a 251." Practiced with dedicated CAGED-shape arpeggios per chord, out of time first, then in a timed voice-leading exercise through the changes. → [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]], [[Secondary Dominants]] — Jack Ruch, [How To Break Down Complex Songs and Play Over Changes](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XHNgYFoZMW4)
