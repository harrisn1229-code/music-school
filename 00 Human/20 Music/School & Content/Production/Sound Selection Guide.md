# Sound Selection Guide

Recommended **sounds and tones by role** — the "what sound do I reach for" companion to [[Sound Palette]] (which is about *slots/frequencies*) and [[Sampling and Sound Selection]] (which is about *how* to sample). This page collects specific sound/sample/tone picks pulled from breakdowns via `/music-enrich`, so when you want "a Disclosure-style kick" or "a bass with real front-end attack" you have a shortlist. When a pick names a vintage unit (808, LinnDrum, Juno, SP-1200…), the [[Vintage Gear Index]] has the deep-dive: what makes that sound + the digital versions owned.

## 🥁 Drums

- **Bake a cheap crash cymbal in a crisp-and-vinegar paste to physically corrode it into a lo-fi, near-infinite-sustain crash — a Sylvia Massey (*Recording Unhinged*) recipe.** Strip the lacquer off a cheap crash (Overmono used a Rogers White Hot they knew nothing about, bought nearest-available on Facebook Marketplace) with a wire brush, coat it in a thick paste of blended crisps (~2kg), ~1L vinegar and ground coffee, bake it in the oven overnight, then chip off the crusted coating in the morning. The corroded metal underneath resonates unusually — some hits sustain for close to a minute — which undoes the "science" of normal cymbal manufacturing and gives a deliberately "dead"/lo-fi character used as a track's actual crash, not a novelty effect layer. Their stated reasoning for using a crash at all: crashes "can sound a bit naff" by default, so baking one and getting it "sounding right" was their way of justifying using a crash at all after going years without one. → [[Layering and Stacking]] — Overmono (Tape Notes), [[TN 187 Overmono - Full Video Episode]]
- **A narrow, deliberately-repeated two-source drum signature reused across a whole album, instead of a fresh drum sound per song — "the *Deadbeat* sound."** Kevin Parker (Tame Impala): a Roland TR-808 kick/hit run through a guitar amp for grit, layered with a Vermona DRM1 analog drum synth synced to it — the two blasted/recorded together in the studio in various ways, then combined. Stated goal: make the album recognizable "a block away," the way the first Strokes album is — a small, consistent sonic palette reused rather than varied per track, in deliberate contrast to the more varied drum-sound approach on his prior album (*The Slow Rush*). *(Vermona DRM1 not yet in [[Vintage Gear Index]] — added to worklist.)* → [[Vintage Gear Worklist]] — Kevin Parker (Tape Notes), [[TN 188 Tame Impala - Full Video Episode (Part 1)]]
- **A straight LinnDrum blended against a swung/galloping Juno bass as an entire track's two founding sonic sources — the rhythmic tension between "straight" and "swung" IS the sound-selection decision, not a mix afterthought.** Jack Antonoff, on Sabrina Carpenter's "Manchild": "most of the sonic information is really just coming from the Juno... straight and swung are the spectrum, there's everything in between, the space between is where the magic happens." → [[LinnDrum]], [[Roland Juno-106]], [[Rhythm and Groove]] — Mix with the Masters (Jack Antonoff), [[Behind the Track｜'Manchild' by Sabrina Carpenter]]
- **Reggaeton dembow clicks: pick from ordinary, widely-available online percussion libraries — the individual samples are expected to sound the same as every other producer's, and the genre-defining texture comes from stacking 4+ of them together, not from finding rarer sources.** A reggaeton mixer's stack: a "12-beat" layer for top-end air, the classic four-beat dembow click, a distortion-carrying layer pushing the kick's punch forward, and a layer emphasizing the kick's own low fundamental — none distinctive alone, the sum is the signature dembow texture. Full breakdown on [[Genre Drum Patterns]]. → [[Genre Drum Patterns]], [[Layering and Stacking]] — Mosty (Mix with the Masters), [[Mosty mixing a reggaeton track]]
- **When a producer's delivered drum sounds are already top-notch, the right move is almost none — just balance the blend and leave the sounds themselves alone.** "I didn't do anything to the kicks. The drums I kind of left how they were because they sounded so good when I got them. Like, I just messed with the blend, I didn't change anything that didn't need to be changed." A useful counterweight to this page's more elaborate reinforcement/layering entries — sometimes the correct sound-selection decision is to not touch the selection at all. → [[Mix Handoff]], [[2 Drums Group]], [[Dua Lipa Vocal Chain]] — Mix with the Masters (Josh Gudwin), [[Mixing Dua Lipa's vocals with Josh Gudwin]]
- **A deliberately "crappy"-toned tom sound, coupled onto one snare hit and reserved for a specific section (chorus) rather than run through the whole track — chosen because it amused/reminded him of a "20th Century Fox"-style fanfare stab, not for a clean tone.** Full kit: sparse "one and one" (downbeat) kick + a second "1-and-3" kick pattern, claps layered onto the 2-and-4 backbeat snare, and the character tom doubled onto a second snare. Reached for consciously against a simplicity/minimalism preference — "anything that's just a wonderful foundation for other things to develop is really exciting to me." *(auto-captioning is ambiguous on whether the tom is chorus-exclusive or chorus-absent)* → [[Layering and Stacking]], [[Kick Design]], [[Snare Design]], [[Finneas Drum Sampling Chain]] — Finneas (Mix with the Masters), [[Creative drum sampling with Finneas]]
- **Section-staged drum sample reinforcement on a hit record: a bass drum sample runs the whole song, but every other reinforcement layer (snare, claps, tambourine) is chorus-only.** Tom Elmhirst, mixing Adele's "Rolling in the Deep," reviewing what's layered under the mic'd kit: a bass drum sample under the entire song; a **snare sample only in the chorus** (the verse is a bare stomp with no snare hit to reinforce at all); an extra clap layer bounced up **specifically in the chorus**; and TWO tambourine layers, also chorus-only — a **dry/close** tambourine and a separate, **wider tambourine carrying its own effects** (reverb/width). Everything else is one single mic'd kit — a small, deliberately section-specific set of reinforcement samples rather than a blanket drum-replacement pass. Worth reaching for whenever a chorus needs more weight/size than the verse's kit alone provides: don't reinforce every hit type everywhere, only reinforce what the chorus actually needs, and only there. → [[Layering and Stacking]], [[2 Drums Group|Drums Group]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]] — Mix with the Masters (Tom Elmhirst), [[Tom Elmhirst breaks down his mix of 'Rolling in the Deep' by Adele]]
- **A live percussion part played on an actual glass beer bottle instead of the demo's triangle — suggested by the drummer himself.** "Every time you hear live drums on this record, it's Vincent Taeger playing. On the demo, we made this with a triangle. And when we played it to him, we asked him 'can you play the triangle part' — he listened to this part, and he told us: 'no, we're going to make it with a bottle.' So he asked for a beer to be served to him. So we gave him a beer, and once he drank it, he played it — so that's this part." ### Snare / Clap
- **A snap-and-rim-shot pair as the FIRST drum elements built when flipping a finished sample into a beat — snap trimmed to two hits, rim shot filling the gap between them.** Build order for a 2000s R&B sample flip: snap (trimmed to hit only twice) → rim shot (placed between the two snap hits) → a small hi-hat roll → a "short and stabby" second bass as a switch-up layer. ## 🌫 Texture ([[TEXTURE Slot]])
- **Synthesized thunder/wind recipe: low-frequency-weighted noise through a low-pass filter, WITHOUT resonance for "thunderous," WITH resonance added for more "windy."** The presence/absence of resonance is the deciding factor between the two characters, not the cutoff frequency alone — honestly framed as a "cartoon character version" of the real sound, a simplified punchy stand-in rather than a full recreation. Separately, a jagged-but-not-harsh stepped texture: sample-and-hold noise with its high end rolled off over time by an envelope generator, routed through a lag processor before the filter to round off otherwise-sharp edges (tunable from extreme/jagged to smoothed by how much lag is applied). → [[Synth Patching and Editing Method]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[How I break down ANY sound]]
- **Environmental SFX layering: a continuous ambient bed plus a manually-placed, tastefully-timed accent, treated as two different jobs rather than one "weather sound."** "Riders on the Storm" (The Doors) — a rain loop plays constant throughout as a bed; thunderclaps are separately cued and dropped in/out by hand at chosen moments, per drummer John Densmore's first-hand account of the 1971 session (engineer Bruce Botnick had thunderclap tape machines cued "so we could drop them in wherever we wanted just like God"). The general principle: an ambient texture (continuous, low-attention) and an accent hit (placed, high-attention) inside the same "weather"/environmental sound are two different production decisions, not one layer treated uniformly. → [[Ambient Texture and Field Recording]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [I recreated "Riders On The Storm" by The Doors from scratch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0PSLvxH21B8)
