# 40 Songs that use Descending Stepwise chord progressions

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dyDFcchDl2M)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dyDFcchDl2M)

## Synopsis

A harmonized, step-by-step walk down the major scale in the bass — Bennett surveys how often it appears as a partial excerpt (4, 5, or 6 steps) rather than the full 7-step form, and notes its close family resemblance to the Pachelbel's Canon progression.

## Techniques

- **Full form**: I → (V or iii, in inversion) → vi → (IV or ii, in inversion) → V (the most fixed/consistent step of the whole sequence) → I (1st inversion) → ii → V–I turnaround, every chord voiced so a walking bass note lands on each consecutive major-scale degree in order.
- **5-step is the most common real-world length** — walks from I down to IV (five consecutive bass notes), then the progression moves elsewhere rather than completing the full descent. Example: the verse of "Piano Man" (Billy Joel).
- **6-step**: "Can You Feel the Love Tonight" (Elton John).
- **4-step** (down to V) also occurs, as a shorter excerpt of the same idea.
- **Why it works**: it's literally a harmonized major scale, so it inherits the scale's own maximally predictable, satisfying motion. It's also easy for a songwriter to stumble onto by accident, since each next chord is just "whatever harmonizes the next scale degree" rather than a creative leap.
- **Close relative**: the Pachelbel's Canon progression (I–V–vi–iii–IV–I–IV–V) is nearly identical — the two progressions differ only in the penultimate chord.
