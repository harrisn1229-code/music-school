# What everyone misunderstands about CAGED in under 5 minutes

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E8T32__XJ_E)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E8T32__XJ_E)

## Synopsis

Another "5-minute Monday" short-form lesson from guitar teacher Jesse Glick, this one answering a question he says he gets constantly from students: "is there a CAGE system for minor chords?" His answer is that there's nothing new to learn — the five major CAGED shapes (C-A-G-E-D) each become their minor equivalent by finding the single note that's the chord's major 3rd and flatting it one fret, leaving everything else in the shape untouched. He walks the rule through all five shapes in order A, G, E, D, then C (skipped at the start, picked up at the end), calling out the G shape as the trickiest because its full 6-string voicing doubles the 3rd. He closes by extending the same rule past full chord shapes to bare triads (two D-triad examples) and briefly ties it to soloing — knowing where a chord's 3rd sits also tells you which fret to avoid (major 3rd) and which to target (minor 3rd) when riffing over a minor-treated chord. It's a compact companion to his other CAGE-system videos, aimed at making "you need a flat third for a minor chord" a physical, on-the-fretboard skill instead of a memorized phrase.

## Techniques

- **[~0:00] The question this video answers.** Students repeatedly ask if there's "a CAGE system for minor chords" — framed as needing only "5% more brain power," implying the answer builds directly on CAGED major-shape knowledge rather than being a separate system.
- **[~0:45] The core mechanism: find the 3rd, flat it a fret.** "To make a minor chord, you need a flat third" is the theory statement most players can repeat without knowing what it means physically. The mechanism: find the note in the chord shape that's the major 3rd (count up the major scale from the root — root, 2nd, 3rd), then move that one note down one fret (one half step). Every other fretted note in the shape stays exactly the same. This single rule, applied per-shape, is the entire "minor CAGED system."
- **[~1:20] A shape.** Find the 3rd by counting up the major scale (C-D-E for a C chord); the chord's actual fretted 3rd is an octave above where that counting lands (the literal 3rd scale degree isn't a fretted note in this shape). Flat that fretted note one fret → A-shape minor.
- **[~2:15] G shape — the trickiest, because the 3rd is doubled.** The full 6-string G shape has the major 3rd appearing twice in the voicing. Lower instance: root, 3rd (A string), 5th — flatting the A-string 3rd alone is described as already producing the minor sound ("that's a C minor chord"). Higher instance: the second 3rd can't simply be slid down a fret at its own position, so instead a **new note is added** a half step below the natural 3rd's expected pitch — Glick names it directly as an **E♭** — to properly resolve the doubled 3rd. *(The transcript is a captions pull and doesn't carry precise fret numbers/finger assignments for this shape's mechanics — captured qualitatively per the transcript's own vagueness rather than guessed.)*
- **[~3:45] E shape (standard barre chord).** Called "super easy": for a barred E-shape C chord, C minor is just lifting the middle finger — the physical equivalent of flatting the 3rd a fret, since removing that fretted note exposes the barre's own lower-fretted note on that string.
- **[~4:15] Soloing tie-in.** Once you know which fret is a chord's 3rd, that's also the note to avoid when riffing over the chord treated as minor (the major 3rd) and the note to target instead (the minor 3rd) — chord-shape knowledge and lead note-choice are the same underlying information used two ways.
- **[~4:45] D shape.** One 3rd in this shape, on the highest string; bring it down one fret, play the rest of the shape unchanged → D-shape minor.
- **[~5:20] C shape (saved for last).** Root note plus the 3rd on the D string; drop that D-string note one fret → C minor triad.
- **[~5:50] Extends to bare triads, not just full chord shapes.** Framed as especially valuable for triad work: two separate D-triad examples (different shapes/positions) where identifying the fretted 3rd (in one, the note under the index finger) and moving it down one fret converts the triad to D minor — same rule, smaller shape.
- **General framing tip:** the takeaway is explicitly to stop treating this as five things to memorize — "you're playing a chord, you know the chord, you can find the third in the chord" — and instead internalize the one rule (identify the 3rd, flat it a fret) as a chord-tone skill that applies to any shape, CAGED or otherwise.
