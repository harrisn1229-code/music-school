# Voice Leading

Choosing WHICH inversion/voicing of each chord in a progression to play, rather than defaulting to root position, so the individual notes move by the smallest possible steps from one chord to the next. Companion to [[Species Counterpoint]] (the same melodic-line-independence discipline, applied here to chord voicing rather than free counterpoint) and [[Part-Writing]] (the four-voice chorale-writing tradition this technique generalizes from).

## The algorithm

1. **Find the note(s) shared between the current chord and the next chord in the progression** — a note common to both.
2. **Keep that shared note exactly where it is** (don't move it at all) — it becomes the pivot the hand/voicing rotates around.
3. **Move the remaining notes to the nearest available note of the next chord** — by the smallest possible interval, usually a step, not a leap.
4. Repeat chord-to-chord through the whole progression.

You don't need to identify which inversion results (root position, first inversion, etc.) to do this — the method is entirely about minimizing motion between chords, not about naming the resulting voicing. Worked example: Am → F, sharing the note C — keep C fixed, move A→A (already shared) note stays, and the remaining note moves by step (E→F) rather than leaping down to the next chord's root.

## Why it works — the choir analogy

Give each note of a chord to a different singer in a choir. If every chord is played in root position, each singer has to leap around unpredictably from chord to chord (root-position-to-root-position motion can be a large jump even between closely related chords). Voice-led inversions let most singers hold their note or move by a single step, which is both easier to sing/play AND reads as smoother, more melodic motion to the ear — the individual voices stop sounding like they're "randomly jumping around the scale."

## The bass exception

**The lowest voice in an arrangement (the piano left hand, a bass part) is usually NOT voice-led** — it stays on each chord's actual root note throughout, even while the upper voices are inverted and voice-led above it. Voice-leading the bass too would smooth out the bass motion but lose the harmonic clarity and character the root-motion bassline provides — a full arrangement's overall chord identity (root position vs. inversion) is usually judged from the bass note regardless of how the upper voices are voiced.
