# 74 Songs in 7/4 time

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C9IBxtMxTLI)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C9IBxtMxTLI)

## Synopsis

A large compiled catalog of songs using 7/4 time, organized around the different ways songwriters actually group the seven beats — the single richest source in the vault for how 7/4 is used in practice rather than just defined on paper.

## Techniques

- **The dominant grouping: 4+3.** Most 7/4 songs feel like a bar of 4/4 followed by a bar of 3/4 glued together — "Have a Good Time" (Paul Simon), "Winslow" (Bon Iver), "Do You Want It All?" (Two Door Cinema Club). Feels like being in 4/4 but every other bar getting cut short.
- **The less common inverse grouping: 3+4.** Feels like two bars of 3/4 followed by one extra beat instead — "$20" (boygenius).
- **A subdivided variant, arguably better renamed 14/8**: "Look at Me Now" (Caroline Polachek) groups the bar as 12 eighth notes + 2 eighth notes rather than as quarter-note beats — the eighth note, not the quarter note, is the real pulse here.
- **A 5+2 grouping**: "Death and All His Friends" (Coldplay) groups the seven beats as 5 then 2.
- **Mixed-meter use — full-song vs. section-only 7/4.** Some songs commit to 7/4 throughout ("Solsbury Hill," Peter Gabriel — a recommended first song to learn for getting used to feeling the meter). Others use it only in one section against a 4/4 base: Coldplay's "Glass of Water" (4/4 verse, 7/4 chorus), Willow Smith's "Symptom of Life" (7/4 verse, 4/4 chorus) and "Big Feelings" (7/4), Green Day's "Before the Lobotomy" (4/4 with a late switch to 7/4), Bowie's "Soul Love" (4/4 body, 7/4 verse), "All You Need Is Love" (Beatles, 4/4 with a 2-bar 7/4 verse opening), Blondie's "Heart of Glass" and "I Love Rock 'n' Roll" (both largely 4/4 with a 7/4 instrumental bridge).
- **The "skipped beat" perceptual effect, named explicitly.** When a mostly-4/4 song drops in an occasional 7/4 bar, it reads as if a beat got cut/skipped from an expected 4/4 bar, pulling the listener into the next phrase one beat early — the unifying feel across most of the section-only examples above.
- **A layered-oddity case**: Foo Fighters' "Times Like These" opens on a 7/4 riff that ALSO loops as a 3-bar phrase rather than the more typical 4-bar loop — two independent layers of "odd" stacked on top of each other.
- **The 7/4 vs. 7/8 distinction, and a real disagreement case.** On paper, 7/4 has seven quarter-note beats per bar and 7/8 has seven eighth-note beats per bar; in practice the difference is about the FELT pulse — 7/4 is felt as seven distinct beats, while 7/8 (with the quarter note still the underlying pulse) is felt as 3½ beats per bar, since only 3.5 quarter notes fit in a 7/8 bar. Pink Floyd's "Money" is the calibration case: guitarist David Gilmour has described it as 7/8, but by this felt-pulse definition the song is actually swung 7/4 — a real, named disagreement about how to classify the same passage, not just a labeling technicality.
- **Grunge-era cluster**: Alice in Chains' "Them Bones," The Toadies' "Possum Kingdom" (alternating 7/4 and 4/4 versions of the same riff), and two Soundgarden tracks ("Spoonman," "Outshined") all confirmed 7/4 users.
