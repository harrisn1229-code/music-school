# Modal Interchange

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Chord Progressions]]. Also called **modal mixture**. Keeping the tonal center (the tonic note) fixed, while borrowing chords from a *different* scale built on that same tonic — most commonly, borrowing from the parallel minor into a major key, or vice versa.

## The idea

"The tonic note stays the same... but which flavor of that tonic — major, minor, Mixolydian... — is changing." A chord borrowed this way doesn't belong to the song's main diatonic key, but reads as related rather than foreign because it shares the tonic.

**Why it can pass as consonant rather than clashing**: the borrowed chord's "wrong" note (e.g., a ♭3 in an otherwise major-key song) only clashes if the melody actually lands on the conflicting natural-scale note at the same moment. In practice, songwriters often keep the melody diatonic to the main key throughout and simply avoid singing the one note that would collide with the borrowed chord's altered tone — letting the harmony and melody each stay internally consistent while still blending two "flavors" of the same tonic.

## The most common single instance — the borrowed minor iv

Replacing a major key's normally-major IV chord with its minor version (Fm instead of F, in C major) is the single most common modal-interchange move in pop/rock — see [[Cadences]] for the **minor plagal cadence** (iv→I) this produces, and the common IV→iv→I extended form.

## Other worked examples

- **"Black Hole Sun" (Soundgarden)** — verse blends parallel G major and G minor: tonic chord is G major, but the very next chord (B♭) is borrowed from G minor, followed by another minor-key borrow (F). A consistent tonic-note common tone (G) glues the major/minor blend together across the borrowed chords.
- **"All the Young Dudes" (David Bowie/Mott the Hoople)** — first half of the progression solidly in C major; second half borrows from C minor (Gm = v of C minor, E♭ = III, B♭ = ♭VII) — the vocal melody stays entirely within the C major scale through the borrowed second half, which is the evidence this is modal mixture rather than a genuine key change (a real key change would typically show up in the melody too).
- **"Our House" (Madness)** — takes the common I–V–ii–IV pop loop and makes BOTH the V and the IV chords minor (I–v–ii–iv), introducing two non-diatonic notes for an otherwise-familiar shape — rare enough that Bennett calls the resulting progression "unique to this song" in practice. Playing the same song with the plain all-major version of the progression is "totally fine" but loses "that unusual bittersweet flavor of mixing major and minor."

## From videos

- **A single-artist deep case study: the major-II chord (borrowed from Lydian) as a quantified stylistic signature, plus a whole-song major/minor blending habit.** Elliott Smith uses a MAJOR ii chord in 40% of his songs overall, 64.5% of his major-key songs specifically — common pattern I→major-II→IV ("Ballad of Big Nothing," "Speed Trials," "Clementine," "Coming Up Roses"), the borrowed brightness resolving back down into IV for a felt release. **Dual analysis, with a stated preference**: the same chord reads equally as a secondary dominant (V/V) or as modal interchange from Lydian (Lydian's own ii chord is naturally major) — modal interchange preferred here because it connects to Smith's second, broader technique: genuine bar-to-bar major/minor ambiguity across a whole song, not just one borrowed chord. "LA" opens on an ambiguous E5 power chord (no 3rd), moves through F minor (from E major) and Em7/Cmaj7 (from E minor) before the chorus resolves to E major; "Son of Sam" swings between A minor and A major at a structural level rather than settling in one. → [[Chord Naming by Context]] for the secondary-dominant-vs-modal-interchange dual framing — David Bennett Music Theory, [[How Elliott Smith writes chord progressions]]
- **A three-way modal blend (major/minor/Lydian layered, not just a pair), sustained across a whole song rather than a single borrowed chord.** Radiohead's "Sail to the Moon" hovers in A major/minor through its verses — chords keep resolving to A major even while the surrounding harmony implies A minor — then adds a raised 4th (D♯) suggesting Lydian on top of that blend during the intro/interlude, so three modal colors coexist rather than one clean borrow. Companion instability on the same song: a countdown-structured metric intro and a key-dissolving chromatic-mediant ending, on [[Time Signatures &amp; Note Values]] and [[Chromatic Mediants and Pop Modulation]] respectively. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Radiohead's most complex song]]
- **The minor iv borrow (Aeolian's iv into an Ionian song) is modal interchange's single most common move — plus the reverse (major vi into a minor song), a 50/50-blended song where "major or minor" stops being a meaningful question, and a real disagreement about how to even analyze the tonic chord.** "In My Life" (Beatles, A major) swaps in D minor (Aeolian's iv) instead of the diatonic D major — described as adding "extra depth" the diatonic chord lacks. Same device: "Wake Me Up When September Ends" (Green Day), "Every Time We Say Goodbye" (Cole Porter) — the latter's lyric literally narrates "from major to minor" at the exact harmonic shift. **Reverse direction** (borrowing FROM the parallel major while IN a minor key): "Light My Fire" (Doors, A minor verse) uses F♯ minor (A major's vi) instead of the diatonic F major — more exotic/intriguing than the native chord. **A song where "which key is it" stops being answerable**: "Are You Gonna Be My Girl" (Jet) draws roughly half its chords from A major (A, D) and half from A minor (G, C), while the melody itself sits in A minor (uses the minor 3rd) — the honest description is just "in the key of A," full stop, rather than forcing a major/minor label. **A genuinely disputed case for what the TONIC chord even is**: "Give Me Shelter" (Rolling Stones) sits almost entirely in C♯ minor except its tonic chord, which is C♯ MAJOR — describable either as "C♯ major with modal interchange on everything else" or "C♯ minor with modal interchange on the tonic," and the second framing is awkward precisely because a minor key's own tonic "should" be minor. Same ambiguity in "Lola" (The Kinks, E/A major-key chords mixed with C/D minor-key chords). **A companion Aeolian↔Phrygian blend** (a step beyond the usual major/minor pair) is documented in detail already at this page's/[[Diatonic Modes]]'s "Nights in White Satin" coverage — same borrow mechanism, different mode pair. **Ionian↔Lydian blend, with an explicit two-valid-readings caveat**: "Waterloo" (ABBA, D major) uses an E MAJOR chord (Lydian's raised-4th-flavored ii) instead of the diatonic E minor, reinforced by a G♯ in the melody; this exact chord shape (major II in a major key) is also analyzable as a **secondary dominant** — both readings are correct, since music theory terminology developed piecemeal across centuries/traditions rather than as one unified system; pick whichever framing is more useful for the task at hand. → [[Secondary Dominants]] (the competing valid analysis for the Waterloo chord) — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use Modal Mixture]]
- **A catalog of parallel major↔minor shifts (the "brightness spectrum" endpoints), with the placement/mechanism varied per example — plus how the SAME device generalizes to any two adjacent modes.** Minor→major at the chorus (opens the song up, reads as brighter): "While My Guitar Gently Weeps" (A minor→A major), "Happy Together" (The Turtles), "Runaway" (Del Shannon). Major→minor at the chorus (closes the song in, reads as darker/more intense): "I'm Still Standing" (Elton John, B♭ major verse→B♭ minor chorus). **Mid-verse shift, not tied to a section boundary**: "Penny Lane" — B major verse slides to B minor partway through, before modulating to an entirely different key (A major) for the chorus — the parallel shift adds a shade of darkness as a transitional color, distinct from the modulation that follows it. **A shift where the melody itself doesn't need to change at all**: "My Favorite Things" (Rodgers & Hammerstein) — E♭ minor for the first two verses, E♭ major for the third, but the melody is built purely from scale degrees 1, 2, and 5 — identical in both major and minor — so the harmony can swap tonality underneath a completely unchanged vocal line. **A single-chord flicker (Picardy third), not a sustained shift**: "Crazy" (Gnarls Barkley) — one bar of C major swapped into an otherwise all-C-minor 8-bar loop at the start of verse 2, then straight back to minor; "Exit Music (For a Film)" (Radiohead) resolves its very last chord to B major after a whole song in B minor — the classic full Picardy-third cadence. Classical precedent: Mozart's *Rondo alla Turca* shifts A minor→A major to introduce its second theme. **Not technically a modulation** — since the tonic note never moves, only the tonality (mode) around it — better described as a mode shift/interchange than a key change. **Generalizes past the major/minor pair**: "Norwegian Wood" shifts E Mixolydian (verse) → E Dorian (middle eight) — only one step apart on the modal-brightness spectrum, so the shift reads as far more subtle than a full major↔minor swap, even though it's mechanically the same kind of parallel-mode interchange. → [[Diatonic Modes]] (the brightness spectrum itself), [[Modulation (Key Change)]] (why this is deliberately NOT classified as modulation despite sounding like a key change) — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that change to the Parallel Key]]
- **A worked full-song parallel-key ARRANGEMENT technique — a real cover version, not just an analysis — that deliberately avoids the "obvious" mechanical parallel-key conversion because it sounds wrong.** Hozier's 2023 cover of Arctic Monkeys' "Do I Wanna Know" (originally G minor) converts to G major. **The mechanically correct version** — just raise the 3rd, 6th and 7th scale degrees (B♭→B, E♭→E, F→F♯) wherever they occur, the standard parallel-major conversion — is demonstrated and rejected: it works but reads as "too prim and proper," too bright and happy for the intended mood. **What Hozier actually did instead, and why it works better**: raise the 3rd/6th/7th degrees as normal (the correct parallel-major notes), but ALSO transpose almost every note in the riff down by a third (except the tonic note itself, both because it's the anchor and because it's the riff's very first, most-recognizable note) — described as easier to think of as "down a third" even though Hozier's version is actually up a sixth (he sings it an octave higher). **Why moving notes down a third preserves recognizability while still changing the mood**: the melodic CONTOUR (the up/down shape) is preserved exactly — every note still moves in the same direction at the same points — only the interval sizes change, so the riff stays identifiable as "the Do I Wanna Know riff" despite sounding substantially softer/more tender than either the original minor or the mechanically-converted major version. The chords move by the same third to stay consistent with the shifted melody. **General takeaway**: a parallel major/minor conversion doesn't have to be applied uniformly to every scale degree at the same "distance" — deliberately under- or over-shifting some notes relative to the mechanical rule is itself a usable arranging lever for landing between the two mode's usual emotional readings, here specifically to avoid major's default "bright and happy" connotation while still leaving minor's "dark" mood behind. Companion instrumentation/tempo/dynamics softening (mentioned but not this bullet's focus): slower tempo, mellower arrangement — all contributing to the same tender effect alongside the harmonic device. → [[Songs that change to the Parallel Key]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[How Hozier transformed Arctic Monkey's "Do I Wanna Know"]]
- **"Trouble" (Coldplay): a simple single-chord borrow, useful as a first/beginner-level modal-interchange example.** Verse sits in G major/G Mixolydian; occasional F major chords appear that aren't diatonic to G major but ARE diatonic to G Mixolydian (its flat-7 chord) — each occurrence borrows from the parallel Mixolydian mode mid-phrase, audibly shifting toward Mixolydian's open/mellow color. → [[Diatonic Modes]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [How Coldplay use Music Theory](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1sL4KzKwwns)
- **"Just" (Radiohead) — four interchange sources chained in one 4-chord riff, made audible by using full triads instead of power chords.** C major (home) → E♭ major (parallel C minor) → D major (parallel C **Lydian** — a rarer interchange source than the usual major/minor pair) → F major (back to C major). A power chord has no 3rd, so it can't distinguish major/minor — swapping in full triads is what makes each borrow read as a genuine modal shift. Stays cohesive despite the churn because it's a melodic sequence (same phrase repeated up a minor 3rd), not four unrelated chords. → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]] (same song, the octatonic line and blue-note material) — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Analysis of Just by Radiohead]]
- **"Hey Jude" outro as a worked example of testing which of two identical-note-set readings is the real tonic.** F Mixolydian and B♭ major share the exact same 7 notes, so the outro is sometimes misheard as "really" B♭ major — resolved by substituting a sustained B♭ chord where the F chord belongs: it still sounds unresolved, while the actual F chord fully resolves, confirming F Mixolydian (the borrowed ♭VII chord, E♭, is what supplies the modal color) rather than a plain-major reading in disguise. → [[Diatonic Modes]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[How The Beatles use Modes]]
- **Two Radiohead modal-interchange progressions, each split cleanly into a major "half" and a borrowed-minor "half."** "Everything in Its Right Place": F major→C major (solid I-V, major-sounding) then D♭maj13 and Cm/E♭ (both borrowed from parallel Aeolian) — later, dropping the F major chord entirely (leaving C sustained alone) pulls the tonal center toward minor even though the remaining chord is itself major, since F major was the thing anchoring the major-key feel. "Burn the Witch": F♯6/9↔E major verse reads as Mixolydian (E = ♭VII of F♯); chorus strips chord 3rds to power chords and introduces a D chord (native to F♯ Aeolian, foreign to F♯ major/Mixolydian) plus an A natural in the vocal (Aeolian's ♭3) — pulling from Mixolydian into Aeolian specifically to darken the chorus's mood. → David Bennett Music Theory, [[How Radiohead use Modes]]
- **"Heroes" (Bowie, D major) as a clean test case for spotting modal interchange vs. a genuine key-wide mode**: the chorus lands on a C major chord and an A minor chord, both borrowed from D Mixolydian (C being the ♭VII), but the vocal melody never touches the borrowed C♮ — it stays diatonic to D major throughout. Contrasted against "Golden Years" (also Bowie, fully committed F♯ Mixolydian where the melody DOES land on the ♭7) as the borrow-vs.-real-mode distinction in practice. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[How David Bowie uses Modes]]
- **The minor v chord as a Mixolydian borrow — since Mixolydian's lowered 7th degree sits inside the V chord itself, being genuinely IN Mixolydian turns V minor as a natural diatonic consequence, not a chromatic alteration.** "Clocks" (Coldplay), "The Tourist" (Radiohead), "Strawberry Fields Forever," "All Things Must Pass" all use a straight minor v for mood. Mid-song major-to-minor V switches for a mood shift: "Yellow," "Angels" (Robbie Williams), "The Darkness on the Edge of Town" (Springsteen, major V immediately followed by minor v in the same phrase). **A full Mixolydian chord set reading as diatonic rather than borrowed**: "Chicago" (Sufjan Stevens, D Mixolydian) — I-v-♭VII-IV, where both the minor v (Am) and ♭VII (C) are natively diatonic to D Mixolydian despite being chromatic borrows in plain D major. A second, distinct explanation for minor v (the secondary supertonic, not modal interchange) → [[Secondary Dominants]]. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use the Minor 5 chord]]
- **The subtonic chord (♭VII) as the most common borrowed chord of all — the de facto "7th chord" of a major key, filling in for the almost-never-used diatonic vii°.** "Don't Stop" (Fleetwood Mac), "Mr. Blue Sky" (ELO), "I'll Be There for You" (Rembrandts) all use a plain ♭VII borrowed from the parallel minor. "It's Only Love" (Beatles) alternates the borrowed ♭VII and the natural leading-tone chord within the same progression — audible blues-style major/minor blending. "Sweet Child o' Mine" (GN'R) pairs V and ♭VII, spelled with the correct lowered-7th enharmonic (C♭, not B) for notational precision. Full writeup, plus the parallel Mixolydian framing and why vii° itself is avoided → [[Subtonic Chord]]. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use the Subtonic chord]]
