# Jamerson's Root-Fifth-Octave Exercise

**James Jamerson** (Motown's uncredited house bassist, the Funk Brothers, Rock and Roll Hall of Fame) taught this exercise one-on-one to Ron Brown (bassist on later West Coast Motown sessions) as a way to drill his own **chromatic approach to bass playing**; it's documented in the *Standing in the Shadows of Motown* book, though that book only writes it out in a handful of keys. It's not just a finger-warmup — it's a compressed formula for how Jamerson generated hit basslines on the fly, and BassBuzz reverse-engineers it into three combinable ingredients: **root-fifth-octave** as the harmonic skeleton, **chromatic approach walks** as the connective tissue, and **syncopation** as the rhythmic engine. Fill gap: this is the vault's first dedicated note for Jamerson's formula and the step-39 ("Roots, fifths, and octaves") entry on [[The BassBuzz 55-Step Bass Learning Roadmap]] had no linked note until this one.

## The exercise itself

Played over a static C: root-fifth-octave (**C, G, C**) as the skeleton, then a **chromatic walk starting three frets below the target**, working up chromatically into the fifth, and the same walk-up into the octave — Jamerson deliberately re-inserts the fifth *in between* the walk-up notes to "hammer home" the target rather than just running straight chromatic scale tones. Rhythmically it's played in **swing eighths** (offbeat notes land two-thirds of the way past the beat rather than the even halfway point of straight eighths) — noted as sounding "kind of lumpy" compared to a straight-eighths read, and worth learning in **all 12 keys**, not just C, so the shape is recognizable by ear in any song's key rather than only in the one key it was first learned in. *(general starting point for the walk: three chromatic notes leading into each target — adjust the walk's length to fit however many beats are available before the target lands.)*

## Ingredient 1 — Root, fifth, octave as the harmonic skeleton

- **Root** — the home-base note for the current key/chord.
- **Octave** — the same note name, one octave up.
- **Fifth** — a strong supportive note that adds movement without adding new harmonic color (i.e. it doesn't change the listener's read of the chord).
- This is deliberately the most generic possible note set — "the bread and butter of bass playing" (cross-referenced from a sibling BassBuzz video, see [[Bass Pentatonic Riff-Writing Recipe]]'s method-3 safety note: songs as different as "Sittin' On the Dock of the Bay," "I Just Called to Say I Love You," and "Have You Ever Seen the Rain" all share root-fifth skeletons without anyone calling it a copy). Root-fifth-octave alone is what today's bassists still reach for by default, largely *because* of how thoroughly Jamerson normalized it in the '60s.
- → links the [[BASS Slot]] directly: root-fifth-octave is the low-end harmonic scaffold this slot is built around.

## Ingredient 2 — Chromatic approach walks (tension and release)

**Chromatic** = drawing from any of the 12 notes rather than staying inside a 5-or-7-note scale. Played *without a destination*, chromatic notes just sound wrong; played *toward* a target (the fifth or the octave, in this exercise), the out-of-scale notes create tension that resolves the instant the target lands — "tension and release is a huge part of what makes life non-boring," and specifically what gives Motown's bass parts their depth/journey quality rather than sitting flat on scale tones. Jamerson's own habit: start the walk **three frets below the target**, step up chromatically, and interpose the fifth partway through the walk-up to the octave so the ear re-anchors on a scale tone before the final chromatic step lands.

**Worked example — "Bernadette" (Four Tops):** the riff moves E♭ → D♭. On the E♭ chord, root-fifth-octave = **E♭, B♭, E♭**; on D♭, it's **D♭, A♭, D♭** — between the two chords that accounts for nearly every note in the riff, plus the third of each chord (also present). The one note root-fifth-octave *can't* explain is a **D natural** — which only makes sense as a chromatic approach tone connecting the two chord centers, i.e. exactly ingredient 2 in action.

**Worked example — "For Once in My Life" (Stevie Wonder), key of F:** F is root/octave, C is the fifth — the line is described as "hard... to be more literally copy-pasted from Jamerson's exercise": heavy root-fifth-octave usage, a **three-fret chromatic walk up to the fifth** (with a small interruption, close to but not identical to the plain exercise), then a **three-fret chromatic walk straight to the octave**.

**Two more directions on the same device — walk down, and swap chromatic for the scale.** A separate BassBuzz daily-practice exercise (root-fifth-octave-fifth-root over one bar, same three-note skeleton as this note's exercise) names two additional walking patterns beyond the chromatic walk-up above: a **chromatic walk down** (the same three-half-steps-ignoring-the-scale logic as the walk-up, just descending back toward the root — visually a "weird looking shape" since it crosses strings, but mechanically the walk-up device in reverse), and a **scale walk down** (using the diatonic major scale instead of chromatic motion to walk back down to the root — tension-and-release from stepwise motion rather than from chromatic dissonance). Framed the same way as this note's own devices: portable well past the bluesy context it's demonstrated in, named as usable for walking jazz bass, gospel, and even the "Smoke on the Water" intro. **Applied to a chord change (moving roots):** the same walking logic connects two different chords rather than just returning to one static root — worked example moving from C to F: the first three bars sit on C, then a **scale walk-up** in the beat before the change leads into F, and the return trip back to C uses a **scale-walk-down variation** that resolves onto F first before dropping to the new root — the general recipe (chord roots as the anchor, walk the beats before a change into the next target) matches this note's own "Applying the formula to write a bassline" section below, just demonstrated on a single static exercise rather than a full progression. ## Ingredient 3 — Syncopation (the rhythmic secret ingredient)

Jamerson didn't build syncopation into the written exercise itself, but it's called the ingredient most central to his sound. **Syncopation = emphasizing notes that land off the beat** (the "e" and "a" of a beat, in 16th-note terms) rather than on it — exactly the same tension-and-release logic as ingredient 2, just applied to rhythm instead of pitch: non-syncopated notes (quarter notes, on-beat eighths) sound straight/unresolved-tension-free ("just chugging along"); syncopated notes build tension that only resolves when the line lands back on a strong beat. Distinct from **swing eighths** (a feel/timing choice — offbeat notes delayed to 2/3 past the beat instead of the even halfway point) — the exercise itself is played with swing eighths, but syncopation is a separate, additional rhythmic device layered on top in Jamerson's real basslines. → [[Syncopation and Metric Displacement]] for the general theory (the three ways to syncopate: accent a weak position, tie a weak-position note over the strong beat, or rest on the strong beat).

**Worked example — "Darling Dear" (Jackson 5):** across two bars, essentially every note after the initial C lands on an "e" or an "a" — maximally syncopated — building tension that resolves only when the line lands squarely on beat 1 of the F chord. The same two bars also carry ingredient 1 (root-fifth-octave on the C chord) and ingredient 2 (a chromatic walk to the fifth on the F chord), making this the single clearest three-ingredients-at-once worked example in the lesson.

## Applying the formula to write a bassline

Worked demo over a **G major7 – B minor7 – A minor7 – D7 – G major** progression (a 1‑3‑2‑5‑1, in a "What's Going On"-style soul feel): start from the baseline default of hitting each chord's root on the downbeat, then upgrade it — G chord: play Jamerson's exercise directly (root-fifth-octave + chromatic walk); chromatic walk into the root of the B chord; a little octave-fifth-root motion there; chromatic walk into A; more root-fifth-octave; chromatic walk into D; repeat; chromatic walk back into G. **The general recipe for turning a plain root-note bassline into a Jamerson-style one:** hit the chord roots on schedule as the fallback, then layer in root-fifth-octave shapes on longer-held chords and chromatic walks in the beats leading into each chord change, with syncopated placement on the connecting notes rather than everything landing squarely on the beat.

## Gear and technique for the tone

Jamerson's own setup, named directly as part of "getting this sounding right": **plucked with one finger**, on a **Fender Precision Bass (P-bass)**, strung with **flatwound strings**, set up with **high action**. → [[Sound Selection Guide]] (Bass section) has this logged as a tone recommendation alongside the vault's other flatwound-bass reference.

## Beyond Motown — the formula in other genres

Framed directly as why Jamerson is in the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame — not just a soul/Motown figure, but an influence traceable across rock, alt-rock, and prog:

- **The Beatles — "Nowhere Man" (Paul McCartney):** in the verse, over an E chord, McCartney runs a chromatic walk up to B (the chord's fifth, and also the root of the next chord) — the exact ingredient-2 device — plus heavy syncopation (ingredient 3).
- **Stone Temple Pilots — "Plush" (Robert DeLeo):** a walk up to the four, then a three-fret chromatic walk up to that chord's fifth (ingredient 2), plus heavy syncopation (ingredient 3).
- **Rush — "The Spirit of Radio" (Geddy Lee):** a chromatic walk leading into the A, plus syncopation leading into each chord change — both ingredients again, cited as Jamerson influence surfacing in prog rock.

**Takeaway stated directly in the lesson:** starting from a generic, straightforward root-note-only bassline over any track, layering in chromatic walks plus a few fifths/octaves is what makes it "spicier" — the formula travels across genres because it's a set of *devices* (harmonic skeleton + approach-tone connective tissue + rhythmic displacement), not a Motown-specific style choice.

**A direct genre-crossover confirmation, funk side — "Chameleon" (Herbie Hancock & The Headhunters; the line as usually played on bass is actually Paul Jackson's synth part, not a bass part in the original recording, though it functions as the bassline whenever a band's keys player isn't covering it).** Learned in a Motown/soul cover band, this line uses **ingredient 2 in its plainest, most portable form** — start the walk three frets below the target root, walk up chromatically into it, no fifth/octave pairing required to make it work. Named directly in the source video as a "soap killer" pattern usable across **funk, disco, gospel, and slap**, not just this one tune — independent, real-world confirmation of this note's own "Beyond Motown" claim that the formula is a set of portable devices rather than a Motown-specific style choice. → [[Bass Walkup and Walkdown Fills]] (that video's own pointer to its "walk-ups" video for more of the same family) — BassBuzz, [[10 Songs that Taught Me Bass (Easy to Effin’ Hard)]]
