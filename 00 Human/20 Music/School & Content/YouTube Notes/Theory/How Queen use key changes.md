# How Queen use key changes

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tq2z5hlXiCs)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tq2z5hlXiCs)

## Synopsis

Five worked Queen key-change examples, organized around a single thesis: Queen's modulations are never complexity for its own sake — each one is argued to reinforce the specific theme/mood of the song it's in, and each is checked against the (worse) alternative of just staying in one key.

## Techniques

- **"We Are the Champions"** — opens in C minor, quickly moves to its relative major (E♭ major) for the verse; the chorus itself is reached via an abrupt **truck-driver modulation** (whole-step-up, E♭→F major) right as the lyric turns triumphant — argued as a direct match for the song's "victory" theme, contrasted against an A/B'd version that stays in one key throughout and noticeably loses its lift.
- **"The Show Must Go On"** — verse 1/chorus 1 in B minor; verse 2 jumps up a whole step to C♯ minor — an unusual place to insert a key change (mid-song, second verse rather than at the chorus), argued to musically embody "soldiering on despite everything holding you back."
- **"Under Pressure"** — mostly D major, pivots down to G major after the second chorus for the bridge (G major read as relatively "darker/more closed-in" next to D major, matching the bridge's more reflective mood), then modulates back UP to D major at the bridge's climax ("give ourselves one more chance") for a triumphant lift — the whole down-then-up arc is the point, not just the destination key.
- **"Killer Queen"** — C minor → relative major E♭ major (verse), then TWO consecutive **fifth-related modulations** in the chorus: E♭ major → B♭ major → F major, walking around the circle of fifths one step at a time. Explains why a fifth-apart key change (which sounds like a "large" jump) actually feels smooth: keys a fifth apart share all but one note (E♭ major and B♭ major differ only in A♭ vs. A natural; B♭ major and F major differ only in E♭ vs. E natural) — argued to give the song a "classical-esque," "grand and regal" quality rather than tracking the lyric moment-to-moment the way the previous two examples do.
- **"Bohemian Rhapsody"** — modulates at least 8 times across 6 minutes (full breakdown deferred to a separate, earlier video by the same channel); the specific moment highlighted here is the closing piano figure, which walks E♭ major → B♭ major → F major (the same circle-of-fifths, one-new-note-at-a-time mechanism as "Killer Queen") to land in a key the song has never visited before, right as the song's narrative of searching for identity/belonging finally resolves — framed as one of the presenter's favorite moments in recorded music specifically because the harmonic device (a fresh, never-before-visited key arriving exactly at the moment of narrative resolution) so closely mirrors the lyric's own arc.
- **General mechanism, stated across multiple examples**: a modulation a perfect 5th away is smooth specifically because it only changes ONE note of the diatonic scale — minimal friction, easy to chain (walking the circle of fifths one step at a time, as both "Killer Queen" and "Bohemian Rhapsody" do).
