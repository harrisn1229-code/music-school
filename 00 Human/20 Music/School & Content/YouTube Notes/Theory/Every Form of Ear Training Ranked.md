# Every Form of Ear Training Ranked

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=w_pXQU5q1Vo)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=w_pXQU5q1Vo)

## Synopsis

Brandon D'Eon ranks 18 distinct forms of ear training on an S-to-F tier list by raw difficulty, demonstrating several live (scale-by-ear ID, chord-quality-by-ear ID) rather than just asserting the ranking. The value here is the **difficulty map itself** — a roadmap for sequencing ear-training study from easiest wins to hardest — rather than teaching any single skill in depth. He closes with the caveat that no single form is essential and that time with real musicians beats any of them, but the ranking is still a useful study-order guide.

## Techniques

- **F tier (trivially easy — anyone can do these):** **Timbre** (tone-color difference between the same pitch on different sources — guitar vs. piano vs. trumpet vs. voice on the same note); **Dynamics** (loud vs. quiet, pianissimo to fortissimo).
- **D tier (easy):** **Tonal center / key identification** ("the note that sounds like home"); **Baseline/root-motion recognition** (hearing the bass notes = the roots of a chord progression, distinct from a melodic bassline); **Articulation** (staccato/legato/palm-muted/pizzicato — how a note is played, not what it is).
- **C tier:** **Ascending intervals**; **Descending intervals** (rated slightly harder than ascending — going down from the starting note is a bit less intuitive); **Scales by ear** — he argues scales are easier than most people think and demonstrates going ~20-for-20 identifying random scales (major, harmonic minor, melodic minor, whole tone, diminished whole tone, Locrian, Phrygian, pentatonic) using an ear-training app; the stated reason people struggle isn't a "bad ear" but **not knowing the scale's interval structure** — learn the structure first, and recognition gets much easier. *(General starting-point framing, not a specific claimed statistic.)*
- **B tier:** **Harmonic intervals** (both notes sounding simultaneously — harder than sequential/melodic intervals because there's no "landing" reference note); specific intervals named as hardest to hear even within interval training: **major 6ths and minor 7ths**; **Melodic dictation** (learning a melody by ear — the practical, real-world form of interval/scale-degree training, made easier in practice because you usually have your instrument in hand while doing it); **Rhythm-by-ear** — counterintuitively harder than melody, because rhythm alone (no pitch content) has nothing memorable to hang onto; a rhythm becomes far easier to identify once paired with its actual melody.
- **A tier (hard — "you need a damn good ear"):** **Microtonal recognition** (pitches between the notes of Western 12-tone tuning) — rare in Western music generally but load-bearing in **string bending**: hitting the correct bent pitch by ear gets much harder in a loud band mix where self-monitoring is degraded, so bending accuracy is reframed as a genuine, underrated ear-training skill, not just a physical technique; **Complex chord-quality identification** — not major/minor/diminished (called easy — "major sounds happy, minor sounds sad, diminished sounds like a demon from hell") but dense extended/altered qualities (7sus♭13, dominant 11♭9, dominant 7♯5♯9, major 9♯11, minor 6/9); demonstrated live with a mixed success rate against exactly this difficulty; **Chord progression functionality** — identifying how each chord in a progression relates back to the tonic (I–V–vi–IV, ii–V–I, etc.) — explicitly gated behind being able to identify the tonal center first, since functional labeling is meaningless without a fixed reference point; **Time signature identification by ear** — demonstrated live on 7/4 and 5/4 examples (backing tracks built for his own program), with a genuinely hard example cited (Plini's "Electric Sunrise" in 13/8) that he says he'd need to slow down to catch.
- **S tier (hardest — "elite ears"):** **Individual note recognition with no reference note** = perfect pitch; **Recognizing the individual pitches that make up a chord** (harder than single-note perfect pitch — cites Rick Beato's son Dylan as an extreme example, and argues this skill is far easier to build in childhood than as an adult); **Comping-by-ear** — hearing not just chord qualities but a whole progression's chords *and* their rhythm as played by a comping instrument (piano/guitar), made harder because chords are usually subordinate to the melody/vocal in a mix and don't sit at the sonic surface (cites transcribing Oscar Peterson's comping as a benchmark-hard task); ranked below "individual pitches within a chord" specifically because in practice most people transcribe comping *with* an instrument for reference, unlike true no-reference perfect pitch.
- **S+ tier (promoted as the single hardest on the list):** **Identifying all the individual notes that make up a chord, by ear, with no instrument** — framed as perfect pitch combined with harmonic-interval hearing simultaneously.
- **F− tier (demoted as the single easiest on the list):** **Dynamics** (loud vs. quiet) — reiterated as the floor of the whole ranking.
- **Closing caveat (important context, not just a sign-off):** no single ear-training form on this list is presented as essential; the biggest lever named is **spending time playing with real musicians**, with the list itself framed as a difficulty map for sequencing study, not a checklist everyone must complete.
