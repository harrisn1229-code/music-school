# Songs that use 2/4 time

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0g-4jSkBo0c)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0g-4jSkBo0c)

## Synopsis

Continues the ongoing odd/simple-time-signature series with 2/4 — most usefully as a "half bar" briefly dropped into an otherwise 4/4 song, a subtle mixed-meter trick Bennett says listeners rarely consciously notice until they try to learn the song.

## Techniques

- **The workhorse device: one 2/4 "half bar" dropped into a 4/4 song.** A single two-beat bar inserted where a 4/4 bar would normally sit, then straight back to 4/4. Examples: "Africa" (Toto, verse), "Revolution" (The Beatles, two bars of 4/4 then one bar of 2/4), "To the End" (Blur, verse), "Assassin" (Muse, verse).
- **What it does perceptually**: creates a subtle "stop-start" feel that breaks up 4/4's straight-ahead drive — described as making the music feel like it "can't quite get into its flow." The payoff comes later: once the song *does* settle into uninterrupted 4/4 (e.g. a chorus), that section lands with more relief and drive specifically because of the earlier interruption — a mixed-meter device used to make a later straight section feel bigger by contrast.
- **A 2/4 bar can be baked into the main loop itself, not just a one-off**: "Hey Ya" (OutKast) — the entire song is built on a repeating pattern of two bars of 4/4, one bar of 2/4, two more bars of 4/4 (a 22-beat loop). Because it's woven into the groove itself rather than dropped once, most listeners never consciously register it.
- **The notation-ambiguity point**: any 4/4 song can be equivalently transcribed as 2/4 by doubling the bar count — so a song that occasionally drops a 2/4 half-bar could arguably be notated as consistent 2/4 throughout instead, avoiding the time-signature switches altogether. This is a real transcription choice, not just a technicality — some analysts argue songs like "Hey Jude" read more cleanly in constant 2/4 than in 4/4-with-exceptions.
