# The 4 Biggest Guitar Practice MISTAKES! (this is why you're stuck)

## Synopsis

BERNTH narrates the burnout cycle (build a routine → grind without visible progress → dread practicing → quit) as his own past experience, then walks through four science-informed fixes he credits with pulling himself out of it: start small, practice with zero distraction (true deliberate practice), batch work into long uninterrupted stretches, and deliberately end each session on the most enjoyable block. ## What's taught

- **Start small — 15-60 minutes, not an intimidating 3-hour block.** The mere act of starting a session tends to motivate continuing past the planned time; an oversized daily commitment looming over the whole day kills the will to begin at all. Add time gradually as consistency becomes comfortable.
- **Deliberate practice requires zero distraction — it cannot coexist with divided attention.** Defines deliberate practice as tight, sustained focus on a specific skill with active feedback correction; practicing while half-watching TV or with a wandering mind can reduce a full hour's session to the effective value of ~20 minutes. Cites the (informally stated) neuroscience framing that skill improvement comes from repeatedly firing a specific neural circuit in isolation — i.e. the point of focus is that it's what actually drives the physiological change, not just a productivity nicety.
- **Batch practice into long, uninterrupted stretches — avoid task-switching entirely, including phone checks.** Task-switching leaves attentional "residue" from the previous task that bleeds into the next one, even for something as small as a 10-minute phone check; BERNTH's own fix is physically leaving the phone in another room during practice. Framed as a bigger lever than most people expect — a message you can't act on immediately but have now seen is itself a lingering distraction.
- **Sequence the routine so the most enjoyable/musical block comes last, as a built-in reward.** BERNTH's own routine ends with ~20 minutes of improvisation, deliberately placed after the harder technique/theory work rather than at the start — the payoff being that he's warmed up for it by then, and finishing on something fun makes it easier to want to return the next day. Explicitly recommends against front-loading the fun material "to convince yourself to start," since ending happy matters more for long-run consistency than starting easy.
- **Three starter exercises named for beginners with no existing routine:** the "spider exercise" (finger independence/positioning), an alternate-picking drill, and a note-location/fretboard-theory exercise — offered as a pick-one-or-all-three on-ramp, not elaborated on beyond naming them.
