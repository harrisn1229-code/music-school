# After 27 years of practice, I'd only do 5 things

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Mhtks8TvfTA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Mhtks8TvfTA)

## Synopsis

Daniel Seriff distills 27 years of playing and 25,000 taught lessons into the five things he'd focus on exclusively if starting over — a practice-priorities video, not a single-technique lesson. His framing throughout is that most of what players spend time on isn't *wrong*, just not the highest-leverage use of practice time. The five: (1) rhythm — specifically the picking hand and groove-feel, via two named backing-track drills (Consistent Rhythms, Targeted Rhythms); (2) immersive listening to inspiring playing before trying to reproduce it, using slow-down apps and ear-training software; (3) never running scales/arpeggios straight up and down once they're learned — apply them into "limitation exercises" over a backing track instead; (4) fretboard knowledge and core theory in short daily bursts, anchored on mastering the major scale in all 12 keys; (5) embellishment — the "mojo" that turns technically-correct-but-bland playing into something with vibe, via five named ornaments (slides, hammer-ons, pull-offs, bends, vibrato). Rock/blues-oriented throughout (D minor pentatonic/Dorian examples, Gilmour/Beck/Hendrix/Eric Johnson name-drops), not jazz-specific — a useful counterpart to the jazz-framed practice-methodology material already in the vault. ## What's taught

**1. Rhythm — the most-ignored fundamental**
- Guitarists routinely ignore the picking hand and rhythm in general, associating "rhythm" only with chords — but it's present in every note played, lead lines included.
- **Consistent Rhythms drill:** backing track (demoed over a D minor one-chord vamp — D Dorian or D minor pentatonic both fit), pick a subdivision (quarters → eighths → triplets → 16ths), improvise locked to that subdivision the whole time — not scale-running up and down.
- **"Slowing the rate of change" tool:** when a subdivision is too fast, repeat each note 4× at that rate, then 2×, then 1× (full speed) as a graduated ramp into it.
- Push tempo over time, staying "on the edge of your ability" — Seriff speeds the backing track up gradually in Ableton Live; any backing-track player works.
- **Targeted Rhythms drill:** same backing track, still improvising, but restrict yourself to playing on only one specific beat (e.g. only beat 2, or only "3-and" in eighths) and try to make music out of just that hit — cycle target beats/subdivisions. Hard to make musical at first; builds beat recognition and a felt sense of where you are in the groove.

**2. Immersive listening**
- Find genuinely inspiring playing (classic — Gilmour, Beck, Hendrix — or modern, found on Instagram/YouTube).
- Screen-record or audio-record the clip on your phone, load into a slow-down/loop app — **Amazing Slow Downer** or **Transcriber** named — and isolate the inspiring fragment.
- Listen to that isolated fragment on repeat, many times, before trying to play any of it — cites the book *Decoding Greatness*: presence around great work builds skill via absorption/audiation (hearing it in your head) before you're technically able to execute it.
- Go deep on a few favorites, not wide across many recordings.
- Once ready, pick notes out by ear, or fall back to learning from the video — okay if the ear isn't there yet.
- **Ear-training app named: Functional Ear Trainer** — trains the ear ahead of the fretboard catching up.

**3. Scales and arpeggios — never straight up and down once learned**
- Core rule: once a scale/arpeggio/pattern is learned well enough to visualize and play decently, **stop practicing it verbatim up/down immediately** — verbatim repetition never becomes musical, regardless of volume of reps. Calls out the classical-pedagogy assumption that up/down scale drilling builds creative playing as false.
- **Limitation-exercise method, demoed on a three-note-groups pattern in D minor:** apply the pattern over a backing track in the same key; don't start on the bottom/root — start in the middle; mix up note order rather than strict ascend/descend; add hammer-ons/pull-offs; play short musical fragments rather than the full run. Mistakes are okay.
- Same treatment applies to **two-string-group exercises** (the shapes behind Eric Johnson-style licks) — good for speed, but only pays off musically once limitation-exercised the same way.
- **Metronome vs. backing track:** doesn't recommend metronome practice as the default — good for advanced players, but gives no real rhythmic *feel* feedback (just a click). Prefers practicing scale/arpeggio material against real backing tracks or downloaded drummer loops (cheap/free online), training the ear and the feel simultaneously with the fingers.

**4. Fretboard knowledge and core theory**
- Do it in short daily bursts (a few minutes), not marathon sessions — Seriff regrets his own early "ad nauseam" long-session approach.
- Foundation: master the major scale first — "all music theory comes from the major scale." Goal: all 15 major scales (every key/enharmonic spelling), forwards and backwards, both spoken/sung aloud and played on the fretboard.
- Concrete starting drill: in the key of C, spend a few minutes a day saying or singing the natural note names on each string while playing them, up the neck to the 12th fret and back, then as high as possible and back — expand key by key from there.

**5. Embellishment — the "mojo"**
- Insight: technically-correct, in-key playing that still sounds bland/vanilla isn't a vocabulary gap — the great masters are often playing the same notes, just with more embellishment. A friend (Ben Forehand) named this directly as Seriff needing "more vibe."
- **Five core embellishments to layer onto essentially every note:** slides (from another scale tone, or from a half step below), hammer-ons, pull-offs, bends (e.g. from a half step below), and vibrato.
- Drill: revisit existing licks/lines and deliberately insert these ornaments onto notes that were previously played clean — goal is maximum "sauce" in every moment, not just correct pitches/rhythm.
