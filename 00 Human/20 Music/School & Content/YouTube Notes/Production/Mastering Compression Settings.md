# Mastering Compression Settings

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YUn31aE9X_8)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YUn31aE9X_8)

## Synopsis

A 12-chapter, plugin-demonstrated survey of every compression-adjacent tool in a mastering engineer's kit, from Sage Audio (the same analog mastering studio behind [[The Only Music Mastering Video You’ll Ever Need]], [[Audio Mastering Science: Using Research to Make Informed Decisions]], [[Reverb on a Master: Everything You Need to Know]], [[Stop Limiting Your Masters]], and [[All Processing is Connected - Crucial Knowledge for Pro Mixes & Masters]]). Where those videos build toward one coherent chain, this one is a menu — glue compression, transparent RMS/Peak compression, multiband-as-EQ, ear-mimicking multiband, New York style parallel compression, the Omnipressor's simultaneous compression+expansion, mid-image compression for stereo width, upward compression (and a New York-style upward variant isolated to a mid frequency band), mastering-stage de-essing, multiband resonance reduction, and dynamic-EQ out-of-key attenuation — twelve genuinely distinct techniques, each demoed with a named plugin (several free), each with concrete numbers. The throughline across nearly every chapter is **timing compression to the track's BPM** (the video gives the actual formula, not just "match the tempo") and **keeping attack/release times slow enough to avoid harmonic distortion** — the same "don't let the processing announce itself" restraint that runs through the channel's other mastering videos, applied here specifically to the compression family of tools rather than the whole chain.

## Techniques

- **Glue compression, BPM-timed release (the actual formula).** Glue compression = a long release so multiple transients get compressed collectively, reading as cohesive — but an out-of-time release makes the signal's return-to-unity land like a rogue transient and can mess up perceived timing. Formula: `60,000 ÷ BPM = one quarter note in ms` → multiply by 4 for one whole note → subtract the attack time → use that as the release. (At 140 BPM: 60,000 ÷ 140 = 429 ms per quarter note → ×4 = 1,716 ms whole note, minus attack.) A **vari-mu ("very-mu") compressor** — one that imparts tone via harmonics + subtle EQ, not just gain reduction — is the classic tool for this; demoed on Pulsar Audio Mu (free alternative: Klanghelm). → [[Glue Compressor]], [[Compression]], [[Master Chain]]
- **Transparent RMS/Peak compression, 16th-note-timed release, mid-biased stereo sensitivity, capped at 1.5 dB.** For compression that should be inaudible as processing: attack no quicker than 10 ms, release no quicker than 50 ms (faster cuts into the transient → harmonic distortion), release timed to a **16th note** (`60,000 ÷ BPM ÷ 4`), stereo sensitivity set toward the **center/mid image** (detects where the master's energy actually lives), and threshold/ratio dialed so total gain reduction never exceeds ~1.5 dB. Blend Peak detection in with RMS to catch loud peaks RMS alone would let through. Demoed on Tokyo Dawn Labs Kotelnikov (free). → [[Compression]], [[Glue Compressor]], [[Master Chain]]
- **Multiband compression as dynamic EQ — attenuate a problem band only when it's actually loud, not statically.** Same BPM-timed-release and slow-attack/release rules apply, except the highest band can run much faster attack/release (the distortion these settings cause is a low-frequency artifact). Two worked examples: a muddy mix with buried vocals → center a band on **250 Hz, attenuate 1–2 dB**; a reverb masking the vocal → dynamically attenuate **2–5 kHz on the side image only** so the mid-image vocal cuts through whenever it's present. Demoed on FabFilter Pro-MB (free alternative: TDR Nova). → [[Pro-MB]], [[Multiband Compression]], [[Masking and Carving]], [[Master Chain]]
- **Multiband compression tuned to mimic how human ears compress loud sound.** The ear compresses frequencies **below 1 kHz** by 1–20 dB (louder = more attenuation) whenever a sound gets loud enough, and the two structures responsible can't react instantly: ~**40 ms** to contract, staying contracted ~**150 ms**. Recipe: multiband, attenuate a couple dB below 1 kHz, **attack 40 ms, release 150 ms**, softer knee for gradual compression, no lookahead. The result reads as more "natural" sounding compression because it mirrors the ear's own response curve rather than an arbitrary setting. Demoed on FabFilter Pro-MB (free alternative: TDR Nova). → [[Pro-MB]], [[Multiband Compression]]
- **New York style parallel compression, full recipe — and a "drive the compressor harder" character move.** Send + aux track; on the aux, a compressor (glue-style or transparent, but pushed to heavy attenuation since it'll be blended in) followed by a fully parametric or stylistic (e.g. Pultec-style) EQ, **linear phase to avoid phase cancellation** against the dry channel. EQ choices are free — the video's example boosts kick + vocal range, subs the highs. **To make the compression itself more audible/characterful:** insert a second EQ *before* the compressor and drive specific frequencies into it — the compressor works harder on those bands, so the compression on those frequencies reads as more apparent, and the aux fader becomes an easy single-knob automation target for the whole effect. Demoed on FabFilter Pro-C2 (owned) and a free SSL emulation by Analog Obsession. → [[Parallel Processing]], [[Pro-C 2]], [[Master Chain]]
- **The Omnipressor — simultaneous compression above threshold and expansion below it, in one device.** Attenuates the loud parts and amplifies the quiet parts at the same time, keeping the signal inside a controlled dynamic range (loud parts quieter, quiet parts louder) without a single heavy limiting move. Mastering-safe settings: limit both the compression and expansion ranges to **~1 dB each**, ratio/function near the middle, threshold set carefully. Works especially well **right before the limiter** — gets a master louder without heavy peak attenuation. No exact free equivalent for the single-device routing, but two instances of a free compressor (Melda M Compressor) run one for compression, one for expansion can approximate the effect. → [[Compression]], [[Parallel Processing]], [[Plugin Wishlist]]
- **Mid-image-only compression as a stereo-width move — a genuinely different mechanism from EQ-based imaging.** With a mid/side-capable compressor, compress **only the mid (center) image** by 0.5–1.5 dB and skip makeup gain (makeup gain would cancel the relative loudness shift the technique depends on) — attenuating the mid image makes the side image sound louder *relative* to the center, reading as stereo expansion, without ever boosting the sides directly. Described as one of the video's favorite mastering moves for a natural-sounding width increase. Frequency-specific version: a multiband/mid-side tool (FabFilter Pro-MB) so only certain bands widen. Free option: Melda M Compressor's routing switched from Stereo to Mid — Side. → [[Width and Stereo]], [[Pro-MB]], [[Master Chain]]
- **Upward compression — four named tools, one shared mechanism (amplify quiet detail instead of attenuating peaks).** Waves MV2 measures and lifts quiet sections; Sonnox Oxford Inflator (owned) prioritizes and amplifies signal detail while adding harmonics; the free Melda M Compressor's custom-shape mode does a similar job with a different final character; Xfer OTT offers frequency-specific maximization but needs very subtle settings (it's an aggressive effect by default). Stated personal favorite for this role: the **Sonnox Oxford Limiter's Enhance function** — increases perceived detail/loudness without attenuating peaks at all. → [[Oxford Inflator]], [[OTT]], [[Clipping and Loudness]], [[Master Chain]], [[Plugin Wishlist]]
- **New York style *upward* compression, isolated to a mid frequency band — a distinct recipe from the plain parallel-compression chapter above.** Send + aux; linear-phase EQ high-pass + low-pass to isolate a mid band (worked example: 400 Hz–4 kHz); an upward compressor/maximizer on that isolated band pushed hard (Sonnox Inflator's Effect at 100%); blend the processed band back in with the original until balanced. Result: the dynamic range of that mid band shrinks and previously-masked detail in it becomes clearly audible. Same "drive it with EQ first" option as the plain NY chapter — the worked example adds a 2 kHz bell for vocal clarity and boosts the side image ~500 Hz, both purely by ear/taste, not a fixed rule. → [[Parallel Processing]], [[Oxford Inflator]], [[Master Chain]]
- **De-essing during mastering — narrower and more careful than a mix-stage de-esser.** Sibilant energy usually sits **5–8 kHz** (confirm by ear or on the spectrum, it varies by track) — isolate the compression band there and set the threshold carefully: too loose and it attenuates unrelated high-frequency content (cymbals, air) whenever it triggers. The correctness check is listening to whether attenuation only fires *with* the sibilant, not just "some" high end. Multiband compression is a valid substitute for a dedicated de-esser. Demoed on a Weiss de-esser; free options: TDR de-esser, Tone Boosters Sibilance 4 ("a lot of control, really affordable"). → [[De-essing]], [[Pro-DS]], [[Master Chain]]
- **Resonance reduction reframed as multiband compression with a preset threshold, not "a form of EQ."** Demoed on Soothe2's **"Bounce to the Grammy Awards"** preset, which measures the **mid and side images separately** so resonance suppression can differ between center and sides. From there, adjust the pre-emphasis EQ bands to target whatever's too prevalent — commonly the low-mids and the sibilance range, but confirm by ear. Higher-quality/oversampling settings reduce phase cancellation; dial the final amount with the mix knob. Free-alternative-tier option: Baby Audio Smooth Operator ("a good affordable option," not a full free equivalent). → [[Soothe2]], [[Master Chain]]
- **Dynamic EQ to attenuate out-of-key frequencies — a musicality move, not a corrective one.** In-key content becomes relatively louder (and reads as more musical) when out-of-key frequencies get gently ducked. Workflow: run the track through a free online tuner/key analyzer to find the key, look up which frequencies are in vs. out of key, use a piano-roll-aware EQ (FabFilter Pro-Q's piano roll — Nate owns Pro-Q 4, video demos Pro-Q 3) to snap band center frequencies exactly to notes (or look up exact Hz values by hand without that feature), enable Dynamics per band, target **0.5–1 dB of attenuation**, and keep **Q narrow** so the cut lands mostly on the specific out-of-key note rather than bleeding into neighboring in-key content. → [[Pro-Q 4]], [[EQ]], [[Master Chain]]
