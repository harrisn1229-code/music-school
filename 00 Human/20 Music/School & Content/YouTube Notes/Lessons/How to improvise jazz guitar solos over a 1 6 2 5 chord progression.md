# How to improvise jazz guitar solos over a 1 6 2 5 chord progression

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7aQUQUGcUk)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7aQUQUGcUk)

## Synopsis
Jared Borkowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared) tackles a specific practical problem: improvising over a **1–6–2–5 turnaround at two beats per chord** — the classic [[Turnarounds|I–vi–ii–V]] loop, and the same skeleton that forms the A section of [[Rhythm Changes]] — where the chords move too fast to consciously chase each one. Demonstrated live over a looped `Dmaj7 Bm7 Em7 A7` progression comped with a walking-bass-line accompaniment (his own separate technique, not covered here). The video is structured as an ordered practice ladder rather than a single tip: start by treating the whole progression as one sustained tonic, then deliberately target the downbeat, then add chromatic tension before landing on it, then switch to strict chord-tone outlining (called the "be-all end-all" exercise), then practice pure real-time chord awareness independent of what you're actually playing. All of that scaffolding exists to set up the video's real payoff — **prolongation**: treating a fast-moving progression as an extended stay on fewer harmonic functions than the chord symbols show, demonstrated at three levels of aggressiveness from "the whole loop is just I" up to "eight bars as I, eight bars as V" against a rhythm section still playing the real changes underneath. Closes with a confidence drill — deliberately play something that sounds bad, then land it cleanly — and points to two of his own resources (a free chord-tone/arpeggio PDF and a phrasing playlist). Strongest material for Nate: the prolongation grouping logic (which pairs of chords collapse to which function, and why more than one grouping is valid for the same four chords) and the explicit sequencing argument for *why* chord-tone outlining has to come before prolongation, not instead of it — full extraction and generalized write-up now lives on the new [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] technique note.

## What's taught
- **The progression and its context** — `Dmaj7 Bm7 Em7 A7`, two beats per chord, described explicitly as "the one chord, the six chord, the two chord, the five chord" and identified as a portion of [[Rhythm Changes]] (the Gershwin *I Got Rhythm* form). Comped live with a walking-bass-line loop; a link to his separate walking-bass-with-chords mini-series is mentioned but not part of this lesson. [~0:00] → [[Rhythm Changes]], [[Turnarounds]]
- **You don't have to target every chord — but practicing as if you do earns you the freedom not to.** States up front that the answer to "do you really need to target and outline every chord" is "definitely not," but that outlining anyway conditions the internal ear and body to what the harmony is doing, so targeting later becomes optional rather than mandatory. This is the framing thesis for the whole video. [~0:24] → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]
- **Step 1 — play the tonic's scale/chord tones across the entire loop, ignoring the other three chords.** Over the D-based progression: D major pentatonic and/or D major-7 chord tones the whole way through, treating the fast-moving turnaround as if it were a single sustained D major. Framed as the seed of the prolongation concept introduced properly later. [~1:24] → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]
- **Step 2 — deliberately target beat 1 of the progression.** Once undirected scale noodling starts to feel arbitrary against the harmony, make landing on the root or a chord tone of chord 1, exactly when it recurs, a conscious goal — whether that's after playing continuously through a phrase or after a pause. The point isn't restraint (you can build tension by playing through) — it's knowing, physically, exactly when the downbeat lands. [~2:52] → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]
- **Step 3 — add chromatic tension before that same landing point.** Same target (beat 1), but now approach it with chromatic notes dropped into an otherwise-diatonic line (D major pentatonic / D major blues / diatonic scale). Explicitly normalizes missing the landing while learning this — over-chromaticizing and losing the target is called out as expected, useful feedback rather than failure. [~4:35] → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]], [[Bebop Vocabulary]]
- **Step 4 — outline the chord tones of every single chord, called the "be-all end-all" exercise.** Play only chord tones — Dmaj7, Bm7, Em7, A7 in turn — with musical phrasing rather than mechanical arpeggios. Called out as worth **weeks or months** of dedicated slow/out-of-tempo practice, and framed as "learn it, then forget it" — the goal is for chord-tone landing to become unconscious. [~5:53] → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]], [[Guide Tones]]
- **Step 5 — the awareness drill: track which chord you're on while playing anything.** Play freely, sound quality irrelevant, while continuously naming/feeling which chord is currently sounding ("that's the one... there's the two... the five... the six"). Explicitly decoupled from targeting — pure real-time harmonic awareness, made possible only because Step 4's drilling already built the underlying ear. [~7:36] → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]
- **Prolongation, defined** — "the practice of taking any one type of harmony and asking how you can stay on that harmony for a long time and still make it interesting." Introduced as the single most powerful concept in the video: the 1-6-2-5 is reframed as fundamentally a way of prolonging *one* chord, and can be soloed over that way regardless of what the rhythm section is actually playing underneath. [~8:26] → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]
- **Prolongation level 1 — the whole loop as a single function.** Play D major (tonic) across all four chords for the entire progression, same as Step 1 but now named and understood as prolongation rather than a warm-up drill. [~8:26] → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]
- **Prolongation level 2 — split the loop in half, one function per half, more than one valid grouping.** First two chords (`Dmaj7 Bm7`) as I, second two (`Em7 A7`) as V — outline chord tones first to internalize the split, then play more freely while still thinking "one... five... one... five." Explicitly offered as an alternative grouping over the *same four chords*: first two as vi, second two as ii (`Dmaj7 Bm7` heard as B-minor-ish, `Em7 A7` heard as E-minor-ish) — demonstrated live, noting the ear can pull toward the major reading even while consciously grouping as minor. The takeaway: which functional grouping you hear is a choice, not a fixed fact about the chart. [~9:24]–[~13:16] → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]], [[Turnarounds]]
- **Prolongation level 3 — extreme, multi-bar blocks.** Four full cycles of the progression treated entirely as I, then four more cycles treated entirely as V — effectively "eight measures of one, eight measures of five" laid over a rhythm section still playing the real, unchanged 1-6-2-5 underneath every two beats. States plainly you can be "really, really loose" with the reading as long as the phrase eventually resolves. In practice, played roughly 2 cycles of I / 2 cycles of V rather than the full 4+4 — called out on camera as fine, "try all kinds of things." [~13:34] → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]
- **Tension isn't only dissonance — repetition creates tension too.** Explicit reframe: playing something consonant but repeating it builds tension through anticipation ("what are they going to do — stop doing it?") rather than through clashing notes. Broadens the definition of "tension and release" beyond chromaticism. [~14:19] → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]
- **Closing confidence drill — deliberately play something that sounds bad to you, then land cleanly.** After all the prior targeting/awareness work, intentionally push into a passage that feels genuinely "off" or ugly in the moment, then resolve it back onto a chord tone or the tonic. Framed as proof you can recover from anything and as a way to stop treating any single phrase preciously; explicitly states the recovery only works *because* the earlier steps built a reliable sense of "home." [~19:15] → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]
- **Genre-agnostic framing, stated explicitly.** Repeated point that the method isn't jazz-specific — "you don't have to play jazz over this... we're just talking about how to make sense of playing over seventh chords" — the ladder applies to any fast-moving turnaround, not only rhythm-changes-style progressions. [~2:07], [~21:00-ish] → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]
- **Two referenced (not covered in this video) resources** — a free chord-tone/arpeggio-vocabulary PDF ("every arpeggio shape you need to improvise over any jazz chord that comes up," soundguitarlessons.com/chord-tones) and a separate phrasing playlist for lyrical soloing. Named as the two things that most directly extend this lesson; neither is a plugin or purchasable tool, so nothing routes to the wishlist. [~19:50]–[~20:40]
