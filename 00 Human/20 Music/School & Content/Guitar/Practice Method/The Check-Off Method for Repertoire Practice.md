# The Check-Off Method for Repertoire Practice

A named practice-order method for learning **written repertoire** (a classical piece, a fingerstyle arrangement, solo guitar, any fully-notated melody) — Jared's own name for it is the "check-off method." Same channel/author as [[Backward Chaining Practice]] and shares that note's clean-reps-in-a-row gating mechanic, but a **different combination order**: Backward Chaining works end-to-beginning, one measure at a time; the check-off method builds a piece **forward in pairs**, deliberately alternating between drilling small units alone and drilling them combined, in a fixed pyramid pattern. Best suited to through-composed/written material rather than looser improvisational playing — Jared is explicit that this is for "something that is just written," not a looser or more improvised context.

## The pyramid combination order

Worked example on an 8-measure passage (the opening of Bach's Bourrée in E minor from the Lute Suite, BWV 996, Jared's own solo-guitar arrangement):

1. Measure 1 alone.
2. Measure 2 alone.
3. Measures 1+2 together.
4. Measure 3 alone.
5. Measure 4 alone.
6. Measures 3+4 together.
7. Measures 1–4 all together.
8. Measure 5 alone.
9. Measure 6 alone.
10. Measures 5+6 together.
11. Measure 7 alone.
12. Measure 8 alone.
13. Measures 7+8 together.
14. Measures 5–8 together.
15. Measures 1–8 (the whole passage) together.

The shape is: drill each single unit → drill the pair combined → once two pairs are both solid, combine the pair-of-pairs into a group of 4 → once two groups of 4 are solid, combine into a group of 8. This doubling structure generalizes to any passage length by continuing the same pattern (groups of 8 combine into 16, and so on) rather than being fixed at 8 measures. **Segment boundaries follow phrasing, not always a literal barline** — where a segment starts with a pickup beat, Jared always lands the segment on beat 1 of the following measure rather than cutting it exactly at the bar line.

*(general starting point on segment size: single measures is "the most broken-down version you can do," explicitly recommended especially for classical material — but Jared says he often skips straight to a larger first unit, e.g. starting at 2 or 4 measures at once, when the material allows it, rather than always starting at 1 measure)*

## The gate: N clean reps in a row, not N reps total

Each segment isn't "checked off" until it's played correctly **a set number of times consecutively** — a genuine unbroken streak, not an accumulated tally. **A single wrong note resets the count to zero**, even if you were on the last rep of the target count.

- **Baseline demoed: 3 correct in a row.** For anything higher-stakes or where real certainty is needed, push the target count well above 3 — Jared references wanting counts as high as **10 in a row** for real assurance.
- **Why push the count up rather than stopping at 3:** at a low count you risk "accidentally" getting it right three times without the passage actually being secure. At a high count (e.g. approaching 10), a slip becomes useful information rather than a setback — Jared describes an almost paradoxical relief at failing near the top of a high count ("Oh, good... that means I don't have it") because it confirms the segment genuinely isn't locked in yet, rather than letting a lucky streak create false confidence. The goal is explicitly **not to minimize mistakes but to eliminate the possibility of them** — over-program the segment so execution requires no conscious thought when it counts, freeing full attention for musical expression (dynamics, phrasing) instead.
- Judge each rep at your own standard of strictness — a technically-correct rep with one small tone issue can still count if you decide it does; the point is a real, honest streak against whatever bar you set, not a rigid note-perfect requirement.

## Tracking mechanism

Two interchangeable versions of the same system, either works:

- **On the printed sheet music itself, in pencil:** bracket the measure groupings directly on the score (a bracket over measure 1, over measure 2, over measures 1+2, etc.) with a checkbox next to each bracket, checked off once that segment hits its clean-rep target. This was Jared's method "for years" — print the piece, draw brackets and checkboxes by hand.
- **A spreadsheet, one row per segment** — lists every segment (measure 1, measure 2, measures 1+2, measure 3…) down the piece, with a column to track completion; scales to holding an entire repertoire list (multiple pieces) in one place rather than loose marked-up sheet music per piece.
- **Rep counter app:** Jared uses an app called **Multi Timer** as a simple tap-counter for tracking reps toward the target streak in real time, especially once the target count goes past 3 or 4 and becomes hard to track by feel; the same app also runs his overall practice-session timer and a break timer (to cap how long a rest between segments runs).
- **No metronome for classical/expressive material** — deliberately not used here, even though the demoed Bach passage is fairly metronomic by nature; the point is to leave room for expressive timing rather than mechanical timing, which a metronome would suppress during this drilling phase.

## Why it beats normal "practice until it feels done"

**The "shiny spot" bias:** left unstructured, players default to replaying whatever is already comfortable, because it feels good and low-risk — Jared's analogy is cleaning a bathroom and gravitating back to the one spot that's already sparkling instead of the genuinely dirty one. The result (without a system) is a passage with a few measures drilled a hundred times and others drilled four times — an uneven "heat map" of security across the piece, with the real trouble spots the least rehearsed. **Checking a segment off removes it from what you keep re-drilling**, forcing attention onto whatever's still unchecked — i.e., onto the actual weak material — instead of onto the parts that already feel good.

**Refresh is built in, not a separate maintenance step.** Because later stages recombine already-checked-off smaller units (playing measures 1–4 together necessarily replays measures 1, 2, 3, and 4 again), the early segments get naturally re-exposed as the piece is built up, without needing a dedicated spaced-repetition pass bolted on afterward. If a checked-off segment later proves shaky when it resurfaces inside a bigger combination, the fix is simple: drill it again, at a higher rep count than before, rather than reworking the whole tracking system.

## Origin and use case

Developed under real time pressure: Jared traces the method back roughly 10 years to being called, with only a few weeks' notice, to play a technically demanding new guitar/orchestra score (Stuart Copeland's re-imagined score for *Ben Hur*, performed with the Seattle Rock Orchestra and Copeland himself on drums) while already overcommitted elsewhere. The check-off method — used exclusively to prepare that part — is credited as the specific reason the performance came together in time and went well. Framed explicitly as a "secret weapon" for compressed prep time, but usable for any written piece regardless of deadline pressure.
