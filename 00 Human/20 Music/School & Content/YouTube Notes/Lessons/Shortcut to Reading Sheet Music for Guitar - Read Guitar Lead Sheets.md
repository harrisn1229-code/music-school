# Shortcut to Reading Sheet Music for Guitar | Read Guitar Lead Sheets

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NMQkJj0jreU)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NMQkJj0jreU)

## Synopsis

Jared Borkowski (soundguitarlessons.com) answers a student's question — "what's a good way to start reading jazz lead sheets out of the real book?" — with one specific, unconventional shortcut he built for himself: **pick exactly one fretboard spot per written pitch, in one register, and drill until the mapping is automatic.** The framing is a direct comparison to piano: a pianist has exactly one key per written pitch, so sight-reading pitch recognition is a solved problem before you even touch rhythm; a guitarist has that same pitch playable in four to six different places, and *that* — not note-naming — is the actual source of guitar reading's reputation for being uniquely hard. Rather than teaching a full position system top to bottom (the conventional method-book approach, which he says can take years to reach the point of reading fluently around the whole neck), he deliberately narrows the problem: freeze one location per note in a single working register, ignore rhythm completely at first, and just drill "jump to the note" against real lead-sheet melodies (he demos on Pat Metheny's "Bright Size Life," a second unnamed tune, and "What a Wonderful World"). This is a companion piece to [[Reading Notation on Guitar]] rather than a replacement for the position system taught there — it's a specific *training method* for building fluency once (or instead of) working through the full CAGED/position map, and it's aimed squarely at "I know some theory, I want to start actually reading real book melodies" rather than at beginners.

## What's taught

**The core method — fix one location per pitch, drill pitch before rhythm**
- The guitar-specific reading problem, stated precisely: on piano, one written pitch = one key, full stop — the instrument removes the location decision entirely. On guitar, the *same* written pitch can be played in up to five or six different places on the neck, each a different fingering/tone/what's-reachable-next. Reading on guitar is therefore a **pitch → position** decision, not just a pitch → note-name decision, and that's the actual bottleneck, not note recognition itself.
- **The shortcut: deliberately imitate the piano's constraint.** Pick ONE specific fret/string location for each note name, in one working register/position, and commit to playing that note there *every single time it appears in any piece of sheet music* — even when the note is reachable elsewhere (open string, a different position). Jared's own example: F♯ in the register he demos always goes to one specific fretted spot, never the open-adjacent alternative, never a different position — no exceptions, so there's zero decision-making left when the note appears mid-phrase.
- **Deliberately starts in the middle of the fretboard, not open position.** Most reading method books start beginners in open/first position and work outward, which Jared says can take years (his estimate: "you gotta get to like the third volume... takes you like five years") to reach fluent reading further up the neck. This method skips that ladder — you can design the one-spot-per-note map anywhere on the neck (any register, not necessarily his), the point is committing to a single fixed answer per pitch, not which specific register you pick.
- **Pitch before rhythm, strictly sequenced.** Step 1: ignore rhythm entirely. Look at a lead sheet and just quiz yourself — "where's that note, where's that note" — jumping straight from notehead to fretboard spot as fast as possible, treating it as a flashcard drill, not a performance. Step 2 (only once pitch-jumping feels comfortable): go back and add rhythm on top of the now-automatic pitch map. Framed explicitly as "worry about one thing at a time" — don't try to solve pitch-location and rhythm simultaneously while the pitch map is still new.
- **It's fine to not know the note names yet.** If you're still at "can I even identify what the notes are here," that's an expected phase — take the time, it passes once the initial hurdle clears.
- **Claimed timeline:** roughly two weeks of this drilling to reach "grab any tune and start plunking out the melody," versus the years-long trajectory of a conventional position-by-position method book.
- **Practice material recommendation — read real repertoire, not exercises.** Pull actual lead sheets/real-book melodies rather than method-book drills once you're doing this: demoed on Pat Metheny's "Bright Size Life" and "What a Wonderful World." For a denser workout, he names the **Charlie Parker Omnibook** specifically — dense, chromatic, transcribed improvised lines (not just heads) — as the place to go once the basic drill feels easy and you want to push it in a jazz context.
- **Working with tunes you already know vs. don't.** Recognizing a familiar tune as you decode it (his in-video reaction to landing on a phrase from "Bright Size Life") is called out as a good feedback signal and genuinely motivating; he explicitly also recommends practicing on tunes you *don't* know, since that's closer to real cold-reading conditions.

**Reinforced background (already documented, confirmed by this video)**
- Guitar is a transposing instrument — written pitch sounds an octave below what's played/heard relative to piano or violin; classical guitar scores sometimes mark this with a small **8** below the treble clef (the `8vb` clef), and many guitarists separately learn to read "up an octave" so they can play piano/violin sheet music at true pitch. Matches [[Reading Notation on Guitar]] §1 exactly — no new information here, just an independent confirmation from a second source.
- Pay attention to the key signature before naming any note — several of his on-camera reads are explicitly gated on checking sharps/flats first (e.g., catching an F♯ because the key signature carries it, catching a B♭ the same way) rather than misreading the note as natural.

**Free companion resource (chords, not notation)**
- Jared offers a free "Any Jazz Chord" PDF booklet (soundguitarlessons.com, linked in the video description) — a complementary chord-voicing method using **only eight shapes** that he says cover any chord symbol that comes up on a jazz tune. It's the chord-comping counterpart to this video's single-note reading shortcut — same "reduce a large decision space to a small fixed set" philosophy, applied to voicings instead of note locations.
