> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **Sing the tonic, play any random note, judge that note's degree against what you sang, back-calculate**
> You never need to know the pitch of the note you sang. The random note is the one you *do* know, so the degree relationship converts it into a key.
>
> **Q2.** **D major**
> *Also accepted:* D
> If G is degree 4, the tonic is a perfect 4th below G, which is D.
>
> **Q3.** **Because the method only ever asks for relationships, and one known pitch supplies the anchor**
> *Rubric (5):* says perfect pitch would mean naming a pitch in isolation (1) · says this method instead judges a *relationship* between the sung tonic and a played note (2) · says the instrument supplies the one absolute reference needed, so the ear never has to (2)
> Perfect pitch names a note with no reference. Everything here is relative: you can hear that a note is degree 4 without knowing what either note is called. The trick is that the instrument already knows — playing a random note gives you one pitch whose name is certain, and a single known pitch plus a felt relationship is enough to fix the key. The ear does the part it's good at and the instrument does the part it's good at.
>
> **Q4.** **Memorise · contour · sing slowly and pencil in felt degrees · deduce the rest · verify**
> Verification is on the instrument, and it's where the error analysis happens.
>
> **Q5.** **Contour before degrees — step 2**
> Getting the shape first constrains the degrees you can't hear by the ones you can. Skipping it means guessing each note independently with nothing narrowing the options.
>
> **Q6.** **Direction and rough size — up or down, step, leap or big leap. No degree numbers yet**
> Just the shape. Committing to degrees this early is what step 2 exists to prevent.
>
> **Q7.** **In which degree got confused for which**
> *Also accepted:* the specific substitution · not the fact of being wrong
> "I heard 6 as 3" is a repeatable finding and points at a same-world confusion. "I got it wrong" isn't information.
>
> **Q8.** **Melodic scale-degree · bass-note · chord-quality · chord-number/position**
>
> **Q9.** **Under five minutes — short enough that it actually happens every day**
> Three minutes a day beats an hour a month for a perceptual skill, so brevity is the mechanism rather than a concession to a busy schedule.
>
> **Q10.** **Because the skill decays, so the maintenance is the real deliverable rather than the syllabus**
> *Rubric (5):* says ear training is a perceptual skill that decays without use (2) · says the lessons exist to install the routine rather than to deliver a body of knowledge (2) · notes that passing the final doesn't stop the routine — the course is standing for exactly this reason (1)
> Every other course hands over facts, and facts sit still once learned — the spaced-repetition drip is enough to hold them. A perceptual skill isn't like that: stop using it and it fades, regardless of how well you once knew the material. So the lessons aren't really delivering content; each one installs a daily habit and the content is the instruction manual for that habit. Passing the final marks the course complete and changes nothing about the practice, which is why this class is standing rather than scheduled.
