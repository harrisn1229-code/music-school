## Techniques

**1. Pedal point on the main riff.** The iconic main riff (doubled by the guitar) uses pedal point: a low note or group of low notes repeats while other instruments play a series of different chords over the top. Here, the keyboards move **E minor → C major → D major → back to E minor**, while the bass stays on **E minor the whole time** — creating tension against the keyboard chords the bass doesn't "agree" with, which resolves when everyone lands back on E minor together. Demoed by contrast: a version where the bass instead followed the keyboard's chord changes note-for-note sounds noticeably flatter/less interesting than the pedal-point original. **How to apply it:** don't change your bass note when the other instruments change chords underneath you — it can feel tense (possibly very tense, depending on the specific changes), but the payoff lands when everyone converges back together. → [[POP-C03 — Pedal point|Pedal Point]] section of [[Loop-Based Harmony]], [[POP-C03 — Pedal point]]

**2. Bass runs (fills) as melodic connectors between chords.** "A bass run is just another word for a bass fill" — extra transition notes added to the bassline at a chord change, layered on top of an otherwise-plain part. Three worked examples, escalating in ambition:
- **Verse → pre-chorus (E minor → C major).** Weaker alternatives demoed first: jump straight from E to C, or just sit on the root until the section changes — both work but read as flat. The actual bass run instead climbs the E minor scale using **scale degrees 1, 2, 3, then 5, then 6** to melodically connect E to C, creating forward momentum into the new section.
- **Bar 4 of the pre-chorus.** Rather than the plain D→E motion the guitar chords themselves outline, this run mimics the **rhythm of the vocal line** at "make it or not" — bass and vocal move together rhythmically at that moment, a deliberate call-back to the vocal rather than an independent bass idea.
- **The chorus run — one run spanning three chords (D, G, C).** Called out as one of BassBuzz's own favorite melodic moments in the song. Full target-note method and "playing over the bar line" framing → [[Bass Runs and Target Notes]].

**3. The bassline tracks the song's overall intensity arc.** Most pop-structured songs start less intense and build — a good bassline follows that in terms of **busyness**: how many runs/fills there are, and how "crazy" they get. Demonstrated bluntly by A/B-ing the song's intro against its outro — a huge difference in activity level. Zoomed in further: **first chorus vs. last chorus.** The first chorus is mostly scripted, chugging eighth notes, with only one fill at the end of the phrase (a 16th-note "hiccup" that briefly disrupts the rhythm). By the last chorus — after the song has modulated up a key (the transcript says "G minor"; worth sanity-checking against the actual recording, since the commonly-cited key change for this song is E minor up to F# minor, and this transcript is auto-captioned) — the same four bars carry noticeably more: a slide at the end of bar 1, the scale-run fill from bar 2 extended further, and bar 4 packed with 16th notes. **General rule of thumb:** listen to a song's felt intensity arc first, then decide how much space to fill at the beginning vs. the end, or in a verse vs. a chorus — save runs, fills, and fast notes for the song's more intense parts. **Explicit guardrail:** don't go overboard on the intensity variation, or the busy sections start to sound bad rather than exciting (a deliberately-overdone demo is played to make the point audible). → [[Bassline Writing]]
