## What's taught

**Book structure:** two main sections — chord construction, and rhythm. Borton samples both rather than exhaustively covering either, and explicitly asks in the video whether viewers want a part 2 covering more of the chord-construction section.

**Chord construction — voice leading (three variants, increasing complexity):**
- **Single-note voice leading (the foundation case):** one note/voice in the chord moves through the changes in stepwise motion while the rest of the chord changes underneath it. Borton's own worked example: through most of a ii-V-I, the **7th of the ii chord moves to the 3rd of the dominant** — this is the same 7th→3rd guide-tone mechanic already fully documented on [[Guide Tones]] (§"Guide tones move by step"), here confirmed independently from a second, book-sourced example.
- **Multi-note voice leading (the advanced variant):** more than one voice moves stepwise at once — demonstrated over a chord progression built on a **B♭ blues**, with voice leading happening simultaneously in the **middle voices of the chords** and on the **top note**. **Practice-method tip, stated as important:** for this multi-voice form specifically, Green recommends memorizing and working the movement out **beforehand** rather than trying to improvise it live in real time — treat each multi-voice voice-leading move "almost as cord licks" (chord licks), the same way a single-note melodic lick gets memorized and deployed, not derived on the fly. *(General starting point: write out a multi-voice voice-leading move over a static progression, memorize it as a fixed shape-to-shape unit, then drop it into a tune where the harmony matches, rather than trying to construct the moving inner voices by ear mid-performance.)*
- **Common-tone voice leading (the third variant, distinct from stepwise motion):** instead of a voice moving by step, one note of a chord is a **common tone** that's also present in — and carries over/holds into — the next chord in the progression. Usually done with the **top voice** of the chord. This is the same device documented at length (with a full ii-V-I "which top note, which extension" catalog) on the video page [[IMPROVE your chords with COMMON TONES and VOICE LEADING!]] and routed to [[Chord Voicings]]/[[Extended Chords]]/[[The ii-V-I]]/[[Guide Tones]] — Green's book is a second, independent source confirming the same top-note-holds-through-the-changes mechanic.

**Rhythm — anticipation and delay:**
- **The problem stated first:** comping that always lands squarely on the downbeat is predictable and boring — the fix is rhythmic displacement relative to the beat.
- **Anticipation:** play the target chord an **eighth note before** the downbeat instead of on it.
- **Delay:** play the target chord an **eighth note after** the downbeat instead — the same displacement in the opposite direction.
- **Mix and match both** across a tune rather than picking one displacement direction and sticking to it exclusively.

**Rhythm — long and short notes:**
- Vary the **duration** chords are held for, not just when they're struck — chords can be cut short or let ring long.
- **The actual selection rule, stated as the most important point: don't default to a fixed length either way — listen to what the soloist (or vocalist/lead instrument) is doing and respond to it.** Note duration is a reactive/ensemble choice, not a fixed comping habit — directly the same "read the room, respond to what's happening around you" logic already documented as [[Ensemble Listening and Comping Restraint]].

**Closing demonstration:** Borton combines the voice-leading types with the anticipation/delay/long-short rhythmic vocabulary in a live comping pass under the standard "I Should Care," with trombonist Chris Glassman playing the melody — framed as a worked synthesis rather than a new isolated device, no new technique introduced beyond what's listed above.
