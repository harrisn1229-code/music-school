# Chord Classification from 2 to 12 Notes

Part of [[Music Theory]]. [[Chords]], [[Seventh Chords]], [[Extended Chords]] and
[[Chord Naming by Context]] each teach a slice. This is the **complete enumeration** — how many
distinct chords exist at each size, and what governs whether a name is honest. From Nick Clark's
~153,000-character "Chord Theory."

## The count

Combinations from one fixed root inside an octave, with inversions folded together:

| Notes | Raw combinations | Symmetry adjustment | **Unique types** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **2** — dyads | 11 | — | **11 intervals** |
| **3** — triads | 55 | 1 symmetrical (augmented) → (55−1)/3 = 18 | **19** |
| **4** — tetrads | 165 | 1 fully symmetrical (º7) + 2 partly symmetrical pairs → (165−5)/4 = 40 | **43** |
| **5** — pentads | 330 | none symmetrical → 330/5 = 66 | **66** |
| **6** | — | 11th chords | not enumerated |
| **7** | — | 13th chords — "most extended 13th chords can be matched with their respective 7-note scale" | — |
| **12** | — | he has one, and it spans nearly 4½ octaves | 1 |

**Dyads.** Each of the 11 tempered intervals stands for **at least two named intervals**. He
colour-codes provenance: 12 of the names occur in the major scale and its modes, 4 more in harmonic
minor, 2 in Neapolitan minor — **18 that cover "the vast majority of music."** The last 4, including
the **augmented third** and **diminished sixth**, need a scale with both ♭2 and ♯4 — which points
straight at Raag Todi. All 20 exist inside the thaat system; see
[[The 32 Thaats (32 Modes)]], which reaches the same 20 from the other direction.

**Triads (19).** The four named ones (major, minor, diminished, augmented) plus the suspended family
(sus4, sus♯4, sus♭2 — sus2 is treated as an inversion of sus4). **The other eleven are all "no 5"
chords** — extended triads with the fifth removed. He excludes "no 3" chords outright, on the
grounds that every one is an inverted "no 5."

**Tetrads (43).** Method: take each named triad, add every remaining note one at a time. What each
tetrad reads as, in each key it could belong to. His two rules for what will actually attach:

- **To a major or augmented triad**: ♯1, ♭2, 2, ♯2, ♭3, 4, ♯4, ♯5, ♭6, 6, ♯6, ♭7, 7.
- **To a minor or diminished triad**: ♭1, ♭2, 2, 3, ♭4, 4, ♯4, ♭5, ♭6, 6, 𝄫7, ♭7, 7.
- Suspended chords sit in between. The ♯9/♭10 only attaches to major; the ♭9/♯8 to anything.

**Pentads.** Three framings, and the third is the practical one: *"think of a tetrad played over an
additional bass note. For me, this is the easiest way to think of 5-note chords."* Which is the
[[Slash Chords]] and [[Upper Structure Chords]] view, arrived at by counting.

## The seventh-chord availability chart

His most directly usable artifact. Rows are chord quality — **maj7, 7, (♯6), 6, m(maj7), m7, m6,
ºmaj7, m7♭5, º7, +maj7, +7, +(♯6), 7♭5**. Columns are scale degree — **I, ♭II, II, ♭III, III, IV,
♭V, ♯IV, V, ♭VI, VI, ♭VII, VII**. Each cell names the scale in which that quality occurs on that
degree, filled in a stated priority order: Ionian → Aeolian → the other church modes → harmonic
minor → melodic minor → harmonic major → Neapolitan minor → Neapolitan major → Hungarian minor →
Todi.

**It deliberately keeps ♭V and ♯IV as separate columns, and 7 vs ♯6 and +7 vs +(♯6) as separate
rows** — enharmonically identical, harmonically not.

What falls out of it:

- **Diminished and augmented chords are harmonically scarce** (m7♭5 excepted). That scarcity, not
  taste, is why they are rare in mainstream writing ([[Diminished Chords]], [[Augmented Chords]]).
- **7♭5 is only available on II and V.**
- **ºmaj7 requires an augmented third**, so it needs a ♭2+♯4 scale — essentially Raag Todi only.
  Which is why, he argues, *"in most cases a Cºmaj7 chord is more accurately a Cº(♭8) chord."*

## The naming argument

This is the through-line of every document he has written, and it is worth engaging with even where
the school won't adopt the notation.

> "The ♭ in ♭3 stands for minor third, not flattened third. Flats and sharps are used to describe
> notes and pitches… minor, major, perfect, augmented, diminished are used to describe intervals."

From which: **♭10 not ♯9** wherever the pitch is genuinely a minor third over a major third; **♯8**
(augmented unison) and **♭8** (diminished unison) as real chord tones; **(♯6)** written into a
symbol to separate a true augmented sixth from a ♭7. See [[Augmented Sixth Chords]] and
[[Chord Naming by Context]].

**Where the school lands**: keep standard names on charts and in concept notes — his own conclusion
concedes the point ("it is easier to write it as D13… this also gives each musician the room to use
their own favorite 13th chord voicings"). Reach for his spelling when an analysis has stopped making
sense, which is precisely when the distinction earns its keep.

## Five claims a textbook wouldn't make

1. **Identity comes from the root you pick, not the notes.** Imagine a chord's notes repeating up and
   down forever: …C, E♭, G, B♭, C, E♭, G, B♭… From that one sequence you can build Cm7, E♭6, Cm/B♭ or
   Cm7/G. The pitches decide nothing on their own ([[Chord Inversions]]).
2. **Voicing decides quality.** *"If the ♯9 is voiced above the major 3 then it feels more like a
   C(♯9/♭10) chord, but if the 3 is voiced above the ♯9 then it might start to sound like a minor
   chord with an added 10th."* And ordering can flip function outright: G, B♭, D, F♯ reads as
   Gm(maj7); put the F♯ on the bottom and it reads as F♯+(♭9).
3. **The instrument's own overtones fight altered fifths.** Because the 5th sounds anyway as an
   overtone, *"chords with a ♭5 or ♯5 have the risk of sounding more like an added ♯11 or ♭13
   chord."* Cmaj7♭5 becomes Cmaj7(♯11); C+7 becomes C7(♭13).
4. **You have to convince the listener.** *"You can't just play the notes and call it a chord, you
   have to convince the listener that that is what they're hearing."* Some intervals are essentially
   unsellable — a Cm(♭4) will be heard as a major third, a Cm(𝄫7) as a 6.
5. **7sus4 chords are disguised.** There are four in a major key (on II, III, V, VI); only the V
   reads as dominant. The others get heard as minor sevenths purely because of expected function
   ([[Suspended Chords]]).

He is honest about where the obsession came from, which is a nice piece of context: **the ukulele
has four strings.** He worked out fingerings for 30 of the 43 tetrads in all 12 keys — 360 four-note
voicings.

And the closing frame, borrowed from the Tao Te Ching: one → two → three → ten thousand. One is the
root; two is major/minor (Ernst Levy) or bright/dark (George Russell); three is T/SD/D
([[HAR-C01 — The three functions: T, SD, D]]) *or* relating every chord to one of the three fully
diminished sevenths (Barry Harris — the mechanism behind
[[Dominant Chord Movement and Flexibility]]).

> "There are 10,000 different ways to categorize chords… but at the end of the day they can all be
> simplified into at most 3 categories."

## "Interesting Chords" — no separate note

**The eighth document in his collection, "Interesting Chords," is a republication of this
document's own chapter** — he says so himself in the hub. It is a flat sequence of ~21 song
vignettes, each a prose analysis plus stacked multi-key Roman numerals. **It gets no note of its
own**; the only material unique to the standalone is a spelling convention `(aug6)` where the parent
writes `(♯6)`, and one Roman-numeral typo that differs between the two. The parent adds a Louis Cole
"Everytime" citation the standalone lacks.

The catalogue itself, since it is the useful part:

| Chord | What it really is | Song |
|---|---|---|
| **E/E♯** | not E/F, not a ºmaj7 — E is the ♭VII of the coming F♯m7 and E♯ is the bass leading tone | The 1975, "Sincerity is Scary" |
| **F/D♭** | D♭+maj7 = ♭III+maj7 from harmonic minor, embellishing D♭maj7 | Mac Miller, "Cinderella" |
| **D/C** | Cmaj13(♯11) no 3/5/7 — "a sort of lydian tonic chord," or an inverted D7 | Thundercat, "What Is Left to Say" |
| **IV6sus♯4** | "the only sus♯4 chord diatonic to a major key" | Thundercat, "What Is Left to Say" |
| **Gm/F♯** | contains a B♭+; he weighs and rejects F♯+(♭9), B♭+(13)/F♯, Gm(maj7) inverted, D+7(11) | James Blake, "Unluck" |
| **A7♭5** | usually really A7(♯11) no 5 — here he argues both readings are live at once | "Fly Me to the Moon" (Charles Cornell) |
| **Dadd4/C** | Csus♯4(9,13) — and **the melody sings the ♯4**, which is what makes it a sus♯4 rather than an inverted D7. *"Two identical chords can sound completely different depending on context"* | Billy Joel, "Lullabye" |
| **Cm(♭8)** | normally written Cm(maj7); he hears C→C♭ vs C→B as genuinely undecidable in 12-TET | Knower, "Same Smile Different Face" |
| **E♭7♯9** (Hendrix) | his reading: **E♭(♯6,♯9)**, the ♭V augmented sixth of A major | Mac Miller, "God Is Fair, Sexy Nasty" |
| **A13(♯11)** | play it as **Gmaj7♭5/A**. True spelling in A♭: a B𝄫 triad + augmented sixth = the ♭II augmented sixth, i.e. the tritone sub | Michael Jackson, "I Can't Help It" |
| **six maj7♭5 shapes down the whole-tone scale over an E pedal** | only one of the six actually contains a B; the constant structure creates "the illusion that these chords are simply moving down by whole step" | Stevie Wonder, "Too High" |
| **Cm(maj7)(10)** | a *minor* chord with an added *major* third — the mirror of 7♯9 | Matt Maltese, "As the World Caves In" |
| **Dm7(9,11,13) → G7(♭9,11,13) → Cmaj7(9,♯11,13)+♯8** | his own cadence, 7+7+8 notes, given four ways at once: chord names, note lists, polychords, and scales | Nick Clark |
