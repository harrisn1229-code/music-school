# This ONE Question Will Improve EVERY Song You Make (World's Best Producers All Ask This)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QZf8mzMD0Oo)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QZf8mzMD0Oo)

## Synopsis

A compilation of Skrillex, Morgan Page, Ian Kirkpatrick, Zedd, and Jay Eskar independently converging on the same question: what are the 2-3 elements of this song that cannot be deleted? Each gives a different concrete method for finding and protecting that core — a mute test, a piano test, a brutal post-production reduction pass, a memory-of-when-it-was-simple check, and a guiding theme/concept. The throughline across all five: production's job is to support a strong musical core, never to manufacture one that isn't there.

## Techniques

- **Skrillex's mute test: identify the 1-2 elements that, alone, still ARE the song — everything else is decoration on top.** His process: strip everything back until muting all but a couple of core sounds still leaves something recognizable as "the song." Once that core is identified, growls/whips/tricks/whatever else can be layered around it freely, because the identity doesn't depend on them. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **Morgan Page's "piano test": play the core musical idea on a real piano, sung, with no production at all — if it's not compelling there, no sound design will save it.** Production supports a musical core, it doesn't create one; a chord progression/melody that only works with fancy plugins and presets probably isn't the real idea yet. Worth doing specifically because it strips away every crutch simultaneously. → [[Melody Writing]]
- **Ian Kirkpatrick's "make singable phrases": the core might be a group of sounds, not just one or two — but the bar is the same, something a listener could hum.** The listener never perceives layer count or production complexity, only the resulting outline; a track can carry a huge number of layers and still read as one simple, memorable phrase if the arrangement resolves down to something singable.
- **Zedd's brutal post-production reduction pass: after the song is fully produced, go back and ruthlessly cut anything that doesn't add real musical information.** Explicit goal on the referenced track: make the verse as catchy as the chorus, not just build toward a drop — anything that isn't earning its place gets muted, filtered, or removed entirely, even after significant work went into it. Framed as one of the hardest skills: making something simple that's genuinely good, "something the Beatles would do that sounds easy but really isn't." → [[Committing and Printing]]
- **Ian Kirkpatrick's restraint principle: "don't destroy something beautiful" — half the job after finding a good idea is not overworking it.** Ego-driven over-production, not under-production, is the actual risk once a strong idea exists — "your only job is to serve the song." Concrete story: on "Bad Liar" (Selena Gomez), the A&R kept telling him to remove things because the song was already strong without them. **Personal check against over-complicating: recall the excitement felt when the song was just vocal + piano**, as a reference point for whether a new addition is actually helping — "you're not building a tower, you're making sure not to knock down the tower you've already built." → [[Committing and Printing]]
- **Jay Eskar's "pick a song theme" as a creative constraint that keeps elements cohering around one idea instead of floating independently.** A concrete narrative/conceptual anchor (his worked example: "Atlantis" as a symbol of a perfect, spiritually/scientifically advanced civilization) shapes every emotional and environmental choice in the track, giving "positive creative constraints" rather than an open-ended blank page. His definition of depth in music: conveying emotions that go beyond what plain language can say — if a listener feels something words alone couldn't communicate, that's the depth working. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **Refine "what's the core" again once the mix is more polished, not only at the very start.** A more finished mix makes it clearer which elements are actually load-bearing versus which were never necessary — Jay Eskar's own worked example: a French-house track whose irreducible core turned out to be exactly three things — the drum pattern (kick/snare/hat groove), the chords (progression + sound design), and the bass (counter-melody) — everything else in the arrangement gets built on top of that three-part foundation, none of it optional.
- **Summary framing across all five producers: "keep the signal, eliminate the noise."** The recurring diagnostic question for any song in progress: what are the 2-3 elements that cannot be deleted?
