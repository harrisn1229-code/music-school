# 9 Songs That 'Rip Off' Other Songs

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gMbm-mHNIe4)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gMbm-mHNIe4)

## Synopsis

Continues the series' catalog of melodic/lyrical/sample borrowing cases with wildly inconsistent legal outcomes — one of the richer entries in the series, including two lawsuit-free single-line borrows, a knowingly-vs-unknowingly-plagiarized double case (Rod Stewart), an "arrangement vs. melody" copyright argument, and the full Bittersweet Symphony story through to its 2019 resolution.

## Techniques

- **A single opening line lifted, credited two different ways**: TLC's "Waterfalls" (1995) opens by borrowing a line from Paul McCartney's "Waterfalls" (1980) — McCartney has publicly acknowledged the borrow but never pursued legal action, and TLC's writers aren't credited to him. Michael McDonald's "Keep Forgetting" similarly opens with the exact line from Chuck Jackson's 1962 "Keep Forgetting" (written by Jerry Leiber/Mike Stoller) — same shape of borrow, but here Leiber and Stoller DID end up credited as songwriters. Same mechanism, opposite outcomes — a clean illustration that whether a borrow gets formally credited often comes down to whether anyone pursues it, not the size of the lift.
- **A hook similarity that settled out of court with no writer credit added**: Avril Lavigne/Dr. Luke's "Girlfriend" chorus vs. The Rubinoos' "I Want to Be Your Boyfriend" (1979) — sued, settled undisclosed, but the Rubinoos writers were never added as credited songwriters on "Girlfriend," unlike the McDonald/Jackson case above.
- **Knowing vs. unknowing plagiarism in the SAME song, with different consequences for each**: Rod Stewart's "Do Ya Think I'm Sexy?" (1978) lifts from two different sources. The main vocal melody closely follows George Benjor's "Taj Mahal" — Stewart admitted the melody had "lodged in my memory" from hearing it at Rio Carnival and "unknowingly" resurfaced; the case settled in Benjor's favor and Stewart handed over royalties. Separately, the song's string line is knowingly lifted from Bobby Womack's "(If You Want My Love) Put Something Down on It" — Stewart's own defense was that you can lift a line from an *arrangement* rather than a vocal melody without infringing; the video pushes back on that claim (an instrumental line can still count as melody for copyright purposes, and *Marvin Gaye v. Blurred Lines* later succeeded on arrangement/vibe similarity, not melody) — but no legal action was ever brought over the Womack lift specifically.
- **A falsetto hook borrowed over a shared chord progression, uncredited**: Elton John's "Crocodile Rock" (1972) vs. Speedy Gonzalez (1961) — sued by songwriter Buddy Kaye, apparently settled, but Speedy Gonzalez's writers were never credited on "Crocodile Rock."
- **A deep lift across melody AND lyrics, not just a hook** — the contrast case for "how much borrowing is too much": Johnny Cash's "Folsom Prison Blues" (1955, credited as an original Cash composition) is extensively based on Gordon Jenkins' "Crescent City Blues" (1953) — near-identical melody plus lyrics that closely paraphrase the original, well beyond shared 12-bar-blues convention. Jenkins sued in the 1970s and Cash paid a ~$75,000 settlement.
- **A publisher pursuing a claim the original songwriter didn't want pursued**: The Doors' "Hello, I Love You" (1968) vs. The Kinks' "All Day and All of the Night" — Kinks songwriter Ray Davies was personally unwilling to sue, but his publisher wanted to and apparently reached some undisclosed deal regardless. Framed as a general pattern: publishers, being in the business of money rather than music, will pursue infringement claims even against the original writer's wishes.
- **The full Bittersweet Symphony case, including its 2019 resolution**: The Verve's "Bittersweet Symphony" (1997) sampled not the original Rolling Stones recording of "The Last Time" but a specific orchestral cover by the Andrew Oldham Orchestra. The Verve got clearance from Decca Records but not from Allen Klein, the Stones' former manager who held the song's copyright — Klein sued and took 100% of the song's royalties plus full writing credit for Jagger/Richards, even though the sampled string arrangement was actually composed by David Whitaker (who received nothing). In 2019, Jagger and Richards voluntarily returned the royalties and songwriting credit to Richard Ashcroft.
- **A shared generic chord progression producing near-identical melodic hooks across FOUR unrelated songs, none of which is really "the original"**: Coldplay's "Viva la Vida" (2008) was separately sued (or accused) by Joe Satriani ("If I Could Fly," 2004 — first two bars near-identical, both over the same I-V-vi-IV progression), Cat Stevens/Yusuf Islam (his "Foreigner Suite"), and Creaky Boards ("The Songs I Didn't Write"). Satriani's lawsuit was dismissed. The video's own conclusion: because all four songs are built on the same very common major-key progression, and melodies are written to fit their chords, independent convergence on similar melodic shapes is the more likely explanation than any one song copying another — and if it really were about authorship, the accusers should logically have been suing each other's prior claims too, which none of them did. High-profile success ("Viva la Vida" was a #1 international hit) is the actual common factor across all four accusations, per Coldplay drummer Will Champion's own observation.
