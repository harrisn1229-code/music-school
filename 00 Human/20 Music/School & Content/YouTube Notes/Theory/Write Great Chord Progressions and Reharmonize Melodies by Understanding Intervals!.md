# Write Great Chord Progressions and Reharmonize Melodies by Understanding Intervals!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlOf2Dqq_e4)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlOf2Dqq_e4)

## Synopsis

Jack Ruch's core lesson (guitar, but the content is instrument-agnostic theory) is a single reframe of what an interval "is": rather than memorizing intervals as isolated flashcard facts, he walks through **every one of the 12 notes relative to a fixed root**, showing exactly what chord family each interval choice unlocks. He then runs the same exercise **in reverse** — holding a note constant and moving the bass/root underneath it through all 12 notes — to show that a single melody note can be reharmonized as a chord tone or extension of 12 different chord roots. The back half turns this into practice: a chord progression built entirely around chords that all contain the same note C, played against a simple melody to demonstrate how the *identical* melodic material takes on totally different character depending on the harmony underneath, and then a worked reharmonization of a plain two-chord (C–Dm) melody into several richer substitute progressions. It's a compact, well-organized bridge between "name that interval" ear-training and actually using interval relationships as a compositional/reharmonization tool — strong pairing with the vault's existing [[Reharmonization]] technique catalog (which covers the *techniques* — this video supplies the *mechanism*, i.e. why a melody note tolerates so many different chords under it) and [[Extended Chords]] (9/11/13 as octave-displaced 2/4/6).

## What's taught

**Interval-number theory (basics, ~0:50–2:00)**
- An interval = pitch difference between two notes; its **number** comes from counting scale degrees on the major scale of the bottom note (C→E = 3 notes counted = "third"), its **quality** (major/minor) is layered on top of the number.
- Upper extensions (9, 11, 13) are literally the **2, 4, 6** an octave higher — same note, different name, used when there's already a root/3rd/5th/7th below it to stack onto. Concrete voicing moves: replacing the 3rd of a chord with the 2 gives a sus2/add2 (C2/Csus2) rather than a 9-chord; replacing the 3rd with the 4 gives a sus4; keeping the 3rd and adding the 4/11 on top of an already-complete chord gives an 11-chord (dominant 11, minor 11).

**Every interval against a fixed root, and the chord family it opens up (~2:00–8:30)** — root held at C, moving chromatically down from the octave through all 12 intervals:
- **Major 7th (B)** → major 7th chords, major 9 chords.
- **Flat 7 (B♭)** → dominant chords, minor 7 chords.
- **Major 6 (A)** → major 6 chords; also present in a diminished 7th chord.
- **Flat 6 / sharp 5 (A♭)** → augmented chords, altered-dominant voicings (♭13, ♭6). Names a specific voicing he learned from **Joe Pass**: a rootless altered-dominant shape built ♭7 (bottom) – 3rd – ♭13 – ♯9 (top) — i.e. a shell (♭7, 3) with a ♭13 and a ♯9 stacked on, deliberately omitting the root and 5th.
- **Perfect 5th (G)** → basic major/minor triads.
- **Flat 5 (G♭)** → diminished chords, minor 7♭5 (half-diminished).
- **Perfect 4th (F)** → sus4 chords, and (with the 3rd retained) dominant/minor 11 chords.
- **Major 3rd (E)** → major triads, major 7, major 6 chords.
- **Minor 3rd (E♭)** → minor triads, minor 7 chords.
- **9th / 2nd (D)** → sus2 chords, 9th chords, minor 9 voicings.
- **Flat 9 / flat 2nd (D♭)** → altered-dominant ♭9 chords — explicitly dissonant standing alone but works well as a chord **a half step above the target**, resolving down (e.g. a C7♭9 built a half step above an F minor target, functioning as an altered V).

**The flip side — fixed upper note, moving root/bass through all 12 (~8:30–12:30)** — held note is C, bass moves chromatically up, showing what role that C plays in the resulting chord: root of C major; major 7th of D♭maj7; ♭7 of D7/Dm7; major 6th of E♭maj6; ♯5/♭13 of an E augmented/altered chord; 5th of F major/minor; ♭5 of G♭m7♭5; sus4/11 of a G chord; major 3rd of A♭major; minor 3rd of A minor; 9/2 of B♭sus2 or B♭9; ♭9/♭2 of a B altered-dominant chord. **The point stated explicitly:** the same melody note is a legal, usable tone of 12 different chord qualities depending purely on which root sits under it — this is the mechanical justification behind reharmonization's "the melody note must remain a usable chord tone" rule already logged on [[Reharmonization]].

**Worked demonstration chord progression, all built around the shared note C (~12:30–17:00):** Dm7 → D♭maj7 → Cmaj → D7 → Fmaj7 → E(altered, ♭13) → Am7 → B♭11(dominant), played against a simple melody that keeps circling C — demonstrating live how identical melodic material reads completely differently as the harmony underneath cycles through all these different roles for the same note (root → major7 → root → ♭7 → 5th → ♭13 → minor3rd → 9th).

**Worked reharmonization exercise (~17:00–21:30):** starts from a bare two-chord vamp (C major → D minor → D minor → C major) under a simple melody, then reharmonizes it multiple ways by reinterpreting what role each melody note plays:
- The melody's D note (originally the root of Dm) reinterpreted as the **9th of C9** (or an implied C11) instead of moving to a new chord at all — stays on C, richer color.
- Same D reinterpreted as the **13th of an F dominant 13 chord**.
- The following note reinterpreted as the **minor 3rd of A minor** instead of resolving back to the C root.
- Further substitution options demoed: **B♭6** (melody note as the 3rd), **A sus** (melody note as the 4th), **A♭ major** (melody note as the major 3rd) — same melody note, six different destination chords, directly illustrating the "6 different meanings for one melody note" table already on [[Reharmonization]] §1.

**Practice prescription stated at the close:** take one note, build every chord type it can be a root/3rd/5th/7th/9th/11th/13th of; take a note and cycle all 12 possible bass notes under it and listen to how the color changes; then apply it — write original progressions or reharmonize a simple existing melody using this framework rather than by ear alone.
