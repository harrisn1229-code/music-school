# Suspended Chords

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Chords]]. "Sus" = suspension: the 3rd is **replaced** by the 2nd (sus2) or 4th (sus4). No 3rd = neither major nor minor — open, unresolved sound.

## Types

| Chord | Formula | C example |
|---|---|---|
| Csus2 | 1 2 5 | C D G |
| Csus4 (or just Csus) | 1 4 5 | C F G |
| C7sus2 | 1 2 5 ♭7 | C D G B♭ |
| C7sus4 | 1 4 5 ♭7 | C F G B♭ |
| C9sus4 | 1 4 5 ♭7 9 | C F G B♭ D |
| Csus2/4 (rare) | 1 2 4 5 | C D F G |

- C9sus4 ≈ C11 without the 3rd — see [[Extended Chords]]
- In practice only two get used much: **sus4 on major triads** and **7sus4 on dominants** ([[Seventh Chords]])
- Old chart notation: C4 = Csus4

## How sus4 chords get used (three patterns)
1. **Suspension → resolution**: sus4 chord then its normal version — Csus4 → C (F falls to E), G7sus4 → G7. Usually on the V or I chord. Classic "special effect" — e.g. the Gsus4 → G in Gnarls Barkley's *Crazy* ([[Chord Progressions]])
2. **V chord under a tonic melody**: melody sits on the tonic note while the V plays → tonic clashes with the V's 3rd. Vsus4 swaps that 3rd for the tonic note itself. (Key of C: melody on C over G → Gsus4 instead.)
3. **Keep the suspension AND add the resolution, for tension that never fully releases** — instead of dropping the suspended 4th when the chord "resolves," hold it while adding the resolved 3rd underneath/alongside it (e.g. a sus4 chord's D held together with the C♯ it would normally resolve to). Reads as resolving while a residue of the original tension stays audible on top — a way to color a resolution instead of fully releasing it. → You Suck at Producing, [You Suck at Music Theory No. 2: Creating Tension](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9eTRtWcuzWc)

## From videos

- **Sus4 resolves, sus2 usually doesn't — a real functional contrast, plus a concrete bass-note trick that flips one into the other.** Sus4 on V, dragging out a V-I cadence: "For No One" (Beatles), "Crazy" (Gnarls Barkley). Sus4 on I: "Pinball Wizard" (The Who), "Crazy Little Thing Called Love" (Queen), "Cold as Ice" (Foreigner). **Sus2's weaker pull means it often just sits unresolved as an open color chord instead**: "Love Song" (Sara Bareilles, never resolves), "Don't Dream It's Over" (Crowded House, opens on sus2 before establishing major/minor — deliberately ambiguous), "Champagne Supernova" (Oasis, the ENTIRE progression is one Asus2 with only the bass note changing underneath). **A literal sus4↔sus2 reinterpretation via bass note**: "Free Fallin'" (Tom Petty) — an Fsus4 (F-C-B♭) gets a B♭ bass note added in verse 2, and the identical three notes now read as B♭sus2 instead, since the ear reassigns B♭ as tonic — concrete proof a sus chord's identity depends on bass context, not just its notes. **Quartal harmony** (chords stacked in 4ths, which the standard naming system frequently mislabels as sus4): "Tarkus" (ELP), "Maiden Voyage" (Herbie Hancock) → [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use Sus4 and Sus2 chords]]
- **Dominant 7sus4's biggest soloing trap: because the chord has no 3rd at all, the ear reflexively wants to fill in a minor 3rd — which reads as minor pentatonic, NOT the chord's actual (deliberately ambiguous, no-3rd) sound.** Stated directly against a C7sus4 arpeggio: adding the "obvious"-sounding minor 3rd starts to imply a minor 11 color, which can work once you're deliberately choosing it but is a mistake if the underlying harmony is a plain sus4 — "that minor third is not really part of the harmony." Recommended step-5 add-note scale for coloring the arpeggio: **Mixolydian** (the chord's own ♭7 makes it the natural fit), not the plain major scale. Full arpeggio shapes/fingerings → [[DOMINANT 7 Sus4 Arpeggio Guitar Shapes for Soloing with Chordal Tones]]. → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]], [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]] — Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared, [[DOMINANT 7 Sus4 Arpeggio Guitar Shapes for Soloing with Chordal Tones]]
- **A sus4/sus2 open-chord voicing can BE the "color note" version of a plain triad, not a separate chord to consciously choose** — when a recording's full arrangement (layered guitars, bass, synth) actually contains an extended/suspended tone, substituting the sus voicing for the plain open triad on guitar reproduces the recorded sound more accurately than the "textbook" plain chord most beginner tutorials teach. Worked examples: a **Dsus4** and **Asus4** in place of plain D/A in "Free Fallin'" (the color note is literally present in the underlying G chord when a bassist plays under it), and an **Em7 → G → Dsus4 → A(sus4♭7)** progression in "Wonderwall" reached by anchoring two fretting fingers on strings 1–2 throughout — both the plain and sus versions are "correct," they just carry different amounts of color/brightness. → [[10 Iconic & Easy Guitar Songs for Beginners]] — Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared, [[10 Iconic & Easy Guitar Songs for Beginners]]
- **Csus2 (1-2-5) vs. Cadd9 (1-3-5-9) is the clean test case for the extension-naming rule: same added note (D over C), different label purely on whether the 3rd is present.** With the 3rd present, the D reads as the **9** (add9, a true extension); with the 3rd replaced by the D instead, it's the **2** (sus2) — the 3rd's presence/absence is the whole distinction, not the note's octave placement. General rule this illustrates: a chord tone above 7 only counts as an extension (9/11/13) when it's stacked on a *complete* triad or seventh chord underneath; pull the corresponding lower tone and the "extension" reverts to its plain scale-degree name. Full rule (incl. the ♯11-vs-♯4 companion case) → [[Extended Chords]]. → [[Extended Chords]], [[Add Chords]], [[Intervals]] — Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared, [[Guitar Theory! - Demystifying Compound Intervals vs Chord Extensions]]
- **A sus4 chord used as a delayed-resolution device: holding a suspended dominant so the reharmonized tonic note stays audibly "unresolved" for a second beat before the drop to the real tonic.** Worked as a two-chord delaying progression over a I chord: **A♭maj7 → G7sus4 → C** — the sus4 deliberately kept unresolved (not walked down to the plain 3rd) so the harmonized tonic note stays held through the dominant, adding a second layer of delay on top of the A♭maj7 substitution before the actual resolution lands. - **Four sus-chord substitutes pulled from one long V-chord reharmonization catalog on a major ii–V–I in C (Dm7–G7–Cmaj7), spanning the full distance from "barely a substitution" to "far outside the key."** **G7sus4** — same notes as Dm7/G or Fmaj7/G; missing the 3rd makes it a deliberately weak/non-committal reading of the dominant, flagged as a color the presenter says he personally underuses. **D♭7sus4** — the tritone sub (D♭7) played as a sus chord instead of a straight dominant; described as going "even further away" than the plain tritone sub because it drops the note (G, within D♭ Lydian dominant) that still ties the substitute back to the home key. **B♭7sus4♭9** — reached via the "reharmonize the substitute again" meta-trick (see [[Chord Substitution]]): pulled out of G7 altered's own scale (A♭ melodic minor) as a further replacement for literal G7, described as a strongly melodic-minor-flavored voicing. **G7sus4♭9 ("Phrygian" dominant)** — from harmonic minor, scale = G Phrygian (same notes as E♭ major / C natural minor); called safe in most melodic contexts (only clashes if the melody itself carries a 9th or 3rd over the dominant) and specifically recommended as **a pedal point for intros** for its "mysterious" quality; a variant with a natural 13 comes from F melodic minor instead. - **Voice the tonic chord OVER the IV bass note as a "four sus" substitute for a plain IV major** (functionally a IVm7sus/i7sus-type slash voicing) — tried as an alternate cadence chord in a synthwave progression in place of a straight IV major; described as reading distinctly "synth-wavy," a usable substitution to file even when not committed to for a given track. → [[Synthwave]] — Max Konyi, [Synthwave in Studio One with Stock Instruments | Weekly Challenge #9](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IA61_2JAd84)
- **A maj7sus2 voicing (1-5-7-9, no 3rd at all) reads as a genuinely pretty, open suspended color — not just an add9.** Note the distinction: this is a true suspension (the 3rd is fully replaced by the 2nd/9th, not supplemented alongside it), which is why it sounds airier/less jazzy-dense than a maj9 or add9 voicing that keeps the 3rd. Worked as the closing chord (F, the IV) of a I–V–vi–IV "beautification" progression, guitar fret numbers on the A string: 8-10-9-8. → [[Extended Chords]], [[Chord Voicings]] — Max Konyi, [[Beautifying Simple Chords]]
- **Quartal chords are sus4's further-out cousin** — a sus4 is already built from a 4th interval instead of a 3rd; stack *another* 4th on top and you get a quartal chord. Rare in pop, deliberately rootless — described as a "seasick" quality since it doesn't resolve toward major or minor. → [[Extended Chords]], [[Quartal Chord Voicings]] (fretboard shapes, the *So What* voicing, and a reharmonization trick) — Synthet, [the sus chord](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xW1LljOK_A0)
