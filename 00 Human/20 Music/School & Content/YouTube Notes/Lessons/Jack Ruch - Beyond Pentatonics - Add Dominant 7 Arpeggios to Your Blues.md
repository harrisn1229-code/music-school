# Jack Ruch — Beyond Pentatonics: Add Dominant 7 Arpeggios to Your Blues

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=13tUHaV31yc)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=13tUHaV31yc)

## Synopsis

Jack Ruch (blues/jazz guitar teacher, previously sourced in this vault mostly for lesson content — see [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]) demonstrates his personal approach to layering dominant-7 arpeggios onto a standard pentatonic-scale blues solo. The pitch: pentatonic scales cover a huge amount of blues/rock playing on their own, but once a player starts thinking in arpeggios — the chord tones (root, 3rd, 5th, ♭7) of the chord actually sounding at that moment — even a simple three-chord 12-bar blues opens up, because a couple of key notes (most notably the major 3rd of the V chord) simply aren't available anywhere in the pentatonic scales. He demonstrates over a 12-bar blues shuffle in G: first a solo built entirely from G major/minor pentatonic, then a second solo where he sprinkles in C7 (IV) and D7 (V) arpeggio tone targeting on the chord changes, then breaks the system down across all five CAGED-anchored positions on the neck. The most valuable, reusable material is the practice progression itself (map all four shapes — G major pentatonic, G minor pentatonic, C7 arpeggio, D7 arpeggio — at each of the five positions, solo confined to one position at a time, then connect positions horizontally) and the specific, named target note (D7's major 3rd, F♯) that neither pentatonic scale contains and that's called out as the strongest single note for "driving home the sound of the chord."

## What's taught

- **Core method: stay in pentatonic, drop into the chord's own dominant-7 arpeggio the instant IV or V hits.** G major pentatonic and G minor pentatonic (freely mixed) are the default vocabulary for the whole 12-bar blues in G; C7 (root-3-5-♭7) and D7 (root-3-5-♭7) arpeggios are reached for specifically on the bars where those chords sound, then abandoned again once the chord passes. The I chord (G) is never given its own arpeggio — only IV and V get one, because the pentatonics already outline the I chord well.
- **The key insight on why it works:** most of the arpeggio's notes are already close by/shared with the nearby pentatonic shape, so only a few notes actually need conscious targeting. The clearest example given: **the major 3rd of D7 is not in either G major pentatonic or G minor pentatonic** — landing on that note is called out as "really strong for driving home the sound of that chord." (0:00–3:00 range, paraphrased from captions — exact timestamps not carried in the transcript.)
- **Shared-note pivoting:** demonstrated starting a G minor pentatonic phrase and ending it on a note (G) that's *also* in the C7 arpeggio, using that shared note as the seam to weave from a pentatonic lick into arpeggio-outlined material over the C7 bar, then dropping back to G minor/major pentonic when the chord resolves.
- **Five CAGED positions, same four shapes at each.** The whole system (G major pentatonic, G minor pentatonic, C7 arpeggio, D7 arpeggio) is mapped and demonstrated at all five CAGED-anchored positions up the neck, including the position anchored around the well-known "BB King box" for major pentatonic. Only **five dominant-7 arpeggio shapes total** are needed (one per CAGED root) — reused for both C7 and D7 by moving the same shape to a different root, not five separate shapes per chord.
- **Practice progression, stated explicitly:** (1) at one position, limit yourself to just that position's four shapes and build solos confined there; (2) once a position is comfortable, move to the next position and repeat; (3) once all five are individually solid, start connecting them horizontally across the neck mid-solo, using the chord tones as landmarks so "you're never going to get lost wherever you are."
- **Turnaround note:** the classic V-to-IV turnaround lick (bar 10→11 of a 12-bar blues) is pointed out as something players often already play as an arpeggio-shape-based move without labeling it as such — the same concept applies to any similar short chord-to-chord connector.
- **Practical advice for learners:** slow the video down and work one position at a time if the full five-position pass is too much to absorb in one sitting — explicitly flagged as taking real time to get a grasp on for a player new to arpeggio-over-pentatonic playing.
