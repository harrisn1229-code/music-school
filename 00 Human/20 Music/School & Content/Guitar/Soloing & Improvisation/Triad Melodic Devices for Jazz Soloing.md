# Triad Melodic Devices for Jazz Soloing

Part of [[Jazz Harmony]] and [[Bebop Vocabulary]]. A fourth distinct triad-soloing angle alongside the vault's other three: [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]] relocates **one** triad shape across three neck zones; [[Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords]] extracts a triad from **inside** a single seventh chord; [[Harmonized Scale Triads for Soloing]] harmonizes a **whole parent scale** into seven triads used in sequence up the neck. This page instead treats triads as **melodic raw material to be varied** — superimposing diatonic triads onto changes, reordering a triad's notes into different **inversions**, reshuffling the note order into **patterns** (3-1-5 instead of 1-3-5), and stacking a triad into a wide **spread/open voicing** — all worked over jazz ii-V-I and turnaround changes rather than a single vamp.

## 1. Superimposing diatonic triads onto chords
The base move: instead of running the full 4-note chord/scale, pick out just a **3-note diatonic triad that sits inside it** and use that as the melodic core of the phrase. Worked example over a **Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7** ii-V-I:
- **Dm7 (ii):** enter with a chromatic enclosure targeting **F** on beat 3 (see [[Enclosures Through the Scale]] / [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] for the enclosure device itself), then play an **F major triad** — the top three notes of Dm7 (D-F-A-C → F-A-C), i.e. the **3rd-5th-7th** of the chord as an open-structure triad.
- **G7 (V):** a **G major triad** (root position, the chord's own 1-3-5), followed by a short **D minor pentatonic** fragment.
- **Cmaj7 (I):** three different diatonic triads back to back, each one a clean chord-tone read — **E minor** (3rd-5th-7th of Cmaj7), **A minor** (the 6th/13th, root, and 3rd — a borrowed-feeling extension that still resolves cleanly), then **G major** (5th, 7th, and 9th).

**General rule extracted:** any diatonic triad built from a chord's extensions (3rd, 5th, 7th, 9th, 13th) reads as a coherent chord-tone unit as long as you can name which scale degrees it covers — work this out per chord before soloing, not on the fly.

**Practice methods (two, use both):**
1. **Straight through the scale** — take a major scale (worked in C major) and play every diatonic triad in root position, one per scale degree, ascending.
2. **Along the neck, by string set** — instead of scale-degree order, take one string set (e.g. the middle string set) and find the diatonic triad that sits there physically first, then build outward from that shape rather than from scale-degree order. Requires memorizing where each of the seven diatonic triads (C major, D minor, E minor, F major, G major, A minor, B diminished) sits relative to a given root, and relating each one back to the chord it might be superimposed over (e.g. "if I play F major over D minor, I get the 3rd-5th-7th; if I play B diminished over D minor, I get the 13th-root-3rd").

## 2. Triad inversions as a melodic device
An inversion just moves the lowest note of a triad up an octave: C major root position **C-E-G** → first inversion **E-G-C** → second inversion **G-C-E**. The technique is deliberately practicing triads **starting from the 3rd or 5th, not the root** — melodically different from root-position triads even though the pitch content is identical, and it forces you to think of the triad's identity independent of where the root falls.

**First-inversion worked example — a full ii-V-I lick:**
- **Dm7:** D minor triad in **1st inversion**, starting on the 3rd — **F-A-D**.
- Moves to an **A minor triad**, **C-E-A** — against the D minor this reads as the **7th (C), 9th (E), 5th (A)**.
- **G7alt:** a **B augmented triad** first (augmented triads are symmetrical, so "inverting" one just produces another augmented triad — not a true inversion in the usual sense).
- Then a **D♭ major triad, 1st inversion** — **F-A♭-D♭** — reading as the **7th, ♭9th, ♭5th** of G7alt.
- **Resolving to Cmaj7:** landing on G, then a **C major triad, 1st inversion** (**E-G-C**), then down to an **A minor triad, 1st inversion** (**A-E-C**... starting on the 3rd C, i.e. C-E-A relocated).

*(general starting point, not stated as a rule in the source but implied: augmented and diminished triads behave differently under inversion than major/minor — augmented triads are symmetrical (every inversion is still an augmented triad), diminished triads are not — worth knowing before assuming "invert" always changes the sound the way it does for major/minor.)*

**The hard part, named directly in the source:** you have to start thinking of the triad **from the 3rd**, not the root, which fights the habit of always naming/finding a triad from its root. Drill it through a full scale of 1st-inversion triads to build that reflex — "okay, I'm playing a G major first-inversion triad, so I'm starting on B, then D, then G" — and it's explicitly fine to learn the *melodic shape by ear* first, without naming notes, then backfill the theory once the sound is internalized.

**Second-inversion worked example — using a borrowed dominant color:** a passing-chord lick built from **diatonic triads in 2nd inversion** (start on the 5th, up to the root, up to the 3rd): **D minor (2nd inv) → E minor (2nd inv) → F minor (2nd inv) → G major**, resolving with a short scale run to the **3rd of Cmaj7**. The **F minor** triad here is deliberately **borrowed from C harmonic minor** (not diatonic to C major) — sourced from treating the **D7** in this spot as if it were built on **C harmonic minor** rather than plain C major, which is why D minor → E minor → F minor forms a chromatically-connected passing run up to G7 before resolving to C. Named explicitly as **"a nice trick... using material from the minor key on the dominant is a really good idea if you want to get some variation into the lines."** Cross-links [[Borrowed Chords|Borrowed Chords]] and [[Altered Chords]] — the underlying move is the same modal-interchange logic, applied melodically (triad choice) rather than harmonically (chord choice).

## 3. Triad patterns — reordering the notes (3-1-5 instead of 1-3-5)
Beyond inversion (which note is lowest), the **order you play the notes in** is its own melodic variable. A triad played straight is **1-3-5**; the pattern demonstrated here is **3-1-5** — start on the 3rd, drop down to the root, then jump up to the 5th. This introduces a wide **5th interval as a melodic leap** mid-phrase that a straight 1-3-5 run doesn't have, described as breaking up the line "in a really nice way."

**Worked example over a Dm7-G7alt-Cmaj7:**
- **Dm7:** F major triad played **3-1-5** (A, down to F, up to C), then a **D sus4 triad** (not elaborated further in this lesson), then a small diatonic third (E to G).
- **G7alt:** an **F diminished triad**, also played **3-1-5**, then a **G diminished triad, 3-1-5**, up to E♭, down to B, resolving to the **9th of Cmaj7**.
- **Cmaj7:** an **A minor triad**, again **3-1-5**.

Practice the same way as the other triad devices — run the 3-1-5 pattern through every diatonic triad of a scale, ascending, to internalize the shape as a reflex rather than a one-off lick.

## 4. Spread (open-voiced) triads
**Construction:** take a close-position triad (C major: **C-E-G**) and move the **middle note up an octave** — C-E-G becomes **C-G-E(up an octave)**, spreading the three notes across a wider interval span. Works as both a **chord voicing** (see [[Quartal Chord Voicings]] and [[Chord Voicings]] for other wide-interval comping shapes) and a strong **arpeggio/melodic shape**, because the large leaps are still fully consonant (it's still just a triad) while sounding much more open than a close-position run.

**Worked example over Dm7-G7alt-Cmaj7:**
- **Dm7:** **D minor, 1st inversion, spread voicing** — F, then D, then up to A (the 5th) — followed by a **descending scale run**, deliberately resolving the wide-interval opening phrase by stepping back down in the **opposite direction** it opened in (a general shape-balancing principle: after a big ascending leap, resolve with stepwise descending motion).
- **G7alt:** an **E♭ major triad, 1st inversion, spread** on top of G7 — reads as the **♭13, root, and ♯9** (altered-dominant color tones) — E♭ down to G, back up to E♭, then B♭, followed by an **"A♭ minor Coltrane pattern"** (named directly, not elaborated — flag for further research if a Coltrane-pattern reference note gets built later).
- **Cmaj7:** a **G major triad, 1st inversion, open-voiced/spread** — down to B, up to G, then D, ending the line on the **3rd (E)**.

**Practical notes on playing spread triads:**
- **Harder to play than close-position triads** — but also a genuinely good **right-hand technique exercise** because of the wide string skips involved (skipping non-adjacent strings, repeatedly, moving up the neck).
- Drill them the same two ways as the other triad devices here: **in isolation through all inversions**, then **through a full scale**, ascending.
- The **string-skipping itself** is worth isolating as its own drill focus, separate from the harmonic/melodic content — "you're skipping different strings all the way up," which is a right-hand coordination problem independent of what triad you're playing.

## 7. Triad + fixed enclosure on every note (the Charlie Parker device)
A different generative move from §1–4: instead of varying inversion/order/spread, wrap **every note of a plain diatonic triad** in the same fixed enclosure shape — **one diatonic scale step above** the target, then **one chromatic half step below**, landing on the target. This is a real Charlie Parker line, transcribed off a **C major triad over Cmaj7**, and it's exactly the vault's "diatonic above, chromatic below" enclosure row (see [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §2's table — D–B–C off a target of C) applied systematically to all three notes of a triad rather than to one isolated chord tone. **Triad-agnostic:** the same wrap-every-note treatment was demonstrated off the **G triad** (V, the 5th chord in C) and the **A minor triad** (vi, the 6th chord in C), both confirmed to work — pick any diatonic triad in the key and the device holds.

**Avoid-note filter for choosing which diatonic triad.** When running this device as a full scalar pass over the tonic (Cmaj7), the source restricts the triad choice to the four diatonic triads that **don't contain the note F** — **C major, E minor, A minor, G major** — leaving out D minor and B diminished, both of which carry F. F is Cmaj7's own **avoid note** (the 4th, a half step above the major 3rd), so this is triad selection built directly on avoid-tone theory: work out which of the seven diatonic triads carry the tonic's avoid tone, exclude them, and apply the enclosure-wrap device freely inside what's left. *(general starting point: the same check generalizes to any diatonic major-7 tonic — find its avoid tone, then find which diatonic triads contain it, before soloing.)*

## 8. Harmonized passing-tone triads (targeting between two chord tones)
A melodic-connective device distinct from the other devices on this page: instead of picking one triad and decorating it, pick **two notes of the underlying chord's own arpeggio to connect** — worked example: **C (root) to E (3rd)** over Cmaj7. Walk the major scale stepwise between them (C-D-E). **Harmonize each scale tone passed through with its own diatonic triad in the key**, treating that passing tone as **the 5th of the harmonizing triad**. Apply **§7's enclosure pattern** (diatonic step above, chromatic half step below) to target each harmonized triad's top note, then **arpeggiate** the triad once landed, before moving to the next passing tone and repeating. The worked C→E line closes with an **E minor triad** tacked on the end (E minor is one of the tonic's non-avoid-note triads from §7). **Generalizes to any two-note stretch of the same arpeggio** — also demonstrated **3rd (E) to 5th (G)** and **5th (G) to 7th (B)**.

**Both §7 and §8 transfer across any diatonic chord in the key, by function** — because the material is drawn from the parent scale rather than tied to one specific chord voicing, lines built this way over the tonic also work over whichever other diatonic chord happens to be sounding.

**Transplanted onto the ii chord, via ii/V interchangeability.** Both devices, already drilled over the tonic, get reapplied to **D minor (ii)** — and because ii-chord vocabulary and its paired V chord are treated as functionally interchangeable targets ("you can play D Minor ideas over G7 and vice versa"), a D-minor-built line also plays straight over G7 unmodified. Chained into a full **Dm7 → G7 → Cmaj7** 251: the D-minor-derived material carries the ii-V, then the tonic-based §7/§8 material takes over once the progression resolves. → [[The ii-V-I]] for the cadence/function relationships this leans on. Once the note-choice devices are solid, the video's own next steps are to **add rhythmic variation** (these are taught as straight eighths specifically so rhythm can be layered on after) and to **freely mix with general bebop/jazz language** ([[Bebop Vocabulary]]) rather than treating the triad devices as a closed system.

## 5. Modal color triads over a static major7 (worked over Out of Nowhere's opening G major7)
Beyond the basic diatonic set, a **static** major7 chord (two bars or more, no motion) is an invitation to reach for progressively more "outside" triad families — worked over the tune's opening G major7:
- **Basic diatonic set (4 triads):** the chord itself (**G major**), the top-three-notes triad (**B minor**, 3-5-7), the extensions triad (**D major**, 5-7-9), and the 6th/13th triad (**E minor**, root-3-6). **Deliberately left out: A minor (ii), C major (IV), F♯ diminished (vii°)** — all three contain the plain natural 4th (C against G major7), the "weak"/avoid note against a major7 sound; with only three notes in a triad, one that contains a note you don't want is a lot less useful than one that doesn't.
- **Lydian (G major7♯11) reopens those three:** raising the 4th to C♯ removes the avoid-note problem — **C♯ diminished** (still the least useful, diminished triads read unstable), **A major** (genuinely strong — a clear "outside the key but still resolves" color), **F♯ minor** (also solid).
- **Lydian augmented (= the parent major scale's IV-flavor swapped for melodic minor rooted a major 3rd up — here, E melodic minor over G):** adds **B major** and **G augmented** as further colors, a clear step further out than plain Lydian.
- **Augmented scale (hexatonic, symmetric — two major triads a major 3rd apart):** for the "augmented major7" sound specifically, combine **G major + E♭ major** triads.
- **General escalation ladder (reusable on any long static major7 vamp, not just this tune):** basic diatonic → Lydian → Lydian augmented → augmented scale is, in that order, an increasingly "outside" set of triad colors for a static major7 — treat it as a menu to work through across multiple choruses rather than choosing one color and staying there. → [[Diatonic Modes]] · [[Scale Modes Reference]] · [[Augmented Chords]]

## 6. Cross-chord connective devices
Two reusable ways to link triad material *across* a chord change rather than treating each chord as an isolated puzzle, both demonstrated over Out of Nowhere's Gmaj7 → B♭m7-E♭7 move:
- **Half-step motif transposition:** a triad idea built on one chord (here, the **B minor** triad on Gmaj7) is directly reusable a **half step down** on the following chord (B♭m7-E♭7) — using the shape as a transposable motif, rather than inventing unrelated material per chord, builds continuity across a whole phrase or section. Landing on the target in a **less literal, non-parallel way** is usually the nicer-sounding version of the same idea than a straight parallel slide.
- **One-note scale-difference trick:** two adjacent chords' soloing scales sometimes differ by **exactly one note** — Gmaj7's own major scale and the G melodic minor used over the following C7 (a Lydian-dominant reharmonization) differ only in the 7th (**B → B♭**). Practical use: take whatever melodic idea is already being played on the first chord and swap that one note for the second chord's version, generating a connected line across the change almost for free (caveat: if the idea is triad-based, the swapped note still has to actually get used somewhere in the phrase, not just theoretically available).

## Practice sequence
1. Pick a key/progression (start with a static ii-V-I, e.g. Dm7-G7-Cmaj7 in C).
2. Work out which diatonic triads sit inside each chord's extensions (3-5-7, 5-7-9, 6/13-root-3, etc.) and drill superimposing them, root position, over the changes.
3. Drill the same diatonic triads **starting from the 3rd** (1st inversion) through a full scale, then **starting from the 5th** (2nd inversion) through a full scale — both by ear first, then confirm the theory.
4. Once inversions are solid, drill the **3-1-5 pattern** through the same scale of triads.
5. Build **spread/open voicings** from the close-position triads (move the middle note up an octave) and drill those through inversions, then through the scale — treat the wide string-skips as their own right-hand exercise.
6. Combine devices freely inside a real ii-V-I or turnaround lick — mix diatonic superimposition, inversion choice, pattern order, and spread voicing in the same phrase rather than treating them as separate isolated exercises once each is internalized.
7. Where a dominant chord allows it, try **borrowing a passing chord from the parallel harmonic minor** (e.g. an F minor triad on the way to G7 in a C-major context) for extra color/variation.
