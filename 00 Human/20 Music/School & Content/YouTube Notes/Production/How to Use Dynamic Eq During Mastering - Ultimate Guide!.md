# How to Use Dynamic Eq During Mastering | Ultimate Guide!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tvuBplNrYMs)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tvuBplNrYMs)

## Synopsis

A dedicated Sage Audio breakdown (same studio/voice as [[How to EQ Your Master]], [[EQ Masterclass]], [[How to Master a Song Start to Finish]], and the rest of this vault's Sage Audio mastering sourcing) that narrows all the way in on **dynamic EQ specifically**, organized around one clean split: **subtractive** dynamic EQ (early in the chain, corrective) vs. **additive** dynamic EQ (late, after compression and saturation, tonal/expansive). Four worked techniques on each side, each demoed live on a stereo mix (FabFilter Pro-Q 3 — Nate's owned [[Pro-Q 4]] is the direct successor). Subtractive: taming sibilance by matching a dynamic band to an analyzer's high-frequency spikes, alleviating low-mid muddiness with an isolate/solo-assisted band around 200 Hz, "augmenting" the vocal by dynamically ducking the side image at 2 kHz to reduce masking (a subtractive move used for an additive-sounding result), and taming hi-hats with a dynamic high shelf around 8 kHz. Additive: bumping the kick's fundamental with a dynamic expansion band, bumping vocal presence around 2 kHz on the mid/stereo channel, dynamically widening the stereo image with an expanding side shelf around 7 kHz+, and adding mid-range power/impact with a dynamic band on the mid frequencies (with a genuinely different effect depending on whether it's run on the mid image — more dynamic drums/vocal — or the side image — more dynamic guitar body/reverb tails). The clearest new idea not already carried elsewhere in this vault's dynamic-EQ documentation is the **sequencing rule**: subtractive dynamic EQ belongs at the *start* of a mastering chain, additive dynamic EQ belongs *after* compression and saturation, specifically so additive moves don't amplify the signal ahead of the compressor and provoke more aggressive gain reduction than intended. Everything else here is a set of concrete, reusable dynamic-EQ recipes (exact bands, ranges, and the reasoning behind each) rather than a wholesale new mastering-chain shape.

## Techniques

- **Subtractive dynamic EQ, four named uses, placed at the START of the mastering chain.** Framed use cases: control sibilance, alleviate low-frequency muddiness, augment the vocal (via masking reduction, not a boost), and tame hi-hats when they get too prominent. Placement rule: run subtractive dynamic EQ **early in the signal chain** — before the tonal/additive dynamic EQ work described below. → [[EQ]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [How to Use Dynamic Eq During Mastering](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tvuBplNrYMs)
- **Tame sibilance by matching a dynamic band's timing to an analyzer's spikes, not by ear-sweeping alone.** Use a spectrum analyzer to see exactly where the signal spikes in the high-frequency range, and match that spike's timing against when an S is actually sung or spoken — that confirms the spike is genuinely the sibilance, not something else living at the same frequency. Once confirmed, create a band centered on the most prominent part of the sibilance and set it **Dynamic** (automatic threshold, or set the threshold/range manually). The band then only attenuates when sibilance occurs — cymbals and other high-frequency instrumentation, which don't share the sibilant timing, stay relatively unaffected even though they overlap the same frequency range. → [[Pro-Q 4]], [[De-essing]], [[EQ]] — Sage Audio, [How to Use Dynamic Eq During Mastering](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tvuBplNrYMs)
- **Tame low-mid muddiness with a dynamic band around 200 Hz, using the Isolate/solo tool to find the exact culprit frequency by ear.** Create a band around **200 Hz**, set it Dynamic, and decide whether the threshold needs attenuating. To find precisely where inside that bell's bandwidth the muddiness actually lives, use the **isolate feature** (solo just that band's frequency content) and listen for the spot where the bass frequencies are most prominent — that's where the attenuation should center, not just wherever the band happens to sit by default. Caution: don't drag the band too far down the spectrum chasing the mud, or it starts attenuating content that isn't actually part of the muddiness problem. → [[Pro-Q 4]], [[Masking and Carving]], [[EQ]] — Sage Audio, [How to Use Dynamic Eq During Mastering](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tvuBplNrYMs)
- **"Augment" the vocal with a subtractive move: a dynamic bell on the SIDE image at 2 kHz reduces masking so the mono vocal reads louder without ever boosting it.** Create a bell filter on the **side image** centered at **2 kHz**, set it Dynamic, and adjust the range to **2 dB** — listen for whether it makes an audible difference. Mechanism: reducing the side image's content at the vocal's frequency reduces the amount of masking acting on that frequency, which in turn increases the *perceived* amplitude of the mono-centered vocal — an additive-sounding result achieved with a subtractive, side-only move. Because it's dynamic, it only triggers meaningfully while the vocal is actually singing. → [[Pro-Q 4]], [[Masking and Carving]], [[Width and Stereo]] — Sage Audio, [How to Use Dynamic Eq During Mastering](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tvuBplNrYMs)
- **Tame hi-hats with a dynamic high shelf around 8 kHz and the fastest attack/release the EQ allows.** Use a dynamic shelf filter starting around **8 kHz**; if the EQ offers it, pull attack and release down to roughly **2 ms** so the shelf snaps in and out fast enough to catch just the hat hits. *(Pro-Q 3, the plugin demoed, doesn't expose that fast an attack/release on a dynamic band — worth checking whether Pro-Q 4's newer dynamic-EQ behavior added it, since this note's owned plugin is the direct successor.)* Keep the range conservative — **no more than 2 dB** — because changes to the high-frequency range are easy to notice, so anything done here should stay subtle. Result: the high end keeps its overall detail while just the hat gets attenuated. → [[Pro-Q 4]], [[EQ]] — Sage Audio, [How to Use Dynamic Eq During Mastering](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tvuBplNrYMs)
- **Additive dynamic EQ belongs AFTER compression and saturation — the sequencing reason, not just a preference.** Running additive dynamic EQ before the compressor/saturator amplifies the signal ahead of those stages, which in turn provokes more aggressive compression than intended (a hotter signal trips a compressor's threshold harder and more often). Placing additive dynamic EQ after compression and saturation avoids that feedback loop. Secondary rule: keep changes **small** — additive dynamic EQ increases a master's dynamic range regardless of which specific technique below is used, so restraint matters more here than on the subtractive side. → [[EQ]], [[Master Chain]], [[Compression Staging]] — Sage Audio, [How to Use Dynamic Eq During Mastering](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tvuBplNrYMs)
- **Bump the kick with a dynamic expansion band centered exactly on its most prominent frequency.** Read the spectrum to find the kick's most prominent frequency, place a bell filter's center frequency directly over it, set the band Dynamic, and increase the **range above the filter by about 3 dB** (an expansion move, not attenuation) — lower the threshold manually or use the automatic threshold feature. → [[Pro-Q 4]], [[Kick Design]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [How to Use Dynamic Eq During Mastering](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tvuBplNrYMs)
- **Bump vocal presence with the same dynamic-expansion mechanism as the kick move above, but on the mid or stereo channel at 2 kHz.** Same technique as the kick bump directly above — bell filter, Dynamic, expansion — but centered around **2 kHz** and placed on the **mid or full-stereo channel** (not the side image, unlike the earlier masking-reduction vocal move). Triggers expansion at those frequencies whenever the vocal is actually sung, making the vocal louder and helping it cut through the mix in the moment it needs to. → [[Pro-Q 4]], [[Masking and Carving]] — Sage Audio, [How to Use Dynamic Eq During Mastering](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tvuBplNrYMs)
- **Dynamically widen the stereo image with an expanding shelf on the side image around 7 kHz and up.** Create a shelf filter on the **side image**, placed around **7 kHz and up**, using a range of about **2 dB** — lower the threshold or use an automatic setting until the band audibly triggers and expansion occurs. Because the band only fires when triggered, the widening isn't constant — it depends on the presence of high-frequency instrumentation in the mix at any given moment, so the width itself becomes a dynamic, musical effect rather than a flat, always-on setting. → [[Pro-Q 4]], [[Width and Stereo]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [How to Use Dynamic Eq During Mastering](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tvuBplNrYMs)
- **Add mid-range power/impact with a dynamic band on the mid frequencies — and a genuinely different result depending on mid vs. side placement.** The mid-frequency range holds most of a mix's instrumentation, so boosting it and making that boost dynamic adds impact to the master overall. The same move on the **mid image** mainly makes drums and vocal read more impressive and dynamic; the identical move on the **side image** instead makes guitar body and temporal processing (reverb) more dynamic. Framed as worth experimenting with on both images to find the right balance between the two, rather than picking one by default. → [[Pro-Q 4]], [[Width and Stereo]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [How to Use Dynamic Eq During Mastering](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tvuBplNrYMs)
