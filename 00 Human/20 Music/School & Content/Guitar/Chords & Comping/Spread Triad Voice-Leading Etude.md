# Spread Triad Voice-Leading Etude

Part of [[Chord Inversions]] and [[Song Practice]]. A composed practice **etude** for internalizing spread-triad ("open position") voicings across an entire diatonic key by voice-leading rule rather than by drilling shapes in isolation. Distinct from [[Open Voice Triads for Soloing]] — that page's Jens Larsen material uses the same physical spread/open-voice triad shapes as **soloing vocabulary** over jazz changes; this etude uses them as a **fretboard-mapping and voice-leading study**, walking the whole key rather than improvising over a progression. Also distinct from [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]] and [[Harmonized Scale Triads for Soloing]], both of which use **close-position** triads — this page's triads are spread/open voicings throughout (see below for the difference).

## Spread triad, defined
A **close-position** triad stacks its three notes on adjacent strings/scale-tones (e.g. D minor root position: root-♭3-5 = D-F-A). A **spread** (a.k.a. "open position voicing") triad takes that same three-note chord and moves the **middle note up an octave**, reordering the stack to **1-5-♭3** — same notes, same chord, wider interval spacing with a string skip in the middle, an "open," harp-like sound. Worked example: D minor close position frets pinky-5th-fret/A-string (root), middle-finger-3rd-fret/D-string (♭3), index-2nd-fret/G-string (5th); moving the middle note (the ♭3, on the D string) up an octave produces the spread voicing 1-5-♭3. **Terminology trap:** "open position" in this sense is music-theory/composition jargon for *spread voicing* — it has nothing to do with the guitar-specific meaning of "open position" (playing near the nut using open strings). Same term, two unrelated meanings; don't conflate them.

## The construction rule (this is the whole etude)
1. **Start on the tonic triad** (worked in C major) in a spread voicing, high up the neck.
2. **Chain through the diatonic triads a third at a time:** C → E minor → G major → B diminished → D minor → F major → A minor → back to C — the full seven diatonic triads of the key, taken in ascending-third order, repeating the cycle.
3. **Every chord change moves exactly one note of the three-note spread voicing**, by a half step or a whole step — never more than one voice moves, never more than a step. Which of the three voices (top/middle/bottom of the spread shape) moves next rotates through the voicing in a repeating sequence as the chain proceeds.
4. **The whole etude is one continuous descent down the fretboard**, chord by chord, using the single-voice-move rule to always land on the *closest* spread voicing of the next chord in the third-chain, until it reaches the lowest playable position on the neck.
5. **Close with a real cadence, not another triad:** the etude ends **G7 → C** — the only point where an actual dominant-7th chord (not a bare triad) appears, a deliberate "how to land a triad study" move rather than just fading out on another spread triad.
6. **Coverage is a side effect, not a separate drill:** because the third-chain touches every diatonic triad of the key, and the closest-voicing rule forces a different inversion each time a given triad quality recurs, the etude ends up covering **every diatonic triad of the key in every one of its inversions** — full spread-triad vocabulary for the key, generated by the voice-leading rule alone.

## Figured-bass / Roman-numeral reading (taught alongside the etude)
Classical figured-bass shorthand, used on the etude's own analysis: plain Roman numeral = **root position** (root in the bass); **"6/4"** = second inversion (5th in the bass — the "6" and "4" are the interval sizes up from the bass note once the chord is collapsed to close position: a 6th and a 4th); **"6"** (short for "6/3," the 3 dropped by convention) = **first inversion** (3rd in the bass). Applied to the etude's own third-chain: C = root position → E minor = 6/4 (5th in bass) → G = 6 (3rd in bass) → the pattern repeats every three chords (**root → 6/4 → 6, root → 6/4 → 6…**), cycling against the seven-chord third-chain — a 3-against-7 relationship, so the pairing of "which triad" with "which inversion" shifts phase each time the chain repeats through the full key. See [[Chord Inversions]] for the general inversion/figured-bass framework this is a worked instance of.

## How to practice it
1. **Play it off the page first.** Read the tab/notation as written and get it under the fingers and into the ear before attempting any theoretical analysis — don't try to reason through it cold.
2. **Then name the anatomy of every voicing.** For each chord in the chain: which chord tone is in the bass (root/3rd/5th)? What inversion does that make it? What single note is about to move to reach the next chord, and by how much?
3. **Internalize it structurally, not by rote.** Target state: land on any chord in the chain (e.g. D minor) and reason it out live — "up a third in the key is F major, and I know the middle voice moves here, so that gives me this shape" — rather than a memorized finger sequence with no theory underneath it.
4. **A standalone drill this etude's logic generates, usable on any spread-triad material:** grab any spread-triad voicing at random and work out, by ear and by theory, the single note that has to move a half or whole step to reach the next diatonic chord a third up. This is literally how the etude was composed — noticing the one-note connection between two spread triads and extending it through the whole diatonic key.

**Practice-philosophy point worth generalizing beyond this etude:** this kind of slow, low-note-count, analytical "why is this the voicing" practice is flagged explicitly as some of the most potent practice available for building theoretical/fretboard structural understanding — distinct from the high-repetition technical practice needed for playing tunes or building chops. Match the practice mode to the goal: reps for technique, slow analysis for internalized structure.
