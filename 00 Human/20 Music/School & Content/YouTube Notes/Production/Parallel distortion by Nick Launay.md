# Parallel distortion by Nick Launay

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7xpMahhQoRA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7xpMahhQoRA)

## Synopsis

A short Mix with the Masters session walkthrough with veteran rock producer/engineer Nick Launay (Nick Cave, Arcade Fire, Yeah Yeah Yeahs, Black Rebel Motorcycle Club) mixing a BRMC track. Launay tracked at home in Pro Tools as far as he could, then brought the session into Sunset Sound to route it through an API-based analog console — not just for hands-on control, but because the desk itself adds warmth/distortion/clarity he wanted the home-tracked material to match. Two named parallel-distortion tricks anchor the video: a guitar distortion pedal (SansAmp) blended under the direct kick mic for thickness, and a Distressor compressor driven hard purely for a low-end, thuddy parallel layer under the direct snare mic (top rolled off, low boosted). He also shows a genuinely clever bass move — splitting a close mic to a second channel with a sample-accurate phase adjuster, deliberately re-phasing the same mic against the DI on choruses to get a different tonal color than the verse, only possible because he tracked with two sources on the bass in the first place. Two full drum kits (a lighter one and a heavier one) run through the same output channels but stay separately controllable "in the box." The stated reason for splitting everything this way: printing individual stems (kick, snare, rooms) after the mix is locked so specific elements can still be pushed up or down later without reopening the whole mix. The back half of the video turns philosophical — Launay argues that capturing the mood the songwriter felt when writing the song is the single most important job in production, that the best take is the one with the best feeling rather than the most accurate one, and uses the famous fluffed guitar-pedal switch in the Rolling Stones' "Satisfaction" as the textbook example of a mistake that became the record's charm — a mistake he says would get "fixed" (and therefore ruined) by most engineers today.

## Techniques

**Analog console pass for home-tracked material — 0:00**
- Bringing Pro Tools tracks recorded at home into an **API-based console** (Sunset Sound) specifically to add "a warmth and a type of distortion and clarity" and make the home-tracked parts sit alongside the drums/bass/guitars that were recorded straight to the desk in the studio — analog summing/coloration used as a deliberate matching tool between two different recording environments, not just a mix-control convenience. → [[Committing and Printing]], [[Saturation and Distortion]]

**Two full drum kits, same output channels, kept separately controllable — 1:00**
- The song runs **two entire drum kits**: a lighter/more intimate kit for some sections and a heavier, more normal kit for the rest. Both are routed up through the same physical Neve channels on the console, but the balance/character difference between the two kits is controlled entirely **in the box** before it ever hits the desk — the console doesn't need a separate channel per kit because the choice of which kit is "live" for a given section is already baked in upstream. *(general starting point — no exact routing/automation mechanism shown on screen)* → [[2 Drums Group|Drums Group]]

**Parallel-distorted kick via a guitar distortion pedal (SansAmp) — 1:20**
- Direct/close-mic kick blended under a **second kick signal run through a guitar distortion pedal (SansAmp)**, purely for thickness — Launay calls this "that trick I've explained before on other Mix with the Masters sessions," i.e. a repeatable go-to, not a one-off. No drive/blend amount given (auto-caption/qualitative only), but the mechanics are explicit: a full parallel channel, not an insert on the direct mic. → [[Parallel Processing]], [[Drum Mic Technique]], [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[2 Drums/1 Drums Kick Track|Drums Kick Track]], [[Plugin Wishlist]]

**Parallel low-end-only distorted snare via a Distressor, driven hard — 1:35**
- A **direct snare mic (an SM57)** blended under a **second snare channel run through a Distressor compressor "hitting it hard,"** used specifically for the **low end** of the snare, not the whole signal. EQ on that low channel: **top end rolled way off, low end boosted** — described and demonstrated as a "thuddy" sound on its own, which sums with the direct 57 to read noticeably thicker together than either alone. Same parallel-blend shape as the kick trick above, different tool (compressor driven into distortion/character rather than a guitar pedal) and a narrower frequency target (low end only, via EQ on the parallel channel, not the whole distorted signal). → [[Parallel Processing]], [[Compression]], [[Drum Mic Technique]], [[2 Drums/1 Drums Snare-Clap Track|Drums Snare-Clap Track]], [[Vintage Gear Worklist]]

**Room mics + overdub brushes layered on top — 2:05**
- Beyond the direct+distorted close-mic pair on kick/snare, the second verse also carries the drum kit's **room mics** plus **overdub brushes** added afterward — a third and fourth texture layer stacked on top of the direct/parallel-distorted foundation, demonstrated by soloing each layer in turn (direct+distorted close mics → + room mics → + brushes) before hearing them all together. → [[Drum Mic Technique]], [[Layering and Stacking]]

**Print individual stems post-mix for later flexibility, even though sources share output channels — 2:20**
- Once the mix is finished and approved, everything gets **printed as individual stems** (kick, snare, rooms, etc.) specifically so those elements stay adjustable afterward without reopening the full mix — "if I want the snare louder, I can use the snare stem... or I can put the rooms up." The same discipline the two-drum-kit setup above depends on: even though both kits (and both direct/distorted layers) share physical output channels, each gets printed back as its own stem so the in-the-box control used to build the mix survives into the delivered/printable format. → [[Committing and Printing]]

**Bass: deliberate re-phasing between verse and chorus for a tonal shift — 3:15**
- A wide-range, slide-heavy melodic bass part needed a different tone on the chorus than the verse. The fix: the bass's **close mic is split to a second channel** that engages specifically on choruses, carrying a **sample-accurate time/phase adjuster** that shifts the phase relationship between that close mic and the DI (and the other close mic). Nudging the phase relationship — not adding any EQ or a different mic — is what changes the tone between sections. **Only possible because the bass was tracked with two sources (DI + close mic) to begin with** — a single DI or a single mic wouldn't have given him a second phase relationship to manipulate. *(no exact sample count given — shown by ear/by eye on the phase display, not read out)* → [[Phase Alignment]], [[1 Electric Bass DI Track]], [[Align Delay]]

**Mood/feel as the real job, not accuracy — 5:15**
- Stated as the single most important thing about making records: **capturing the mood the songwriter felt when they wrote the song**, and exaggerating/amplifying it through overdubs, reverb, and delay choices — reverb/delay picks framed explicitly as *feeling* tools, not just space tools. Black Rebel Motorcycle Club's songs specifically named as "all about mood." → [[Vocal Comping and Takes]]
- **Take selection: choose the take with the best feeling, not the most in-tune/in-time one.** When a band has done a few takes of a song, Launay picks the keeper based on the mood/feeling it creates — and notes that the *most accurately played* take is often NOT the one with the best feeling; a tempo shift from verse to chorus, or a bit of sloppiness, can be exactly what makes a take work emotionally. Contrasted against his own earlier career, tracking in a pre-Pro-Tools/pre-time-editing era, when getting a take maximally *in time* was the whole goal — a philosophy he says he's moved away from with experience. → [[Vocal Comping and Takes]]
- **The Rolling Stones' "Satisfaction" as the textbook case for mistakes-as-charm.** The Stones were reportedly unhappy with the take at the time, but the record endured — Launay points to an audible guitar-pedal switch flub on the chorus transition (a late click into the new tone, then missing the first riff of the second verse) as something you can still hear on the record today, and argues it's now part of the song's charm. Explicit warning about modern practice: **"a lot of people would fix that... and then the charm's gone"** — an easy digital fix (comping around it, time-editing it out) would remove exactly the thing that makes the take special. Directly reinforces the take-selection point above with a specific, famous example.
