# This chord progression is Madness!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dm0mzX-XW50)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dm0mzX-XW50)

## Synopsis

Prompted by a viewer comment, a close analysis of Madness's "Our House" — a common I–V–ii–IV pop loop turned genuinely unusual by making the v and IV chords minor (modal mixture), plus a distinct arrangement device: the same chord progression repeated in three different keys across verse/chorus/second-chorus at different harmonic speeds.

## Techniques

- **The progression**: C–Gm–Dm–Fm in the verse (key of C) — i.e., I–v–ii–iv. The **common, "vanilla" version** of this shape (the "Believe" progression, per Bennett's own earlier video in this batch) would be I–V–ii–IV, all major/diatonic. Madness instead makes BOTH the v and the iv chords minor — introducing two black-key/non-diatonic notes (B♭ and A♭ in the key of C) that Bennett calls surprisingly rare for such an otherwise-recognizable pop shape, making the progression "unique to this song" in practice.
- **Modal mixture, defined explicitly**: keeping the tonal center (tonic note) fixed while blending chords from different parallel scales built on that same tonic (here: C major mixed with elements pulling toward C Mixolydian/C minor) — "the tonic note stays the same... but which flavor of C... is changing." Demonstrated by ear: playing the same song with the vanilla all-major version of the progression "sounds totally fine" but "loses that edge... that unusual bittersweet flavor of mixing major and minor."
- **Melody stays diatonic even while the harmony doesn't**: the vocal melody never actually uses a note that would clash with the borrowed minor chords (e.g., the melody never sings a B natural during the Gm chord, so the melody's C-major B-natural never collides with the chord's B♭) — this is WHY the modal mixture can pass as consonant/pleasant rather than dissonant: the melody simply avoids the exact notes where the two "flavors" of C would actually clash.
- **Same-song multi-key device, layered on top of the modal mixture**: verse — key of C, one chord per bar (relaxed pace). First chorus — SAME chord shapes (I–v–ii–iv), transposed up to the key of D, AND the harmonic rhythm doubles (two chords per bar instead of one) — the key change and the rhythm speed-up compound to inject a big jump in energy at the chorus. Second chorus — same progression again, transposed down to the key of B, before returning to C for the next verse. **General takeaway, worth preserving as a standalone arrangement technique**: repeating an identical chord *shape* in a new key (and often a new harmonic rhythm) is a reliable, low-effort way to signal "new section" without needing a genuinely different progression — the recognizability of the shape carries continuity while the transposition and pacing change carry the sense of escalation.
