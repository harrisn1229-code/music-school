# How to Write Advanced MIDI Basslines (Part 1) - Essential Knowledge

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5DwAajF9ZXs)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5DwAajF9ZXs)

## Synopsis

Part 1 of a 3-part series (→ [[Creating the Perfect Bass Groove - Advanced MIDI Bass (Part 2)]], [[Techniques for Complex Funky Lines - Advanced MIDI Bass (Part 3)]]) laying out the analytical framework Max Konyi uses for every bassline he writes: **Tone, Note Choice, Groove** — the sound of the instrument, the notes chosen against the chords, and how the part locks into the drums. This video is the foundation; parts 2 and 3 apply the same three-part lens to progressively busier, more syncopated examples. Before any theory, he leads with listening — training the ear on bass specifically, the same way you'd train it on melody or drums — because feel doesn't come from the framework, it comes from imitation. The worked example is a relaxed lo-fi-ish chill track in C major over an 8-bar ii-V/vi-style progression, analyzed bar by bar for how the line connects each chord to the next.

## Techniques

- **Listen to bass specifically, before anything else.** Watch bass players on YouTube — live, at home, teaching — because they've spent their life inside this one instrument; sing back what they play (approximate pitch is fine, the *vibe/feel* is the target) the same way you'd mimic speech. This is presented as more important than any of the theory that follows — a bassline built on correct theory but no listening-derived feel "still falls flat." → [[Bassline Writing]]
- **The three-lens framework for analyzing/writing any bassline: Tone, Note Choice, Groove.** Tone = the sound design decision (what the instrument itself sounds like and how its envelope behaves); Note Choice = which notes, relative to the chords and key; Groove = how the part locks against the drums/tempo/syncopation. Applied consistently across all three videos in the series as the whole method. → [[Bassline Writing]]
- **Tune the bass sound's decay/release to the length of your longest held note**, so a sustained root note fully "closes" (fades out) right as the next note re-triggers — gives a satisfying sense of closure/reignition rather than an abrupt cutoff or an unwanted overlap. Match the tone's overall character (here: rounded, soft, dark, fast-fading) to the track's emotional vibe (here: relaxed/chill). → [[Bass Design]]
- **Root notes on strong beats, but never "just jump to the root."** Land chord tones on strong beats (root preferred, then fifth, then third), and use smooth connective motion to get there rather than leaping blind — walking up through root-fifth-root before dropping to the third, alternating root/fifth on a held chord instead of repeating the root, and specifically choosing an approach note that sits a half-step below/above the next chord's root for a smooth landing.
- **Enclosure: approach the target note from both above and below before landing on it.** A small grace-note/ghost-note slide (root → fifth → third → target) used as a connective run into the next downbeat; described as "going above the target, below the target, hitting the target."
- **Runs don't have to be pure arpeggios — walk the key's scale too, not just the current chord's chord tones**, especially in transitional moments between chords, as long as important beats still land on strong chord tones (root/fifth/third).
- **Respect altered/non-diatonic chord tones inside a key-based run.** When a chord borrows a note outside the home key (his example: C7's ♭7 vs C major 7's natural 7 within a C major key), the run must use the borrowed note where that chord is sounding, not the diatonic default — otherwise the harmony reads as wrong. → [[Chords by Key]], [[Chord Naming by Context]]
- **Build a fill's contour by singing/humming it first, before knowing the actual notes** — get the melodic shape you want to hear, then work out which specific notes satisfy that shape against the current chord and the key. Explicitly stated as his general fill-writing workflow, not just a one-off trick.
- **Bass and kick should read as one unified low-end "boom."** Layer (mute) a copy of the kick pattern onto the bass MIDI track to visually check where kicks land relative to bass note onsets — when a kick hits, something should generally be happening in the bass, so the two low elements fuse into a single perceived hit rather than sounding like two competing low sounds. → [[Masking and Carving]], [[Kick Design]]
- **The snare can act as an on/off switch for bass notes** — used more heavily in the busier examples later in the series, but flagged here as one of the roles the snare can play against a bass line, a personal/genre-dependent choice rather than a rule.
- **"Am I working with the drums or fighting them?"** — the core groove-writing question: if the kick pattern and the bass pattern are pulling in different directions, the part won't read as unified; either the drums adapt to the bass or the bass adapts to the drums, but they can't be written in isolation from each other. → [[Rhythm and Groove]]
