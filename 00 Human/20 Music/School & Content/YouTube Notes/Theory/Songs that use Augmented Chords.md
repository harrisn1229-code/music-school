# Songs that use Augmented Chords

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ys6SDpBgWzc)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ys6SDpBgWzc)

## Synopsis

Augmented chords (major triad, ♯5) as a dominant-substitute and voice-leading tool: resolving up a 4th from V or from the tonic, teasing a resolution and falling back instead, and their recurring role in line clichés — plus a genuine notational quirk (augmented chords can't be truly inverted; they're enharmonically symmetric).

## Techniques

- **Augmented triad = major triad with the 5th raised a semitone** (labeled `+`, `aug`, or `♯5`). Dissonant/unresolved in isolation but purposeful in context.
- **As a dominant substitute, resolving up a 4th (built on V)**: "Oh! Darling" (The Beatles) opens on E+ instead of the more expected E7, resolving to the tonic A major — both E7 and E+ resolve the same distance (a semitone) toward the target chord's 3rd, but from opposite directions (E7's 7th, D, falls to C♯; E+'s raised 5th, C natural, rises to C♯), giving a subtly different flavor for the same functional job. Likely modeled on Chuck Berry's "School Days" and "No Particular Place to Go," which both open the same way. The Beatles used augmented chords in at least 20 songs — George Harrison reportedly nicknamed it "the naughty chord." "From Me to You" (Beatles, middle 8) raises a plain G to G+ for extra tension before returning to the verse. "The Warmth of the Sun" (The Beach Boys) does the identical G→G+ move resolving to C major. "Blackout" (Muse) — F+ resolving to the tonic B♭, with Matt Bellamy singing the augmented 5th itself to foreground the tension.
- **Built on the tonic instead of V, resolving up a 4th to IV**: "Crying" (Roy Orbison) — the tonic D+ pulls up to IV (G). "It's My Party" (Lesley Gore) uses the identical tonic-augmented-to-IV progression.
- **Alternative resolution targets** (since the tension note is the raised 5th wanting to rise to the 6th degree, any chord containing that 6th degree works, not just IV): "Let There Be Love" (Oasis) resolves an augmented chord to vi (A minor) instead of IV, since vi also contains the target 6th degree.
- **Deliberately NOT resolving — falls back to the plain chord instead**: "Let There Be Love" (Oasis, intro) and "Mamma Mia" (ABBA, intro) both pivot from the tonic to the tonic-augmented and back down to the plain tonic — teasing the ear with the upward-resolution possibility without ever cashing it in.
- **Voice-leading / line-cliché role**: "Stairway to Heaven" (Led Zeppelin, acoustic intro) — a descending line cliché where G♯+ is functionally A minor with the root lowered a semitone, giving smooth stepwise motion between chords. "Life on Mars?" (David Bowie, pre-chorus) — an ASCENDING line cliché (A♭/E♭ → E+ → Fm → A♭7/G♭, repeated a step higher for the second half) where the augmented chord appears as one step of the rising bass line. Composers likely arrive at these augmented chords as a side effect of moving one voice by step, not by deliberately reaching for "an augmented chord." → [[Line Cliché]].
- **Genuine notational quirk — augmented chords can't be truly inverted**: because an augmented triad is built from two stacked major 3rds (fully symmetric), "inverting" C+ (putting E at the bottom) doesn't produce "C+/E" — the resulting notes ARE literally E+ instead, and inverting again yields G♯+. Any given augmented chord can correctly be named three different ways (its three "inversions" are really three separate augmented chords sharing the same notes) — real transcriptions of "Life on Mars?" disagree on whether to call the same sound "E+" or "C+/E." Bennett's rule of thumb: pick whichever name is simplest to read, since chord symbols exist to make playing easier, not to be maximally "correct."
