# Why "Take Me To Church" is an unusual pop song

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=12QOHTfZdLU)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=12QOHTfZdLU)

## Synopsis

An analysis of Hozier's "Take Me to Church" as harmonically and rhythmically unusual for a pop hit: mixed 3/4-and-4/4 verses, a natural-minor-to-harmonic-minor tonal shift at the chorus, and — the sharpest single moment — a plagal ("Amen") cadence sung under the literal word "Amen," deliberately corrupted on its third occurrence as an ironic jab given the song's anti-church lyrics.

## Techniques

- **Mixed meter as verse texture**: verses open in 3/4, with an occasional single bar of 4/4 mixed in at varying ratios between verses — described as a subtle rhythmic unsettledness that mirrors the lyrical unease, resolving into a stable, uninterrupted 4/4 specifically at the chorus for a deliberate sense of relief and arrival.
- **The plagal/"Amen" cadence, used literally**: during the pre-chorus, the repeated lyric "Amen" is sung over a IV→I cadence (C→G in G major) — the plagal cadence's actual nickname is the "Amen cadence" because it's the traditional cadence used to sing "amen" in church music. Hozier stages the word directly over the cadence that shares its name.
- **The ironic corruption move**: on the cadence's third and final appearance, Hozier swaps the major iv for a **minor iv** (Cm→G instead of C→G) — same plagal shape, but the minor coloring turns the traditionally warm, resolved "Amen" cadence bitter and acrimonious, matching the song's critique-not-endorsement stance toward the lyric it's setting.
- **Tonal shift from verse to chorus via a borrowed dominant**: the song lives mostly in G major's relative minor (E natural minor) through the verses, but the chorus plants a B major chord (V of E minor, raising the natural minor's 7th) — shifting the color to **E harmonic minor**, which reads as noticeably darker/more serious than the verse's E natural minor. A clean example of harmonic-minor-via-secondary-dominant used specifically as a mood pivot between song sections.
