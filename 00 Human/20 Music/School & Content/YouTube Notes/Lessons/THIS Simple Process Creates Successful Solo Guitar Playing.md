## Synopsis

A condensed, step-by-step **build order** for turning a bare melody into a solo jazz-guitar chord-melody arrangement, using the standard "All of Me" as the worked example throughout. Rather than presenting isolated tricks, Nathan Borton lays out the process as an explicit sequence — pick the melody, learn it correctly (by ear, from a period vocal/film recording, not off a lead sheet by eye), lock it to a metronome with a groove, layer in chord roots at the phrase pauses, upgrade roots to full chords, add chromatic passing chords, harmonize the melody itself by matching voicings to their top note, then stitch connective lines between phrases — and finishes by playing the fully-assembled arrangement of "All of Me" straight through. The value here isn't any single device (each one — chromatic passing chords, top-note voicing lookup, enclosure-to-third connective lines — is already documented in more depth elsewhere in the vault, principally [[Chord Melody Arranging]]) but the **ordering**: this is the practice-room sequence for building an arrangement from scratch, one layer at a time, each layer played in time before the next is added, rather than trying to assemble a full arrangement in one pass.

## What's taught

**1. Pick and learn the melody by ear, from a period source, not off a chart.**
- Choose a standard to arrange (worked example: "All of Me," a 1932 standard).
- **Research the tune before learning it** — find the era/context it comes from; "All of Me" was featured in the 1932 film *Careless Lady*, so Borton goes back to that film-era vocal recording (or one from around the same time) rather than a modern jazz recording, and links a period-recording video in his own description.
- **Learn the melody into your ears and hands, not your eyes** — deliberately avoid reading it off a lead sheet as the primary learning method. Reasoning given: once you start embellishing/harmonizing later, you need the melody solid from memory — if you're still visually reading it, the added chords/lines have nothing stable to attach to.

**2. Lock the melody to a metronome with an accented downbeat, not 2-and-4.**
- Play the bare melody in time, with swing feel and a real groove, before adding anything to it.
- **Metronome setting: accent beat 1 only, not 2 and 4.** Practical reasoning: accenting 2-and-4 is the more "final" sound most players default to, but if your internal sense of where beat 1 falls isn't already solid, 2-and-4 accents let you fake it — you can groove along without ever really locking to the downbeat, whereas accenting only 1 forces you to feel/track the downbeat directly, since there's nothing else marking the bar. **Only switch to accenting 2-and-4 once your beat-1 feel is already solid** — otherwise it's guessing dressed up as groove. Tool named: the **Pro Metronome** app, set (in its settings) to make the beat-1 click the loudest/biggest bar in the pattern.

**3. Add chord roots into the melody's rests, in time.**
- Wherever the melody pauses/holds, fill the gap with the **root note** of whatever chord is currently sounding — don't leave dead space between phrases.
- **Optional embellishment on the same slot:** swing-eighth-note rhythms under the root instead of a single sustained note, once the plain version is solid.

**4. Upgrade roots to full chords in the same slots.**
- Once root-filling is comfortable and in time, swap the single root notes for full chord voicings in the same phrase-gap slots — described as the point where the arrangement first really starts to sound like solo jazz guitar.

**5. Add chromatic passing chords to the chord slots.**
- Slide the **same voicing shape** up a half step or down a half step as a passing move before/after the target chord (worked over a C major voicing) — matches the "slide the whole shape a fret" chromatic-approach-chord device already on [[Chord Melody Arranging]] and [[Passing Chords]] § *2. Chromatic passing/approach chords*.
- **Can extend to two chromatic steps**, approaching from two frets away instead of one, for a longer chromatic run-up into the target.
- Everything up to this point must stay **strictly in time** — Borton repeats this as the thing that actually "solidifies" the skill, not just knowing the moves.

**6. Harmonize the melody itself by top-note voicing lookup.**
- The stated fear to defuse: players assume chord-melody means harmonizing *every single melody note*, which isn't necessary — most of a standard guitar voicing vocabulary is already enough if matched correctly.
- **Method:** for each voicing you already know, identify **what its top note is**; then, reading through the melody, match each melody note against your own mental catalog of "voicings with that note on top" for the chord currently sounding. This is the informal, on-the-fly version of the string-indexed voicing-lookup method already documented in depth on [[Chord Melody Arranging]] § *Step-by-step lead-sheet method*.
- **Worked through "All of Me"'s opening phrase, chord by chord:**
  - Opening phrase is a plain **C major triad** melody; the phrase-ending melody note (E) is harmonized with a **C6 shape** (E as the top note of a C6 voicing).
  - Next phrase sits over **E7**; harmonized with a common E7 voicing that also has E on top.
  - Next phrase over **A7**: uses a standard **A7 drop 3 voicing**, continuing the same top-note-E pattern down through the progression.
  - The tricky spot: melody note **D over an F chord** — uses an **F major 13 voicing** (equivalent to an **F major 7 drop 3 shape** with the top note moved up from C to D) rather than a plain F triad, because the plain shape doesn't have D available on top.
- **General takeaway stated directly:** review your existing voicing vocabulary specifically by top note, not just by chord type/root, so you have a fast lookup ready mid-arrangement — the same "index by which note lands on top" principle [[Chord Melody Arranging]] already generalizes into a full string-indexed reference method.

**7. Add connective lines between phrases — an enclosure to the 3rd, then down to the 5th.**
- Same slot as the chord-roots-in-the-rests step (3), but instead of a chord, insert a short single-note line.
- **The line's shape:** an enclosure that targets the **3rd** of whatever chord is currently sounding, then **jumps down to the 5th**. Worked examples given over E7 and over A7 (same shape, moved to each chord's own 3rd/5th).
- **Two ways to deploy it once learned:** practice the line in isolation over every chord in the progression first, then either play the connective **line** in a given gap, or the **chord** from step 4 — treated as interchangeable options for the same phrase-gap slot, chosen by ear/feel in the moment.

**8. Assemble and play the whole arrangement.**
- Once every layer (melody, roots, chords, chromatic passing chords, harmonized melody notes, connective lines) is solid individually, combine them into a single full pass — demonstrated as a complete "All of Me" performance closing out the lesson.
- **Stated payoff beyond solo performance:** an arrangement built this way is also easier to play *in a group* afterward, since the melody, harmony, and connective vocabulary are already internalized together rather than learned separately.
- **Restated closing principle:** time and groove are the most important aspect of solo-in-time guitar — repeated as the thing that must never slip while adding the other layers, not a separate concern from them.
