# Love You To — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

This goes well past the era's standard-issue add-a-sitar gesture: **mode, melodic construction, rhythm and instrumentation are all handled in genuinely Indian terms**, and even the boxy rock form preserves the Indian convention of a free-tempo improvised slow introduction. Pollack, who studied the tradition formally, is warm about the authenticity and blunt about the failure — the Western and Indian elements sit side by side rather than fusing, and the imported studio players comprehensively upstage George.

**Harmony.** **A drone, with occasional implied oscillation toward ♭VII, and nothing else** ([[POP-C03 — Pedal point]]). The drone is voiced as an open fifth so it never states the third, which leaves the home chord's major/minor identity formally undecided even though the sitar leans hard on the minor third ([[SCL-C05 — Characteristic notes]]). This is the Indian aesthetic doing what harmony normally does in Western music: no teleology, no progress, elaboration over a fixed centre.

**Melody.** Dorian in Western terms, but a raga is more than a mode — it also carries characteristic riffs and a division of the scale into two registral halves, and **the song honours both**, with recurrent riffs staying within one half or the other. If static harmony is going to hold interest, that registral structure is what has to carry it.

**Rhythm.** Trickier than the "sixteen beats" instruction in the session literature suggests. A **3/4 bar is hidden in the lead-in to the refrain**, and you don't notice because the same sitar hook fills a normal 4/4 bar a couple of bars later — the ear matches the figure and never audits the bar. Another 3/4 sits inside the refrain, filled the same way. The verse plods in even quarters and then breaks into a syncopated melisma that loosens your grip on the downbeat, with **the drums dropping out for the two bars where that happens**. The solo resists the analysis entirely, partly because the sitar declaims in irrational groupings against the steady pulse.

**Arrangement.** Effectively George plus hired Indian studio musicians, with tambourine added in verse 2 and a fuzz-toned embellishment on the ♭VII. The refrain is a **call-and-response between voice and sitar**, and the solo is the peak of the record.
