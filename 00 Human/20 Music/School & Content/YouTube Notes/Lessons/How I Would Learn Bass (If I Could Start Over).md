# How I Would Learn Bass (If I Could Start Over)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DNjI9ruFQIg)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DNjI9ruFQIg)

## Synopsis

A BassBuzz "what I'd change" video that deliberately rules out the obvious answers up front — Josh opens by stating he would *not* prioritize establishing a practice routine, learning proper fretting technique, getting his bass professionally set up, or practicing with a metronome differently. Those are "outer game" fixes (technique and theory), and his claim is they only get a self-taught player halfway there. The real answer, and the entire content of the video, is the **inner game** — the psychology that determines whether someone sticks with bass for decades or quietly drifts away, offered as the explanation for why far more people used to play an instrument than currently play one, and why so few players are still going in their 50s and 60s. Four keys are walked through, each with a personal case study rather than abstract advice: **don't let comparison run your life** (a fixed-vs-growth-mindset reframe plus a "question-markify" tool for limiting beliefs, illustrated by his own ego-crushing move to LA and retreat to Phoenix); **know your why** (why a modest why is just as valid as a grand one, why whys drift over time, illustrated by his own shift away from a teenage "become a virtuoso like Victor Wooten" goal); **don't wait for inspiration, just show up** (naming "the inspiration myth" directly, backed by Stephen King and Flea references, with an honest admission he still loses to this himself); and **embrace the suck** (a "Forget Me Not" relearning case study, a Michael Jordan failure-learning tie-in, and a concrete "flip the bass and play it opposite-handed" grit-building drill). ## What's taught

- **Cold open — ruling out the "outer game" answers.** Explicitly *not* what he'd change: establishing a practice routine, learning proper fretting technique, getting the bass professionally set up, practicing with a metronome. Named as insufficient by themselves — "that good stuff will only get you halfway there." Framed stakes: neglecting the inner game leads to plateau, frustration, aggravation, and stagnation rather than the "I can play something I couldn't play last month" feeling — offered as the reason so many more people used to play an instrument than currently play one, and why so few players are still active in their 50s/60s.
- **Key 1 — Don't let comparison run your life.** The trap: feeling good about progress, then seeing a nine-year-old shred harder than you ever have and wanting to quit. Personal case study: moving to LA overconfident, ego crushed comparing himself to LA musicians, fleeing to Phoenix instead of connecting with the new players and working through the gap. The reframe: comparison itself isn't the problem — *"I love how tastefully she chose her bass fills, I'm inspired to improve"* (helpful) vs. *"she's so much better than me, I quit"* (unhelpful); brains default to the negative framing, so the helpful version takes deliberate work. Underlying mechanism: fixed mindset (talent is a static trait) makes comparison discouraging and self-fulfilling; growth mindset (talent develops through work/strategy/mentoring) makes comparison inspiring and self-reinforcing. Concrete tool: the "question-markify" technique — turn a flat limiting statement ("I can't get better," "I'm too old to learn bass") into a question ("Can I get better?" "Am I too old to learn bass?") so it prompts an actual reality-check instead of being accepted as fact. Caveat: nobody runs a clean growth mindset in every area of life.
- **Key 2 — Know your why.** Without a clear why, drift is the default — his own 20s, cited directly: "stumble aimlessly, forget to practice for years at a time, generally suck." A why is simply what you want out of learning bass — examples deliberately range from grand (be famous) to modest (play along with your favorite songs) to establish both are equally legitimate. Two named ways to get this wrong: (1) thinking the why has to be grandiose — a simple "I just want to play music and have fun" is a great why if it's true, and it does real work: keeps you connected to your original inspiration and gives you a practice agenda (work on what boosts enjoyment); (2) forgetting whys change over time, which is natural and possibly a sign of maturing rather than inconsistency. Worked example: his teenage why was "become a world-renowned virtuoso like Victor Wooten," and he did the matching work — but the why quietly shifted toward groove and musical communication as he got older, and because he didn't notice the shift, he kept running the old virtuoso-practice regimen against a why that no longer existed, eventually stopping practice altogether for a long stretch. Actionable takeaway: periodically check whether your why has moved and adjust what you practice to match the *current* one.
- **Key 3 — Don't wait for inspiration, just show up.** Names "the inspiration myth" directly — the belief you'll consistently feel like practicing, which keeps you "stuck passively waiting for lightning to strike" instead of acting. Backed by Stephen King ("amateurs sit and wait for inspiration, the rest of us just get up and go to work") and Flea ("if you want to be good at something, you have to do it when it's not fun"). Self-honesty beat: Josh admits he still loses to this — money spent on pedals/a looper for an idea untouched for six months. The fix: show up and do the work regardless of feeling inspired; this is where a clear why (Key 2) pays off, since it's what carries you through resistance ("I'm tired, I don't feel like it"). Practical lever named for making showing-up easier: remove the daily "what do I even practice" decision — a structured course (his own "Beginner to Badass" is the named example) works because you execute the next lesson rather than inventing a routine from scratch each day.
- **Key 4 (the final key) — Embrace the suck.** Reframe: skill acquisition isn't a smooth climb — there's suck woven through the whole process (a bad comparison, an uninspired scale session, a week you don't feel like practicing at all are named as instances), and the fix is leaning into it intentionally rather than just tolerating it. Case study: relearning the "Forget Me Not" bassline (the Patrice Rushen line later sampled in Will Smith's "Men in Black") after 15 years of playing — embarrassed he couldn't play a fast passage cleanly, tempted to permanently fake it with hammer-ons, instead spent weeks practicing the specific weak-point thumb movement daily at his own actual speed until it was real, and later demonstrated it on camera. Reframe that made it sustainable: the intensity of the suck is itself a signpost of moving in the right direction. Failure-based learning, backed by Michael Jordan ("I've missed more than 9,000 shots... I have failed over and over and over again, and that is why I succeed") — people who fail and learn from it improve faster than people who avoid risking failure. Concrete grit-building drill given: flip the bass over and play it "opposite-handed" (string order doesn't matter, the point is guaranteed incompetence), try something simple in that state (his example: "some Pink Floyd"), and use the resulting funny/harmless mistakes to rebuild a non-defensive relationship to error — "this is how I sounded on day one." Named as needing repetition ("day after day") to actually shift the mindset, not a one-time fix. Closing pointer to a "10-minute daily practice exercise" that both embraces the suck and builds real technique — already in the vault as [[Practice THIS Daily (10 Min Technique Fix)]].
