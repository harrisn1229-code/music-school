# Let It Be — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

The whole song runs on **four chords and a five-note scale** and never once leaves the home key — one passing chromatic note in the coda is the only non-diatonic pitch in the record. Everything that makes it sound larger than that is ornamentation and voice leading. Pollack treats it as the diatonic-Paul counterpart to "Hey Jude", and uses the bootlegged *Get Back* mix as his reference text because it's the least worked-over of the three.

**Form.** The **verse is built directly on the intro's eight bars**, so the intro pre-loads the harmony without giving away the tune — the listener arrives at the vocal already oriented and still surprised. The refrain is a plain AB pair of four-bar phrases; the coda is convergent, and on its first appearance is doubled.

**Harmony.** Four chords and nothing else. The modal flavour is a **cadence habit, not a chord choice**: despite V appearing constantly, resolution is nearly always **plagal** ([[HAR-C03 — Plagal cadence]]), and where V does move it tends to slide to IV (blues style) or to vi (a classical deceptive move) rather than home ([[HAR-C04 — Deceptive cadence]]). Starve V of its resolution and an entirely ordinary diatonic set turns modal. The refrain's first phrase is a symmetrical root progression — a falling fourth twice — deliberately camouflaged by a stepwise bass and parallel tenths above it, and two spots stay productively ambiguous between two chord labels depending on which appoggiatura you privilege.

**Melody.** Almost entirely **pentatonic** ([[SCL-C03 — Pentatonic scales]]), including the guitar solo; the fourth degree appears essentially once, as a neighbour tone. Verse and refrain each span a ninth but the refrain sits a third higher, and the two sections **end with the identical phrase**, which reads differently each time — near the top of the arch in the verse, part of its descent in the refrain ([[HAR-C09 — Non-harmonic tones]]).

**Arrangement.** A textbook energy ramp: piano alone, then cymbals, then bass in verse 2, full kit for the refrains and solo, percussion pulled back for the final verse, everything restored for the finish. The octave vocal flips are held back until after the guitar solo, and the outro plays the coda phrase **once** where the earlier coda played it twice — halving a returning section on its last appearance avoids a pompous ending.
