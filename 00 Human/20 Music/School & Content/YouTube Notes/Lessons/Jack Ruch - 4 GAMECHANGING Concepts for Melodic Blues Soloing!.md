# Jack Ruch — 4 GAMECHANGING Concepts for Melodic Blues Soloing!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HJqTF9Vm2uI)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HJqTF9Vm2uI)

## Synopsis

An abbreviated, promotional walkthrough of Jack Ruch's paid course (with Brett Papa), *Melodic Blues Soloing*, condensing the course's structure into a single worked example: **four soloing approaches, played in sequence over the same backing track (a blues in B minor), each building directly on the previous one** rather than replacing it — the stated intent is that a player keeps every earlier layer's phrasing vocabulary and adds a new harmonic tool on top each time. The four approaches are demonstrated back-to-back with a short solo after each: (1) key-center soloing (B minor pentatonic over the whole form), (2) chord-tone soloing (arpeggio-derived chord tones on the changes), (3) chord substitution (a relative-major arpeggio over the i chord, a diminished-7 arpeggio over the dominant), (4) advanced scales (Lydian dominant and the altered scale, both modes of melodic minor). Because this is a course trailer rather than a full lesson, it's lighter on exact tab/fret detail than Ruch's usual free lessons — no fretboard diagrams or fingerings are given in the video itself (those are gated behind the course/Patreon) — but the harmonic content (which scale/arpeggio over which chord, and why) is stated clearly and transcribes cleanly. Chord progression as described: **i (Bm) → IV (E7) → ... → (turnaround) G7 → F♯7 → back to Bm**, i.e. a minor blues in B minor with a dominant V (F♯7) — matching [[Blues Form and Harmony]]'s "minor blues: the V frequently stays a dominant" rule exactly.

## What's taught

**The four-layer structure itself is the headline teaching point** — Ruch frames it explicitly as a deliberate pedagogical stack ("each concept isn't meant to replace the previous concept, but build on it") using the *same* opening pentatonic blues phrases as the seed material for all four solos, so each new layer is heard grafted onto familiar phrasing rather than as an isolated exercise. Concretely:

1. **Key-center soloing (entry level).** Identify the key (B minor), find a scale that fits (B minor pentatonic, played from a position around where the video's box sits), and string together simple phrases — deliberately not targeting individual chord tones yet. Practice discipline named directly: play a phrase, leave space, then use **repetition to build motifs/themes** across the solo, rather than continuous note-running. Over the chord changes (up to G7, down to F♯7 in the turnaround) the instruction is explicitly to keep playing pentatonic and use the ear rather than chase specific chord tones — this layer is a phrasing/motif foundation, not a harmony lesson. **Drill:** loop the Bm blues backing track, play only B minor pentatonic for multiple choruses, and enforce "phrase → rest → repeat/vary the phrase" — no continuous 8th-note running — until 2–3 usable motifs emerge that can be recalled on demand.
2. **Chord-tone soloing (intermediate).** Layer in the actual chord tones — root, 3rd, 5th, ♭7 — of each chord as it passes, sourced from **CAGED-based single-note arpeggio shapes** taken off the underlying bar-chord grips (the same shape-picking logic as [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]). Worked over the IV chord (E7): outline the E7 arpeggio's notes in place of/alongside the pentatonic. Same treatment repeated over G7 and F♯7 in the turnaround. **Drill:** for each chord in the progression (Bm, E7, G7, F♯7), find the closest CAGED-derived arpeggio shape to the previous chord's shape (don't jump position), drill root-3-5-♭7 in isolation first, then splice it into the pentatonic phrases from layer 1 — target landing on the 3rd and root specifically on the beat the chord change happens (matches [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]'s Step 2 "target beat 1 on purpose").
3. **Chord substitution (advanced-intermediate).**
   - **Over the i chord (Bm): play a D major 7 arpeggio instead of a Bm arpeggio.** Because D major 7 (D-F♯-A-C♯) and B minor 9 (B-D-F♯-A-C♯) share every note except the root, playing the D maj7 shape over Bm gives a **B minor 9 sound with the root implied rather than stated** — minor 3rd, 5th, ♭7, and 9th, no root. Stated explicitly as "a really pretty sound," meant to be tied directly back into the pentatonic blues phrasing rather than run as an isolated scale exercise. This is the **relative-major-arpeggio-over-the-relative-minor** trick — same device already logged on [[Chord Substitution]] (D-major-family relative-major-over-minor examples) — confirmed again here in a third key (D maj7 / B minor).
   - **Over the V chord (F♯7, the "dominant 5 chord" of the progression): play a diminished-7th arpeggio built off the chord's own major 3rd** — Ruch names it a "B♭ diminished 7th arpeggio" over F♯7. F♯7's major 3rd is A♯, enharmonically B♭ — so this is the standard **dim7-off-the-3rd-of-the-dominant** substitution (already documented as a device on [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]] and [[Diminished Chords]]): because the diminished-7 chord is fully symmetric, it can be entered/started on any of its four notes, and Ruch notes he specifically anchors it to stay near the current hand position rather than the "textbook" starting note. Only hinted at briefly (not run as a full scale) before resolving back into B minor pentatonic.
   - **Drill:** (a) loop just the Bm bar — alternate a plain Bm pentatonic phrase with a D major 7 arpeggio phrase, listening for the B minor 9 color; (b) loop just the F♯7 bar — play the B♭(A♯) diminished-7 arpeggio (B♭-D♭-E-G, or enharmonically A♯-C♯-E-G) starting from whichever of its four notes sits closest to your current hand position, resolve back to a Bm chord tone; (c) chain a full chorus: Bm (D maj7 arpeggio) → E7 (chord-tone/arpeggio from layer 2) → G7 (chord-tone) → F♯7 (B♭dim7 arpeggio) → back to Bm pentatonic to close.
4. **Advanced scales (the "advanced" layer).** Two modes of the melodic minor scale, placed on three specific spots in the progression:
   - **Lydian dominant** (melodic minor's IV mode — a major scale with a ♭7 and a ♯4) over **both dominant chords built on scale steps other than the true V**: E Lydian dominant over the E7 (IV) chord, and **G Lydian dominant over the G7 chord** — in both cases hinted at with a short arpeggio-derived lick outlining the sound rather than run start-to-finish, always resolving back to B minor pentatonic. Matches [[Scale Modes Reference]]'s existing "Lydian dominant on secondary/approach dominants a step or half-step from the true V" pattern, confirmed here on two different non-V dominants in the same tune.
   - **Altered scale** (another melodic minor mode) over the **true V chord, F♯7** — the standard altered-scale-on-the-functioning-dominant choice.
   - **Drill:** isolate each of the three spots separately before chaining — loop E7 alone and drill E Lydian dominant (major scale with ♭7 + ♯4: E-F♯-G♯-A♯-B-C♯-D), loop G7 alone and drill G Lydian dominant (G-A-B-C♯-D-E-F), loop F♯7 alone and drill F♯ altered (F♯-G-A-B♭-C-D-E) — in each case, outline the chord tones first as a skeleton (matches [[Scale Modes Reference]]'s "outline chord tones first, then layer the mode's color" practice note), then add the mode's characteristic color tone (the ♯4/♯11 for Lydian dominant, the ♭9/♯9/♭5/♯5 stack for altered) before resolving back to Bm pentatonic. Once each spot is solid alone, play a full chorus chaining i (pentatonic/chord-tone/substitution vocabulary from layers 1–3) → E7 (Lydian dominant) → G7 (Lydian dominant) → F♯7 (altered) → resolve.
- **Meta-drill across all four layers, stated as the course's actual teaching method:** don't treat the four layers as four separate songs to learn — replay the *same* opening pentatonic phrases at the start of each solo, so the ear hears one continuous idea getting progressively more harmonically sophisticated rather than four unrelated exercises. Practically: pick 2–3 pentatonic motifs from layer 1, then re-use those exact motifs as the opening of the layer-2, layer-3, and layer-4 solos, only changing what happens over the chord changes each time.
