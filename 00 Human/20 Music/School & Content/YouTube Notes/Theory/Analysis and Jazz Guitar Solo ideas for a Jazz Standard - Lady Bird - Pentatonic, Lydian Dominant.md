## Techniques

### Harmonic analysis of the form
- **Lady Bird is a 16-bar form in C major, written by Tadd Dameron** — Larsen notes there are several differing "real book" melody versions in circulation, and separately flags Miles Davis's own bebop contrafact on these changes, "Half Nelson," as a related tune worth knowing (he has a recorded version on his own channel). → [[🎵 TADD DAMERON - Lady Bird]]
- **Bar-by-bar reading of the A section:** Cmaj7 (I, tonic) → **Fm7–B♭7** (read as a ii-V *in E♭*, i.e. "an F minor operation" resolving back to C rather than staying in E♭) → cadence to **A♭maj7**. Larsen explicitly flags this A♭maj7 as a spot he treats as *not* a true modulation, even though plenty of people analyze it as one — his reasoning: A♭ is simply the ♭VI of C major (the flat 6 out of C minor, borrowed), so hearing the A♭maj7 as a borrowed chord rather than a new key center is a legitimate, arguably simpler reading than calling it a modulation. → [[Borrowed Chords]], [[Modulation (Key Change)]]
- **"Dominant of the dominant" section:** from A♭maj7 back toward the cadence, the form runs **Am7–D7** (treated explicitly as one continuous two-chord unit, not two separate things to "spit out" — Larsen's stated general habit is to always think a ii-V as one connected shape rather than naming the two chords separately) functioning as **V7/V**, then the **final V (G7)** resolving back to the **I (Cmaj7)**. General point about song form: a "dominant of the dominant" section like this is a common place in a major-key standard for the harmony to cadence toward V before the tune's real ending. → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Secondary Dominants]]
- **The turnaround — the tune's most-cited feature — is a modified 1-6-2-5, substituting Fm7 (iv minor) in place of the expected ii chord**, i.e. Cmaj7–**Fm7**–B♭7(-ish)–[cadence] rather than the base-form Cmaj7–Am7–Dm7–G7 documented on [[Turnarounds]]. Larsen notes the vi slot is interchangeable with its usual secondary-dominant substitutes (A7, or an altered/diminished version of A7) with essentially the same function, and the same interchangeability applies to G7 in the ii-V that follows. → [[Turnarounds]]
- **The turnaround's other quirk: the ♭VI7 chord (A♭7) resolves DOWN, not as a tritone substitute.** Rather than functioning as the tritone sub of D7 (which is the "normal" reading of a chord living a half step above the target), this A♭7 is used to resolve directly onward, moving by descending major third through the form (C → A♭ → ... ) — Larsen calls this out specifically as one of the places that may have inspired Coltrane's "Giant Steps" cycle (he says he doesn't know for certain, but flags the resemblance directly), since Giant Steps' whole harmonic engine is the same descending-major-third modulation cycle. **Practical payoff stated directly:** if you already have turnaround vocabulary that moves by descending major thirds (this one), you have a natural, non-scary entry point into Giant Steps-style changes rather than treating them as a wholly separate, alien vocabulary — "you already have a lot of material that is quite natural" to bring across. → [[Chord Progressions]], [[The Axis Progression]]
- **Learning-standpoint case for the tune:** every chord change in Lady Bird moves to a harmonically distant/contrasting chord (C→Fm, C→B♭m, A♭→Am), so the ear can track "something's happening" every two or four bars very clearly — flagged as specifically useful for a beginner improviser building the habit of hearing form and chord change locations by ear. → [[Musical Form]]

### Soloing vocabulary, chord by chord
- **Core practice principle stated up front, generalizable to any tune: play toward target notes in the NEXT chord, not toward the chord you're currently on.** The stated reasoning: practicing target-note lines trains you to think one chord ahead instead of reactively "running after" the harmony as it goes by — framed as directly analogous to reading ahead of where you're speaking. Practice method given: pick reachable notes in the upcoming chord, then work backward to build short connective lines that land on them. → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]], [[Guide Tones]]
- **Cmaj7 (tonic) — keep it simple; straight C major scale/arpeggio is the default**, deliberately avoiding "very out there" color this early in a solo (stated as a general solo-shape principle: don't rush into outside sounds at the start). One stated exception: he's played a Lydian pass over the tonic in some choruses, but treats plain major as the default. → [[Scales]]
- **Cmaj7 → Fm7 half-step pentatonic shift: E minor pentatonic over Cmaj7, shifted UP a half step to F minor pentatonic the instant Fm7 hits.** E minor pentatonic works over Cmaj7 as a "10th line scale" color (the relative-minor-a-third-up pentatonic device); the payoff of choosing *that* particular pentatonic is that its relative, F minor pentatonic, is exactly a half step up and lands squarely on the next chord (Fm7) — so the same movable box just slides one fret, with no new shape to learn. Framed as a mild counterpoint device against the bass (the line moves independently of/against the literal chord-tone motion rather than tracking it note-for-note), which keeps the melody from sounding like it's "just laying there" restating the chord. Caution given directly: don't overuse the half-step-shift move on its own or it gets "boring" — vary it, don't lean on it as a crutch. → new technique note [[Half-Step Pentatonic Shift Over Moving Changes]]
- **Pentatonic "suspect fourths"/diatonic-4ths exercise, used throughout on both the C major and F minor pentatonic passes** — referenced back to an earlier dedicated pentatonic-scales video (not yet in this vault) covering pentatonic-fourths patterns; used here specifically because it produces melodies that stay pentatonic-sonic but avoid the "just running the box" feel, which Larsen calls a large part of the modern-jazz pentatonic sound generally. → [[Diagonal Pentatonic Blues Soloing]], [[String-Group Pentatonic Bursts]]
- **B♭7 (the turnaround's ♭VII/backdoor-adjacent dominant, functioning off the iv-minor's own ♭7) — Lydian dominant is the default color, with a plain altered-dominant reading as the alternative.** B♭7 here is heard as effectively an "Fm6 with a B♭ bass" (i.e. voiced/derived from the iv-minor sound, not a plain independent dominant), and the target of the phrase is landing back on C major, so the actual note choice is really about finding available notes that get there smoothly. Concretely: the ♭6 of the key (A♭) is named as the important target/color note of the Fm chord underneath, with the goal notes reached being either the (garbled in captions, read from context as) 9th or 3rd of the following chord. → [[Scale Modes Reference]] § Lydian Dominant
- **B♭7 → Cmaj7 arpeggio-planing device: play a D♭maj7 arpeggio over B♭m7(♭5)-adjacent material a half step above the target, resolving down onto C major.** Explicit framing of *why* this works without actually "thinking" a new chord: even while playing notes that spell a D♭ major 7 arpeggio over a chord that isn't D♭, Larsen says he's still mentally hearing/tracking the real underlying chord — the half-step-up arpeggio is treated purely as a **melodic observation** (a fingering/interval relationship that happens to produce good target notes), not a conscious substitute-harmony reharm. He generalizes this explicitly: "a lot of the harmony tends to move up" a half step in this tune, so the half-step-planing melodic device recurs across multiple chord changes, not just this one spot. → [[Chord Substitution]], [[Half-Step Pentatonic Shift Over Moving Changes]]
- **A♭maj7 → Am7 voice-leading approach: work out the individual note-to-note motion between the two chords rather than reaching for a scale.** Stated method: sit down, write out each chord's notes, and figure out literally which note goes to which note when the harmony moves A♭maj7→Am7 (a half step up on the root, a semitone-and-a-fifth-ish relationship on the guide tones) — described as generally useful groundwork to do "on any chord you come across" once you're used to the process, even though it's slow and effortful the first few times. → [[Guide Tones]]
- **A♭maj7 → Am7 also gets a diminished-family color pass**, leaning on a diminished sound because (his framing) the tune's turnaround already has this half-step-up-diminished character baked in, so diminished vocabulary keeps recurring naturally through the tune rather than feeling bolted on. He also names **Lydian dominant / tritone-substitution color** as fitting the same spot ("the altered sound and the tritone-sub sound are very related") and separately calls out the **altered scale** generally as good for "getting away" cleanly from a chord — deliberately dissonant/outside material that the ear still parses as inside because of how it resolves. → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Altered Chords]]
- **Am7 (as a substitute tonic / vi chord) gets the same vocabulary as the Cmaj7 tonic — no separate treatment needed**, because functionally Am7 reads as a rootless/related extension of the tonic sound (his direct statement: "there's really no real difference"). Whether it's heard as a new tonic or a vi with a 6th, the practical material used doesn't change. → [[Chord Substitution]] § *Diatonic substitution*
- **Am7 → D7 (dominant of the dominant): use notes that are NOT in the previous chord as targets, and avoid landing on the root over the bass.** General device stated plainly: pick target notes that contrast against what just sounded (rather than simply continuing chord tones), and specifically avoid the plain root when it would double the bass note in unison — "it doesn't sound so nice." The 9th of the following chord is named as a good, smooth transition target here. → [[Guide Tones]]
- **D7 (V7/V) — Lydian dominant is the default color choice for a dominant-of-the-dominant that sits for a real stretch, matching the general rule already on [[Scale Modes Reference]].** Also demonstrated: a **melodic minor pass** (naming a G♯7♭5 arpeggio/shape drawn from the parent scale as one of his go-to colors within it). → [[Scale Modes Reference]] § Lydian Dominant, [[Scale Modes Reference]] § Melodic Minor
- **D7 → triad pairs: the D and E major triads, mixed and alternated rather than played as two separate runs.** Explicitly identified as a genuine triad *pair* (not a plain arpeggio exercise) because — despite superficially looking like they'd share no common notes — the two triads do share notes and still work well combined as melody-generating material; recommends deliberately randomizing/mixing the order they're combined in rather than always running low-to-high. → [[Triad Pairs]]
- **D7 alternate colors, named as options rather than a single "correct" answer:** a **D7♭5** reading, and a separate **E7 altered** reading treated as usable over the same spot ("you can also look at that as ... the E7 altered"). Presented as "there's not a whole lot more to talk about — any minor 2-5 you want to check out, you've got the basic vocabulary from the first ii-V" — i.e. this D7 is functionally just another dominant-of-a-minor-chord spot, reusable vocabulary from earlier in the tune. → [[Altered Chords]]
- **E7 (later in the form) — third-shell voicing used as a soloing anchor, plus altered-dominant color.** Names a **shell voicing rooted on the 3rd** as the reference shape for orienting the line (an F major 7 shell shape used as the reference point), then treats E7 itself with an altered-dominant pass. Separately, a **half-diminished/diminished-scale pass** is used elsewhere on the dominant chords in this stretch because the tune's own turnaround already leans on that half-step-up-diminished color (see the A♭maj7→Am7 bullet above) — described as "wanting to use diminished a lot" throughout the piece for that reason. → [[Chord Voicings]] § shell voicings, [[Diminished Chords]]
- **G7 (final turnaround dominant) — triad pairs again, this time out of the *whole-tone-adjacent* diminished/dominant scale rather than melodic minor**, naming the triads available as (read from context, transcript garbled) roughly a set including a **D♭ major triad** and further triad-pair combinations drawn from that scale, explicitly flagged as **not the standard augmented/major triad-pair type** used earlier — a genuinely different triad-pair family (a diminished triad plus a major triad, rather than two major/minor triads a scale-step apart). → [[Triad Pairs]], [[Diminished Chords]]
- **Closing turnaround line (C→A♭7 stretch) — the same half-step-planing device from earlier in the tune reused explicitly as the closing vocabulary.** Larsen states directly he reuses the same "move up a half step" idea from the C→B♭7 spot here, moving material up a half step and resolving down, closing the loop on the tune's own repeated half-step-planing character. Practice method demonstrated live: play the line slowly in tempo, aiming for one target note per chord, gradually filling in connective material once the "skeleton" targets are solid — the same target-note practice principle stated at the top of the video, applied concretely to the hardest transition in the form. → [[Half-Step Pentatonic Shift Over Moving Changes]], [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]
