# Songs That Only Use 3 Chords

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v0i-VYHHk4A)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v0i-VYHHk4A)

## Synopsis

Not just any 3-chord songs — specifically songs built entirely from the SAME three chords (I, IV, V), framed as the "primary colors of music" and tied to the "three chords and the truth" songwriting philosophy where simple harmony deliberately gets out of the way of the lyrics.

## Techniques

- **I-IV-V as functionally identical across every key** — the reason to think in Roman numerals rather than letter names: the same 1-4-5 relationship sounds the same whether it's C-F-G, E-A-B, or F-B♭-C, because the numbers name each chord's function in the key, not its pitch.
- **"Three chords and the truth" as a genre-linked songwriting philosophy** (phrase credited to country songwriter Harlan Howard): styles that prioritize lyrics/storytelling over harmonic sophistication — country, folk, and punk — lean heavily on I-IV-V precisely because simple, functionally-complete harmony doesn't compete with the words for attention. Cited examples: Motown's "My Girl," Pulp's "Common People" (explicitly a storytelling song).
- **Disguised simplicity via a mid-song key change.** Paul Simon's "Diamonds on the Soles of Her Shoes" uses I-IV-V in E (E-A-B), then shifts up a semitone to F (F-B♭-C) for a later section — on paper this looks like a chord-rich, sophisticated progression, but it's the identical simple I-IV-V pattern just transposed, not new harmonic material.
- **The same trick works in minor, with a harmonic-minor variant available on V.** Natural minor i-iv-v (all three chords minor): Gorillaz's "Feel Good Inc." Harmonic-minor variant — raise the 7th degree specifically to turn the v chord major (a "quite classical" sound) while i and iv stay minor: Muse's "Uno."
