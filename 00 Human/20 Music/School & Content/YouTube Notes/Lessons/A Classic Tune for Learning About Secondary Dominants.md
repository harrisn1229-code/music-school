# A Classic Tune for Learning About Secondary Dominants

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2X2MS4TBFUU)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2X2MS4TBFUU)

## Synopsis
Jack Ruch teaches a single tune — Ray Charles's arrangement of "You Don't Know Me," in the key of E♭ major — end to end, using it as a vehicle for one specific soloing device: a movable diminished-7 arpeggio lick for outlining **functioning dominant chords**, both the primary V7 (B♭7) and every secondary dominant in the progression (E♭7 → A♭, C7 → Fm, G7 → Cm). He plays the tune down first with chord names on screen so it can be learned by ear/eye, then names and locates every functioning dominant in it, teaches one diminished-arpeggio lick built off the chord's own 3rd, and drills moving that single lick across all four dominant spots plus a diminished passing chord (A♭ → A°). The back half of the lesson is about **integration, not just vocabulary**: he plays a plain E♭ major pentatonic solo through the changes as a template, then rebuilds it substituting the diminished lick in one dominant spot at a time (C7→Fm, then G7→Cm, then E♭7→A♭), before playing a full solo blending pentatonic, bluesy minor phrasing, and the diminished lick together — a concrete demonstration of how a single harmonic-analysis device becomes usable, musical vocabulary rather than a party trick played over its own backing track in isolation. Most valuable here: naming the general principle ("be on the lookout for where functioning dominant chords appear in other songs") while grounding every step in one real, specific tune rather than an abstract example — the same "identify → drill in isolation → blend into a solo template" arc Jack Ruch uses across his catalog (see [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]).

## What's taught
- **The tune and its key** — Ray Charles's arrangement of "You Don't Know Me" (the Eddy Arnold/Cindy Walker standard), key of **E♭ major**, played through once at the top with on-screen chord names as a play-along reference. *(0:00)*
- **Primary vs. secondary dominant, defined plainly:** the **primary dominant** is the V7 of the key you're in — here, E♭ major's V7 is **B♭7**. A **secondary dominant** is a non-diatonic chord acting as the V7 of some *other* chord in the progression, used to build tension that resolves into that chord. → [[Secondary Dominants]]
- **The tune's secondary dominants, named and mapped:**
  - **E♭7 → A♭ major** — E♭7 is **V7/IV** (the dominant of A♭, the IV chord of E♭ major). Adding the ♭7 (D♭) to the E♭ chord is what makes it pull — that D♭ voice-leads down and resolves to G, the major 3rd of A♭.
  - **C7 → F minor** — **V7/ii** (F minor is the ii of E♭ major).
  - **G7 → C minor** — **V7/vi** (C minor is the vi of E♭ major).
  - Explicit general point: a secondary dominant **still functions as a dominant even when it resolves to a *minor* chord** — the ii and vi targets above are both minor, and the V7/x relationship holds regardless.
- **A diminished-7 arpeggio lick built off the chord's own 3rd, for outlining any functioning dominant.** Worked over **B♭7 → E♭ major**: start on the **3rd** (D), ascend a diminished-7 arpeggio from there — **D → F → A♭ → B** (3rd–5th–♭7–[a leading tone functioning like a raised 9th/♭9-ish upper tone over the dominant]) — then work back down (**B → B♭ → A♭**) to the **root/♭7 area**, and slide/resolve into **G, the 3rd of the target E♭ major chord**. → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]
- **The lick is movable — same shape, relocated to every functioning dominant in the tune:**
  - B♭7 → E♭ (the primary dominant resolution)
  - E♭7 → A♭ (V7/IV)
  - C7 → F minor (V7/ii)
  - G7 → C minor (V7/vi)
  - **A♭ → A° (a diminished passing chord)** — not a functioning dominant, but addressed with "a similar line" since it's already a diminished sound and there's a long enough space in the progression to use it, treated as a bonus fifth spot for the same device family. → [[Diminished Chords]] § applied/passing diminished
- **Two-stage integration method, demonstrated live rather than just described:**
  1. **Isolation pass** — play the diminished lick, alone, over the backing track at each of the five spots in turn, purely to "identify where these cadences happen and how we can use these licks."
  2. **Template pass** — play a plain solo using only the **E♭ major pentatonic scale** through the whole form as a starting template, then **rebuild the same solo three times**, substituting the diminished lick into one dominant spot per pass: first at C7→Fm, then at G7→Cm, then at E♭7→A♭ — teaching the lick as an *insertion* into existing solo material rather than a stand-alone phrase.
  3. **Full blend** — a final solo combining E♭ major pentatonic phrases, "minor-y bluesy" phrasing, and the diminished lick across multiple spots and multiple positions on the fretboard, framed explicitly as the payoff "toolbox" for soloing over functioning dominant chords.
- **Generalization callout** — the closing advice is to listen for functioning dominant chords (primary or secondary) in *other* tunes being learned, since this exact lick and approach transfers directly to any V7-resolving-somewhere progression, not just this tune.
