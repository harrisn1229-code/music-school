## What's taught

- **Chord→arpeggio→scale build-up (restated, see linked videos above).** E minor triad → add a doubled note for a 3-note arpeggio shape → add the ♭7 for a 7th arpeggio → add 3 more notes for the full scale, all at one fretboard position, so chord/arpeggio/scale are seen as one connected object rather than separate topics.
- **Per-chord arpeggio targeting with octave extension (restated).** Over a B♭m–C°–D♭–E♭m riff, play each chord's own root-3-5 (then add the 7th for level 2) instead of one scale over everything; extend any arpeggio shape to cover more of the neck by moving its notes to the next octave rather than looking up bigger shapes.
- **Pentatonic + one characteristic note = a fast, practical way into modal color.** Instead of jumping straight to learning a full 7-note mode shape, add just that mode's one characteristic interval to a pentatonic scale already known — worked example: C major pentatonic (C D E G A) plus the ♯4 gives a "pentatonic Lydian hybrid" that captures Lydian's characteristic brightness without learning the full Lydian scale shape. States the same trick generalizes to Dorian, Phrygian, and Mixolydian, each via their own one characteristic note added to a pentatonic base — framed as a way to learn a mode's *sound/color* first, before its full fingering.
- **Favor color tones over root/5th when choosing a solo melody's note centers.** A/B comparison over the same backing: take 1 centers melodic ideas on the root and perfect 5th — "the most boring notes possible" — take 2 centers on the major 2nd, minor 3rd, perfect 4th, major 6th, and minor 7th instead, and reads as clearly more melodic/sophisticated. General rule: consciously visualize which scale degree you're landing on relative to the underlying chord, and default away from root/5th for melodic interest (matches [[The 6 WORST Guitar Solo MISTAKES! (Fix These Bad Habits TODAY)]]'s non-root-centering fix, here extended with a fuller list of preferred color tones).
- **Spread chord voicings wide across the neck and strings for a richer sound, with no new theory required.** A/B comparison: a normal close-voiced chord progression vs. the same progression re-voiced with notes spread across more strings/frets (wider intervals between adjacent chord tones) — described as dramatically more interesting-sounding from the identical harmonic content, achievable without deep chord-extension knowledge.
- **Stack 7th/9th "jazz chords" onto a rock/metal power-chord riff for tension and dissonance.** Starting from a plain power-chord (root-5th) riff foundation, progressively add the 3rd, 7th, and a 9th tension to build 4-note (root-3-5-7) and denser voicings — explicitly recommends this "jazz chord" vocabulary as underused in rock/metal, where it reads as unusual dissonance/tension rather than a jazz signifier. Framed as a gradual layering process: start from the plain power-chord foundation, add detail incrementally.
