# Getting Better — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

A deliberate step down in intensity after the preceding track, and Pollack is explicit that its job is **pacing the album side** — relaxing tension between peaks without letting the pot go cold. The analytical interest is entirely in the arrangement and in a McCartney specialty: **long phrases built over pedal points** ([[POP-C03 — Pedal point]]). Tune and chords are pan-diatonic; nothing outside the key appears anywhere, and everything is treated as consonant with everything else.

**Form.** Elusive on purpose. Pollack parses the first refrain as slightly short and the later refrains as followed by a bridge, but you could as reasonably call the bridge an extension of the refrain — the dividing lines genuinely blur. The final verse inserts an extra bar between its two halves.

**Harmony.** C major with its centre of gravity pulled toward V by how much airtime the dominant gets. The intro is a **plagal cadence** ([[HAR-C03 — Plagal cadence]]) with a jazzy ninth produced by a sustained octave rather than by a chord choice. The verse sits on a **G pedal**, and Pollack's advice there is to stop transcribing verticals: the whole section is *experienced* as one elaborate V chord. Where a bass note sustains, the chords above it matter less than the envelope that bass note creates. The last chord sounded is I, but the octave chiming on into the fade implies an open ending on V, and the next track's intro exploits the ambiguity.

**Arrangement.** Thick with overdubs but texturally spare, and patterned by department ([[ARR-C04 — Arrangement interest is scheduled absence]]). The **chiming octave** is present in the intro, every refrain, both bridges and the outro, and **absent from every verse** — that is the entire rule, and it's what makes the verses feel different. Percussion accumulates by section: cymbal slashes on a syncopated half-beat, then four-in-the-bar, then handclaps, then tabla, with the outro running the layering in reverse until only the tabla is left. Verse 1 carries the most complicated vocal arrangement in the song and verse 2 deliberately does something simpler, because a literal repeat would have cost more than it gained.
