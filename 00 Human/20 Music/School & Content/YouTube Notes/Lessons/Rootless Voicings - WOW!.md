## Synopsis

Episode 22 of Jared Borkowski's "chord theory and chord techniques" series (soundguitarlessons.com, Seattle) — a theory-first lesson on **rootless voicings**: the idea that any chord shape's root doesn't have to be a note you're actually fretting, and that a single physical shape can therefore be re-labeled as several different chords depending on where you decide the (unplayed) root is. Jared works three progressively bigger derivation examples (triads as the upper 3 notes of a 7th chord, 7th chords as the upper 4 notes of a 9 chord, and directly replacing a root with the 9th above or the 7th below), then spends the back half on the video's real payoff — the half-diminished chord shape used as a "favorite example" of a single grip supporting **six different chord identities** depending on assumed root — worked into a full chord progression and a pop-songwriting example. Closes with a caveat concept (ambiguous chords that resist any single root/label) and a practical ear-training exercise. This is a **theory-heavy but fully guitar-fretboard-worked** lesson — same "root droppable, one shape many identities" territory the vault already documents extensively via Jack Ruch, Jens Larsen, and Nathan Borton (see Related below), but Jared's own contribution is the *replace-the-root-with-a-neighbor-tone* mechanical trick and the unusually rich six-way half-diminished-shape example, both new to this vault's coverage. ## What's taught

**Core framing:** any chord always has a root in theory, but it doesn't have to be a note you're playing — the most common reason to drop it is a bass player already covering it (freeing a finger for colour), but rootless voicings are legitimate to use any time, bass player or not. The big idea: the *same* physical shape can be legitimately interpreted as several different chords depending on which of its notes you decide is (or implies) the root.

**1. Triads = the upper 3 notes of a 7th chord.** Rule: the root of a 7th chord that contains a given triad as its upper structure sits **two scale steps (three notes) below** the triad's own root, in the same key/scale.
- Worked example: open **A minor** triad (root-♭3-5) → two scale steps down from A (in C major/A minor) = **F** → play F as the root under the same A-minor shape and it's a familiar **F major 7** grip (root-7-3-5). So the Am triad shape *is* the upper 3 notes of Fmaj7.
- Worked example: **D major** triad, 5th position → two scale steps down = **B** → same triad shape over B = a common **B minor 7** voicing.
- Matches the vault's existing four-rule table on [[Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords]] exactly (major7 → minor triad a major 3rd above the root; minor7 → major triad a minor 3rd above the root) — a third independent source confirming the same rule table, reached via the "two scale steps down" framing rather than that page's "3-5-7 stack" framing.

**2. 7th chords = the upper 4 notes of a rootless 9 chord.** Generalizes the same logic one chord size up: stack a scale's tonic 9th chord as 1-3-5-7-9; drop the 1 (root) and the remaining 3-5-7-9 are exactly the four notes of the **iii chord** of that key (a minor 7 chord).
- Worked example: **C major 9**, rootless → the iii of C is **E minor 7** → an Em7 grip played over (or without) a C bass sounds like Cmaj9 with no root (3-5-7-9 of C = E-G-B-D).
- **Soloing application, stated directly:** arpeggiate the iii-minor-7 (Em7) over the I chord (Cmaj7) for a "fresher sound" than the plain 1-3-5-7 arpeggio of the I chord itself.
- New to the vault's coverage — extends [[Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords]]'s existing triad-from-7th-chord rule one level up to 7th-chord-from-9-chord.

**3. Direct root-replacement trick: swap the root for the 9th (whole step above) or the 7th (whole step below).** Rather than recalculating from a rule table, take a known triad or dominant-7 shape, identify its root, and physically replace it with the diatonic neighbor tone a whole step away:
- **F major triad** (a "D-shape"-style fretting, played where it sounds F major; 3rd on top, root, 5th) → replace the root with the **major 7th** (whole step below) → a rootless **F major 7** voicing (3-7-5) — and the resulting shape is *physically identical* to an A minor triad shape, the same Fmaj7/Am relationship as example 1 reached a second way.
- **G7 chord, first inversion** (open G string as the root) → replace the root with the **9th** (whole step above, A) → the shape becomes a **half-diminished chord shape in root position**, reinterpreted as a rootless **G9** (bottom to top: 3rd-♭7-9th-5th).
- **Stacked with the "5th is droppable/replaceable too" idea** (already on [[Chord Voicings]]): take that same rootless G9 and replace its 5th (the top note) with the **13th** (whole step above the 5th) → a **G13** with no root and no 5th (3-♭7-9-13) — the same voicing is playable an octave up on the top four strings.

**4. The half-diminished shape as a "favorite example" — one grip, six chord identities.** A root-position half-diminished chord shape on the top four strings (root-♭5-♭7-♭3, worked as A♭/G♯ half-diminished) can be re-labeled as **six different chords** purely by choosing a different one of its four notes as the (possibly unplayed) root:
- Its own root → plain **half-diminished** (m7♭5).
- A different note as root → **minor 6** chord.
- Another note as root → **dominant 9** chord.
- Another → **dominant 7 ♭13 ♭9**.
- The *same* root as the previous reading, but reinterpreting the "♭13" note as a **♯5** instead → **dominant 7 ♯5 ♭9** — same notes, same assumed root, different scale/label (stated as an important distinction because it changes which scale you'd solo from, even though nothing physically changes).
- The remaining note as root → **major 13 ♯11** (equivalently maj7♯11).
- **Worked as a full progression, holding the identical top voicing while only the bass/assumed-root moves:** shell voicings first — Dmaj7 → Bm6 → E7 → B♭7 → Amaj7 — then the *same held top-note shape* reread against each moving root: **D major 13♯11 → B minor 6 → E9 → B♭7(♭13,♭9) → resolve to A major 7**. Demonstrates that a chord progression can sound like real harmonic motion in the bass while the guitar's rootless voicing on top never physically changes.
- **Ear-training exercise, stated directly:** hold a rootless voicing and try to *sing* the unplayed root — there's no reference pitch to latch onto, so it forces hearing the root purely in relationship to the voicing, "one of the best ways to actually internalize the real harmonic progression."

**5. Pop-songwriting application: a static triad shape held over a moving chord progression underneath.** Arpeggiate an **A minor triad** and hold that exact shape/idea constant while the implied harmony underneath cycles through five different chords that each legitimately contain the Am triad: **Am** (plain) → **F♯ half-diminished** (contains Am) → **F major 7** (contains Am, per example 3 above) → **C major 6** (this specific voicing of C6 contains Am) → **D minor** (Am's root becomes D minor's ♭7 → makes it Dm7; Am's 5th becomes D minor's 9th → makes it Dm9). **Arrangement simplification given directly:** rather than playing the full rich chord underneath, just play the **1st and 3rd** of each chord in the sequence — a two-note fragment repeated identically while the harmony underneath moves — described as producing a repetitive, "energy build" feel under a moving progression. A concrete, worked instance of "one small shape, many harmonic contexts," aimed at composition rather than jazz comping.

**6. Ambiguous chords — a stated caveat/stopping point, not a failure mode.** Some incomplete voicings (most often missing the 3rd, so major/minor can't be determined) genuinely don't resolve to one clear chord label no matter which note is assumed to be the root. Worked example: a shape where (a) the bottom note as root gives ♭9+5 with no 3rd; (b) a different note as root gives major-7+♭5; (c) the *unplayed* note as root gives a rootless reading with ♭9, 5, and ♯11 — none of the three readings is a complete, unambiguous chord. Jared's explicit advice: recognizing "this is ambiguous" is a legitimate analytical endpoint, not something to force a label onto — context (key, direction of resolution) can sometimes narrow it down, but in a vacuum it's fine to just note what tensions are present and move on.
