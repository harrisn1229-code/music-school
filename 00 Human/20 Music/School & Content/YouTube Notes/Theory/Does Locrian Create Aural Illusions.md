# Does Locrian Create Aural Illusions?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mhDxvNY9J68)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mhDxvNY9J68)

## Synopsis

A direct follow-up to [[A Song That Actually Uses Locrian]], responding to viewers who heard "Army of Me" as B♭ Aeolian rather than C Locrian. Frames the disagreement as a genuine perceptual "aural illusion" (same notes, two valid root readings, analogous to a visual optical illusion), then delivers the payoff the previous video was still looking for: a real song that stays committed to Locrian throughout.

## Techniques

- **The "same notes, different root" ambiguity, generalized beyond this one song**: C Locrian and B♭ Aeolian are both modes of the same parent scale (D♭ major) — identical note content, differing only in which note the listener's ear locks onto as "home." Demonstrated with an A/B test: the same bassline played once with a C drone underneath, once with a B♭ drone, asking the listener which reading feels resolved. Corroborated as a real, non-idiosyncratic disagreement by two independent published sources (musicologist Ethan Hein's write-up, Allan Moore's *Song Means*) both hearing the song as Locrian, same as the presenter — not a fringe reading. **General mechanism for why Locrian specifically is prone to this**: because Locrian's tonic triad is diminished (no perfect 5th), a Locrian passage often "wants" to resolve toward the major key it's derived from (here, C major), which has a far stronger sense of tonic — making the *intended* Locrian root perceptually unstable even when the composer meant it as the root.
- **"Dust to Dust" (John Kirkpatrick) — a full worked example of a song that STAYS in Locrian throughout, with two concrete avoidance techniques that make it possible.** (1) **Never voice the note a perfect 5th above the root** — for B Locrian, that means never using G (the perfect 5th of C major, the "gravity well" key Locrian naturally wants to slip into); hearing G would start pulling the ear toward C major and away from B Locrian, so the composer avoids it entirely as "avoiding an important piece of C major." (2) **No chords at all — harmony emerges purely from the interaction of independent melodic lines**, deliberately sidestepping the fact that Locrian's tonic triad (B diminished) itself wants to resolve to C major; voicing an actual B° chord would likely pull the piece out of Locrian on its own. Thematically apt: the song is about grave-digging/death, and the unresolved, "spooky" Locrian sound is used deliberately to match the lyric.
- **A useful category-clarifying framing for Locrian's place among the modes**: still a genuine mode meeting every technical criterion, but functionally the outlier of the set (a commenter's analogy, endorsed by the presenter: "Locrian is to the other modes what Pluto is to the planets") — useful shorthand for why Locrian gets disproportionate attention despite being the least usable mode in practice.
