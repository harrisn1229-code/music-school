# Killer Bass Lines Made Easy with the Pentatonic Scale

## Synopsis

A BassBuzz lesson that picks up exactly where the channel's scale-shape teaching ([[7 Days to Learning Bass Scales (Beginner Lesson)]] / [[Bass Scale Shapes — Major, Minor, Pentatonic & Blues]]) leaves off: knowing a scale shape's five notes isn't the same as being able to write a good riff with them — "that's like buying a bunch of meat and veg with no recipe and expecting to be the Iron Chef." Running a cooking metaphor start to finish, the video packages riff *composition* into a small, repeatable recipe (start on the root, favor sequential motion, add variation for a longer phrase, and — once those are solid — stretch the shape's range with a copy-paste-octave move) and teaches it entirely through real riffs rather than abstract rules: "Bombtrack" (Rage Against the Machine) for the base three-step recipe in minor pentatonic, "August 10" (Khruangbin, via bassist Laura Lee) for the fourth step and a second hand shape, "My Girl" (The Temptations) for major pentatonic, and an unnamed major-pentatonic riff credited to bassist "Deacy" for a blues-derived, slide-driven finish. The video's second major thread, woven through the same examples, is a choice most scale-shape lessons skip entirely: the same five pentatonic notes can be fretted in a **static shape** (one hand position, wider stretch) or a **shifting shape** (two hand positions, tighter reach, and the only one of the two that can set up a slide) — and which one to use is dictated by what the specific riff needs, not a fixed rule. Laura Lee's own playing approach (fretting with only three fingers because her ring and pinky are physically glued together, which pushes her toward the shifting shape's more relaxed span) is used as the credibility anchor for why the shifting shape matters, not just a theoretical alternative. No plugins, effects, or production content anywhere — pure instrument/theory/technique, same lane as its sibling scale-shapes video.

## What's taught

- **[~0:00–1:00] The pentatonic scale as five ingredients, not a finished dish.** Reframes "I learned the scale shapes" as necessary but not sufficient — the video's whole premise is that the *recipe* for combining five notes into a riff is the missing piece most scale lessons (including BassBuzz's own prior one) don't cover.
- **[~1:00–2:30] Step 1 of the recipe: start on the root note.** The riff's starting note should be the scale shape's root — it anchors the listener's ear to the key/context. Demonstrated first via the shape itself (a static-shape run that "should instantly" let a beginner sound like "Bombtrack").
- **[~2:30–4:00] Steps 2–3, taught directly on "Bombtrack" (minor pentatonic, static shape).** Step 2: favor **sequential** scale motion — "never jumping beyond the next note in the shape in either direction" — for a flowing, non-angular sound; explicitly flagged as a foolproofing default, not a hard rule. Step 3: **longer phrases via variation** — repeat the riff with a small change every other time (bar 2 varies from bar 1, bar 4 varies again) instead of looping the exact same bar four times, which the video calls out as **the single trick beginners miss most** for beating repetitive-sounding riffs.
- **[~4:00–5:20] Applied fix-a-bad-riff demo, minor pentatonic.** A deliberately "kind of crappy" improvised riff starting on G is run through steps 1–3 live — anchor on root, reorganize toward sequential motion, then vary every-other-repeat — audibly improving each step, with no new notes or theory introduced.
- **[~5:20–6:30] Static shape vs. shifting shape, introduced via Laura Lee (Khruangbin).** Names the shape already taught ("static shape") for the first time and contrasts it with a second option some riffs specifically require. Laura Lee, quoted directly: she frets with only three working fingers (index, middle, pinky) because her ring and pinky fingers are physically attached together, which pushes her away from the static shape's ~4-fret root-to-next-note stretch and toward a more relaxed **~3-fret span**, which in her words "creates a limitation" but "for me it's fun and bouncy."
- **[~5:20–6:30, fingering] The shifting shape's mechanics.** Pinky frets the root, index-pinky grab the next two notes, then the hand **shifts up two frets** to finish the remaining scale notes — two hand positions instead of the static shape's one. Called out as unlocking **slides** the static shape structurally can't reach, because the shifting shape's position-change motion doubles as a slide's physical travel; a note that requires this move sits on a different string in the static shape, with "nowhere to slide to."
- **[~6:30–7:00] Step 4 of the recipe: the copy-paste octave technique, via "August 10" (Khruangbin/Laura Lee), minor pentatonic shifting shape.** Take one or two notes already in the scale shape and replay them **one octave higher** — reached on bass by moving **two strings up and two frets over** for the same note name — to add melodic range beyond the shape's drawn "box" while staying fully inside the scale (same note name, same harmonic vibe). The riff's four-bar length plus this move is what the video credits for pushing a riff toward "Khruangbin level coolness."
- **[~7:00–9:00] Applied fix-a-bad-riff demo, minor pentatonic shifting shape.** A second "crappy" starting riff is run through the full recipe: root/sequential check first, then a **switch from static to shifting shape** because a move in the riff isn't landing cleanly on the static shape's strings, then the copy-paste octave technique layered on top, plus a lengthened phrase — the fullest worked demo of all four steps together in the video.
- **[~9:00–10:35] Major pentatonic, via "My Girl" (The Temptations, 1964; guitar riff originally by Robert White of the Motown Funk Brothers).** Static shape, fingered starting on the middle finger. Notably runs on **only a two-step recipe** — root note + sequential motion, no built-in phrase variation — and would get repetitive on a straight loop, except the actual song solves that a different way than "Bombtrack" did: guitarist Robert White **moves the whole riff to a new starting root note every bar** to track the chord progression, rather than varying the riff's internal notes.
- **[~10:35–11:30] Major pentatonic is forgiving — root note alone mostly works.** Demonstrated live: an intentionally-attempted "bad" riff in C major pentatonic starting on the root still "kind of sounds fine" — the video's explicit takeaway that major pentatonic's main failure mode isn't a bad note choice, it's **using the wrong shape** for a given riff (see next point), not a bad recipe application.
- **[~11:30–13:20] Major pentatonic shifting shape, via the "Deacy"-credited riff.** A riff described as adapted from "a super common blues pattern" and credited to a bassist referred to only as "Deacy" in the transcript (near-certainly **John Deacon of Queen**, based on the phrasing and slide-driven pentatonic style, though the video doesn't name the source song on-camera — treat this attribution as a strong inference). Demonstrated failing outright in the static shape ("super awkward," loses "those cool slidey sounds"), then working cleanly in the shifting shape (index-pinky fretting, Laura Lee's relaxed hand again). Mechanically: starts on the root, plays mostly sequential notes but **deliberately skips the second note of the shape**, and its signature hook is a **bass slide built into the position shift itself** — only reachable via the shifting shape. Closing tip: **hammer-ons and pull-offs get "a similar effect"** to the slide when a full slide isn't wanted or possible.
- **[~13:20–end] Closing framing + practice plug.** Every classic pentatonic riff learned adds to a personal "recipe collection" that improves future riff-writing over time; points to a 10-minute daily fretting/plucking/speed practice routine (external link, not detailed on-screen) to make sure technique keeps pace with more ambitious riff ideas.
