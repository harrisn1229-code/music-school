## Synopsis

Video #18 in Jared Borkowski's (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared) top-25-jazz-standards series, covering "Body and Soul" — Johnny Green's 1929 ballad, one of the most-recorded jazz songs ever (~3,000 versions), landmark-ized by Coleman Hawkins' 1939 recording, which largely abandoned the written melody in favor of extended improvisation over the tune's dense harmony and helped establish the tenor sax as a leading jazz voice. The lesson is dense specifically because the tune is dense: frequent 2-5-1s, a key change in the bridge, and a lead sheet full of altered-dominant symbols that reward a strong default interpretation rather than agonizing over "the one correct" reading. Two ideas anchor the video and generalize well beyond this tune: a plain-language walkthrough of tritone substitution (any dominant a half-step above a target can resolve into it, and you can freely insert one in front of *any* chord as decoration, understood or not), and a "default to harmonic minor with implied ♭9/♭13" habit for any dominant resolving to a minor chord, applied even when the lead sheet doesn't spell it out. A comping tip demonstrates that walking-chords idea live across a real passage, and a voicing-reuse observation in the bridge (a rootless Dmaj9 shape and an Em7 shape being physically identical) continues the "same shape, different chord identity" theme already documented from earlier videos in this channel.

## What's taught

- **Song background.** Composed 1929, Johnny Green (music), Edward Heyman/Robert Sour/Frank Eyton (lyrics), written for Gertrude Lawrence, introduced in London 1930. Louis Armstrong recorded it first (Oct 1930); Coleman Hawkins' 1939 recording (largely improvised over the harmony rather than the melody) is the landmark version and is called out as one of the presenter's all-time favorite recordings, listened to "hundreds if not thousands of times." Typically performed in D♭ major, 32-bar AABA, harmonically dense with frequent 2-5-1s and an unusual bridge modulation.
- **Key-signature shortcut: with multiple flats, the second-to-last flat in the key signature names the key.** A quick sight-reading habit demonstrated live on this tune's D♭ (5-flat) key signature.
- **Default-to-harmonic-minor practice for any dominant resolving to a minor chord, applied regardless of the written chord symbol.** The tune's 2nd chord is technically "the 5 of 2" and, compared strictly to the parent-scale, would carry a *natural* 9 rather than a ♭9 — but the stated personal default, applied almost everywhere in practice, is to treat any dominant-to-minor resolution as coming from that minor's harmonic-minor scale and add the implied ♭9/♭13 whether or not the lead sheet spells them out, "because that's what it's wanting in my mind." Natural-9 (from melodic minor instead) sounds equally good — the point isn't that one is objectively correct, it's picking *one* reliable default so you're not paralyzed by options mid-tune. → [[Altered Chords]] § *From videos*
- **Tritone substitution, explained without jargon, plus a decorative reharmonization exercise built on it.** A dominant 7th a half-step above any target chord can always resolve down into it (because it's the tritone-away chord of the "real" V) — you don't need to know or explain *why* to use it. Concrete drill: insert a dominant-7-a-half-step-above (as a full chord, or just its root note) in front of every chord of a passage, even where nothing is written — worked live across the tune's first four bars, walking a half-step-above chord into each target. Explicitly framed as usable both in comping (adding harmonic embellishment/density players enjoy) and in lead playing (superimposing it as a passing color even if no one else is playing it). → [[Chord Substitution]] § *Tritone substitution*
- **Chromatic bass motion is a deliberate composed feature, not incidental — and worth always playing even solo.** The tune's slash chords create a stepwise descending bass line (root → down a whole step to the ♭7 in the bass → down another half step) underneath otherwise-static or slow-moving harmony; described as a favorite device to always voice explicitly, with or without a bassist present, "because I just love that intentionality." → [[Chromatic Bass Lines]] § *From videos*
- **Major 6th and Major 7th chords off the same root are treated as fully interchangeable throughout the tune** — if the chart says major 6, major 7 works and vice versa; they carry genuinely different colors/moods, and taste develops for when to reach for which, but neither reading is ever "wrong." → [[Seventh Chords]] § *From videos*
- **Bridge voicing shape-reuse: a rootless, first-inversion Dmaj9 voicing and an Em7 (ii of D) voicing use the identical physical shape.** The bridge moves Dmaj7 → Em7 (I → ii of D); the Em7 grip taught (3rd-7th-root-5th on adjacent strings) is physically the same shape as a rootless, first-inversion Dmaj9 (3rd-7th-9th-5th) — meaning it barely matters whether you mentally label that bar "Em7" or "D major, colored," since the physical execution and the sound are nearly identical either way. Framed as one instance of a broader principle taught in the channel's "Jazz Comping Mastery" course: a large fraction of usable rootless/inverted voicings are shapes already known from a *different* chord, simply reused. → [[Chord Voicings]] § *From videos*
- **Comping tip, extended demonstration.** The half-step-above-walking-dominant idea above, played through a longer passage at tempo, sparse-to-dense per taste — reharmonizing on the fly, described as feeling "more interactive and more improvisational" than only playing exactly what was planned.
- **Scales — complete vs. simplified, and picking a scale by the chord's *diatonic role*, not just its quality.** D♭ major over most of the A section; E♭ harmonic minor (or E♭ melodic minor for a natural-9 flavor) over the "5 of 2" dominant discussed above; D major after the bridge's key change; D natural minor for a **backdoor dominant** moment — described explicitly as "like a 2-5 in the key of D" — a bVII-style approach rather than a straight V. → [[Chord Substitution]] § *Backdoor substitution*
- **G♭ Lydian dominant chosen specifically because the chord functions as the IV of D♭, as a dominant 7th** — not a default Mixolydian reach. Reasoning shown explicitly: the 4th mode of a major scale is Lydian; turn that major-quality IV into a dominant 7 and the matching scale becomes Lydian dominant (Mixolydian with a raised 4th/♯11) — a worked example of choosing a chord-scale from the chord's *function in the key*, not its symbol alone. → [[Scale Modes Reference]] § *From videos*
- **Simplified-scales pass:** mostly D♭ major throughout, deliberately ignoring several passing/secondary chords — except the E♭ harmonic minor moment above, kept even in the simplified pass because it's judged too strong a harmonic landmark to skip.
- **Internalization tip: dance around the melody while improvising.** After separately working the melody and some form of scale/chord-tone improvisation, deliberately blend them — play roughly the actual melody, depart into improvisation, then return to "that zone of the melody" — rather than defaulting to generic vocabulary on every tune; makes an improvised solo read as belonging to *this* song specifically.
- **Listening recommendation:** Mary Osborne, "A Girl and Her Guitar" (plus a strong personal endorsement of repeated deep listening to the Coleman Hawkins original).
