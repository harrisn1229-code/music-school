## What's taught

**Step 1 — Truss rod** (sets forward bow in the neck; fixes buzzing or strings sitting too far off the neck):
- *Truss rod test trifecta*: press E string at 1st fret → press E string at 12th fret → measure gap at 7th fret with a business card. Can't slide the card in = neck too straight (loosen). >1–2 cards of clearance = too much forward bow (tighten). 1–2 cards = correct.
- Alternate test: tap the E string against the 7th fret — small click = correct, no click = too straight, lots of click/travel = too much bow.
- Adjuster location varies: headstock, body-end/heel, or (vintage-style necks) inside the neck joint, requiring the neck to be pulled.
- Use the correctly-fitting *thicker* Allen wrench, no wiggle (avoid stripping the nut). De-stick with an initial 1/8 turn, then return to start before making a real adjustment.
- Tighten clockwise (fixes too much bow), loosen counterclockwise (fixes too-straight neck) — 1/8 turn at a time, firm pressure is normal, forcing/large turns is not.
- Always retune and re-measure **in playing position**, never flat on a bench. Truss rod changes take **hours** to fully settle — make small moves, recheck later rather than chasing the number in one sitting.

**Step 2 — Bridge saddle height / action** (the strings' height off the fretboard):
- Target: medium action — easy to fret, but can be plucked hard without rattling.
- Measure from the top of the 12th fret wire to the bottom of the string, fretted/capo'd at the 1st fret. ~2–3 mm (≈3/32") on the E string is a good medium starting point *(general starting point, not a universal spec)*; G string can run slightly lower.
- Inner strings (A, D) sit slightly higher than outer strings (E, G) due to the fingerboard's radius.
- Adjust with the smaller Allen wrench (some bridges need a locking screw loosened first). Clockwise raises the saddle, counterclockwise lowers it — 1/4–1/2 turn at a time, kept even side-to-side.
- Retune after every turn (saddle height changes pitch). Test by plucking every fret medium-hard, pressing with full technique so weak fretting isn't misread as an action problem.
- Do all four strings in order: E, G, then A and D.

**Step 3 — Nut clearance** (open-string buzz/height — diagnosis only, no self-fix):
- Slide a business card between the string and the 1st fret wire with nothing fretted. Slides cleanly = fine. Too tight = slot may be low (only a real problem if the open string actually rattles). Lots of clearance = slot too high, needs re-cutting.
- This is the one step you can diagnose but not fix yourself — re-cutting a nut slot needs special files. The point of checking is knowing whether to take it to a pro, not fixing it in the moment.

**Step 4 — Intonation** (per-string length at the bridge, so fretted notes stay in tune up the neck):
- Comes fourth deliberately — truss rod, action, and nut settings all subtly affect effective string length, so this has to be last of the mechanical checks.
- Check: tune the open string on a real tuner, fret the 12th fret without bending (should read the same note an octave up), also check the 19th fret as a second data point.
- Flat fretted notes → string too long → shorten it (loosen the bridge intonation screw, counterclockwise). Sharp fretted notes → string too short → lengthen it (tighten, clockwise).
- Slack the string slightly while adjusting so the saddle isn't fighting full tension against the bridge (avoids scraping the bridge finish). Tap the screwdriver into the screw head afterward to reseat it.
- Cycle adjust → retune → recheck fretted notes (without bending) until dialed — expect multiple passes per string, repeated across all four strings.

**Step 5 — Pickup height** (tone and volume through the amp):
- Too low = thin, quiet tone. Too high = distortion and popping/clicking as strings hit the pole pieces.
- Manufacturer spec used as a reference starting point on this bass: ~6/64" (E side) / ~5/64" (G side), or ~2.5 mm / ~2 mm — measured pole-piece-top to string-bottom with that string fretted at the last fret. (Not universal — check your own bass's spec.)
- Manual/by-ear method with no spec needed: solo one pickup, play at real intensity (including slap technique if relevant), lower it if distorting/hitting strings, raise it if thin/quiet. Then A/B the two pickups for roughly matched volume, then check the blend.
- **Split (P-style) pickups**: what looks like two pickups is often one pickup shaped to the fingerboard curve — set the inner pole pieces slightly higher than the outer ones for even output across all four strings.

**The blind test:** Geo, blindfolded, is handed the freshly-set-up $300 bass first — genuinely impressed, says he'd gig on it happily, no complaints.
