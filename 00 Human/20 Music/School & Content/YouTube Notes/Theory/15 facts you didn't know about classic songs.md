# 15 facts you didn't know about classic songs

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4jhO16h_N3A)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4jhO16h_N3A)

## Synopsis

A trivia-format survey — most entries are historical/cultural context rather than transferable technique, but two are genuinely reusable recording tricks worth routing: David Bowie's gated-mic "Heroes" vocal technique, and a lo-fi phone-recorded backing vocal.

## Techniques

- **The "Heroes" gated-distance-mic vocal technique (David Bowie, producer Tony Visconti)**: wanting reverb only on the loud parts of Bowie's vocal, not the quiet parts, with just one spare tape track available, Visconti set up three mics at increasing distances from Bowie — a close mic, one roughly 15-20 feet back, and one roughly 50 feet back at the far end of the room — and put a noise gate on the middle and far mics, set to a threshold that only opened when Bowie sang loud enough. The result: quiet singing stays dry (only the close mic passes), and louder singing progressively opens the more distant, more reverberant mics — an in-room reverb "swell" driven entirely by the performer's own dynamics rather than an effect automated afterward. → [[Gate]].
- **A vocal recorded over the phone for a deliberately lo-fi texture, decades before remote collaboration was normal**: Foo Fighters recorded a female backing vocal (from Veruca Salt's Louise Post, on the song this video calls "Avalon" though the described story — Dave Grohl writing about his relationship with Post — matches "Everlong," so the title here may be a video error) by having her sing along to the guitar riff over a telephone line from Chicago while the band tracked in Hollywood — her monitor was literally the phone handset next to the studio monitor, and her performance was mic'd off a second phone line. To tape, with no ability to comp or move anything afterward.
- **The first fuzz-pedal riff on a hit record, originally meant as a placeholder**: Keith Richards' opening riff on The Rolling Stones' "Satisfaction" used the newly-released Maestro FZ-1 (the first commercially available fuzz pedal) — but Richards originally intended the fuzz line as a stand-in for a horn part he imagined replacing it later; the band ultimately kept the fuzz tone instead.
- **A drum part split across two musicians on one kit because it's physically unplayable by one person**: Blur's "Song 2" opening beat is played by drummer Dave Rowntree on the kit while guitarist Graham Coxon simultaneously adds eighth notes on the rim and hits the toms — a beat that reads as a single drummer's performance but requires two people.
- **Percussion substituted with body sound when no drummer/kit was used**: Buddy Holly and the Crickets' "Everyday" has no drum kit — the simple percussive pulse is the band's drummer playing his own knees.
- **Location field recording used as an intro texture, not a studio effect**: the opening sound on Radiohead's "The Bends" is a real tape recording Thom Yorke made outside a US tour hotel, capturing a marching-band instructor's chants — used essentially as found, not recreated in the studio.
- **A demo lyric written to an existing foreign-language track, later reworked into an unrelated hit**: David Bowie's "Life on Mars?" traces back to Bowie writing (rejected) English lyrics for the French song that later became "My Way" (via Paul Anka) — after losing that job to Anka's lyric, Bowie wrote "Life on Mars?" partly as his own answer, and the two songs share a similar descending chord progression shape.
