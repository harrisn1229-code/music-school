## Synopsis
Jack Ruch's ground-zero explainer for diatonic harmony — the theory foundation his other, more advanced videos already in this vault ([[Get The Most Out Of Simple Triad Shapes!]], [[Write Great Chord Progressions and Reharmonize Melodies by Understanding Intervals!]]) assume the viewer already has. He builds the whole system from scratch in the key of C: derive the major scale from its whole-step/half-step recipe, number each scale degree 1–7, stack triads by skipping every other note ("play a note, skip a note") to get the seven diatonic chords, and assign each triad a Roman-numeral-equivalent number whose *quality* (major/minor/diminished) never changes no matter what key you're in. He then re-derives the whole thing in the key of E to prove the pattern is portable, plays a 16-bar chord progression built from all seven E-major diatonic chords on electric guitar using small three-string triad shapes for rhythm, and closes with a short solo that deliberately targets the same triads' chord tones rather than running the E major scale generically. Explicitly frames the number system as the foundation for later shorthand like "a 2-5-1" or "a 1-4-5 blues." Nothing here is new to the vault's existing theory library ([[Chords by Key]] and [[Chord Progressions]] already document the same construction and function system in more depth), but it's a clean worked repetition of the method with a guitar-specific rhythm/lead payoff, and it's the entry point the channel's own harder videos lean on.

## What's taught
- **Derive the major scale from its interval recipe** — whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half — worked from C (no sharps/flats: C D E F G A B) and then again from E (F♯ G♯ A B C♯ D♯ E), proving the recipe is the constant and the note names are what change per key. → [[Scales]]
- **Number each scale degree 1–7 and read chord-name intervals off those numbers** — 1=C, 2=D, 3=E, 4=F, 5=G, 6=A, 7=B; the distance from 1 to 3 is "a third," 1 to 5 is "a fifth" — and those same numbers are what a major 7 chord's "7" or a sus4's "4" refer to. → [[Intervals]]
- **Interval relationships (not note names) are what transposes** — the distance between root and 3rd, or root and 5th, is identical in every key even though the actual notes change; thinking in interval numbers instead of fixed note names is what makes it easy to move a shape to a different key on the fretboard. *(general reinforcement of the same point already on [[Understanding Chords - The Number System]] and [[Circle of Fifths]].)*
- **Build the seven diatonic triads by the skip-a-note method** — play a scale note, skip one, play one, skip one, play one (1-3-5): from C that's C-E-G (C major); from D it's D-F-A (D minor); continuing up the scale gives E-G-B (E minor), F-A-C (F major), G-B-D (G major), A-C-E (A minor), B-D-F (B diminished) — the full C-major diatonic set, matching [[Chords by Key]]'s table exactly. Same "song, skip a note" stacking logic as [[Harmonized Scale Triads for Soloing]]'s skip-a-note derivation, worked here from the plain major scale (Ionian) rather than Mixolydian.
- **The quality pattern per scale degree is fixed in every major key** — 1=major, 2=minor, 3=minor, 4=major, 5=major, 6=minor, 7=diminished, then back to 1 — re-derived from scratch in E major (F♯ G♯ A B C♯ D♯ E → E-major, F♯-minor, G♯-minor, A-major, B-major, C♯-minor, D♯-diminished) to prove it's portable. Matches [[Chords by Key]] and [[Understanding Chords - The Number System]] exactly (major-minor-minor-major-major-minor-diminished).
- **Two practical uses of the full diatonic set** — a songwriting/chord-progression-writing use (a complete menu of every chord that natively fits a chosen key) and a song-analysis use (any chord found in a song that ISN'T in this set is a **borrowed chord** from a different key). → [[Chords by Key]], [[Borrowed Chords]]
- **Three-string triad groupings as the rhythm-guitar go-to for playing along to a track or with other musicians** — rather than full barre or open-position chords, small three-note triad shapes (found in one physical spot on the neck) let a rhythm part "fit into the mix" cleanly; demonstrated moving E major → B major → C♯ minor → A major (I-V-vi... of the E-major progression) as closely-clustered shapes in a single neck position, with room for hammer-ons/pull-offs inside the shapes.
- **Play a full diatonic progression as a 16-bar demonstration** — all seven E-major diatonic chords strung together over a backing track, played with basic bar-chord/open-chord shapes for the full-band version and then re-played with the small triad shapes, to hear how close together (in both key-relatedness and physical neck position) diatonic chords sit.
- **Anchor a solo to the same triads used for rhythm, not the scale in general** — the stated failure mode: "meandering through the notes of the scale" without connecting melodically to the chord currently sounding. Fix: target the chord tones of each underlying triad (the same shapes played for rhythm) as landing points while soloing, rather than treating the whole progression as one undifferentiated E-major-scale run. Same underlying idea, more developed, on [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] and [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]].
- **Names the Nashville-style number shorthand this whole system feeds** — "a 2-5-1" = the two chord (Dm in the key of C) to the five chord (G) to the one chord (C); "a 1-4-5 blues" = the one, four, and five chords by their interval numbers. Direct confirmation of [[Understanding Chords - The Number System]]'s numbers-carry-quality shorthand, from a second, independent source.
