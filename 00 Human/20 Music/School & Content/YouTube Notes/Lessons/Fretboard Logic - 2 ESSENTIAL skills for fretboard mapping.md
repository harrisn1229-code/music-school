# Fretboard Logic - 2 ESSENTIAL skills for fretboard mapping

## Synopsis

Jared Borkowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared), episode 11 of his ongoing chord-theory series, isolates two prerequisite skills for a stated bigger goal — being able to take any chord you can already play and find many other places to play that exact same chord, freely relocating voicings around the neck "without any sort of chord-shape-and-drill memorization exercises." The two skills: (1) the **Closest Octave Exercise**, a full drill for finding the nearest octave of any note in both directions on every string, and (2) **cross-string unison finding via major-scale chunking**, a fret-counting alternative that uses scale-degree "measuring stick" logic (introduced earlier in his series, episodes 2–3) instead of raw fret-counting to locate the identical pitch on a neighboring string. Both are framed explicitly as groundwork — the actual chord-voicing-relocation payoff is deferred to the next video in the series, not yet published/in this vault. ## What's taught

- **Skill 1 — The Closest Octave Exercise.** Starting on the 6th string, find the nearest octave of a fretted note; repeat identically on every string in turn (6th → 1st), noting explicitly that **the octave shape itself changes from string to string** — most sharply across the G(3rd)→B(2nd) string pair because that's the guitar's one non-perfect-4th-tuned adjacent pair. Where both directions are available (toward the lower/thicker strings and toward the higher/thinner strings), drill both; edge strings (3rd ascending, 4th descending in his worked pass) only have one direction available and that's flagged as expected, not a gap. Run the whole drill **ascending then descending** — descending forces a genuinely different mental approach rather than reversing memorized ascending shapes. No memorized fingering prescribed — treat it as an ear/eye drill. Full extraction → [[Octave and Unison Shapes for Fretboard Navigation]].
- **Skill 2 — Cross-string unison (same-pitch) finding via major-scale chunking.** Explicitly positioned against the common alternative (memorize a fixed number of frets to the same pitch on another string), which he calls "vague... still kind of feels like guessing." Instead: treat the major scale as a measuring stick (his own earlier-series concept, episodes 2–3) and count in scale-degree chunks rather than raw frets. **Toward the next string up (body side):** find scale-degree 3 on that string, then walk back through 2 to 1 — 1 is the unison. **Toward the next string down (nut side):** find scale-degree 5 (same fret as your note, on strings a perfect 4th apart), then walk up through 6, 7, back to 1 — 1 is the unison. Both patterns shift by one fret across the G→B string pair, same tuning caveat as Skill 1. Explicitly **not** tied to any real key or chord root — "this does not mean you're in this key... you're using it as a measuring stick to just quickly see distances," usable regardless of tonality. **Two-stage practice: (1) walk it** — physically play every in-between note (3-2-1 or 5-6-7-1) to build the connection, then **(2) visualize it** — jump straight to the target pitch while still mentally running the same scale-degree bridge, explicitly warned against skipping straight to memorizing a bare fret number (won't transfer outside the specific drill context). Drilled through all six strings, ascending then descending. Full extraction → [[Octave and Unison Shapes for Fretboard Navigation]].
