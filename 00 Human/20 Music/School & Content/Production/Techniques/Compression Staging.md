# Compression Staging

**What it is:** splitting dynamic control across several gentle compressors (tracking → track → group → bus → master) instead of one deep one. Every serious chain in this template compresses in stages.

## Why stages beat one big squash
One compressor doing 10 dB has to have its threshold low enough that peaks slam it — deep GR, audible pumping, dead transients. Three compressors doing 2–4 dB each barely work individually; the signal arrives at each stage *already controlled*, so each can be transparent. Total control is the same; the sound is dramatically better.

## The classic pair: 1176 → LA-2A
- **1176 first (peaks):** fastest attack in the business (50 μs at full clockwise — knobs run backward). 4:1 safe, 8:1/12:1 for surgical peaks-only (higher ratio = steeper knee). 3–7 dB GR *only on peaks*. No threshold — drive the input, match the output.
- **LA-2A second (program):** one knob, slow optical. 1–5 dB riding the whole phrase like a fader.
- **Why they pair:** mirror-image release curves. The 1176 releases *faster* as it approaches zero GR; the LA-2A "stomps on the brakes" — fast at first, slower near zero. Each covers the other's blind spot. ([[3 Vocal Bus|Vocal Bus]], [[1 Main Vocal Track|Main Vocal Track]].)

## The full vocal staging in this template
1. Clip-gain by hand (±∞:1, free) → 2. UAD 1176 at tracking, 2–4 dB peaks → 3. [[Pro-C 2]] on the track, 3–5 dB shape → 4. [[LALA]]/Glue at group, 1–2 dB → 5. LA-2A on the bus, 1–3 dB → 6. Glue on the master, 1–2 dB. ~15 dB of total control; no stage audible.

## Stage-tuning rules
- Each stage gentler than the last (big GR early, whispers late).
- Attack decides punch: slow attack (20–30 ms+) = transients pass = punch; fast = control/density.
- Release decides groove: time it so the meter *breathes with the tempo* (or use auto/variable release — the [[API-2500]]'s variable mode recovers faster after light hits).
- If any single stage regularly exceeds ~4 dB in a mix context, the problem is upstream (level, arrangement, or a hotter earlier stage needed).
- Cap safety: [[Pro-C 2]]'s range slider hard-limits GR per stage.

- **Layer several LIGHT limiters with different characters instead of one limiter doing all the work — each stage catches only 1-2dB, but the different attack/release/algorithm per stage adds up to more level without one obvious "sound."** Overmono's default mastering chain runs four limiters in series (a punchy bus processor → an EQ+comp-flavored limiter → a "smeary" limiter used very lightly since it can "ruin music really quickly" → a final transparent-mode limiter), each doing 1-2dB rather than one limiter pulling 6dB+. The logic: a single heavy limiter with one attack/release setting either acts like a clipper (fast) or squashes transients hard (slow); layering lets you get a little of several different characters instead of committing to one. → [[Master Chain]], [[Limiting]] — Overmono (Tape Notes), [[TN 187 Overmono - Full Video Episode]]
- **Four stackable drum-bus compression techniques: standard glue, parallel, emphasis/de-emphasis, and harmonic-placement.** Standard: attack slow enough to preserve drum transients, **release above ~20 ms** to avoid low-frequency distortion, **3-6 dB of gain reduction** as a subtle-glue guideline. Parallel: a UNITY-gain send (not an output change) off the drum bus to a separate aux, heavily compressed with makeup gain while keeping the same longer-attack/shorter-release philosophy, blended back in via fader to pull up masked low-level detail (snare buzz, stick rattle) without flattening the main bus's transients. Emphasis/de-emphasis (a single-band-compressor workaround for multiband-style frequency-selective compression, borrowed from vinyl mastering): EQ BEFORE the compressor attenuates a range you want left uncompressed (e.g. dip the kick's low end), letting the compressor bear down harder on everything else, then an equal-and-opposite BOOST after the compressor restores tonal balance. Harmonic distortion placed BEFORE compression gets compressed along with the signal (more prevalent, especially with makeup gain or parallel compression); placed AFTER, it's generated off the now-controlled uniform signal instead; both at once is the most intense option, reserved for drums that need to cut aggressively. → [[Parallel Processing]], [[Multiband Compression]], [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[2 Drums Group]] — Sage Audio, [4 Ways to Use Drum Bus Compression](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eY-vIUZzA4s)
- **A small pre-compressor EQ boost gets amplified twice — once by the compressor's own gain-hungry reaction, once by makeup gain — so use it deliberately, not just for tone.** Boosting a range slightly before it hits a compressor makes the compressor work a bit harder on that range specifically; makeup gain afterward then lifts the whole signal including that already-emphasized range further, which can read as a subtly more "processed"/pushed character on that band versus a flat pre-compressor EQ. Useful as a staging tool: decide what you want the compressor emphasizing before it ever sees the signal, rather than only correcting after the fact. → [[EQ]] — Sage Audio, [This Makes Equalizing Vocals A Lot Easier](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dM8TyAYzpxs)
- **Peak level is usually driven by transients, RMS (average loudness) by tonal content — compression's real job is to close that gap by trading transient headroom for overall level.** Most unmixed recordings arrive with peak too high relative to RMS, causing temporal masking (a loud transient covers what follows it). Peak-compressing the transient content frees the headroom needed to raise overall level, lifting RMS-driving tonal content and reducing that masking — worked example: compress an acoustic guitar's transients specifically to gain the headroom to bring its sustain up. **Genre changes how much of this is appropriate:** a well-recorded, well-performed take can already establish much of the target peak/RMS relationship live (see [[Gain Staging]]); in jazz, that dynamic relationship often IS the performance and over-compressing it fights the musicians. In rock, typically just kick/snare drive the peak level while vocal/bass/guitar drive the RMS — so vocal/bass/guitar can usually take heavier compression while kick/snare get compressed more conservatively to retain punch. → [[EQ]] (ADSR framing), [[Gain Staging]], [[Genres Index]] — Sage Audio, [Arrangement and Music Mixing: Understanding the Relationship](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_--yEbcNjAs)
- **Time a compressor's release to the SONG'S groove, not to a stopwatch — align the gain-reduction meter's return to unity with right before the next transient hits.** Since attack/release timing on a compressor can't be solved by the BPM-to-ms formula (see [[Compression]] for why), the practical staging discipline is: watch the GR meter, and adjust release until it returns to 0 dB GR just ahead of the next hit rather than mid-decay or late. Harder to nail on sporadic-transient sources (vocals) than steady ones (a kick on the grid). Same discipline carries over to dynamic EQ release/decay staging. → [[Compression]], [[EQ]] — Sage Audio, [How to Time Every Audio Effect to the BPM](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MXz2hPL36PQ)

- **The SAME bus compressor dialed to two deliberately different intensities depending on the bus's role — one plugin, two jobs.** Marcella Araica adds a UA 33609 to both her drum bus and her music bus (samples, transitional risers, guitars) in the same pass, but drives them differently: on drums, pushed for obvious "extra sauce" that "really makes him hit"; on the music bus, kept light and "breathing" — "I don't want it compressing too much... it's not about really over-compressing it, just allowing it to give it more presence" — riding the gain by ear against the rest of the mix rather than setting a fixed heavy amount. - **Two named engineer philosophies on why compressors — plural, in combination — do more than any single stage's gain reduction: Michael Brauer treats them as tone machines, Chris Lord-Alge stacks a compressor AFTER a limiter for extra energy.** Greg Wells: "the best effect on a vocal is the right kind of and the right combination of compressors and how they react to each other." Brauer "uses them to create different colors and different vibes — even if he's not even really engaging the gain reduction in the compressor, he'll just route things through different compressors" — a compressor reached for as a color stage, whether or not it's doing meaningful leveling work (see [[UAD Teletronix LA-2A]], [[UAD UA 1176LN]]). Lord-Alge, per Wells, "will limit something and then compress the limiter after — you know, like, limiting, it's like there's a ceiling, you wouldn't think it could be compressed more, but he wants to hear how that reacts to a compressor, because it sort of adds this other crazy energy to it" — a fourth stage stacked past this note's own 1176→CL 1B→Pro-C 2→LALA→LA-2A→Glue staging model, added specifically for character rather than more control. *(secondhand — Wells describing Brauer's and Lord-Alge's approaches; no ratio/threshold/GR values given)* → [[Compression]] (effect), [[UAD Teletronix LA-2A]], [[UAD UA 1176LN]], [[1 Main Vocal Track]] — Greg Wells (Mix with the Masters), [[Bringing vocals out of the speakers with Greg Wells]]
- **"Every little thing is doing a little thing" — the general philosophy behind staging, stated directly rather than just demonstrated: many plugins, each doing a small, mostly-inaudible-alone move, gradually molding the sound rather than one drastic pass.** "All these things that I do — when I do multiple plugins like this — every little thing is doing a little thing. Usually, sometimes I might be doing drastic EQ if I need to, but ultimately, sometimes I just like to do things in little stages and just gradually mold it into the mix." Demonstrated across a whole vocal chain (warmth stage, compression, de-ess, several rounds of narrow-Q resonance notching, multiband compression, a low-mid trim, a top-end/air cut) — each individually subtle enough to A/B in and out, cumulative effect being what actually reads as "mixed." Worth reading as this note's own thesis ("stages beat one big squash") extended past compression alone to a whole chain of different processor types. → [[EQ]] (effect), [[De-essing]], [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[Multiband Compression]], [[1 Main Vocal Track|Main Vocal Track]] — Mix with the Masters (Stuart White), [[Mixing vocals in the box with Stuart White]]
- **Feed a fast compressor a boosted low end from the EQ just ahead of it, so it reacts to the source's bass response — not just its peaks — then follow with a much slower unit purely as a leveler, for contrast.** Vocal chain: [[UAD Pultec EQP-1A|Pultec]] (boost ~100 Hz) → [[UAD UA 1176LN|1176]] (fast attack/release — grabs peaks quickly, and grabs the bass response too because the EQ ahead fed it more low end: "you're adding and making a compressor work a bit harder than it actually would do if it didn't have the bottom end in there") → [[UAD Tube-Tech CL 1B mkII|Tube-Tech CL 1B]] (much slower attack/release, run purely to round the voice off consistently). A fast-then-slow stage pair like the classic 1176→LA-2A, but built from two units with genuinely different character, and with an EQ move upstream deliberately shaping what the fast stage reacts to. → [[UAD Pultec EQP-1A]], [[UAD UA 1176LN]], [[UAD Tube-Tech CL 1B mkII]], [[Compression]] (effect), [[BASS Slot]] — Mix with the Masters (Ben Baptie), [[Julian Casablancas' vocal sound with Ben Baptie]]
- **"Make it longer, not louder" — when a kick can't get any louder against the compressor, extend its sustain instead.** The ear judges perceived size by *duration it hears the sound*, not just amplitude — so when a kick needs to "hit a little harder" but pushing more gain into the compressor just distorts the mix, extend the kick's release/decay (a longer tail on the same hit) rather than raising level. Same peak, same processing, but it reads as bigger because the ear hears it for longer. Paired with a reframe of Release as "Weight": once a band's been cut/ducked, restoring it is a release move, not a gain move — see [[Compression]] (effect) for the full recipe. → [[Kick Design]] — Jaycen Joshua (Mix with the Masters), [Mixing Summits with Jaycen Joshua](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L6xx7DWKzng)
- **Drum bus processing kept simple: EQ + Glue Compressor is "so good it's kind of all you need."** Ableton's stock Glue Compressor alone handles most of the glue work; if drums need to "smack super hard," blend a parallel-compressed copy back in rather than crushing the drum bus straight — parallel compression is the real lever for punch, not a heavier bus setting. → [[Glue Compressor]], [[Parallel Processing]] — Oliver (Sol State), [IN THE STUDIO WITH OLIVER](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FOJ-q8k8has)
- **Editing IS the first compression stage** — chop all dead space between vocal phrases before compressing: every comp raises the floor of whatever you leave, and uncut room noise across stacked tracks reads as "muddy vocal" — Kenny Beats, [Recording Vocals the Right Way](https://youtu.be/3Nw1d37nluo)
- **Comp-last glues dirt, comp-first tames peaks** — Kenny's live-drum return chain is EQ → saturation/tape-weirdness → THEN compressor (glues the character in); Disclosure runs comp → tape (tape as transient limiter). Both valid — order by intent — Kenny Beats, [Drum Recording Process 2023](https://youtu.be/HTbdB6rWHNI)
- **Tracking 2A = peak reduction, not tone** — GR "never past 7 on the needle, 10 is really pushing it" even on screams; bring loudest and quietest words closer, color later — Kenny Beats, [Vocal Template](https://youtu.be/X_iDENABLmk)
- **Order it compression → *saturation*, not the reverse** — saturation clips/limits the transients coming *out* of the compressor with little audible change but a real peak reduction, so tape after the comp acts like a transient limiter (tape-into-comp doesn't do this). → [[Saturation and Distortion]] — Disclosure, [Ultimatum](https://youtu.be/uWe3UvI4KlU)
- **What "glue" actually is** — separate signals *affecting each other* tastefully (drums nudging/squashing the bass, the vocal sucked by the kick, shared harmonics doubling). It reads as "finished/natural," and it's **subtle**. Not only compression: **distortion, saturation, and especially tape** glue, and *anytime you combine parts through one piece of hardware* (an MPC, an SSL G-bus, transistors/circuits) they interact. DAWs lose it — total control, no limitations, and limitations are sometimes good (being forced to bus organ+strings+horns down one channel *glued* them). → [[Saturation and Distortion]] — Disclosure, [SSL SIX Mixing Al Green](https://youtu.be/ZCNX8U6oZmE)
- **The "glue bus" is perceptual, not just level** — run related parts (four basses, a whole drum kit, all percussion) through one comp/tape so they *interact*: the kick squashes the cymbals, the loud bass pumps the quiet one. Often barely compressing (Blue-Stripe/CLA-76 just kissing the signal) — the point is to make many sounds read as *one*. Foals "Blue Blood" pumps its bus comp so hard you hear the cymbals breathe: "sounds like something you couldn't do in real life." → [[Parallel Processing]] — Disclosure, [Birthday](https://youtu.be/b-6x_4XRwOQ)
- **Leave headroom on your summing buses to fix things late** — pre-limit the instrument bus a touch and keep a final EQ on the vocal bus, so a "too much vocal top-end" note at mastering is a 5-minute project revisit, not a whole-mix EQ move by the engineer. → [[Master Chain]] — Disclosure, [Birthday](https://youtu.be/b-6x_4XRwOQ)
