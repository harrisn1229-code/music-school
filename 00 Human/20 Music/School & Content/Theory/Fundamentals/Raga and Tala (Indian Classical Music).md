# Raga and Tala (Indian Classical Music)

Part of [[Music Theory]]. The two core organizing concepts of Hindustani/Carnatic classical music — **raga** (melodic framework) and **tala** (rhythmic cycle) — as distinct from, but mappable onto, Western scales and time signatures. Logged here specifically because of how deeply George Harrison and (to a lesser extent) John Lennon pulled these ideas into the Beatles' catalog, both faithfully and loosely.

## Raga — not just "a scale"

- Indian classical music has **no chords or functional harmony**. Instead, a **drone** instrument (traditionally a tambura) sustains a fixed root note as the key center throughout an entire piece, while melodic instruments/voice improvise a **raga** above it.
- A raga uses a set of notes that often maps directly onto a familiar Western scale (e.g. the raga used on *Love You To* = the Western **Dorian** scale; the raga used on *Tomorrow Never Knows* and *Within You Without You* = the Western **Mixolydian** scale) — but a raga is more than its note set: it carries **its own conventions for how those notes should be used**, including a different note sequence ascending vs. descending, characteristic melodic phrases, and (in genuine performance practice) heavy ornamentation/melisma around the notes rather than a plain, repeated Western-style melody.
- **Structure of a raga-based piece**: an **alap** (a free-time introductory section that establishes the key center and raga, with no fixed pulse) → a **gat** (a set, precomposed section, often the closest equivalent to a Western verse/chorus) → a **jhala** (a faster, more energetic closing section, often with elaborate improvisation).
- A tambura/drone instrument is tuned to one root and **cannot follow chord changes or key changes** without being retuned — which is why it's naturally suited to one-chord Western songs (see below) but only occasionally usable on a conventionally chord-changing pop song, and only where the progression happens to sit consistently over one compatible drone note.

## Tala — rhythm cycles, not just time signatures

- Indian classical rhythm is organized into **talas**, which — like a raga vs. a scale — are more than just "the Indian name for a time signature": a tala specifies both a total beat count *and* how those beats subdivide into smaller groups.
- **Teentaal** — the most common tala, a 16-beat cycle grouped as 4+4+4+4 — maps directly onto Western 4/4.
- **Jhaptaal** — a 10-beat cycle, grouped as **3+2+3+2** (not a plain undifferentiated 10) — the closest Western meter equivalent would be something like 10/8 or 5/4, but a straight 10/8 doesn't carry Jhaptaal's specific 3-2-3-2 internal grouping.
- Unlike Western time-signature practice, a tala's cycle is normally held **constant** throughout a traditional piece — the Beatles' use of it (below) deviates from that by shifting meter mid-song, a Western habit grafted onto a borrowed rhythmic vocabulary.

## How the Beatles used it — faithful vs. blended examples

- **Norwegian Wood** (1965) — the first sitar on a Western pop record. Purely textural/coloristic use; no raga/tala structure attached.
- **Love You To** (Revolver, 1966) — the first Beatles song to fully commit to the Indian approach: a static tambura drone (root C) instead of a chord progression, built on the Dorian-equivalent raga, and structured with an alap → gat → (a very short, ~20-second) jhala — a real Indian piece's jhala typically runs several minutes. George's melody is mostly sung in a Western, repetitive style, with only occasional ornamented/melismatic moments — a partial, not full, adoption of raga performance practice. **Blended, not pure**: a B♭ chord is superimposed over the drone during the chorus — a harmonic move a genuine Indian performance wouldn't make.
- **Tomorrow Never Knows** (Revolver, 1966) — the first Beatles song built on a **single chord for its entire duration** (predating *Love You To* by a few weeks, per Paul McCartney's account of Lennon bringing in a song "all on the chord of C" and the band/George Martin being open to it). Built on the Mixolydian-equivalent raga, droned via tambura — but layered with a full Western rock rhythm section (drum kit, guitars, tape loops), and a second chord is superimposed over the drone for a moment of harmonic movement, the same "bend the rule" move as *Love You To*.
- **Within You Without You** (Sgt. Pepper, 1967) — the deepest single commitment: full alap → gat → jhala structure, additional Indian instruments (dilruba, swarmandal, alongside tambura/sitar/tabla), the Mixolydian-equivalent raga again, and an explicit tala reference — a variation on **teentaal** for the verse/chorus, switching to **jhaptaal** for the instrumental section. Even so, meter isn't held perfectly constant Indian-style: extra beats get added mid-verse (Western-style irregular-bar insertion, see [[Time Signatures & Note Values]]).
- **The Inner Light** (1968) — recorded in Bombay, the only Beatles song tracked outside Europe; introduced further Indian instruments (shehnai, pakhavaj) not used elsewhere in the catalog.
- **Blue Jay Way** — a drone-based approach to harmony (root C throughout) but bent Western-style: the chord quality pivots between C major and C diminished over the drone, rather than staying harmony-free.
- **If I Needed Someone** — blends the two systems directly: a full Western chord progression, but with the chords underpinned by a constant drone note (A) running beneath them.
- **Strawberry Fields Forever** and **Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds** — light textural use of swarmandal and tambura respectively, without adopting raga/tala structure; on *Lucy*, George Harrison found that a constant A drone happened to fit the verse's chord progression well enough to add tambura despite the song having chord changes, plus a guitar line imitating a sarangi (a traditional Indian instrument used to shadow a vocalist's melody).
- **Here Comes the Sun** — uses **no Indian instrumentation at all**; the Indian influence shows up purely as an unusual, tala-like grouping of beats in the bridge (repeating 3/8+3/8+3/8+2/8, then a 4/4 bar, then 2/8+2/8+3/8) — not a real tala, but clearly inspired by the tala principle of grouping a cycle into uneven small units rather than a plain time signature.
