# How To Use Diatonic Passing Chords With Easy Practical Examples

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6pGVE0ZRF8s)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6pGVE0ZRF8s)

## Synopsis

A short, dense Jens Larsen jazz-guitar theory lesson that works out diatonic passing chords entirely through one worked example: a basic **1-6-2-5 in C major** (Cmaj7 – Am7 – Dm7 – G7). Larsen explicitly frames this as a follow-up answering questions from an earlier video on using passing chords in improvised solo lines — his point being there's barely any theory to it: a diatonic passing chord is just any chord already sitting in the parent scale (here, the seven diatonic chords of C major: Cmaj7, Dm7, Em7, Fmaj7, G7, Am7, Bø7) placed on the weak beat between two chords of the real progression to add stepwise motion and color, while the strong-beat chord stays the harmonic anchor so the original progression is still audible underneath. He walks through **every transition in the 1-6-2-5** (C→Am, Am→Dm, Dm→G7, G7→C) giving 2-4 concrete passing-chord options per transition — approach from below, approach from above, full stepwise walk-ups, and full stepwise walk-downs — plus one non-diatonic-in-the-strict-sense bonus (a triad-over-bass inversion, G/B). The whole video is a comping-focused companion to a prior solo-line-oriented video, and Larsen is explicit that the same vocabulary applies to both: play it as chords first to test whether it makes musical sense, then move it into single-note lines.

## Techniques

- **Core definition — a diatonic passing chord is simply a chord already present in the parent scale, inserted between two chords of the real progression to create stepwise connective motion.** For a tune in C major the available diatonic chords are Cmaj7, Dm7, Em7, Fmaj7, G7, Am7, Bø7 (and Cmaj7 again an octave up) — pick from that pool for any passing move.
- **Weak-beat placement is what keeps the original progression audible.** The passing chord always sits on the *weaker* beat of the bar; the real progression chord stays on the heavy beat, so the ear still tracks C→Am (etc.) as the actual harmony and hears the passing chord as color, not a competing chord change — same rhythmic logic Larsen used for chromatic passing chords in the companion video. *(general starting point — no specific subdivision given beyond "weak beat"; test by playing the passing chord as an eighth-note pickup into the target's downbeat.)*
- **Worked example progression: 1-6-2-5 in C major (Cmaj7 – Am7 – Dm7 – G7).**
  - **C → Am (I → vi):**
    - **Stepwise-between approach:** Cmaj7 → Bø7 → Am7 — the obvious candidate, since B half-diminished sits directly between C and A in the scale.
    - **Triad-inversion variant (worth knowing, comes up in real jazz charts):** a G major triad over a B bass note (**G/B**) in place of Bø7 for the same passing slot — same bass note, brighter/more consonant color than the half-diminished.
    - **Approach from below:** C → G7 → Am7 (the V7 leading up into the vi, i.e. borrowing a dominant-passing-chord move into a diatonic slot).
    - **Full stepwise walk-down-then-up:** C → F → G → Am (skip down to F, then walk up through G into A) — a longer connective line spanning most of the bar rather than a single passing chord.
  - **Am → Dm (vi → ii):**
    - **Chord-below approach:** Am7 → Cmaj7 → Dm7 (using the diatonic chord a step below the target as the leading chord) — works because Am7 and Cmaj7 share enough tones to sound like the same harmony extended, so slipping it in "does no harm" and tends to sit well under whatever the melody/solo is doing.
    - **Full walk-up:** Am7 → Bø7 → Cmaj7 → Dm7 (walk all the way up through the scale from A to D).
    - **Approach from above:** Am7 → Em7 → Dm7 (skip up to E minor, then step down into D minor).
    - **Extended stepwise walk-down:** add one more chord to the above for a fuller descending line into Dm7 (Am7 → Em7 → [one more diatonic step] → Dm7) — same "walk it down" logic as the C→Am move, mirrored.
  - **Dm → G7 (ii → V):**
    - **Whole-step-below approach:** Dm7 → Fmaj7 → G7 (Fmaj7 sits a whole step under G7 and functions almost like a "G sus" substitute for it, since the two chords sound similar).
    - **Stepwise walk-up:** Dm7 → Em7 → Fmaj7 → G7 (walking E, F up into G).
    - **Beat-displacement move — genuinely distinctive, not just a passing-chord insert:** because Fmaj7 and G7 sound so close, you can push the G7 itself off the strong beat and put the Fmaj7 **on the downbeat instead**, landing G7 afterward — the passing chord temporarily takes over the harmonic-rhythm slot that "belongs" to the real V chord, and it still reads as sensible in context. Worth trying deliberately as a rhythmic-displacement device, not just a color addition.
    - **Full stepwise walk-down:** Dm7 → C → ... down to G7 mirrored the other direction (walking the line down from D to G rather than up).
  - **G7 → C (V → I):**
    - **Full walk-up through the ii-V:** Dm7 → G7 → Am7 → Bø7 → Cmaj7 — treats the *approach into* the V→I as one long diatonic walk (this reuses Am7/Bø7 as passing motion leading into the final Cmaj7, not just G7 resolving directly).
      - **Rhythm placement for this one is more specific than "just weak beat":** because G7 and Bø7 sound so similar (Bø7 functions almost like a rootless G9), put **both of them on the heavy beats** and slot the **Am7 on the offbeat between them** — on-beat (G7), off-beat (Am7), on-beat (Bø7) — so the ear tracks the dominant resolving in two "voices" of the same sound with the vi chord threaded through as connective tissue.
    - **Full stepwise walk-down:** G → F → E → D → C — walk the whole line down by step from G to C, one passing chord per scale step.
- **Practice method Larsen recommends:** sit down with a simple progression (a 2-5-1 or a 1-6-2-5, same one used throughout this video) and try inserting different diatonic passing chords by ear, testing each by **playing it as chords first** — that's the fastest way to check whether a substitution actually makes musical sense — before trying to move the same vocabulary into single-note improvised lines.
- **Where this fits Larsen's broader comping philosophy:** he calls diatonic passing chords especially valuable in a **duo setting behind a horn player or singer**, where you can't easily reharmonize a static chord with inversions of itself and get it to sound convincing — having more distinct chords to draw from (via passing motion) gives more real color and variation than voicing-shuffling the same chord over and over.
- **Explicitly framed as a comping-first companion to a solo-line video:** Larsen states the same diatonic-passing-chord vocabulary generates melodic movement in improvised lines too (his earlier video on passing chords in solos prompted this one), but this video works it out entirely as comped chord shapes, leaving the melodic-line application to the viewer/earlier video.
