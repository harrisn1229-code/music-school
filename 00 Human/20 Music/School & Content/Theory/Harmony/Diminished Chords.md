# Diminished Chords

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Chords]]. Minor 3rd + flatted 5th — the ♭5 gives the tense, non-diatonic sound. Three flavors:

| Chord | Symbol | Formula | C example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Diminished triad | Cdim / C° | 1 ♭3 ♭5 | C E♭ G♭ |
| Diminished 7th | Cdim7 / C°7 | 1 ♭3 ♭5 ♭♭7 | C E♭ G♭ A (B♭♭) |
| Half-diminished | Cm7♭5 / Cø | 1 ♭3 ♭5 ♭7 | C E♭ G♭ B♭ |

- Triad = stacked minor 3rds (3+3 half-steps); dim7 = all minor 3rds (3+3+3) — perfectly symmetrical, so any note can be the root and it repeats every 3 frets/keys
- dim7 vs ø differ only in the 7th: ♭♭7 (= 6th enharmonically) vs ♭7
- ø can also be read as a minor 7th chord with lowered 5th ([[Seventh Chords]])

## Where they live diatonically
- vii° is the 7th-degree triad of every major key; ii° in minor keys ([[Chords by Key]])
- ø is the ii in the minor-key jazz ii–V–i ([[Chord Progressions]])
- Chord-scale sources (Locrian etc.): [[Scale Modes Reference]]

## Usage — passing chords
Mostly "in-between" chords creating tension between stronger chords:
- I → ♭iii° → V7
- Imaj7 → ♭ii°7 → ii7 (chromatic connector)
- i7 → iiø7 → V7

- **Historical/genre framing for the diminished (octatonic) scale, net new to this page — where the sound comes from and who used it.** A synthetic, symmetric scale (alternating whole-half or half-whole steps forever, breaking the octave into 8 notes, hence "octatonic") — used by classical composers moving away from Romantic-era functional harmony around 1900 to get an intentionally ungrounded, non-tonal-center sound (once nicknamed the "Rimsky-Korsakov scale," cf. *Scheherazade*; also Tchaikovsky, Stravinsky, Debussy, Ravel). In jazz it's used more tonally, over diminished-7 and dominant-7 chords specifically — cited heavy users John Coltrane and Herbie Hancock, occasional pop crossover in Frank Zappa and Radiohead. Plus two practice drills for actually nailing a diminished-scale switch on a moving chord: **isolate on the middle two strings first** (play the current chord's scale on just those two strings, switch the instant the chord changes, only then expand to more strings/full fretboard), then **an out-of-time, fixed-note-count drill** (play a fixed count — e.g. 8 notes — of the current scale, then jump to the *closest available note* of the next chord's scale rather than resetting to its root, training smooth voice-leading between scale choices). → [[Scale Modes Reference]], [[Root-to-Root Scale-Form Mapping Method]] — Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared, [[Ultimate DIMINISHED SCALE Guitar Tutorial]]
