# Bebop Scale Guitar Lesson (Level 1) - Master the Art of Intentional Note Placement

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2liZIbikiGQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2liZIbikiGQ)

## Synopsis

Jared Borkowski's "Level 1" introduction to the bebop scale on guitar — a deliberately narrow, hands-on lesson (he flags follow-up "Level 2/3" videos to come covering chord progressions and full song forms). He defines the concept first — a diatonic scale plus one added chromatic passing tone, whose entire purpose is rhythmic rather than melodic: played in straight eighths, it keeps chord tones landing on strong beats instead of drifting onto the offbeats bar to bar — then walks through the identical practice method on three chord/scale types using a single CAGE-system shape (the "E form," played from the open-position A up the neck): major 6 (bebop major), dominant 7 (bebop mixolydian), and minor 7 (bebop dorian, as he frames it — see the discrepancy note below). For each: outline the arpeggio, lay the diatonic scale over it, insert the passing tone at the specified gap, then drill it from every individual chord tone (not just root-to-root) before finally looping a one-chord vamp and improvising over it. The most transferable content isn't the scale theory itself (already covered in more depth on [[Bebop Vocabulary]]) — it's the **practice method**: the directional-slide fretting technique for approaching the passing tone from either side, and the "vocabulary chunk" philosophy of practicing chord-tone-to-chord-tone rather than only the full run, so the passing tone's placement becomes reflexive from any starting point. Worth flagging: his stated Dorian/minor-7 passing-tone placement (between ♭7 and root) **does not match** the Levine/Baker-sourced bebop dorian formula already on [[Bebop Vocabulary]] (passing tone between ♭3 and 4) — captured as a sourced alternate/simplified approach, not a correction, see the bullet below.

## What's taught

- **Bebop scale = a diatonic (7-note) scale plus one added chromatic passing tone, purely to fix beat placement, not to add a color tone.** Play a plain 7-note scale in straight eighths from beat 1 and by the next bar the chord tones have drifted onto the offbeats — add the 8th note and they land on the downbeat every bar, every time. He's explicit that the payoff is rhythmic placement, not which notes are "in" the scale, and separately makes the case that this isn't a bebop-genre-specific device — it's used throughout jazz and Western classical composition/improvisation to keep chord tones in "the harmonic pocket." [~1:25] → [[Bebop Vocabulary]] (section 1, same core claim, sourced from Levine/Baker)
- **Major 6 / major 7 chord — bebop major.** Arpeggio 1-3-5-6 (major 6 and major 7 treated as interchangeable — he'll voice a written "maj6" as a maj7 arpeggio when he wants that sound, and vice versa), diatonic scale over an E-form CAGE shape up the neck from A, chromatic passing tone inserted **between scale degrees 5 and 6.** Matches [[Bebop Vocabulary]]'s existing "Bebop major = Major + ♯5, passing tone between 5 and 6" row exactly — direct confirmation from a second, independent (non-book) source. [~3:44–5:04] → [[Bebop Vocabulary]]
- **Dominant 7 chord — bebop mixolydian.** Arpeggio 1-3-5-♭7, mixolydian scale (major with a ♭7) over the same shape, passing tone inserted **between ♭7 and the root (1).** Also matches [[Bebop Vocabulary]]'s "Bebop dominant = Mixolydian + natural 7, passing tone between ♭7 and root" exactly. [~11:27–12:12] → [[Bebop Vocabulary]], [[Diatonic Modes]]
- **Minor 7 chord — bebop dorian, per this video: passing tone between ♭7 and root, NOT between ♭3 and 4.** He outlines the minor 7 arpeggio (1-♭3-5-♭7), lays a dorian scale (1-2-♭3-4-5-6-♭7) over it, and adds the passing tone **between ♭7 and 1** — same insertion point as the dominant scale, just applied to the dorian mode. This **conflicts with** the "Bebop dorian" row already on [[Bebop Vocabulary]] (sourced from Levine's Jazz Theory Book / Baker's How to Play Bebop), which places the dorian passing tone between ♭3 and 4 instead. Captured here as a genuine, sourced disagreement between two legitimate teaching sources rather than resolved one way — worth knowing both exist: Jared's version is simpler to teach (same gap as the dominant scale, just a different mode over it) and he says himself he's still internalizing it for his own playing; the book version is the "official" named bebop-dorian scale in the standard jazz-theory literature. [~14:23–16:03] → [[Bebop Vocabulary]]
- **The directional-slide fretting technique for the passing tone — a real, transferable playing technique, not just theory.** Ascending, slide INTO the passing tone from below/inside the shape; descending, play the *same physical target note* but reached from the opposite side of the box, sliding into it from above/outside. He plays it explicitly both ways on all three chord types. Practical reason given: slides land the passing tone with the right "feel" and reduce how much needs picking (some notes become slurs rather than picked attacks), which also just makes the chromatic step sound less mechanical/scalar and more idiomatic. [~5:04] → [[Bebop Vocabulary]], [[Chord Voicings]]
- **"Vocabulary chunk" practice method — the actual reusable technique here.** Don't just drill the full scale root-to-root. Instead pick ANY chord tone (root, 3rd, 5th, 6th/♭7) and run one full octave starting and ending on that tone, hitting the passing tone at its fixed spot along the way — e.g. 3-to-3, 5-to-5, 6-to-6 — cycling through every chord tone as a starting point, not just the root. Rationale: root-to-root repetition just becomes one memorized muscle-memory shape, which won't transfer into real improvising; starting from every chord tone forces the passing tone's placement to become independent of where you entered the scale, which is what actually lets you drop a short "chunk" of it into a real solo instead of only ever playing the whole run. [~7:39] → [[Bebop Vocabulary]]
- **Loop-pedal (or backing-track) practice stage, one chord at a time.** Once the octave-drill is solid, loop a single static chord (his own example: a one-chord A major 6 vamp via loop pedal; for a backing track, search YouTube for "[chord] all one chord" or similar) and alternate between (a) playing the full scale run straight through and (b) deliberately breaking it up — playing a short fragment (a "vocabulary chunk"), stepping out into freer/thematic phrasing that ignores the bebop-scale rule entirely, then dropping back into another short fragment. He calls out that this is not a rule to always land chord tones on the beat, but a *habit/feel* you want available, so that even loose, freely-invented phrasing naturally gravitates back to chord tones landing in emphasized spots. [~7:39 onward, all three sections] → [[Bebop Vocabulary]]
- **Extension: free chromatic approach notes below every chord tone, decoupled from the "one official passing tone" rule — demoed in the minor 7/dorian section.** Beyond the single fixed passing tone, he shows placing a chromatic note directly below EACH chord tone in a descending run (not just at the ♭7→1 gap), explicitly noting this works regardless of which minor mode the chord technically implies (Aeolian, Dorian, Phrygian — "doesn't matter, 'cause you're playing chord tones and the other ones are just kind of leading in"). Flagged by him as something he's still developing for his own playing, not a settled rule — and he draws a direct parallel to the ascending/descending asymmetry of the real melodic minor scale. [~16:03] → [[Bebop Vocabulary]]
- **Sound/resource recommendation, not a technique — two companion PDFs from his own site**, mentioned as the natural next step once the bebop-scale concept is down: a "parent scales" PDF (all scales, all fretboard positions) and a "chord tone arpeggio pack" (12 chord types, arpeggio-only).
