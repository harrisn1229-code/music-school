# Songs that use the James Bond chord progression

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rew6FBtC0Mo)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rew6FBtC0Mo)

## Synopsis

A dedicated deep-dive on the ascending line-cliché from Monty Norman/John Barry's James Bond theme — how the mechanism works, songs that borrow the sound directly, and the specific trend (starting with Casino Royale) of Bond film theme songs deliberately weaving the original motif into new original harmony.

## Techniques

- **The mechanism**: Em → C/E (1st inversion, top note climbs Em's 5th, B, up a semitone to C) → Cdim/E (top note climbs again to C♯) → back down to Em — a compact ascending line cliché with a built-in melody (the climbing top line effectively *is* the James Bond motif). This is the same device documented generally on [[Line Cliché]]; this video is the dedicated deep-dive on this one specific instance.
- **Instantly genre-coded**: because the motif is so distinctive, any song using this exact progression reads as evoking "James Bond" whether or not that's the songwriter's intent. Examples: "Everybody's Been Burned" (The Byrds, 1967); "Shades of Cool" (Lana Del Rey); "When We Kiss"/similar mid-batch examples (auto-caption garbled — not claimed with confidence); "Secret Agent Man" (Johnny Rivers) — an explicit tongue-in-cheek nod, using it as the main guitar hook.
- **The Bond-theme-song trend, dated precisely**: "Thunderball" (Tom Jones, 1965) is the first Bond theme to nod to the progression, but only in passing — not built on it. The trend of a new theme song deliberately incorporating the original Bond-theme progression into its own harmony **starts with "You Know My Name"** (Chris Cornell, *Casino Royale*, 2006) — co-written with David Arnold (the franchise's then-composer for *Casino Royale*, *Die Another Day*, *The World Is Not Enough*, *Tomorrow Never Dies*, *Quantum of Solace*), Arnold's explicit idea per Cornell's own account: *"write a song that echoed the film score."* Chorus: Bm–G–E (transposed to Em to compare directly against the original) — has the same signature semitone climb (B→C→C♯) as the original but only reaches C♯ without falling back to C, and voices the chords in root position rather than the original's inversions.
- **The trend continuing through subsequent films** (each analyzed the same way — transposed to Em, compared against the original climb): "Another Way to Die" (Jack White & Alicia Keys, *Quantum of Solace*) — same modification as Cornell's, but power chords throughout, so the melodic third (needed to complete the semitone climb) is simply absent even though the harmonic skeleton matches. "Skyfall" (Adele) — loses the inversions like the other examples, but keeps the full semitone climb up and down. (Video cuts off before covering *Spectre* in the available transcript window, but the pattern through at least three consecutive films is thoroughly documented.)
