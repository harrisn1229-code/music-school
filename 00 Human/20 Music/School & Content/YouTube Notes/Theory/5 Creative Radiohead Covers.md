# 5 Creative Radiohead Covers

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qHvyFiBPORM)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qHvyFiBPORM)

## Synopsis

A survey of five Radiohead covers chosen specifically because they add something new rather than just re-performing the original — a compact case study in what a "creative cover" can do harmonically, texturally, and vocally.

## Techniques

- **Modulate a cover up from the original's key as a structural device**: Robert Glasper's "Everything in Its Right Place" starts in C major (matching the original) then modulates up a whole step to D major after only 13 seconds.
- **Layer a second song's melody over a cover's harmony (mashup-by-melody)**: Glasper's same cover isn't just a Radiohead cover — he layers the melody of Herbie Hancock's "Maiden Voyage" (a compatible jazz standard) on top of the "Everything in Its Right Place" changes, covering two songs at once.
- **A faithful-harmony cover can still land differently purely through vocal timbre**: Gnarls Barkley's "Reckoner" keeps the same guitar riff and groove as the original, but swapping Thom Yorke's soft falsetto for CeeLo Green's booming belt is what does the reinterpreting.
- **Subtraction as the reinterpretation tool**: Regina Spektor's "No Surprises" cover removes drums and guitar entirely, leaving just piano, swelling strings, and voice — makes the song "rawer and more bare," in service of the lyric's exhausted sadness. Same subtractive principle as [[Arrangement and Song Form]]'s scheduled-absence idea, applied to a cover rather than an original arrangement.
- **Sonic motif reused as a personal signature across an artist's catalog**: Spektor's breathing-sound effect (used for suicide/carbon-monoxide imagery in her "No Surprises" cover) reappears, more aggressively, in her original song "Open" — same texture repurposed for a different but related lyrical idea (entrapment).
- **Sample + lyric callback as a compositional move**: Dan le Sac vs Scroobius Pip's "A Letter From God to Man" chops a sample of "Planet Telex"'s intro and keyboard part into a new song, then closes by quoting Planet Telex's own chorus lyric ("everything is broken, everyone is broken") to make the source explicit and thematically pointed rather than just decorative.
