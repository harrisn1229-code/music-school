# Client Communication and Revisions

The professional-practice half of mixing: how to handle an artist or client who wants something changed, before and while it's happening — as distinct from the *how-to-mix* content in the rest of `Production/Techniques/`. Two failure modes sit on either side of the target: capitulating to every request (a mix that ends up belonging to nobody's judgment) and shutting revision requests down (a client who stops trusting you enough to say what they actually think). Companion to [[Mix Handoff]] (the commit boundary this note's first point extends from the client-facing side) and [[Masking and Carving]] (the "definition between instruments" this note's mixes-for-revision point depends on).

## From videos

- **Direct musicians with evocative/synesthetic language (color, taste) rather than technical instructions, then refine from their interpretation.** Quincy Jones' documented method: describing a part as needing to be "more yellow" or "needs a little more garlic salt / red pepper" instead of naming a specific technical change — the musician supplies their own interpretation of what that means (a bassist reading "make it more yellow" as "mellower," for instance), and the director nuances the result from there rather than dictating the exact move up front. The stated value: it gives a player real room to interpret while still steering the outcome, and — because the direction isn't a literal technical instruction — a player who disagreed with a technical note might resist it, but rarely resists an evocative one the same way. Same source's separate habit: physically write down every idea (score paper, note cards, even napkins) with connecting circles/arrows between related thoughts, mind-map style, specifically so a big-picture plan (a whole album's sequencing, not just one part) stays traceable later. → Anthony Marinelli (feat. Ed Eckstine), [[Quincy Jones Life Lessons w Ed Eckstine]]

- **Develop a shared vocabulary with a repeat client so a vague verbal note translates directly into the correct move on the first try — the concrete payoff is skipping the guess-and-resend cycle entirely.** Leslie Brathwaite, on a label head asking for a piano to "bite" more: "one of the things you do when you're mixing records and dealing with the people who are gonna approve the record is you figure out their language, you develop a language with them." Because that vocabulary was already established, "bite" was correctly decoded as a high-frequency attack boost and approved in one pass — "yes, that's it, don't touch it." Stated payoff, generalizable to any repeat client: "when you take the time to get to know your clients and know what they're talking about... you don't have to spend all day going back and forth and sending multiple passes." → [[EQ]] — Mix with the Masters (Leslie Brathwaite), [[Being efficient - Leslie Brathwaite]]
- **Leave a collaborator's existing processing choice alone even when it isn't your preferred tool — protecting a client's trust and your own time outweighs a marginal preference.** Brathwaite, on a co-producer's compressor already sitting on a hook sample: "this is not my compressor of choice, but that doesn't mean I can't honor the fact that this is what he chose, it's doing the job... if it's not broke, you don't have to fix it." Explicit cost of swapping it anyway: re-matching a replacement plugin's settings to the existing sound burns real time, and if the artist later notices something sounds subtly different, "we just wasted a whole lot of back-and-forth for no reason" — a self-inflicted problem against the actual goal of the session. Framed as part of a broader efficiency discipline: "knowing what to spend time on, what not to spend time on... is what I think also adds to being a good mix engineer." → [[Compression]] — Mix with the Masters (Leslie Brathwaite), [[Being efficient - Leslie Brathwaite]]
- **Mix with built-in revision headroom, not a polished/finished-sounding balance, so a client's pushback can move things a lot without breaking the mix.** "If I've done my job properly and gotten things right, hopefully there's a lot of flexibility in that mix where I can bring something up or down a significant amount and not destroy the integrity of what I've established." The mechanism: keep **definition between instruments without prettifying them** — "just keep them raw and powerful" — so pushing one element up or down doesn't suddenly make another "disappear" or "seem too loud." A mixing-craft decision made *in anticipation of* revision requests, not a reaction to them once they arrive — the client-facing mirror of [[Mix Handoff]]'s commit boundary ("leave open: track-to-track balance"). → [[Masking and Carving]], [[Mix Handoff]] — Mix with the Masters (Andy Wallace), [[When you disagree with the mix revisions - Andy Wallace]]
- **Set the ground rules for feedback in the very first meeting, before mixing even starts** — not after a request has already caused friction. "I usually try to address the way in which that would occur very early on, in our first meeting, even before we even start mixing." Naming the process up front (rather than improvising a reaction under a specific request) is what makes everything below actually land. — Mix with the Masters (Andy Wallace), [[When you disagree with the mix revisions - Andy Wallace]]
- **Actively draw out artists who are too intimidated by your reputation to give honest feedback.** Some clients are "too shy... almost afraid to suggest that the great Andy Wallace should do this" — the fix is direct, explicit reassurance rather than waiting for them to volunteer it: "don't be shy, I want your input, that's my job." Feedback that never gets voiced is functionally the same as no feedback — it just surfaces later as quiet dissatisfaction instead of a request you can act on. — Mix with the Masters (Andy Wallace), [[When you disagree with the mix revisions - Andy Wallace]]
- **Offer pushy or over-involved clients a reciprocal-trust deal instead of either capitulating outright or shutting them down.** Stated to the client up front: "you can always make suggestions to me, don't be shy... I'll pull the reins in if I really feel they need to be pulled in. I'll let you know if I think a suggestion you're making is not good for the mix or is going to destroy something else we've established." That standing commitment — not blind agreement — is what actually lets the client speak freely: "if they can have that confidence not just in my ability to do the mix but in my agreeing to reel them in if they've gone too far, that gives them a freedom where they can speak their mind." Explicitly contrasted with just doing whatever's asked ("okay you want that, ladders-ville, okay, you like that, great, it's done") — "obviously I want anything that goes out with my name on it to be something I'm proud of." The deal only works if you actually hold up your end of it (the next point). — Mix with the Masters (Andy Wallace), [[When you disagree with the mix revisions - Andy Wallace]]
- **When a revision genuinely breaks the mix, say so directly and reopen the conversation — don't silently revert it, and don't silently comply and let it ship broken.** "If I feel it doesn't [work], then I'll say that... I'll let you know that this mix isn't working anymore, or whatever change we made there is not good for the mix, it's not helping the mix, we're going away from where we should be." Named as almost always leading to a dialogue that resolves it — the pushback itself is what the trust deal above was built to make safe. — Mix with the Masters (Andy Wallace), [[When you disagree with the mix revisions - Andy Wallace]]
- **Know when a client relationship is fundamentally unworkable, and be willing to decline the job rather than deliver something you can't stand behind.** The rare failure case: a client so completely fixated on their own pre-existing reference mixes that "every single [element] all the way down the line" had to be reverted to match them, leaving no actual room to mix. Resolution, stated respectfully and without hard feelings on either side: "you shouldn't hire me to mix this record because I simply cannot give you what you're looking for, and I don't know that any other mixer is going to do it either... if what you absolutely want are those reference mixes, release those reference mixes, that's the record you want." Framed explicitly as rare, not a common outcome — the escape hatch for when the ground rules and the trust deal above still don't close the gap. — Mix with the Masters (Andy Wallace), [[When you disagree with the mix revisions - Andy Wallace]]
