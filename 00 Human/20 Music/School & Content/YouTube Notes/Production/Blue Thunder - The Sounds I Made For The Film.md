# Blue Thunder: The Sounds I Made For The Film

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9HXTfP4yTXs)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9HXTfP4yTXs)

## Synopsis

The origin story of Marinelli's film-scoring career: in 1981, at ~20 years old, he and Brian Banks were brought in by composer Arthur B. Rubinstein to create Synclavier FM "sketch" tracks for the film *Blue Thunder* — one of the first documented uses of digital synthesis on an orchestral Hollywood score (covered at the time by The Hollywood Reporter and Variety), and a genuinely contentious one among orchestral musicians. Covers the actual sketch-to-orchestra workflow, a Roland System 100 filter/flanger/phase-shifter processing chain automated live to follow the written orchestral score's dynamics, and a clear conceptual framing of FM (additive) vs. analog (subtractive) synthesis as complementary rather than competing approaches.

## Techniques

- **FM "sketch" workflow for a composer who doesn't operate the synth himself**: Marinelli and Banks built a palette of unique FM tones on the Synclavier, played them for Rubinstein at their studio, and Rubinstein then returned to his own piano-based writing setup to compose from memory, sketching parts as 6-note lines. Those sketches were then performed back into the Synclavier and printed to multitrack tape, so a live orchestra could overdub on the same tape (two synchronized tape machines — one carrying synth sounds, one carrying the orchestra).
- **Orchestral-instrument emulation via FM timbre + processing, not sampling**: the Synclavier at this point had no digital sampling — every "French horn," "gong/percussion," etc. character was a pure FM tone shaped to read as that instrument, then reinforced by real orchestral doubling (e.g. contrabasses/cellos playing the same line with expressive vibrato) rather than replacing the real instrument.
- **Roland System 100 (semi-modular) as a live-automated expression processor**: an FM tone (raw sawtooth-like character) was run through the System 100's filter, flanger, and phase-shifter, with the filter manually automated in real time WHILE reading off the actual orchestral score's dynamic markings (mezzo forte, forte) — so the synth part's expression tracked the same phrasing the orchestra would later play, rather than a flat, unshaped tone.
- **FM (additive) + analog (subtractive) framed as deliberately complementary, not competing, synthesis approaches**: FM builds complex tones by adding components together; subtractive/analog synthesis starts full-spectrum and removes harmonics with filters — "opposite directions," but combining them intentionally (FM tone processed through an analog filter chain, as demonstrated here) yields a more dimensional palette than either alone.
- **No DAW — the Synclavier's own sequencer WAS the DAW**: no MIDI, no snip-and-move editing; parts had to be planned mentally in advance since nondestructive rearrangement wasn't possible, and hearing the full combination (synth sketch + live orchestra) for the first time on the scoring stage was a real moment of uncertainty each time ("either magic or a redo").
