# 30+ Chords in C major? 🤔 - Boost your chord progressions and jazz harmony

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Sn9ZLRbRlgo)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Sn9ZLRbRlgo)

## Synopsis

A systematic, completionist survey by Jens Larsen: instead of treating "the key of C major" as just its seven diatonic triads, he runs through **all twelve roots of the chromatic scale**, and for **each root plays five chord types — major 7, dominant 7, minor 7, half-diminished, and diminished** — sixty chords total — judging which ones have a genuine, nameable "place" within the key of C major (as a secondary dominant, a borrowed/modal-interchange chord, a passing/auxiliary diminished, a Neapolitan-family chord, or a modulation target) versus which ones are functionally homeless outside of a two-five. He's explicit up front that he isn't being fully consistent about the diminished/half-diminished chords that only ever show up as the "two" of an isolated two-five (he mostly leaves those out, or he'd end up documenting all twelve chords in all twelve keys), and he repeatedly cites real jazz standards as evidence for each chord's actual place rather than asserting it in the abstract — "How High the Moon," "Tune Up," "Solar," "Love for Sale," "Stella By Starlight," "I Could Write a Book" (out of a dream), "All the Things You Are," "It Could Happen to You," "Ladybird," "Turn Around," "Creep" (Radiohead), "Night and Day," "Tenderly," "Old Devil Moon," "My Foolish Heart," and the tonic-diminished-plus-inversions family (already well documented in the vault from other Larsen videos) all get name-checked as the concrete proof for a given chord's function. The through-line: **C major "really" contains far more than 7 chords once you count every secondary dominant, every borrowed-from-parallel-minor chord, every tritone-related dominant, the Neapolitan subdominant, and every inversion of the symmetric diminished-7 family** — this is offered explicitly as a blueprint/checklist for writing original progressions, reharmonizing, and understanding why a "weird" chord in a standard actually belongs.

## Techniques — the full chromatic-root survey (all judged against the key of C major)

**C**
- **Cmaj7 (I)** — the tonic itself, obviously in the key.
- **C7** — not diatonic, but functions as **V7/IV** (auxiliary dominant resolving to Fmaj7): `C → C7 → Fmaj7`. *(general secondary-dominant mechanism — see [[Secondary Dominants]])*
- **Cm (i, borrowed)** — borrowing the tonic minor from the parallel key. Used as a **modulation pivot**: several standards go major-tonic → minor-tonic, reinterpret the minor tonic as a ii chord, then modulate down a whole step. Named examples: **"How High the Moon," "Tune Up," "Solar."** Also a recurring back-and-forth major/minor device in **"Love for Sale."**
- **C half-diminished** — only appears as the "two" chord of an isolated cadence to B♭ (a ii of a ii–V not otherwise in the key) — Larsen explicitly leaves this class out as not having an independent function; he doesn't know a C-major tune that uses it standing alone.
- **C diminished (tonic diminished)** — genuinely has a place, and it's NOT a tonic sound despite sitting on the root: it's actually **a subdominant chord**, specifically an inversion of the **♯IV diminished** (documented fully elsewhere in the vault — see [[Diminished Chords]] § *tonic diminished / ♯4-diminished family*). Song example harmonized with the tonic diminished on the root: **"Stella (By Starlight)."** Larsen's own mental resolution template for the sound: `C → C7 → F♯dim → C` — he associates the color with gospel and calypso.

**D♭ (C♯)**
- **D♭maj7 (♭II, Neapolitan subdominant)** — has a real place, most commonly as a **suspension inside a ii–V ending on the melody's root**, or as a **pivot chord for modulating to another key** — cited in a standard he calls "out of a dream" (likely *I'll Buy That Dream* / similarly titled) and in **"All the Things You Are."** → [[Neapolitan Sixth]]
- **D♭7** — functions in C as the **tritone substitute of G7** ("the trone of the G7") — you'll hear a progression and recognize it as C major specifically because D♭7 reads as G7's tritone sub. → [[Chord Substitution]]
- **D♭ minor / C♯ minor** — no clear defined function Larsen knows of in a C-major song.
- **D♭ half-diminished** — same as the C half-diminished case: only shows up as part of an isolated two-five, not independently.
- **D♭ diminished (C♯ diminished)** — has a place as an **auxiliary/secondary dominant-diminished**, functioning like a diminished-family dominant leading to the **second degree** (Dm): `[progression] → C♯dim → Dm`. Cites **"It Could Happen to You"** as a standard using this move. Fairly common.

**D**
- **Dmaj7** — no place Larsen knows of in C major.
- **D7** — functions as **the dominant of G7** ("dominant of the dominant," V7/V) — very common to sit on D7 for a few bars at the end of a bridge or the first half of a standard.
- **Dm7 (ii)** — diatonic, the 2nd degree.
- **D half-diminished** — comes out of C minor (borrowed). Shows up at song endings as a color chord; because it's the diatonic ii of a minor-key ii–V–i, it's also allowed to appear in cadences and get mentioned in passing. Song reference for the borrowed-minor color: **"[something] — I love you from C"** (transcript garbled on the title).
- **D diminished** — an inversion of B diminished (see B, below); rare to encounter spelled as D diminished specifically. Not treated as its own independent case.

**E♭ (D♯)**
- **E♭maj7 (♭III, borrowed from C minor)** — genuinely has a place, coming out of the parallel minor.
- **E♭7 (♭III7)** — most commonly heard **as a tritone substitute resolving to D minor** (in place of an A7/A7-family V7/ii); can also resolve to the **♭VI** (A♭), another borrowed-minor color, as heard in **"Ladybird"** and **"Turn Around."**
- **E♭ minor 7** — no independent place; only shows up as part of a ♭III–[V7] two-five embedded elsewhere, not standing alone.
- **E♭ half-diminished** — same non-independent-function category as the other borrowed half-diminished chords.
- **E♭ diminished** — another inversion of the ♯IV-diminished family (an inversion of F♯ diminished) — see the C-entry and [[Diminished Chords]] for the full symmetric-family documentation; also functions as a diminished resolving to an auxiliary diminished-dominant target, most commonly the **♯4-down-to-the-ii** move.

**E**
- **Emaj7** — a very common **modulation target** from C (i.e. you'll modulate TO E major often when playing standards), but the chord itself doesn't independently belong to the key of C.
- **E7** — functions as **V7/vi** (auxiliary dominant to Am — this page's own worked companion to [[Secondary Dominants]]'s existing E7→Am example). Also used to **resolve to F major** (the ♭VI-adjacent target, an F major7) rather than straight to Am — cited in a couple of standards with that exact sound, and explicitly in **Radiohead's "Creep."** So E7 carries two distinct resolution targets in C: Am (plain V7/vi) or F major7 (a second, less-obvious dominant function).
- **Em7 (iii)** — diatonic, the 3rd degree.
- **E half-diminished** — only appears in C major as part of an isolated cadence together with an A7 (i.e. as the "two" of a two-five to vi), never independently.
- **E diminished** — a valid, if less commonly cited, cadence-to-the-fourth-degree diminished; Larsen isn't certain of a specific standard that uses it that way off the top of his head but calls it a theoretically valid progression type.

**F**
- **Fmaj7 (IV)** — diatonic, the 4th degree.
- **F7 (IV7)** — most commonly heard as a **tritone-substitute dominant resolving to E minor**: `C → F7 → Em`. Also appears as a **straight blues-style dominant IV** (not resolving anywhere in particular, just a blues color) — cited directly in the original changes to **"Love for Sale"** and **"Tenderly."**
- **F minor (iv, borrowed)** — genuinely in the key (borrowed from C minor); pairs with the same E7→resolves-to-F-major device noted above (the "Creep" progression uses both).
- **F half-diminished** — no independent place in C major.
- **F diminished** — another inversion of the same B-diminished symmetric family (see B, below); treated later rather than separately.

**F♯ (G♭)**
- **F♯maj7** — no place Larsen can think of; can't name a C-major song harmonizing the ♯4/♭5 as a major 7 chord.
- **F♯7** — occasionally functions as a **tritone-substitute dominant resolving to the [third] degree**.
- **F♯ minor 7** — no place he'd say really appears in C major.
- **F♯ half-diminished** — genuinely fairly common: appears as a **descending line from F to F-minor**-type reharmonization move, cited in **"Night and Day."** Also functions as an ending/turnaround color chord.
- **F♯ diminished (the ♯4-diminished — the chord this whole video keeps circling back to)** — the single most theoretically loaded chord in the survey. Called out as giving improvisers the most trouble because, despite LOOKING like a dominant-family diminished, it does **not resolve like a dominant** — it's a **subdominant** chord. Larsen's own hearing/resolution template: `C → C7 → F♯dim → C`, describing the color as gospel/calypso-adjacent. Cross-references the vault's already-extensive [[Diminished Chords]] documentation of this exact chord family (tonic diminished at C, its inversions at E♭/F♯/A, the ♯4-diminished-as-suspension-of-the-I device, the German-augmented-sixth-vs-♯4-diminished naming question) — this video is an independent, earlier (2017) confirmation of the same chord from a totally different framing (the systematic root-by-root survey rather than a single-tune analysis).

**G**
- **Gmaj7** — no independent place in the key of C, though it's a common **modulation target** (or at least a target you modulate toward without fully arriving) — that's part of why the dominant-of-the-dominant (D7→G7) shows up as often as it does. Song references for actually modulating there: **"Stella By Starlight"** (modulates to F, not G, per Larsen's own correction) and **"My Foolish Heart"** (modulates to the key of the dominant).
- **G7 (V7)** — diatonic, the primary dominant.
- **Gm7 (v, borrowed minor dominant)** — appears in a handful of songs, cited **"Old Devil Moon."** More broadly associated with folk-inflected material and the minor key generally, but does turn up in major-key contexts. Its most common appearance in C major specifically is as part of a **cadence to the fourth degree** (i.e. as a minor v resolving toward IV), which is "extremely common."
- **G half-diminished** — no independent place found.
- **G diminished** — no independent place found.

**A♭ (G♯)**
- **A♭maj7 (♭VI, borrowed from C minor)** — a very common borrowed color, cited as part of the Cadence in **"Night and Day."**
- **A♭7 (♭VI7)** — very common in **C minor** specifically, functioning as the **minor-key relative of the dominant-of-the-dominant** — i.e. it's a tritone substitute standing in for D7 on its way to G7 (`A♭7 → [D7 sub] → G7`). Other extended reharmonizations of the same slot exist under the name "double diminished" (not elaborated in depth here).
- **A♭ minor 7** — no independent place in C major.
- **A♭ half-diminished** — no independent place.
- **A♭ diminished** — another inversion of the B-diminished symmetric family (see B).

**A**
- **Amaj7** — no place in C major on its own; becomes relevant once you actually start moving/modulating away, at which point it can point toward A minor and then pivot to feel like a new tonic.
- **A7** — functions as **V7/ii** (auxiliary dominant to Dm), described as "very common."
- **Am7 (vi)** — diatonic, the tonic-substitute 6th degree.
- **A half-diminished** — diatonic to C minor (the parallel key), but Larsen says he's never actually seen it used that way inside a C-major tune specifically — in C minor you might hear it functioning like a vi-type chord, but in C major it only shows up as part of an isolated two-five.
- **A diminished** — another inversion of the F♯-diminished family; rare to encounter used or named that way; doesn't have its own independent place.

**B♭**
- **B♭maj7 (♭VII, borrowed)** — has a real place, especially common in **bossa novas**, and functions analogously to how a **v–I** (five-minor-to-one) works in a minor key — cited melodically in the verse of **"Gingerbread" [transcript uncertain on exact title]**.
- **B♭7 (♭VII7, the "backdoor dominant")** — the most common way it's heard: as a **"four minor" substitute chord** resolving back to the tonic — `IV → ♭VII7(=iv-minor-6/♭VII bass) → I` — spelled out explicitly as **"B♭7 is an F minor 6 chord with a B♭ in the bass."** This is the vault's existing backdoor-dominant device (already documented on [[Diminished Chords]] and cross-linked from [[Borrowed Chords]]) confirmed here from a second, independent Larsen source with the exact same "Fm6 over B♭ bass" derivation.
- **B♭ minor 7** — no independent place except as part of a cadence.
- **B♭ half-diminished** — same non-independent-function category; theoretically an auxiliary-7th-to-the-7th-degree could exist (A♯dim7 → Bø7) but Larsen flags this as unlikely in practice, not impossible.
- **B♭ diminished** — no separate treatment; folds into the same symmetric family as the others.

**B**
- **Bmaj7** — no place in C major except when actively modulating there.
- **B7** — fairly common, most often heard as **part of a chain of tritone substitutions.**
- **Bm7** — doesn't really happen in C major.
- **B half-diminished (the 7th degree, vii°ø… — actually the diatonic vii)** — diatonic, well-defined; appears "once in a while."
- **B diminished (vii°7)** — the **7th degree borrowed from harmonic minor** (the diatonic C-harmonic-minor vii°7) — comes up "once in a while." (Note: B diminished, D diminished, F diminished, and A♭ diminished are all inversions of the same symmetric chord — see the C, D, F, and A♭ entries above and [[Diminished Chords]] for the full symmetric-family writeup already in the vault.)

## Overall conclusion (Larsen's framing)

Once you count every secondary dominant, every chord borrowed from the parallel minor, every ♯4/tonic-diminished inversion, the Neapolitan subdominant, and the handful of chromatic-mediant modulation targets, **the great majority of the 60 chords surveyed (12 roots × 5 qualities) turn out to have SOME well-defined place within the key of C major** — not just the seven plain diatonic triads. Stated use case: this is meant as an **overview/reference and a source of ideas**, not a rulebook to memorize — a blueprint for writing original progressions and tunes, arranging, and building more interesting/surprising intros, and a checklist for recognizing why an unfamiliar chord in a real standard actually belongs where it sits.
