# Single-Note Pentatonic Substitutions Over Changes

Part of [[Guitar Positions]], and a lighter-weight sibling to [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] and [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]: instead of outlining every chord's own arpeggio or scale, **stay on one pentatonic box the whole way through a progression and swap or add exactly one note at the moment each chord changes.** Uses the same movable [[Dorian from the Minor Pentatonic Box|diagonal pentatonic]] fingering system as that page, but the goal here isn't building toward a mode — it's the minimum viable move that makes a static box sound like it's actually tracking the changes. Worked over a **C minor 12-bar blues** (Cm7–Fm7–Ab7–G7), the same form documented on [[Blues Form and Harmony]].

## The core idea: one box, one note change per chord
Most of a chord progression's pentatonic-compatible notes overlap from chord to chord — usually only **one** note in the box actually clashes with the next chord. Find that one note, swap it (or add a neighbor) exactly on the downbeat of the chord change, and leave everything else in the box alone. The payoff: it sounds like you're "playing the changes" — deliberately targeting each new harmony — while the physical vocabulary stays almost entirely the same box shape throughout. Framed directly against full re-scaling per chord: "you don't have to arpeggiate some crazy arpeggio or even play a whole new scale shape, just simply add the [one note] when the chord comes along."

## Setup: why the minor pentatonic is the right base at all
Cm7 (the i chord) is built from **C – E♭ – G – B♭** (1, ♭3, 5, ♭7) — exactly the four notes of C minor pentatonic (C E♭ F G B♭), plus the pentatonic's extra note, **F (the 11/4)**, which is "super colorful" and safe to hit as much as you want over the i chord. So the whole exercise starts from a scale that's already 100% consonant with the tonic chord, and the per-chord moves below are deviations from that safe home base, not a new scale per chord.

**Fingering used:** the diagonal (movable) C minor pentatonic — same string-pair system as [[Dorian from the Minor Pentatonic Box]]'s Fingering 2, here worked on the **D and G strings**: a two-note set at frets **8, 10** on the D string, then a three-note set at frets **8, 11, 12** on the G string. *(Transcript numbers are consistent but unconfirmed against video footage — verify fret-by-fret before drilling; the relational logic below holds regardless of the exact frets.)*

## Fm7 (iv chord) — add A♭, a half step above the three-note set
Fm7 is **F – A♭ – C – E♭** (1, ♭3, 5, ♭7 of F minor). Every note except **A♭** (F minor's own ♭3) is already in C minor pentatonic. **Target:** the instant Fm7 hits, add A♭ — and on the diagonal pentatonic fingering, A♭ sits **exactly one half step above the highest note of the three-note set**, so there's no new shape or arpeggio to learn, just one adjacent fret. All the other pentatonic notes still sound fine against Fm7, so nothing else about the line needs to change. Because the diagonal shape is movable, this "half step above the three-note set" relationship holds at any root/position — it copy-pastes across the neck the same way [[Dorian from the Minor Pentatonic Box]]'s added notes do.

## Ab7 (♭VI chord) — sub the blue note (G♭) for the pentatonic's G
Ab7 is **A♭ – C – E♭ – G♭** (1, 3, 5, ♭7 of A♭ dominant). The new note is **G♭**, and it happens to be **the blue note of the key of C minor** — see [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]] for the general ♭3/♭5/♭7-against-major definition; here it's functioning as the chord's own ♭7, not a passing dissonance. On the diagonal pentatonic, the blue note sits **wedged between the two highest notes of the three-note set** — a fixed, findable location once you know the shape. **Target:** substitute G♭ for the plain G of C minor pentatonic for the duration of the Ab7 bar — G natural "would sound really bad" here, G♭ "sounds perfect." Every other pentatonic note carries over unchanged.

## G7 (V chord) — drop two weak notes, add the leading tone (B natural)
G7 is **G – B – D – F** (1, 3, 5, ♭7). Rather than adding one note on top of the full pentatonic, this one **simplifies down to three notes and changes one of them**:
- **Drop B♭ and C** from the pentatonic — neither is a strong fit against G7.
- **Add B natural** — the **major 3rd of G7** and, critically, the **leading tone of C minor** (major 7th of the key), giving a strong pull back into the following Cm7 bar. Located at the **9th fret, D string** in the worked fingering.
- **Keep the G-string three-note set as is** — root (G), ♭7 (F), and the outlier **E♭/D♯**, which is technically the chord's ♯5 but adds "a little extra tension and pull back to that C minor."
- **B natural is optional, not mandatory** — "you don't have to hit that B every time" — but when used, it can be bent up into or played an octave up for a different color.

## Why this generalizes beyond a 12-bar minor blues
The method doesn't depend on the specific chords — it depends on **comparing each new chord's notes against the pentatonic box already under your fingers and finding the smallest edit that fixes the clash.** That's a chord-by-chord diagnostic (what's actually different here?) rather than a memorized scale-per-chord chart, which is what makes it fast to apply to an unfamiliar progression on the fly.
