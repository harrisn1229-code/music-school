# Steal Like a Bassist — Ear-Training from Known Melodies

BassBuzz's fix for "the rookie scale mistake" that happens right after a beginner has drilled a scale shape and can transpose/extend it: they still think of the scale as a memorized pattern rather than as music, because they never connected it to melodies already living "deep in your bones" from a lifetime of hearing them (children's songs, holiday music, pub sing-alongs). Named after the "good artists copy, great artists steal" line, applied literally: **shamelessly copy and play existing melodies that use the scale you already know**, using your own ear as the bridge between the shape your fingers know and the sound your ear already knows. Taught on [[Bass Scale Shapes — Major, Minor, Pentatonic & Blues]]'s major scale shape, but the process is scale-agnostic — it works on any shape you've already memorized.

## The four-step process

1. **Pick a melody that's deep in your bones and ears already.** Doesn't need to be "cool" — familiarity beats coolness for this exercise, since the whole point is exploiting a melody your ear already fully knows. Worked twice in the source video: "When the Saints Go Marching In" (useful repertoire for jazz/New Orleans-style gigs) and "The Sound of Music"'s "The Hills Are Alive" (picked as an archetypal, near-universally-known major-scale melody). A companion PDF from the source video supplies a "mega list" of simple, mostly-memorized melodies to draw from.
2. **Pick any starting note and play the scale shape starting on that note**, purely to get your ear oriented to that key before singing anything — the specific starting note doesn't matter (demonstrated on both B♭ and E). This is [[Bass Scale Shapes — Major, Minor, Pentatonic & Blues]]'s transposability step, reused here as an ear-orientation step rather than a fretboard-coverage one.
3. **Play the root note again, then sing it out loud** (bad singing explicitly encouraged — the point is pitch reference, not vocal quality), **then sing the melody starting on that note.**
4. **Find those notes on the bass, inside the scale shape**, starting from the correct scale degree of the melody's first note. This is the step that actually links ear to fingers — the process is iterative-by-ear in practice ("oh when... no, too far... oh when the saints... there we go"), not an instant lookup.

## The hidden stumbling block: not every melody starts on scale degree 1

"When the Saints Go Marching In" is an easy first example specifically because its melody starts on the scale's **root note (degree 1)** — step 4 gets a guaranteed win locating the starting note, since it's the same note the scale shape starts on. Most melodies aren't this convenient. "The Hills Are Alive" starts on the **5th scale degree** instead — worked in the video by counting up the shape (1-2-3-4-5) to find where the melody actually begins before singing/searching from there. **This is named directly as the single biggest beginner stumbling block** in the whole process — without knowing which degree a melody starts on, step 4 has no anchor point to search from. The companion PDF solves this by listing each melody's starting scale degree alongside the melody itself, so a beginner doesn't have to derive it by ear alone (a skill the video says comes with time).

## Payoff: this is also an improvisation/soloing method

The same four steps double as a cure for solo-panic: when a bandleader calls for a solo, "find your scale shape, find the melodies you know... and then you can just doodle around those" — using a known melody as a starting anchor for improvisation rather than freezing or noodling randomly. Explicitly framed as not just a beginner-teacher trick but how working musicians actually think — demonstrated with Jaco Pastorius quoting "The Hills Are Alive" mid-solo in a live recording.
