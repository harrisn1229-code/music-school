# 4 More Songs That Skip a Beat

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=I_Q7obp9AHs)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=I_Q7obp9AHs)

## Synopsis

A viewer-suggestion-driven follow-up to [[Songs That Skip a Beat]], adding a crucial structural rule (why it must be exactly ONE bar, not two), an alternate-notation ambiguity, a related-but-distinct device (beat displacement), and the inverse trick (an added rather than removed beat) previewed via "Golden Brown." Third in the arc, followed by [[Songs That Add a Beat]].

## Techniques

- **"9 in the Afternoon" (Panic! at the Disco)** — the 3/4 trick recurs through the verse and chorus end; presenter speculates (without certainty) that a song lyrically about "losing your sense of time" may have deliberately used a time-disorienting rhythmic device to match.
- **"Ring of Fire" (Johnny Cash)** — a very subtle instance in the horn-break bars between verse lines; genuinely ambiguous how to notate/hear it: either a 4/4 bar cut short to 3/4 (a skipped beat), OR — if you double the tempo you're counting at — the same passage reads as a 2/4 bar spliced INTO 4/4 (two extra beats added rather than one removed). Explicitly framed as a real notational ambiguity with no single correct answer, not a trick question.
- **"Before the Lobotomy" (Green Day)** — the central guitar riff alternates 4/4/3/4 for the skip effect, AND separately uses **beat displacement**: a 3-note motif that lands on strong beats the first time is repeated but shifted a half-beat later (onto the offbeat) the second time — same motif, same notes, purely relocated in time, producing a related but mechanically distinct "skip" sensation from the bar-shortening trick. Both devices recur in the vocal melody later in the song. Thematic tie (speculative): a song about a lobotomy pairs naturally with a rhythm that sounds like "something's been sliced out."
- **Critical structural rule, stated explicitly**: the 3/4-trick effect ONLY reads as "a skipped beat" when exactly ONE bar of 3/4 appears before returning to 4/4. Two or more consecutive 3/4 bars stop sounding like an interruption and start sounding like a genuine (if temporary) meter change to 3/4 — demonstrated on **"It Must Be Love" (Labi Siffre)**, which uses two consecutive 3/4 bars at the end of its chorus, producing more of a brief waltz-time feel than a "skip."
- **The inverse trick, previewed**: "Golden Brown" (The Stranglers) is mostly in 3/4, but its intro adds an extra beat to every third line (three bars of 3/4 + one bar of 4/4), giving an off-kilter feel that makes the verse's settling into plain 3/4 feel more satisfying by contrast. Also renotatable as 6/8 with the "extra" material as 7/8 insertions — the presenter prefers the 3/4-plus-added-4/4-bars notation as clearer. A straightened, fully-3/4 version (used for social dancing, where the extra beat was historically dropped) is also demonstrated for comparison.
