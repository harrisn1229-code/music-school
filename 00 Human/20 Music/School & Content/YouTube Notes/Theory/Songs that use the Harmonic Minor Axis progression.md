# Songs that use the Harmonic Minor Axis progression

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LjOKMNNo7HA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LjOKMNNo7HA)

## Synopsis

The companion piece to "She's Electric" — a second named variant of the minor Axis Progression, this one swapping the closing chord for a harmonic-minor-borrowed major V, which makes the loop far more directional than the plain minor-key version. Several bands are shown mixing both variants within the same song.

## Techniques

- **The progression: i–♭VI–♭III–V(major)** (Am–F–C–E, in A minor) — compare the plain minor Axis Progression (Am–F–C–G).
- **The swap**: G (the plain, non-directional ♭VII-adjacent chord) becomes E major, borrowed from harmonic minor's raised 7th degree (G♯). A major V has real dominant pull back to i, which plain minor-key G doesn't — so this variant sounds noticeably more functional/goal-directed.
- **Technically a scale mix**, not pure harmonic minor: the C chord still carries the natural-minor 7th (G natural), while the E chord switches specifically to harmonic minor's G♯ — only the final chord borrows.
- **The chromatic root motion C→E→A** is the glue across the last three chords.
- **Roman-numeral labeling note**: Bennett always relates minor-key numerals back to the major scale (so F = ♭VI, C = ♭III) rather than dropping the flats, specifically so the same numbering system works identically regardless of what scale a song is actually in.
- **Songs mixing BOTH variants (plain and harmonic-minor) within one song**: Green Day — "Holiday," "Troubled Times," "21 Guns"; Bad Religion's "Punk Rock Song" (1996, possibly Green Day's source for the mixing device); Iggy Pop's "The Passenger" (alternates between the two throughout).
