# The Modes of the MELODIC MINOR

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7IwZas8BMLY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7IwZas8BMLY)

## Synopsis

The third video in this modes series, covering the seven modes of (ascending/jazz) melodic minor — the scale form used consistently in both directions, as distinct from the classical melodic minor's different-descending quirk. Continues the chord-scale framing introduced in the harmonic-minor video, with several of these modes given as textbook jazz chord-scale choices.

## What's taught

- **Mode 1 — melodic minor itself**, specifically the "jazz minor" (ascending) form used throughout this video, not the classical ascending/descending version.
- **Mode 2 — Dorian ♭2** (equivalently "Phrygian ♮6"). Dorian's light, ethereal quality plus Phrygian's darker lowered 2nd. Only real-world example found: jazz guitarist John Scofield's *Aeiou* — the melody plays A Dorian ♭2 over a B♭maj7♯5/A chord (a more intuitive respelling of Am6♭9/11), then repeats the same melodic idea a minor third up in C Dorian ♭2.
- **Mode 3 — Lydian augmented.** Lydian with a raised 5th (an augmented 5th, hence the name). Essentially never used as a whole-song scale; its real use is as a **chord scale for maj7♯5** (its companion Ionian ♯5, harmonic minor's 3rd mode, is the other valid choice for the same chord, differing by one degree — see [[Harmonic Minor Modes]]). Example: Herbie Hancock's piano solo on Miles Davis's *Prince of Darkness*, playing G♭ Lydian augmented over a G♭maj7♯11 chord.
- **Mode 4 — Lydian dominant** (Lydian with a lowered 7th). A common misconception attributes The Simpsons theme to plain Lydian — it's actually Lydian dominant, audible at the 7th scale degree where the theme uses the lowered 7th, not Lydian's natural 7th. Second example: *Pretty Ballerina* by The Left Banke, showing both the raised 4th and lowered 7th.
- **Mode 5 — Mixolydian ♭6.** One of the more commonly used modes in this set. Examples: the Faceless Men theme (Valar Morghulis) from *Game of Thrones* (Ramin Djawadi, B Mixolydian ♭6) — the scale's own light-vs-dark duality is used to musically characterize the Faceless Men as characters straddling good and bad; *Twilight* by ELO (C Mixolydian ♭6) and *Single Ladies* by Beyoncé, both worth noting for **how** the mode is implied: the lead vocal melody in both only uses the first 5 (mode-neutral) scale degrees, and it's the underlying chords/bassline/samples that introduce the flat 6 and flat 7 that actually establish the Mixolydian ♭6 color — the melody alone wouldn't tell you which mode you're in.
- **Mode 6 — F♯ Aeolian ♭5** (also called Locrian ♯2/♯9, or the **half-diminished scale**, since a half-diminished — min7♭5 — chord sits naturally on its tonic degree). No song example could be found; explicitly called out as an "academic" scale that exists as a structural byproduct of the mode system rather than one composers organically reach for.
- **Mode 7 — Super Locrian**, aka the **altered scale**. Locrian taken one step further by also lowering the 4th degree, which (respelled) functions as a simultaneous minor-and-major-3rd color, similar in spirit to harmonic minor's Ultra Locrian. Its real use: as the **chord scale for altered dominant chords** (a dominant chord with its 5th and/or 9th raised/lowered — the ambiguous "alt" chord symbol) — the altered scale contains every note needed to cover any of those alteration combinations, which is why it works as a one-size-fits-all choice over an "alt" chord regardless of which specific alteration is meant. Example: Dexter Gordon's solo on *Blue Bossa*, playing G altered over a G7alt chord.
- Running theme across modes 3, 4, 5 and 7: several of melodic minor's modes are primarily useful as **chord scales** (a scale matched to one specific chord type during improvisation) rather than as scales for an entire song/section — a jazz-specific application distinct from how the major-scale modes get used.
