## Synopsis

Jens Larsen's own functional-harmony analysis of "Stella By Starlight" (Victor Young), the tune he explicitly cites — one video later, in this vault's own "All The Things You Are - Harmonic Analysis" note — as the counter-example of a tune that *looks* harmonically busy and mysterious but barely modulates at all, in contrast to All The Things You Are's four real key centers. This is that video. Larsen's core thesis: Stella is famously confusing to players because it wanders through a long chain of chords before ever landing on its home key (it doesn't touch B♭ major until bar 9), and several of its chords look non-functional at a glance (an A7 resolving to Cm7, a G7 that seems to want a whole-tone scale) — but almost every one of those chords is explainable as a **secondary/auxiliary dominant, a backdoor dominant, or a reharmonized diminished chord**, all still inside the single key of B♭, with only **one genuine modulation** in the whole 32-ish-bar form (a brief, unusual pivot up to F major just before the bridge). The video works section by section: confirm the key from the ending, work out a loose/asymmetric AABA-ish form, assign Roman numerals to the parts that are unambiguous, then tackle the tune's two hardest passages — the famous opening (Em7♭5–A7–Cm7–F7) and the pivot into F major before the bridge — as reharmonized/borrowed devices rather than mysteries. It closes with a full bar-by-bar chord-scale recap for soloing, and flags the exact same ♯4°-diminished opening device this vault already has extensively documented from the All The Things You Are page (Larsen even names Stella there as one of the tunes that shares it). ## Techniques

### Confirming the key and form
- **Confirm the key from the key signature + final chord, not from where the melody happens to land — Stella is one of the rare standards whose melody does NOT end on the tonic note, which is exactly why players find the key "mysterious."** Key signature reads B♭ (or its relative, G minor); the last chord of the tune is B♭maj7 — that's what fixes the key as B♭ major, even though the melody's final note isn't the tonic. Same general method already documented from the All The Things You Are video (confirm key from the ending, not the opening) — here extended one step further: sometimes even the ending's *melody* note doesn't confirm it, and you have to fall back on the final chord/key signature instead. → [[Keys]], [[Cadences]]
- **A loose, "vaguely AABA" form — flagged explicitly as not a clean AABA, unlike a textbook-symmetric example, and diagnosed by TWO chord-motion cues rather than a literal melodic repeat.** The tune doesn't really repeat itself section to section, but reads as: an opening ~16-bar statement that develops and expands → a middle 8 that reads as a bridge → a return that "turns into something else" rather than a literal restatement. **Two stated reasons the middle 8 reads as a bridge, given as a general diagnostic for any ambiguous form:** (1) it moves to the **subdominant** (here, the 2nd degree — Cm as ii, treated as pure subdominant, not part of a ii-V) instead of staying near the tonic, and (2) the **harmonic rhythm and melodic movement change pace** compared to the rest of the tune. A companion diagnostic to the All The Things You Are page's melody-first method (which confirms form from melodic *contour*); this one confirms form from *harmonic motion and rhythm*, useful when the melody itself doesn't repeat cleanly enough to read the form by ear alone. → [[Musical Form]]

### The opening — a reharmonized ♯4° diminished chord, not a mystery cadence
- **The tune's famously "unexplainable" opening move (Em7♭5–A7 resolving to Cm7–F7, i.e. A7 going nowhere sensible into Cm7) is a reharmonization of a diminished chord, not a real dominant-to-minor relationship.** Larsen states there's genuinely no functional relationship between A7 and Cm7 on paper — but checking against the tune's original changes, that spot is written as a plain **B♭ diminished chord** (playable over a static B♭ pedal). Em7♭5–A7 is a **reharmonization of that same E° chord**, which is itself the **♯4 diminished in the key of B♭** — the exact same "♯4°/subdominant diminished, reharmonized as a ii-V" device this vault already has extensively documented on [[Diminished Chords]] from the All The Things You Are video (where Larsen names this very tune, "Stella by Starlight," as sharing the identical device on the identical melody-scale-degree — the leading tone of the key sitting in the melody). → [[Diminished Chords]], [[🎵 VICTOR YOUNG - Stella By Starlight]]
- **Corroborated against a second standard using the identical device on the identical melody note — "I Remember You," bar 2, same cadence, same melody note (the 7th of the scale).** Stated as evidence this is a genuine recurring idiom, not a one-off Stella quirk: the 7th of the key sitting in the melody gets harmonized with a ♯4 sound, then resolved as a ii-V(-I). Larsen also loosely name-checks several other standards sharing some version of the same ♯4°-opening color — "Alone Together," "Embraceable You," "It Could Happen to You," and the tag of "There'll Never Be Another You" — flagged here as bare name-drops (no vault notes for these tunes yet, not created per the "don't force a home for a one-off reference" guidance). → [[Diminished Chords]]
- **A practical improviser's workaround for playing over a non-functional-looking cadence like A7→Cm7: don't try to justify the harmony intellectually while soloing — aim your lines at the notes the chord naturally resolves to.** Read the E♭ø–A7 unit as simply "a cadence in D minor" (that's how it resolves melodically even though the written target is Cm7) and build A7 lines that naturally resolve to **D or F** — F is also the tune's own melody note over the following Cm7, so targeting it lines the improvised line up with the composed melody. General principle stated directly: get from one chord to the next by ear/target-note feel first, worry about the full theoretical justification later — sometimes that means resolving to an extension rather than a "basic" chord tone, and that's fine. → [[Guide Tones]]
- **The ♯4° device generalizes into its own modulation tool: cadence on the sharp-4 to jump up a major 3rd into a new key.** Same chord family as the opening reharmonization, used as a genuine modulation device elsewhere in the standard repertoire: from a tune in B♭, cadence through the sharp-4 to land in D major (a major 3rd up) as the new tonic. Cited examples: "I'm Old Fashioned" and "I Love You" (Cole Porter). Same "modulate up a major 3rd via the sharp-4/secondary-dominant machinery" family already documented on [[Modulation (Key Change)]] from the All The Things You Are video's G7→C-major pivot — direct genealogical link between the two videos' devices. → [[Modulation (Key Change)]]

### Cadence chain that never leaves the key — the tune's actual "mystery," demystified
- **Naming device: "auxiliary cadence" / "auxiliary dominant" — Larsen's term for a secondary ii-V or V7 that briefly tonicizes a diatonic chord without the tune ever actually modulating there — used repeatedly through Stella's first 8 bars, which is the real reason the tune feels unmoored before it "arrives" in B♭ at bar 9.** Concrete chain worked in the video: **Fm7–B♭7 is just a ii-V (auxiliary cadence) into E♭**, even though E♭ (IV) is already diatonic in B♭ — it's decorated with its own secondary ii-V rather than approached plainly; the same "auxiliary/secondary ii-V approach to an already-diatonic chord" device recurs at several other points in the form (the video names a second, similar instance without a clean transcript-legible chord label). **General takeaway, stated directly and worth the whole video's framing:** a tune can look and feel harmonically "busy" and mysterious purely from a long chain of secondary/auxiliary dominants and backdoor cadences, while never actually leaving its home key — check whether an unfamiliar-sounding chord is a real modulation or just a decorated approach to a chord you already know before concluding the tune "has no key." Directly the same lesson Larsen references from the other side in the All The Things You Are video (there, contrasting ATTYA's four real modulations against "his own Stella By Starlight analysis" as the harmonically-busy-but-non-modulating counter-example — this is that video). → [[Secondary Dominants]], [[Cadences]], [[Chord Progressions]]
- **The "backdoor dominant" (♭VII7 resolving to I) used explicitly as a functional label, worked as A♭7 → B♭maj7 (i.e. the ♭VII7 of B♭) — a IV-minor-family cadence into the tonic, not a plain V7 approach.** Same iv-minor "way back to the tonic" family already documented on [[Secondary Subdominants]]/[[Borrowed Chords]] from the All The Things You Are video's D♭m6/G♭7 device — here transposed and voiced the other way round: Larsen derives the same chord as an **E♭ minor 6** (the expected "four minor" voicing for a major key), points out E♭m6 and A♭7 are the same notes with a different bass note, and notes it carries a **♯11 in the melody** when voiced as the minor chord — i.e. functions almost as a minor-major/Lydian-flavored color rather than a plain dominant. Recommended solo scale: **E♭ melodic minor** (which is enharmonically the same material as **A♭ Lydian dominant**). Used TWICE in the form — once heading toward the tonic in the opening 16, and again coming out of the bridge's subdominant (Cm) back toward the tonic. → [[Secondary Subdominants]], [[Borrowed Chords]]
- **The half-diminished ii chord (Cm7♭5, not a plain Cm7) explained as borrowed straight from the parallel minor key, not an isolated color choice.** In the run-up to the bridge, the ii chord shows up as **half-diminished** rather than plain minor — Larsen reads this as simply "borrowing" from B♭ minor (the parallel minor of the tune's home key), the same [[Parallel Minor]]/[[Borrowed Chords]] mechanism used elsewhere in the standard repertoire for a darker pre-dominant color. Reused again at the very end of the form (see the closing sequence below), where BOTH the ii chord and its dominant are pulled from the parallel minor together. → [[Borrowed Chords]], [[Parallel Minor]]

### The one real modulation — a brief pivot up to the fifth degree (F major) via iv-minor
- **Stella's ONLY genuine key change in the whole form, called out as an unusual device: a brief modulation UP to the fifth degree (B♭ → F major) using the IV-minor chord as the pivot, just before the bridge.** Mechanism: **Fm → B♭m7 → E♭7 → (lands on F major)** — Larsen frames the trick as "four minor, when you go up [a 4th], is just the tonic of the new key as a minor chord": B♭m (heard as iv of F) doubles as a pivot, and E♭7 then works as F's own backdoor-adjacent dominant approach. Explicitly flagged as uncommon ("not a very common thing to have a four-minor chord as a pivot in a modulation") but one Larsen personally likes. **Named alternate, functionally equivalent substitute offered for comping:** a plain **Gm7–C7** (ii-V of F) works just as well in place of the iv-minor pivot if you'd rather not use it. **Second standard doing the same "modulate up to the fifth degree" move, cited as evidence it's a real (if rare) device:** "My Foolish Heart." **The modulation is brief — the tune is "already back from the dominant" (i.e. already cadencing back toward B♭/D minor) almost immediately after arriving**, which is why it reads as a passing key change rather than a full new section. Straightforward **F major scale** over the visiting tonic. → [[Modulation (Key Change)]]

### The bridge — an augmented-looking dominant that's really just a secondary dominant
- **A chord written to LOOK like it wants a whole-tone/augmented treatment (G7 with a raised 5th written into the chart) is really just a plain secondary/auxiliary dominant of the ii chord — checked against the melody, not assumed from the chord symbol.** The bridge opens on what's written as an augmented-dominant-looking G7 (suggesting whole-tone scale to a lot of players at a glance) — but the melody note over it is a **plain natural 5th**, not the ♭13/♯5 the chart implies, which tells you it's functioning as a straightforward **auxiliary dominant to the ii chord (Cm)**, not an altered/whole-tone color. **General principle: don't assume an altered-looking chord symbol licenses an altered scale — check what the melody is actually doing over it first.** Since it resolves to a genuinely minor target (Cm), the correct soloing source is the **minor-key dominant scale, C harmonic minor**, held for the full two bars — not whole-tone. → [[Scale Modes Reference]], [[Secondary Dominants]]
- **The ii chord in the bridge (Cm) is a plain subdominant, not part of a ii-V — and the way "back home" from a bare subdominant, restated as a general rule already used earlier in the tune, is through the iv-minor chord (or, alternately, straight to the dominant).** Same E♭m6/A♭7 "backdoor"/iv-minor device documented above, reused here as the bridge's own way out, resolving back toward the tonic. → [[Secondary Subdominants]]

### The closing sequence — turning a single ii-V into a descending chain
- **A reusable prolongation device: instead of resolving a ii-V once, restate the SAME ii-V shape as a descending sequence through several roots before finally landing — "taking the harmony and turning it into a sequence."** Worked in the last A section: what would normally be a single ii-V into the tonic instead runs as a chain — **Em7♭5–A7 → (down a step) Dø7–D7 → (down again) Cm7♭5–F7** — before the progression finally resolves. General technique, reusable in original writing or reharmonization: repeat a cadential shape at successive lower roots to prolong the arrival and build a sense of harmonic "descent" before the real resolution, rather than resolving the first time the shape appears. → [[Chord Progressions]]
- **The final ii-V borrowed wholesale from the parallel minor key, both chords at once — not just the ii chord as documented earlier.** The tune's actual final cadence uses **Cm7♭5** (ii, half-diminished — borrowed from B♭ minor, same device as the bridge-approach ii chord above) moving to a **dominant ALSO borrowed from B♭ minor**, rather than the plain major-key ii-V7 — i.e. both halves of the final cadence are pulled from the parallel-minor key together for extra darkness/tension right before the tonic lands. → [[Borrowed Chords]], [[Parallel Minor]]

### Closing framing
- **Big-picture takeaway, stated directly at the end: Stella is "just pretty much related to one key... except for a few bars"** — the tune's reputation for being harmonically mysterious comes almost entirely from the density and pacing of secondary/auxiliary cadences packed into the first 8 bars, not from actual key-hopping. Practical study advice: recognizing whether a given cadence is a plain major ii-V or a borrowed/minor ii-V is "the main thing" worth being able to do fluently before tackling a tune like this. Composer aside (name-drop only, no technique attached): Larsen rates this Victor Young tune as noticeably more harmonically sophisticated than Young's other standards, name-checking "Beautiful Love" and "Golden Earrings" as comparatively simpler — not routed anywhere, pure opinion/context.
