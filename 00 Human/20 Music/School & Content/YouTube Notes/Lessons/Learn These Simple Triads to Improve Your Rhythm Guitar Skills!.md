# Learn These Simple Triads to Improve Your Rhythm Guitar Skills!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oZWZ81aHoQU)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oZWZ81aHoQU)

## Synopsis

A short, focused rhythm-guitar lesson that teaches how to build a full I–V–vi–IV progression entirely out of small three-note triads confined to one string set (D, G, B), and how to find three different neck positions for that same progression so a rhythm part can be relocated without hunting for new shapes. Jack Ruch opens with the theory groundwork (a triad is root-3rd-5th, found by taking the 1st/3rd/5th degree of the major scale for a major triad, 1st/♭3rd/5th for minor), then works a single example progression — **A major – E major – F♯ minor – D major** (I–V–vi–IV in A) — through three separate close-position triad maps on the D-G-B strings, and finally shows how to stitch adjacent chords together with passing notes drawn from the one parent A-major scale that all four chords belong to (since they're all diatonic to A). The video's real payoff is the structural insight that within each of the three positions, every chord in the progression uses the *same* triad-inversion shape (5th-root-3rd in position 1, root-3rd-5th in position 2, 3rd-5th-root in position 3) — so learning one hand-shape per position is enough to play the whole progression there, which is exactly why the four chords land "right next to each other" in every position. This is presented explicitly as a band-context rhythm-part tool: a way to stay out of another guitarist's way (who might be playing big open chords) by taking a leaner, differently-voiced part, and as a general fretboard-visualization exercise — building a mental blueprint of where triads live so you can improvise rhythm parts with freedom instead of being tied to one spot. No tone/pickup/production content in this one (contrast with the same channel's [[Make Your Rhythm Guitar Sound Like A Record]], which covers the pickup-per-layer separation move for a very similar progression). ## What's taught

**1. Triad basics (theory recap).**
- A triad = a three-note chord: root, 3rd, 5th.
- **Major triad:** take the 1st, 3rd, and 5th notes of the major scale. Worked in A: A major scale gives root **A**, 3rd **C♯**, 5th **E** → A major triad = A-C♯-E.
- **Minor triad:** take the same scale degrees but from the minor scale (root, ♭3rd, 5th) — worked example: A-C-E = A minor triad (root, minor 3rd, 5th).
- **Inversion** = reordering which triad tone is lowest. The same three notes (A, C♯, E) can be stacked root-3rd-5th, 3rd-5th-root, or 5th-root-3rd, and each inversion is playable on different string sets/neck positions — "tons of ways" to play the same triad all over the neck.

**2. The example progression: I–V–vi–IV in A, one string set only.**
- Progression used throughout: **A major → E major → F♯ minor → D major**, described as "a really simple, very common type of chord progression."
- Constraint for the whole lesson: map every chord's triad **on the D string, the G string, and the B string only** — because a triad is 3 notes, that's exactly one note per string on this string set.
- Stated use case: band-context rhythm guitar — when another guitarist is already covering low, full open-chord voicings, these lean triad shapes "take up a different space" instead of doubling the same sound.

**3. Position 1 — the 5th-root-3rd shape family (lowest position worked).**
- **A major:** 5th (E) on the D string, root (A) on the G string, 3rd (C♯) on the B string — i.e. **5th–root–3rd**, bottom to top.
- **E major:** root (E) on D string, 3rd (G♯) on G string, 5th (**open B string**) — root–3rd–5th (note: E major's own shape here reads root-3rd-5th because the open B string anchors it; the position as a whole is still described as sitting "right next to" the A shape).
- **F♯ minor:** root, minor 3rd, 5th, one shape up from E major, same string set.
- **D major:** 3rd–5th–root, one more shape up — "we just move one note" from F♯m to get there.
- Payoff stated directly: "all these triads are right next to each other and very closely connected" — the whole I-V-vi-IV progression fits inside a roughly one-fret-wide zone on these three strings.

**4. Position 2 — the root-3rd-5th shape family (mid-neck).**
- **A major:** root position — root, 3rd, 5th stacked bottom-to-top (root-3rd-5th).
- **E major:** 3rd–5th–root, directly below/adjacent.
- **F♯ minor:** minor-3rd–5th–root.
- **D major:** 5th–root–3rd.
- Same "closely connected, no big bar chords needed" payoff repeated for this position.

**5. Position 3 — the 3rd-5th-root shape family (highest position worked).**
- **A major:** 3rd–5th–root ("on top").
- **E major, F♯ minor, D major** each follow immediately adjacent, same shape family, same string set.
- Explicit takeaway: "we don't have to move around or jump around or play big uh cumbersome bar chords. We can literally make this progression anywhere we want" — i.e. any of the three positions is a complete, self-sufficient way to play the whole progression.

**6. Connecting the triads with scale-tone fills (the "glue" technique).**
- Because A, E, F♯m and D are **all diatonic to A major**, the *entire* connecting vocabulary between any two triads in any position is just **the A major scale fingered in that same position** — no separate lick vocabulary needed per transition.
- Method: play the outgoing chord's triad, insert a short passing-note run pulled from the A major scale shape that sits in the same position, land on the next chord's triad. Demonstrated once per position (positions 1, 2, and 3), each time first playing the bare triads through the full progression, then repeating with scale-tone fills woven between the chord changes to build "connected rhythm parts" and usable fills.

**7. Practice method (the video's own stated order).**
1. Learn and drill **one position at a time** — get comfortable with all four triad shapes (A-E-F♯m-D) in that position before adding anything else. Stated reason: "practice these types of things in one position at a time and get really comfortable with each position... as you're moving around you're really fluent in every spot on the neck."
2. Within a position, first play the **bare triads** through the whole progression (no fills), then repeat adding the **A-major-scale connecting fills** between chord changes.
3. Repeat that same two-pass drill (bare triads, then fills) for **each of the three positions** separately.
4. Once all three positions are solid individually, **string them together** — move freely between positions mid-progression rather than staying locked to one, using the scale-tone connectors to bridge not just adjacent chords but adjacent *positions* too. This is the stage where "you're not tied down to any one position... you can play with total freedom."
5. End goal stated directly: a visual/mental "blueprint" of where the triads sit on the fretboard, so while improvising a rhythm part you can look at the neck and know where to go next, rather than hunting.

**Drill, restated as an actionable practice routine (built directly from steps 3–7 above):**
- Pick any diatonic I–V–vi–IV-type progression (or use A–E–F♯m–D as given).
- On the D-G-B strings only, find the position-1 shape for the I chord, then locate the nearest triad for each subsequent chord (V, vi, IV) on the same three strings — expect them to land within about a fret or two of each other because they're voice-led as the *same inversion shape*.
- Play the four bare triads through the progression at a slow tempo until clean, then add 2–4 note scale runs (drawn from the key's major scale fingered in that same position) between each triad to connect them, and repeat.
- Move up the neck and repeat the whole process to find position 2 (a different inversion-shape family) and position 3 (a third family) for the same four chords.
- Finally, play through the full progression multiple times, freely switching between the three positions from chord to chord, using scale fills as connectors both within a position and when jumping positions — this is the "total freedom" stage the video ends on.
