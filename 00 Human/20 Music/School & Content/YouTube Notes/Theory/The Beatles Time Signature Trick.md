# The Beatles Time Signature Trick

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rC3_0K1vGF0)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rC3_0K1vGF0)

## Synopsis

A survey of Beatles songs that slip occasional odd-length bars into an otherwise steady meter — mostly adding or dropping a single beat rather than committing to a whole new time signature — plus one genuine polymeter case in *Happiness Is a Warm Gun*.

## What's taught

- **Single added beat (4/4 → one-off 5/4 bar)**: *Across the Universe* (John adds a rest-beat at the end of a vocal phrase, giving the line room to breathe before the next one — removing it makes the vocal feel rushed); *Don't Let Me Down* (a 5/4 bar at the start of each verse, during an unaccompanied vocal moment — likely an unconscious add, since there's no band to fall out of sync with at that point).
- **Half-bar insertions and compound-time shifts**: *Strawberry Fields Forever* — one-off 2/4 (half-bar) insertions, plus shifts into 6/8 (compound time, main pulse = dotted quarter note, vs. 4/4's simple-time quarter-note pulse — the 6/8 bars read as a momentary slowing of the perceived pulse even though the tempo doesn't change) and a closing line trimmed to 3/4 instead of 4/4 ("Strawberry Fields forever").
- **Deliberate skipped beat as a compositional effect**: *Back in the USSR* — two bars of 3/4 near the end of the second chorus create a "record skip" feel, as if Paul is repeatedly cut off mid-phrase before finally completing it on the third pass.
- **A "bloated" bar**: *Martha My Dear* — read as a 5/4 bar followed by a return to 4/4, giving the phrase an extra beat before resolving.
- **Frequent light insertions across a whole song**: *Blackbird* (scattered single bars of 2/4 and 3/4 throughout); *Two of Us* (a 2/4 bar mid-verse; the chorus shifts 4/4→3/4 for several bars, capped by a 2/4 bar before returning to 4/4).
- **Elaborate multi-section meter changes + genuine polymeter**: *Happiness Is a Warm Gun* — opening section in loose 4/4 with extra beats added in; a clean 3/4 section; a section alternating 9/4 and 10/4 (equivalently, 3/4 with occasional 4/4 bars); a final half-tempo section that drops from 4/4 into 3/4 — and in that last switch, **Ringo doesn't follow the band into 3/4**, staying in his own 4/4 kick/snare pattern against the rest of the band's 3/4, creating a real polymeter (two simultaneous meters) until the two rhythms resync four bars later. Notated on paper as one meter (3/4) with Ringo's part force-fit into that grid, since sheet music can't show two time signatures literally at once. Speculated (not confirmed) to have originated as Ringo missing a meter-change cue and the band keeping the resulting effect.
- **George Harrison example**: *Here Comes the Sun*'s instrumental passage — a repeating pattern of 3×(3/8) + 2/8 + 4/4 + 2/4 + 3/8, felt as natural rather than jarring because the melody is built to fit the pattern.
- Flags *Good Morning Good Morning* (Sgt. Pepper) as the band's most metrically elaborate song, covered in a separate dedicated video (not summarized here).
