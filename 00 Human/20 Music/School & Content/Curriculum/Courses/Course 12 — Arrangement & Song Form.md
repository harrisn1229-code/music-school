# Course 12 — Arrangement & Song Form

How a loop becomes a song. The three things standing in the way, in the order they actually come up: the loop that stops being interesting, the decision about what leaves and when, and the joins between the pieces.

## The lessons

### Lesson 1 — Loop-itis and the Second Idea

**Read**
- [ ] [[Arrangement and Song Form]] → *The loop-itis diagnosis*, *Writing the B section*
- [ ] [[Musical Form]]

**The material**
- The pattern is specific: make a loop, love it, and thirty to sixty minutes later it feels worthless. **[[ARR-C01 — A boring loop needs a B section, not a new idea|The idea was never bad — it was alone.]]** A loop that bores you isn't asking for a new idea; it's asking for a B section.
- **[[ARR-C02 — How to actually write the B section|The method is three steps.]]** Duplicate the drums and strip everything else. Write a **complementary** part — different chords, opposite density, different mood. Then test **the return**: playing out of B back into A should feel like *we're home*.
- That test is the step people skip, and it's the whole point. **The right B section makes the A section better** — if the return is flat, B isn't finished.
- **[[ARR-C03 — Two harmonic routes into a B section|Two harmonic routes in]]**, both solving the same problem of *new but not random*. The **relative key** — up three semitones from a minor root — gives a different centre with identical scale tones, so the return can't clash.
- The **withheld chord** — audit which diatonic chords the verse and chorus never used, then open the bridge on exactly that one, because the ear hasn't heard it yet in this song.
- One honest ceiling: no amount of subtractive variation on a single loop sustains a song. A genuinely second loop becomes unavoidable, and **most attempts at one will be discardable.** That's the cost of the method, not a sign it's going wrong.

**Concepts**
- [[ARR-C01 — A boring loop needs a B section, not a new idea]]
- [[ARR-C02 — How to actually write the B section]]
- [[ARR-C03 — Two harmonic routes into a B section]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — give the three steps and the test they end in. Then name the two ways to choose B-section chords and what each one guarantees.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — open an abandoned project. Duplicate the drums, strip everything else, and write a B section using the withheld-chord route. Loop B→A four times and rate the return honestly.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 12 — Lesson 1 Test]]

### Lesson 2 — Scheduled Absence and the Energy Curve

**Read**
- [ ] [[Arrangement and Song Form]] → *Sequencing one idea (subtraction scheduling)*, *Jam long, cut the fat*, *The simplicity referee*, and the energy-curve and section-length bullets

**The material**
- "Let the Beat Build" is the masterclass: hi-hat → clap → clap+snares → kick → bass → everything at the hook. **One idea, minutes of interest, purely from when things enter.**
- The principle inverts the instinct: **[[ARR-C04 — Arrangement interest is scheduled absence|arrangement interest is scheduled absence]], not added layers.** Schedule the absences — pull the kick, the 808, all the drums, the melody. A middle ground between gone and full is attenuating an element's top end rather than muting it outright.
- Then the shape of the whole thing. **[[ARR-C05 — The energy curve — one apex, and pull back before the end|Start deliberately low so there's room, build to exactly one apex, then pull back after it.]]** Sustaining the peak through to the outro reads as exhausting rather than climactic.
- Change **3–4 elements simultaneously** at each major boundary so it reads as a jump rather than a drift, and punctuate it with a crash.
- **[[ARR-C06 — Section-length conventions, and breaking them on purpose|Section lengths are conventions worth knowing precisely]]**, because breaking one only reads as deliberate against a known pattern. House is flat 16-bar. Pop holds a proportional template — roughly a 4-bar intro, with verse, pre and chorus about double that — and the specific move that **the chorus gets longer each time it returns.**
- And the referee: **[[ARR-C10 — Too busy is a relative term, and the artist sets it|too busy is a relative term]].** Lil Baby cut Kenny Beats off at five to seven sounds. The extra perc layer always matters less than it feels like it does while you're producing.

**Concepts**
- [[ARR-C04 — Arrangement interest is scheduled absence]]
- [[ARR-C05 — The energy curve — one apex, and pull back before the end]]
- [[ARR-C06 — Section-length conventions, and breaking them on purpose]]
- [[ARR-C10 — Too busy is a relative term, and the artist sets it]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — say how many elements change at a major boundary and what kind. Then say where the apex goes and what has to happen after it.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — take a full 8-bar loop and stagger every element's first entrance across 32 bars without writing a single new note. Then draw the energy curve of a reference track next to your own and find where they diverge.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 12 — Lesson 2 Test]]

### Lesson 3 — Transitions

**Read**
- [ ] [[Arrangement and Song Form]] → the transition bullets
- [ ] [[Automation]] and [[Ear Candy and Hiding Synths]]

**The material**
- The finding worth internalising: an analysis of roughly 100 reference tracks for what makes transitions cohesive found **most weren't leaning on risers, swooshes or reverse cymbals at all.**
- **[[ARR-C07 — Automation is the transition; risers are the last step|The real technique is automating existing devices across the section itself]]** — filters, detune, sends, wet/dry. Risers are fine as deliberate icing once the parts already connect; reaching for one first just papers over a join that doesn't work.
- Three usable shapes: a **micro drop** (an instant filter wet/dry snap plus a gradual percussion sweep), a **macro drop** (maximum contrast via kick filtering, bass removal, slight detune and a timbre shift), and a **wet/dry drop** (send automation contrasting wet against dry, wide against mono, instead of tone). Mine your own elements for a sweep before importing one.
- Two devices do most of the remaining work. **[[ARR-C08 — Carry one element unchanged across the boundary|Carry one element unchanged across the boundary]]** — a motif, a signature sound — so the listener has something to hold onto while everything else changes.
- And **[[ARR-C09 — Strip the layers right before the payoff|strip every added layer right before the payoff]]**, leaving only the core element to carry through. The sudden reduction reads as tension release rather than as a mistake.
- Subtraction reads as suspense generally. A listener can't track many simultaneous parts, so less makes what remains read as more — and a big reverb over an emptied section sells it as intentional.

**Concepts**
- [[ARR-C07 — Automation is the transition; risers are the last step]]
- [[ARR-C08 — Carry one element unchanged across the boundary]]
- [[ARR-C09 — Strip the layers right before the payoff]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — name the three automation-driven transition shapes and what each contrasts. Then say what the last thing to happen before a drop is.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — take the worst transition in a current project. Delete every riser and impact in it. Rebuild it with filter and send automation, one carried element, and a strip before the payoff.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 12 — Lesson 3 Test]]
