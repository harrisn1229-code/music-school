# Writing Michael Jackson's "Beat It" - From Idea To Greatness

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lgMPx6UbMf8)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lgMPx6UbMf8)

## Synopsis

Matt Forger on the origin of "Beat It": Quincy Jones kept asking for "a My Sharona" — meaning that song's crossover pop-rock energy and intensity, not its content — and Jackson interpreted the brief as a feeling to hit rather than a template to copy, landing on an edgier, angrier vocal quite unlike the reference. The conversation then turns to Jackson's own account of his songwriting process: consciously setting a creative goal, deliberately not over-rehearsing it, and trusting the idea to arrive on its own — the same self-conditioning method he used before writing "Billie Jean" ("I have to write a song with a driving, hooky bassline"). Quincy Jones's parallel rule: capture a spontaneous idea the instant it strikes, because the window is fragile.

## What's taught

- **A reference brief names an energy, not content to copy** — "we need a My Sharona" meant hit that song's crossover intensity, not write something like it; "Beat It" shares none of the referenced song's actual musical content.
- **Set a specific creative parameter, then deliberately stop consciously working it** — state the goal ("driving hooky bassline," "rock edge") and let it sit in the back of the mind rather than actively rehearsing toward it; over-rehearsing in conscious thought is described as producing tension that blocks the spontaneous result from arriving.
- **Capture a spontaneous idea immediately, without exception** — Quincy Jones's rule, regardless of when inspiration strikes: write it down or record it right away, because the moment is fragile and won't reliably return.
