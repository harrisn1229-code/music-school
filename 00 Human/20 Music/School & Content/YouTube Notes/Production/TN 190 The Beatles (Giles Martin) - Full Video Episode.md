## Synopsis

Giles Martin — George Martin's son, and Abbey Road's chief remixer for the Beatles catalogue since *Get Back* and *Revolver* — walks through the making of *Rubber Soul: New Special Edition* (2026), the latest in a "demixing" deep-remix series he's been running for roughly ten years. He explains the actual mechanics of that process (adapted from Peter Jackson's dialogue-isolation work on *Get Back*, itself adapted from police surveillance-audio forensics), and is unusually candid — twice self-deprecating about not wanting to "talk technique" — about the philosophy behind it: these records were engineered and creatively decided *for mono*, so his stereo remixes deliberately center drums and vocals rather than hard-panning them Capitol-style. Woven through the philosophy is a genuinely rare, forensic track-by-track breakdown of three Rubber Soul songs' actual 4-track tape layouts and recording order — **Drive My Car**, **Norwegian Wood**, and **In My Life** — including a newly-discovered George Martin organ take that predates the famous half-speed baroque piano solo. The most concretely reusable idea in the whole episode is a throwaway line about quality control: Giles phase-cancels every demixed stem set against the original mix before trusting it, to prove the separation process added or removed nothing.

## Techniques

### The demixing pipeline — how these remixes are actually built
- **Origin story.** The underlying tech traces to Peter Jackson's *Get Back* restoration: his team adapted forensic techniques used on police surveillance tapes (isolating a hidden gangster's speech from background noise) to isolate spoken dialogue from Beatles Nagra location tapes. Giles asked "why don't we apply it to music?" — first tested removing crowd screams from a live recording for the *Eight Days a Week* documentary, then matured during *Get Back* itself for separating drums from guitars and bass on the same messy location tapes.
- **First fully demixed album: *Revolver*.** The *Sgt. Pepper's* demix (his first full-album job, done three months after his father George Martin's death) exploited something specific to that album rather than relying purely on AI separation: because *Sgt. Pepper's* was mixed in mono via **four-track bounces** (sub-mixing several instruments down together to free up tape tracks for overdubs), Giles could manually re-sync the **surviving pre-bounce four-track masters** — an earlier, unbounced tape generation — and recover the pre-submix stems directly from archival tape rather than software-separating them from the finished bounce. "Demixing" here means tape archaeology as much as it means AI source separation; which technique applies depends on what actually survives in the archive for a given album/session.
- **The engine itself is bespoke, not off-the-shelf.** Described as "facial recognition for sound" — proprietary, in-house software trained on what a given instrument/voice actually sounds like, distinct from commercial demixing (he name-checks Logic's built-in stem separation as the more "logical," consumer-grade version of the same underlying idea).
- **Phase-cancellation as a source-separation integrity check — the single most transferable technique in this episode.** After demixing a source into stems, sum the resulting stems back together and phase-invert against the original mixed/bounced source; a correct separation should cancel to silence. "We tend to always make sure that everything phase cancels... if I sum all of the demix multitracks from my four track and if I then phase cancel them I should hear nothing. I don't want the process to add anything or take away anything from what we have." Tonal processing (EQ, etc.) only happens *after* this raw-integrity check passes — the working analogy he uses is a baked cake: "I've got the flour, eggs, etc., and they all weigh exactly the same as the ingredients that went in there." → generalizes directly to any AI stem-separation tool: before trusting a rebalanced or split stem from **[[RX Music Rebalance]]** or **[[Ozone 9 Master Rebalance]]**, sum the separated stems and phase-cancel them against the original source to confirm the split didn't silently alter anything.

### Mixing philosophy — respecting mono while gaining stereo
- **Central thesis: this whole catalogue was engineered and creatively decided for mono, not stereo.** Very few UK households owned stereo players in 1965; the record wasn't made "with stereo in mind." The familiar hard-panned "instruments on one side, vocals on the other" Beatles stereo mixes were **Capitol Records' American commercial decision**, not the band's creative intent — a fact that reframes every remix choice that follows. Giles's own rule: **put drums and lead vocal in the center (where mono would have carried them), and spread only the surrounding instruments to the sides** — "respecting the mono... but making a stereo version of the mono."
- **Stated rationale #1 — power.** "If you want more power from something like a drum kit, it coming out of two speakers as opposed to one is a better option" — a genuinely simple, reusable justification for centering (not spreading) the elements that need to hit hardest.
- **Stated rationale #2 — headphones.** Most listening today happens on headphones, where a hard-panned mono-era mix (a whole instrument jammed into one ear) reads as distracting and dated rather than characterful; centering the power elements avoids that specific failure mode on modern playback.
- **Target-audience framing, stated explicitly:** deliberately mixed so a new/younger listener doesn't dismiss the record as "old" — pointed out that the band were 24–26 years old making Rubber Soul, i.e. objectively younger than most current pop stars, so an old-sounding mix undersells genuinely young musicians' work.
- **Don't separate elements just because the technology now lets you.** Case study: on "Yellow Submarine" (*Revolver*), acoustic guitar and drums were tracked together on one mic/track; splitting them because demixing now technically allows it made the mix sound *wrong* — "it sounded like there was something missing... like I left my keys behind." He put them back together. Some tracked-together element pairs carry a transient/timing relationship that only reads correctly combined — **the fact that a separation is technically possible is not a reason to perform it.**
- **Repositioning or isolating a previously-buried element makes it read as newly added, even when it was always audible.** Demixing recovered isolated finger-snaps buried in a backing-vocal track on "Here, There and Everywhere" (*Revolver*, also this session's demix pass) — once given their own channel and a distinct position, listeners (Giles included, on relistening to the original) perceived them as "new," despite being present in the original mono/stereo mix all along.
- **Panning something far left or right makes it read as louder, even at unchanged gain.** Stated directly as a companion caution to the finger-snap case above — worth remembering whenever a demixed/isolated element gets a *wider* pan position than it originally had; it will read as boosted even though the fader never moved. → confirms this vault's existing [[Width and Stereo]] entry on panning-reads-as-loudness from the opposite direction (there: centering everything competes for one position; here: spreading a previously-buried element out of that crowd makes it audible for the first time).
- **Raw outtakes and alternate takes are deliberately left un-demixed.** Giles treats bonus/session material as a documentary artifact, not a mix to finish: "I tend to not even mix them really... it's quite good to hear what's raw, raw tape and be honest about what's there." The only processing applied is light denoising, and only when it's needed to bring up audible studio dialogue/chatter for clarity — never spatial placement or tonal polish to a finished-mix standard.

### "Drive My Car" — 4-track breakdown + new mix
- **Original 4-track layout, in recording order:** **Track 1** — Paul (rhythm guitar) + Ringo (drums) + John (tambourine), with George's live guide lead-guitar intro captured on the same track. **Track 2** — George's *re-recorded* lead guitar overdub, done in the same pass as Ringo's cowbell. **Track 3** — lead + harmony vocals (recorded to picture of the finished Track 1). **Track 4** — bass (Paul) + doubled backing vocals, recorded **last**, deliberately, specifically to make the bass as loud as possible in the final mono mix — an explicit attempt to compete with the "Motown bass sound" coming out of America. Bass-tracked-last is called out by Giles as genuinely rare practice.
- **New 2026 stereo mix** applies the mono-respecting philosophy above directly: drums and vocals centered, the guitar tracks (George's overdub lead, Paul's original rhythm guitar) spread across the stereo field rather than hard-left as on the original Capitol-style stereo pressing.

### "Norwegian Wood" — take evolution + 4-track breakdown + new mix
- **Alternate-take evolution, documented on the deluxe edition itself:** Take 1 is acoustic guitar + vocal only, no sitar. An intermediate take pushes toward *full* Indian instrumentation and, in Giles's own word, "goes too far" — overshoots the idea. Take 3 pulls back toward the version everyone knows. A rare, audible case of a band trying a new idea at full strength first, then finding the right *dose* of it on a later take.
- **Original 4-track layout:** **Track (main)** — John's acoustic guitar + vocal, recorded live with **Paul's bass bleeding into the same mic/track** (Paul was playing bass in the same room while John sang — the bleed is treated as texture, not a defect to be scrubbed). **Track 2** — George's sitar. An overdub track layers claps + a foot-stomp-style kick + tambourine (Ringo) with leg-slapping percussion (John), all bleeding together as one "Indian flavour" percussion composite.
- **New stereo mix:** John's acoustic guitar moved off-center while his vocal stays dead-center (on the original mono-era stereo mix, guitar and vocal shared *one* side together) — Giles is explicit that guitar and vocal from the *same performer's same take* "have to be together," a direct instance of the Yellow Submarine caution above: separating a single take's own guitar-and-vocal relationship too far breaks it, even though demixing makes it technically possible. Bass panned only slightly toward the guitar's side rather than hard-separated, for the same reason. George's 12-string panned hard opposite for real width. The claps/leg-slap composite gets a *slight* stereo split for extra space. His own framing for these deliberate micro-splits of a naturally-blended performance: **"do unnatural things to make things more natural — that's my trademark."**

### "In My Life" — the baroque piano solo, confirmed, plus a newly found alternate
- **Newly discovered during this remix's archival work: George Martin's FIRST attempt at filling the famous piano-solo "hole" was an organ part**, probably a Hammond or Lowrey (unconfirmed from the recording itself), played in a rock-and-roll style. This take had never surfaced before this project. The 4th track on Beatles 4-tracks was routinely left as a deliberate "hole" for a late overdubbed solo, since — same underlying reason as everything above — the record was being made for mono and didn't need the solo isolated from the rest of the band.
- **The famous solo's mechanism, confirmed directly by George Martin's own son.** George Martin scored a baroque/harpsichord-style counterpoint part too fast and complex to physically play live at normal tempo, then had the tape machine **slowed to half speed (an octave down)**, played the passage comfortably at that slower tempo, and sped the tape back to normal for playback — pitch and tempo both double back up together, producing both the harpsichord-like timbre and the "impossible" playing speed. Giles notes his father already knew this half-speed-piano-reads-as-harpsichord trick from his own pre-Beatles novelty-record work (cited: the *Sparky's Magic Piano*-style comedy recordings) — the technique predates *In My Life* by years in George Martin's own back catalogue, it wasn't invented for this session. → confirms and extends what this vault's own [[🎵 THE BEATLES - In My Life]] page already documents; owned lane for the same varispeed mechanism: [[Varispeed 3.0]].
- **4-track layout:** one track carries guitar, bass and a "ghosted" guide vocal from an early take; the actual released lead vocal is a **replacement** — John sang live to the existing backing track and the guide vocal was wiped and re-sung on take 3, standard practice at this session, *before* Ken Townsend's [[Artificial Double Tracking (ADT)]] existed at Abbey Road. The doubled harmony vocals on the bridge are two genuinely separate live takes of the same part, not an ADT double.
- **Whole-song mixing principle, stated as Giles's overriding rule, not just applied here:** identify the single most important carrier of a song's message — here explicitly the lead vocal, "that's what everything is... it's the message of the song" — and build the *entire* mix around keeping that element centered, panning everything else in relation to it rather than balancing every element independently. → [[Masking and Carving]].
- **Over-correction caution, told as a direct cautionary parallel while discussing this mix:** remixing *All Things Must Pass* (George Harrison, unrelated project) for a film, too much kick drum in isolation made the whole song "immediately sound leaden and lumpy... doesn't float anymore" — a reminder that a mix decision has to be judged against the whole arrangement, never by how good one element sounds soloed.
- **A pan decision that changed a song's emotional read, told as a second real example in the same breath:** remixing "With a Little Help from My Friends" (*Sgt. Pepper's*), the original hard-panned Ringo's bass to one side; Giles moved it to the center — and Ringo's vocal, which reads as lonely and friend-seeking, stopped sounding lonely the moment the bass filled the space beside it. "As soon as I did it, Ringo didn't sound lonely anymore... I've got to put the bass on one side because Ringo should sound lonely on the other." A concrete case of a panning choice altering a song's *emotional* narrative, not just its tonal/spatial balance — frequency and spatial proximity between two elements changing what the listener perceives the relationship between them to be.

### Gear, tools, and general philosophy
- Asked directly what's the most crucial piece of equipment for this project, Giles resists naming one: cites **Pultec-style tube EQ** as a favorite (no specific setting given — a name-drop, not a technique), **ATC monitors** as his personal reference speakers (unsponsored, a friendship/taste choice, not a technical claim), and otherwise credits taste and restraint over any specific box or plugin.
- **Warns explicitly against "mixing with your eyes."** Some newer plugins ship flashy, animated visualizers/meters that create a false impression of doing something significant — "it sort of creates this star scope... that must be doing something terrific" — when the actual audible effect may be minor. Vaguely describes something like a multiband processor with an exciting-looking display as the example that prompted this. Judge by ear, never by the animation.
- States that condenser mics (47/48-series) and tape delay/[[Artificial Double Tracking (ADT)]] became bigger factors for the band from *Revolver* onward, but weren't yet in play on *Rubber Soul* — this era was still driven mostly by instrumentation and arrangement choices, not studio processing technique.
- **Repeated framing across the whole interview: he actively avoids "technique talk" because the real skill, in his view, is a relationship/taste judgment, not a stack of named tricks.** "I don't want you to... listen to the mix, [I want you to] listen to the song." Worth holding as a deliberate counterweight against how technique-forward the rest of this vault's enrichment naturally reads — sometimes the actual lesson from a genuinely great engineer is closer to *listen more, process less* than a new setting to copy.
