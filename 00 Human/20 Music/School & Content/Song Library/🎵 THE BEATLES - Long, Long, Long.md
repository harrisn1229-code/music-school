# Long, Long, Long — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

A George song in which **nearly every section refuses to begin on the tonic, and several refuse to end on it either** — an approach-avoidance pattern in the harmony mirroring the lyric's inability to face its subject head-on. The style is a three-way blend of jazz waltz, folk and period psychedelia: ternary meter, ninth chords, acoustic backing, sitar, and an outro belonging to no other part of the song.

**Form.** Refrain and verse in rotating pairs, one bridge, and an outro grown out of the last verse. Phrases are consistently **stretched a bar or more past where they need to end**, which is where the exhausted quality comes from ([[ARR-C06 — Section-length conventions, and breaking them on purpose]]) — the verse is **13 bars as 6 + 7**, the bridge **15 as 6 + 9**, and the refrain a single four-bar phrase plus a two-bar sitar riff.

**Harmony.** Unambiguously major, but the tonic almost never sits at a section boundary, so the key feels asserted rather than felt. The verse's second phrase **starts where the first phrase's third bar started**, which lets the chord ending phrase 1 do double duty as phrase 2's opening — the seam disappears, and the section stops sounding like blocks. The bridge is where the harmony finally has a goal, and Pollack extracts a specific craft lesson: **phrase 1 closes on ii, holds it an extra bar, and never gets its V — you have to go back and take the whole phrase again to earn the resolution, and on that second pass the wait is only two bars.** Shortening the delay on the repeat is what makes it a climax ([[ARR-C05 — The energy curve — one apex, and pull back before the end]]) — the contraction, not the volume.

**Melody.** Local motion is almost all stepwise, yet the song covers a wide range; the breadth is engineered by **assigning each section its own band of the register** rather than by writing leaps ([[MEL-C05 — Register and intensity]]).

**Arrangement.** Strummed acoustic, an organ mastered with absurdly deep flutter, and sitar used as a psychedelic lead-guitar substitute rather than for anything Indian. Dynamics live at the extremes, and the only quiet percussion on the track is the brushwork in one refrain/verse pair. The ending is a small event of its own — a rattling wine bottle, off-key falsetto chanting, and a last drum hit after the track appears to be over.
