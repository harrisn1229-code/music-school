# Parallel Processing

**What it is:** blend a heavily-processed *copy* under the untouched original, instead of processing the original. The dry path keeps everything you love (transients, tone, dynamics); the wet path contributes only the quality you wanted more of (density, dirt, width, sustain).

## Why it beats serial
Serial processing always trades: compression trades transients for control, distortion trades cleanliness for harmonics. Parallel sidesteps the trade — you *add* the processed quality instead of *converting* the signal into it. This is why you can crush a parallel path absurdly hard ("it's supposed to sound ugly soloed") and the blend still sounds natural.

## Mechanics (Ableton)
- Return track, 100% wet chain, feed it with **post-fader sends** — per-track send levels become per-source "amount" knobs into one shared flavor.
- **Send selectively:** drums crush gets kick/snare/toms, never cymbals/hats (splatty). That selectivity is the whole reason it's a send, not a group insert.
- Blend the return up from silence until the effect is *felt*, then back off 1 dB. Mute-test: you should miss it, not hear it.
- Watch latency/phase: Live compensates plugin delay automatically, but never duplicate a path through different manual routes.
- Alternative for one source: a plugin's own mix knob ([[Pro-C 2]] dry knob, [[API-2500]] mix, Decapitator mix) = parallel-in-place.

## The house parallel returns
- [[Parallel Comp Send]] — 1176 all-buttons crush (density, sustain, room)
- [[Saturation Send]] — Decapitator dirt (attitude, phone-speaker audibility)
- [[Width Send]] — MicroShift micro-pitch (size without losing the mono center)
- Automation trick: ride the *return* up +1 dB in choruses — energy lift without level lift.

## Other parallel recipes
- **Parallel dirty bass:** amp-sim copy bandpassed 160 Hz–1.2 kHz under the clean DI ([[1 Electric Bass DI Track|Electric Bass DI Track]])
- **Parallel wide vocal:** HPF a copy until it's all air, chorus/flange it, tuck it under ([[1 Main Vocal Track|Main Vocal Track]] hooks)
- **Upward parallel (OTT-style):** bring up quiet detail rather than crushing loud detail ([[OTT]] at low depth is this in one plugin)

## From videos
- **Blend a straight (unprocessed) copy of a signal back in alongside a heavily wah/filter-processed copy, specifically to keep the fundamental/pitch audible under a moving effect.** Edgar Winter's "Frankenstein" lead: the ARP 2600's filter output run through a preamp into a Crybaby wah loses pitch clarity as the wah sweeps if left alone; adding an untouched, unprocessed copy of the same signal back in keeps a constant fundamental reference under the wah's moving color, reading as fuller and more like a real instrument than either signal alone. General principle behind the specific case: process a sound, then reintroduce a straight copy alongside it rather than replacing the source entirely — useful whenever an effect's own character (filtering, distortion, wah) threatens to obscure the pitch/fundamental it's applied to. → [[Filters and Resonance]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[How to Make Edgar Winter's Frankenstein on Synth]]
- **Split a MONO synth signal hard left/right — dry on one side, through a resonant filter/effect on the other — and the resulting phantom-panning movement comes free, without any pan automation.** Not the usual "stereo effect on both sides" setup: send the same mono source to two separate paths, one untouched, one through a resonant filter (demonstrated with a triple-resonator module emulating a Korg PS-3100), then hard-pan each path to opposite sides. Because the filtered path's harmonic content shifts and moves independently of the dry path, the two sides develop real volume/phase differences from each other as the filter moves — the ear perceives that as movement between the speakers (a phantom-panning illusion from frequency-dependent cancellation), which a single filtered stereo signal (filter applied identically to both channels) never produces. **Side benefit**: the dimensionality this creates can reduce how much reverb is needed for a sense of depth — the phantom-panning movement itself reads as a kind of natural small-room depth. Also demonstrated with a second dry/wet pairing: run a synth's own filter dry on one signal path and a pedal's independent filter (a Waldorf-style two-pole, in this case) on a parallel path from the same source, sweeping each independently by hand/foot simultaneously — one performer, two live-swept filters, one instrument. → [[Bass Design]], [[Width and Stereo]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[How to Supercharge Your Tracks with Filter Pedals]]
- **A clock-derived envelope patched straight into a filter's cutoff CV, for rhythmic filter modulation that's adjustable live in real time rather than a fixed LFO rate.** Route a MIDI/analog clock into a small utility module that converts clock pulses into an envelope shape (rate directly tied to tempo), then patch that envelope's output into a filter's cutoff frequency input — the filter now pumps/chops in time automatically. Because the utility module's own rate control can double, triple, or quadruple the base clock division live, the resulting rhythmic filter movement can be sped up or slowed down on the fly during a performance/take without touching a DAW automation lane or LFO sync setting — unplug the one cable and the effect is instantly gone, useful for a live on/off toggle. Same trick reused to drive an arpeggiator's rate directly instead of a filter, letting the arpeggio's sweep range (e.g. 2 octaves vs. 4) and rhythmic density change live mid-performance rather than being locked to a fixed note-value setting on the instrument's back panel. → [[Modulation]], [[Automation]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[How to Supercharge Your Tracks with Filter Pedals]]
- **Hardware framing for this note's own "parallel dirty bass" recipe (above): a physical mult splits one signal to N identical outputs — run one straight to the desk, the other through an effects-pedal loop (distortion/phase/etc.), and blend.** The reasoning generalizes past hardware: keeping a straight/dry path alongside the effected one restores definition, punch and high end that a fully-wet effected bass loses, and mixing the phase relationship between the two paths adds width/character beyond what either path gives alone; balance ranges from mostly-dry-with-a-touch-of-effect to mostly-effected-with-a-touch-of-dry-for-definition, both valid depending on how much character is wanted. In Ableton this is just a send/return (see this note's own [[Parallel Comp Send]]-style mechanics above) rather than a physical mult — same principle, no extra hardware needed. → [[Bass Design]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[10 Tips For Synth Bass]]
- **"Parallel harmonic compression" — boost the song's root note and its perfect fifth before heavy parallel compression, so the resulting distortion harmonics land in-key, then pull them back down after.** On a parallel/aux track, insert a linear-phase EQ FIRST and find the song's fundamental (its key's root note — read off an analyzer as the highest-amplitude low frequency if unsure); boost it aggressively with a bell filter (the boost gets compensated later, so don't hold back). Also find and boost the **perfect fifth** of that root (ratio trick: root Hz × 3 ÷ 2 = the fifth — e.g. 55 Hz root → 165 ÷ 2 = 82.5 Hz). THEN insert the parallel compressor and compress heavily — because the boosted content is now in-key, the compressor's wave-shaping distortion is far more likely to generate harmonics that are musically related to the song, instead of harmonics keyed off an arbitrary transient/sibilant peak. Close with a SECOND linear-phase EQ that attenuates the root and fifth back down until balanced — an emphasis/de-emphasis technique for more musical parallel-compression harmonics than a plain send/return with no pre-EQ shaping. → [[EQ]], [[Compression]] — Sage Audio, [5 Advanced Compression Techniques (Simple to Complex)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V1lZaf8NWd0)
- **Gain-ride a parallel bus's own level per song section instead of leaving it static — aggressive parallel compression isn't set-and-forget.** Depending on the instrumentation, pumping reads as more or less noticeable at different points in a song. Dial in parallel-compression settings, listen start to finish, note WHERE pumping is audible, then either automate the compressor's output gain down in those sections, or (if it's on an aux/return track) automate the channel fader there instead. Workflow tip: use the compressor/automation LATCH function and manually ride the aux fader live while the song plays for a first pass, then switch the automation to "read" so the natural human inconsistencies of that live ride stay in — more character than a perfectly smoothed curve. → [[Automation]] — Sage Audio, [5 Advanced Compression Techniques (Simple to Complex)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V1lZaf8NWd0)

- **A shared parallel "kick-snare crush" bus: both dry kick and snare tracks (plus any parallel processing already built into their own tracks) get sent to ONE additional shared parallel compressor.** Andrew Scheps' recipe: a DBX-160-style compressor, **~4:1 to 4.5:1 ratio**, transient-triggered (not a large amount of GR) — "it just adds a little bit of length and it changes the shape of the attack a little bit," taking "that entire kick sound as a whole" (dry + its own upstream processing) and pumping it up together rather than treating kick and snare as fully separate parallel chains. Stacked with TWO further stereo parallel drum-bus compressors on the same drum bus: a Fatso-style unit (heavy peak limiting, triggered hard by the kit's transients) and a Fairchild-670-style "drum crush" plugin run as **two mono instances, deliberately UNLINKED left/right** rather than stereo-linked, so each side compresses independently. All three run in parallel with the dry kit; removing all of them together "drains the excitement" even though none is individually drastic. → [[3 Drums Bus]], [[Plugin Wishlist]], [[Vintage Gear Worklist]] — Mix with the Masters (Andrew Scheps), [[Mix buss & drum processing with Andrew Scheps]]
- **A four-aux subtractive-then-additive drum-bus system: crush the bus hard with compression/limiting, then rebuild only the SPECIFIC qualities that crushing removed, via dedicated purpose-built parallel sends rather than backing off the crush itself.** Jaycen Joshua's full drum-bus recovery system: "with these four, I'm making up what I'm losing by hitting the compression and the limiter and the Black Box super, super hard." Aux 1 ("Transformer") rebuilds transient life: [[Decapitator]] (dark tone, mid-aggressive drive) → an SSL-style "Cross-Eyed" comp (Attack 30, Release 1, Ratio 2:1, ~8 dB GR) → low end stripped so only top-end "spark" returns — see [[Glue Compressor]]. Aux 2 is an MV2-style low-level enhancer run as a front-to-back "attention" panner rather than a volume trick (unowned — see [[Plugin Wishlist]]). Aux 3 ("Transformer 2") runs the same Decapitator+comp recipe on the KICK specifically, with a Chandler-style ([[Decapitator]] Style E) distortion character deliberately emulating a blown-out Fruity-Loops-style hip-hop kick — subtle here, explicitly pushable to full aggression on harder records. → [[Decapitator]], [[Glue Compressor]], [[3 Drums Bus]], [[Parallel Comp Send]], [[TRANSIENT Slot]], [[Kick Design]], [[Plugin Wishlist]] — Mix with the Masters (Jaycen Joshua), [[Jaycen Joshua on creative drum mixing]]
- **Add master/summing-stage parallel compression MIDWAY through the mix, once the track's full energy is present, because its impact depends heavily on how much is already happening — and re-check the kick/snare/vocal afterward, since it measurably widens the stereo field and shifts the center.** Rafa Sardina: "my parallel compression, because it's so [dependent] on my program... midway through the mixing process, when I have all of the energy of the mix happening... then I experiment a little bit with the parallel compression." His own LA-2A-style parallel stage widened the stereo field enough that kick/snare/vocal levels needed re-cueing afterward — a concrete warning that a late parallel-comp pass isn't a free lunch. Contrast: broad tonal EQ moves (his example, boosting ~1 kHz on a mastering EQ) go in EARLY instead, since they change every subsequent mix decision. → [[Compression Staging]], [[Width and Stereo]], [[Master Chain]] — Mix with the Masters (Rafa Sardina), [[Rafa Sardina's mixing workflow]]
- **A clean, textbook A/B/C demonstration of parallel compression on a snare — dry, then EQ-only, then EQ+compression, then the compressed pass blended back under the dry original.** Bob Clearmountain: dry snare → EQ-only pass ([[UAD Pultec EQP-1A]]: top end, ~700 Hz boost, a bit of low end) → the same signal additionally hit with [[UAD UA 1176LN]] → blended with the untouched dry snare. "You get a little bit of the natural sound but you get that compression." A specific reason to reach for parallel over serial here, beyond the usual density/punch: on a rolled/rudimented passage, the parallel-compressed layer pulls up the quieter in-between/ghost notes along with the main accents, which a straight insert compressor set for the loud hits would otherwise leave buried. → [[UAD UA 1176LN]], [[UAD Pultec EQP-1A]], [[1 Drums Snare-Clap Track|Drums Snare-Clap Track]] — Mix with the Masters (Bob Clearmountain), [[Bob Clearmountain's mixing techniques]]
- **A "duplicate twice" parallel kick recipe: duplicate the channel, drive the copy through one aggressive plugin printed without a dry/wet blend, THEN duplicate that already-processed copy a second time purely for extra body — two stacked duplicates of the processed layer, not the dry one.** Distinct from this note's usual send-based or single-duplicate patterns: here the second duplication happens AFTER processing, compounding the effect's body/attack rather than adding a second independent flavor. Needed a phase fix afterward since two processed copies of the same transient rarely stay aligned — full recipe on [[Kick Design]]. *(transcript low-confidence — machine-translated, no exact plugin/dB given)* → [[Kick Design]], [[Phase Alignment]] — Mosty (Mix with the Masters), [[Mosty mixing a reggaeton track]]
- **A "duplicate your aux and blend to taste" parallel vocal trick, framed as the general fix any time a client revision asks for a vocal "fuller/louder" — beats simply raising the fader.** "Duplicate the aux and just put these plug-ins on here and blended it to taste... on my lead vocals here, I'm doing some light parallel. So this is a duplicated master aux, just getting out some low and high [via a default Pro-Q filter pass]. Then it's smashing an 1176, well I guess not a whole smash, but it's hitting an 1176... Then I just blend that up to taste into my mix... it's a little darker, kind of just opens it up for me." Blended back in at roughly **−33 dB**. Stated general recipe: "duplicate your aux and put some different plug-ins on there. Filter out frequencies that you don't need, and accentuate those that you do or take out the ones that are bothering you, and hit it with some compression, and turn it up or down to taste." Sometimes reads as better than turning up the volume, sometimes worse — dial by ear, not by rule. → [[Pro-Q 4]], [[UAD UA 1176LN]], [[Dua Lipa Vocal Chain]] — Mix with the Masters (Josh Gudwin), [[Mixing Dua Lipa's vocals with Josh Gudwin]]
- **Parallel-compress the WHOLE STEM as a unit — the fix for why "compress the overall mix, not individual elements" (Andrew Scheps' pop-mixing philosophy) doesn't transfer directly to film/game scoring.** Scheps' approach works when you're delivering one finished mix. Film/game/media music delivers **separate stems**, so a single mix-bus compressor can't be used the same way — "the threshold is being controlled by, say, a piece of percussion that isn't there in that stem." The workaround sits between the two extremes (per-element vs. whole-mix): apply parallel compression to **an entire stem as one unit** — "congeal the whole stem together" — here, the whole percussion stem run through a blended 1176 + top-end EQ. Reusable any time the deliverable is stems rather than a locked mix (remix stems, game audio, film/TV cues). → [[UAD UA 1176LN]], [[UAD Pultec EQP-1A]], [[Parallel Comp Send]], [[TRANSIENT Slot]] — Mix with the Masters (Alan Meyerson), [[Adding impact to percussions with Alan Meyerson]]
- **Two distinct parallel-distortion recipes on the same live-drum session, one per drum — a guitar distortion pedal on the kick, a driven compressor's low end only on the snare.** Nick Launay: kick = direct/close mic blended under a second kick signal run through a **guitar distortion pedal (a SansAmp)**, "that trick I've explained before on other Mix with the Masters sessions" — a repeatable go-to, not a one-off. Snare = a direct **57** blended under a second snare channel run through a **Distressor compressor "hitting it hard,"** used specifically for the snare's **low end only** — EQ'd top rolled way off, low boosted, so the parallel channel on its own is just a thuddy low-end layer, not a full distorted snare. Both recipes share this note's core shape (a heavily-processed copy under the clean original) but split differently from the send-based Ableton pattern above: each is a full duplicated tracking channel, printed alongside the direct mic rather than a shared return. Two further drum layers (room mics, overdub brushes) stack on top of both direct+parallel pairs. → [[Compression]], [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[Drum Mic Technique]], [[2 Drums/1 Drums Kick Track|Drums Kick Track]], [[2 Drums/1 Drums Snare-Clap Track|Drums Snare-Clap Track]], [[Plugin Wishlist]] — Mix with the Masters (Nick Launay), [[Parallel distortion by Nick Launay]]
- **"Multibus" processing — a fourth distinct parallel-adjacent structure alongside this note's send-based, single-device-blend, and Omnipressor approaches: route the SAME stereo signal to MULTIPLE parallel buses at once (Brauer names four — A/B/C/D), each carrying different processing, then sum them all back together, rather than one send/return pair.** Demonstrated live by toggling whole buses on/off ("I'll take B off, take C off, take D off, and then we'll just send stuff to here"). Doubling folds into the same structure: a source needing extra weight (his example: the guitar solo) gets sent to *two* parallel buses at once instead of gain-boosted on one. Every individual bus stays almost inaudible on its own (an SSL-style bus comp barely hitting 3 dB GR at the loudest chorus; a second unit's Wet/Dry blend parked mostly dry) — the philosophy stated outright: "just doing a little bit on a lot of places, and it adds up and it feels natural," building perceived loudness/punch from many small stacked moves instead of one heavier move on one path, specifically to avoid an "overly compressed" complaint from the artist. Also carries a live A/B teaching method: play a section fully bypassed (straight to the stereo bus) then the identical section with the buses engaged, listening for **openness and excitement** rather than raw level — with the caveat that bypassing buses also changes the summed gain, so the "off" pass isn't level-matched by default. → [[Solid Bus Comp]], [[UAD Pultec EQP-1A]], [[Compression]], [[Master Chain]], [[Stereo Field]] — Michael Brauer (Mix with the Masters), [[The power of multi-bus processing with Michael Brauer]]
- **Parallel compression withheld until a single structural landmark (the outro-chorus lift), then engaged across THREE separate signal groups simultaneously — kit close mics, room mics, AND stomp tracks all at once, not one drum-bus send.** Tom Elmhirst, mixing Adele's "Rolling in the Deep": "for the outro chorus of this song I bring in some parallel compression on the drums, out of the middle and into the last chorus, and I have that set up on the kit tracks, the room mics, and I also have it on stomp tracks" — demoed by simply toggling the whole parallel path in/out, audibly adding "a lot more drive... for that last chorus." The reusable idea beyond the timing (already covered by [[Parallel Comp Send]]'s "automate the crush return up +1 dB in choruses" move): running the SAME crush moment across multiple distinct signal groups at once, so the whole kit's character shifts together at one landmark rather than one bus creeping up gradually. → [[Parallel Comp Send]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]], [[UAD UA 1176LN]] — Mix with the Masters (Tom Elmhirst), [[Tom Elmhirst breaks down his mix of 'Rolling in the Deep' by Adele]]
- **Parallel as the ONLY compression on a lead vocal, specifically to keep a delivered take's dynamics intact rather than the usual "add density" motivation.** "The only compression I've got going on is parallel. It's tucked in, so I'm keeping all the dynamics of their song, but all I'm doing is tucking in, just a bit of a nicer version." Worth flagging as a distinct reason to reach for parallel: not for glue or character, but specifically *because* serial compression would flatten a performance that's already good — here, a wet/pre-mixed vocal delivered by the artist. → [[Pro-C 2]], [[Compression]], [[James Blake Vocal Chain]] — Mix with the Masters, [[MSM Mixing James Blake's Vocals]]
- **A driven, hard-limited parallel duplicate — thinned aggressively then squashed of all peaks — blended under an untouched original to build a stable character bed without sacrificing the original's dynamics.** More extreme than a typical parallel-comp send: duplicate the source, run the copy through distortion/thinning (iZotope Trash, plus a second distortion plugin with an unclear/garbled auto-caption name) then a limiter set to "take off any of the peaks really really hard." The untouched original still carries full natural dynamic range; the driven-and-limited copy just fills in a dense, stable mid-range underneath it — described as "creating a box for him to sit in." A second, milder version of the same duplicate feeds a compressor and EQ ahead of the main chain instead, for a subtler push. Watch phase/sample-rate consistency between the parallel paths when several plugins run simultaneously — see [[Phase Alignment]]. → [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[Phase Alignment]], [[UAD Pultec EQP-1A]], [[MIDS Slot]] — Mix with the Masters (Ben Baptie), [[Julian Casablancas' vocal sound with Ben Baptie]]
- **Use low-latency linear phase specifically when EQing a parallel signal whose original (dry) path isn't also filtered — avoids phase cancellation on recombine.** General rule from a broader EQ-phase-mode discussion: linear phase is for solving specific problems, not a default, and a parallel chain (where the processed copy gets filtered but the dry path doesn't) is one of the two named cases where it's worth the cost (the other being high-pass filtering). At high settings it introduces pre-ringing ahead of transients, though the source's own opinion is that concerns about audibility are often overstated. Default for the dry path and most other EQ moves: zero-latency natural phase or plain low-latency linear phase. → [[Pro-Q 4]], [[EQ]], [[Phase Alignment]] — Sage Audio, [EQ Masterclass](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iPBnL6mx9c0)
- **A mid-frequency upward-processing aux that also feeds a SEPARATE reverb aux — chaining two parallel sends rather than running them independently.** A variant of this note's mid-band-isolated upward-compression recipe below: send + aux, linear-phase EQ isolates just the mids, upward processing on that isolated band brings quieter mid detail forward, blend back via the aux's fader — same shape as the Oxford Inflator mid-band recipe already logged here. The addition: that mid-focused aux's output is *also* routed into a separate "collective reverb" aux (see [[Reverb]]) rather than staying isolated, so the recovered mid detail shares the same reverb space as the rest of the mix instead of sitting apart from it — cohesion as a routing decision, not just a processing one. → [[Reverb]], [[Oxford Inflator]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [How to Master a Song Start to Finish](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aUygMNRx2yE)
- **New York style parallel compression, full recipe — plus a "drive the compressor harder" character move.** Send + aux track; on the aux, a compressor (glue-style or transparent, pushed to heavy attenuation since it's blended in rather than replacing the signal) followed by a linear-phase EQ (avoids phase cancellation against the dry channel) to amplify whatever ranges should stick out. To make the compression itself more audible/characterful: insert a *second* EQ **before** the compressor and drive specific frequencies into it — the compressor works harder on those bands, so compression on those frequencies reads as more apparent, and the aux fader becomes a single-knob automation target for the whole effect's intensity. → [[Pro-C 2]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [Mastering Compression Settings](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YUn31aE9X_8)
- **The same New York recipe run as *upward* compression instead of downward, isolated to one mid frequency band.** Linear-phase EQ high-pass + low-pass on the aux to isolate a mid band (worked example: 400 Hz–4 kHz), an upward compressor/maximizer pushed hard on that isolated band (Oxford Inflator's Effect at 100%), then blend the processed band back in with the original until balanced. The dynamic range of that band shrinks and previously-masked detail inside it becomes clearly audible — a more surgical version of running an upward processor wide-band on the whole master. Same EQ-drives-the-processor option applies for extra emphasis. → [[Oxford Inflator]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [Mastering Compression Settings](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YUn31aE9X_8)
- **The Omnipressor — simultaneous compression above threshold and expansion below it, in one device.** A distinct third parallel-adjacent structure from this note's send-based and single-plugin-blend approaches: rather than blending a processed copy with the dry signal, one device attenuates the loud parts and amplifies the quiet parts *at the same time*, keeping the whole signal inside a controlled dynamic range. Mastering-safe settings: limit both compression and expansion ranges to ~1 dB each, ratio/function near the middle, threshold set carefully — works especially well right before a limiter to get louder without heavy peak attenuation. No exact free equivalent for the single-device routing; two instances of a free compressor (one compressing, one expanding) can approximate it. → [[Compression]], [[Plugin Wishlist]] — Sage Audio, [Mastering Compression Settings](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YUn31aE9X_8)
- **Parallel compression as a limiting alternative on a master, not just a mix-bus trick — compress heavily, blend to roughly 50/50 wet/dry so peak attenuation only lands on half the signal while quiet detail still gets amplified.** [[Pro-C 2]]'s built-in Dry Gain does this in one plugin: compress hard (peaks attenuated the same as a limiter would in the wet path alone), then bring the dry signal up to ~50/50 so the final output's peaks read far less squashed while the wet half's amplified quiet detail still comes through. Vary Knee/Threshold/Ratio/Style to control exactly what the wet half catches, and — since a parallel blend doesn't risk clipping and doesn't need an instant attack — open Attack/Release up for transient retention instead of defaulting fast. → [[Clipping and Loudness]], [[Master Chain]], [[Limiting]] — Sage Audio, [Stop Limiting Your Masters](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s_LDCzWx20g)

Threshold pulled back from the preset's default (less extreme duck) + a [[Utility]] after it to gain-match makes it a fair, controllable blend rather than a fixed preset. → [[Glue Compressor]] — You Suck at Producing, [Warmth](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Yv3v9SsoDMw)
