# Transcribing and Playing by Ear

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Scale Degree Ear Training]]. Where [[Scale Degree Ear Training]] builds the *perceptual foundation* (the 12-degree feeling-state vocabulary, chords as meta feeling-states, drone drilling), this note is the **applied workflow** — Max Konyi's (Mind of Max) step-by-step process for actually finding the key of a real song, working out its chords, playing back a melody, and transcribing a full arrangement in real time, plus the daily practice routine that trains all of it. Ties into [[Song Practice]] — this is the literal method for learning a queued song by ear rather than from a chart.

## Finding the key of a song by ear
A key = **tonic** (the home note) + **tonality** (major or minor). See [[Keys]] for the theory; this is the ear-only workflow:
- **Finding the tonic** — listen to enough of the song to get its context (10–15 seconds, or one loop), then pause and imagine the music resolving to a single note; sing or find that note. Backup: check the **last note of the melody** — a common but *not guaranteed* place for the tonic to land, so treat it as a place to look, not a rule. Second backup: find the **tonic chord** (the most stable-feeling chord, the one the song could end on) and sing its bass note — on a root-position tonic chord, the bass note IS the tonic. For the underlying skill (why the ear does this automatically), see [[Scale Degree Ear Training]] § tonic recognition.
- **Advanced shortcut** — if you can already recognize any scale degree by ear, you don't need the tonic directly: identify any note you recognize (e.g. "that's scale degree 5") and back-calculate the tonic from it.
- **Finding the tonality** — the ideal is pure vibe: listen to the isolated tonic note itself and ask whether it feels bright/warm/major or dark/melancholy/minor — with practice the whole song's color becomes audible in that one note. Backup/check method: once you have a candidate tonic, play the **minor 3rd and the major 3rd above it against the track** and hear which one actually fits — whichever third sounds "correct" tells you major vs. minor. Second backup: find the tonic *chord* and read its quality directly.
- **Common mistakes:** (1) **confusing relative major and minor** — many progressions can genuinely be heard tilted either way (e.g. hearing D minor vs. its relative F major), and sometimes both answers are legitimately defensible depending on framing — know [[Keys|relative major/minor]] cold so you recognize when this is happening rather than treating it as a wrong answer. (2) **Assuming the first or last chord of a song is the tonic** — sometimes true, often not, especially in modern music; it's a place to check, never a rule to rely on.
— Max Konyi, [[Find the Key of Any Song in 30 Seconds (By Ear)]]

**Physical alternative for finding the tonic — chromatic single-string search.** Instead of singing/imagining the target note first, play chromatically up one string (low E is a natural default) against the track until a note sounds like "home" — a direct brute-force search, useful as a fallback for anyone who can't yet reliably sing or imagine the tonic. Works reasonably well even on a rough guess since any key uses 7 of the 12 available notes, so the odds already favor landing in-key. **Confirming minor quality by playability, not just by ear-feel**: play the minor pentatonic scale rooted on the candidate tonic against the track — if it fits cleanly, the key is very likely minor (with the caveat that minor keys don't always read as sad by feel — can read as funky or other moods depending on genre). — Brandon D'Eon, [How to hear the key of a song INSTANTLY](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OIPDxGHKa3s)

## Working out chords by ear
Every chord has three aspects to determine — the **root/degree** (which scale degree is the root), the **quality** (major, minor, sus2, dominant 9, diminished 7…), and the **bass note** (usually the root, but not if the chord is inverted). Each has its own feeling; together they form a holistic meta-feeling that [[Scale Degree Ear Training|immediate recognition]] eventually catches as a single gestalt rather than three separate lookups.

**Three modes of working out a chord, escalating as familiarity runs out:**
1. **Immediate recognition** — the chord's identity is instinctively obvious the moment it's heard, no thinking or guessing. This is the target state ear training builds toward, one chord type at a time.
2. **Deduction** — when a chord doesn't resolve instantly, narrow the (effectively infinite) possibility space using perceptual clues: a recognized quality ("sounds major"), a recognized bass degree, a recognized bass *motion* between chords, or genre-specific harmonic conventions (jazz vs. blues vs. classical vs. R&B each imply different vocabularies). Combine clues with music theory to shrink the candidate set to a handful, then **test** each candidate — either by playing it on an instrument and comparing, or by imagining it (the imagined version doesn't need to be perfectly clear, just close enough to judge a match). Worked example: an unfamiliar major-sounding chord in a major key narrows to I, IV, or V; the tonic is already established so I is ruled out; hearing the chord's bass sits a half-step above the *previous* chord's bass rules out V (no diatonic half-step approach) and confirms IV (which has one, from iii).
3. **Brute force note-finding** — the last resort for a chord that gives no usable clues at all: listen to it repeatedly and pick out individual pitches one at a time. Grab the **outer notes first** (lowest and highest) since they immediately constrain the possibility space, then work inward guessing inner tones; a solid theory background (e.g. recognizing "this must be a flat-9 over a flat-7") lets brute force converge much faster than picking notes with no framework at all. Even a rough guess at a "cluster" of close inner tones (without resolving each pitch individually) is useful evidence.

Once a chord's full identity is worked out, **label it and drill it** (listen, play, name) so it moves permanently into the immediate-recognition category rather than needing deduction again next time. → [[Chords]], [[Chord Inversions]] — Max Konyi, [[Chords by Ear: The Real Process]]

## Playing a melody by ear — the 5-step process
1. **Memorize the melody** — sing/hum it back until it's internalized; if singing pitches back is difficult, that's a skill worth training on its own (Konyi calls it something he uses "in every facet of my musical life" despite not being a singer).
2. **Map the melodic contour** — for each note, note only whether it moves up or down from the previous one and by roughly how much (step / leap / large leap); no scale-degree numbers yet, just the shape. Drawing the contour on paper helps.
3. **Sing it slowly, one note at a time, feeling for the tonic and any other recognizable degrees** — pencil in whichever degrees you can confidently feel as candidates on the contour map (the tonic is usually, but not guaranteed to be, present in the melody).
4. **Deduce the rest** — use the contour's shape relative to the degrees you already have, plus music theory, to work out what the remaining degrees must be; sing the whole thing back and check that the felt states actually match your guesses (some degrees are easy to confuse — see [[Scale Degree Ear Training]]'s confusion table).
5. **Verify on an instrument** — sing the tonic you believe you've found, play a random note, and identify which scale degree that random note is; back-calculate the tonic's real pitch from there (e.g. "if A is scale degree 2, then G must be 1"). Apply the full contour map to the real tonic pitch and play it against the source — if it's wrong, analyze exactly *which* degree got confused for which; that's where the actual learning happens.

Finish by alternating between listening to the real recording with the scale degrees in mind, singing the melody solo with degrees in mind, and singing along *with* the recording — repetition across all three modes fuses the sound, the label, and the instrument together until the melody can be heard and identified in real time with no pausing or singing required. — Max Konyi, [[How to Play Any Melody by Ear - Step by Step Guide]]

## Real-time transcription workflow
The full pipeline for transcribing a song cold, demonstrated live across country, pop, deep-house, video-game (Crosscode, Genshin Impact), and jazz-fusion-adjacent K-pop tracks:
- **The mental map** — an abstracted, key-agnostic chromatic scale (1, ♭2, 2, ♭3, 3, 4, ♯4, 5, ♭6, 6, ♭7, 7) held in mind around an unknown "1," filled in with real letter names only once the actual key is worked out. Every bit of harmonic/melodic vocabulary built through practice slots into this map.
- **No-perfect-pitch trick for finding "1":** sing the note that feels like the tonic (you don't need to know its pitch), then play **any random note** on your instrument and judge what scale degree *that* note feels like relative to the note you sang; back-calculate the tonic's real pitch from the relationship (e.g. "I played a G and it sounds like scale degree 4 relative to what I sang, so the key must be D"). This is the single most-repeated move across every transcription example. — Max Konyi, [[Transcribing Music by Ear in Real-Time Ep.01|Transcribing Music by Ear in Real-Time \| Ep.01]], [[Transcribing Complex Music by Ear in Real-Time Ep.02|Ep.02]]
- **Track bass motion continuously** as the primary chord-to-chord landmark — half-step bass drops, a descending diatonic bass line harmonizing a scale (a very common device: I, V/vi(inverted), vi, V/IV, IV, V/ii, ii, V…), and inversions (signaled by a bass note that isn't the chord's root) are all read off the bass first, then confirmed against the chord quality on top.
- **Half-step bass motion is a strong diatonic landmark** — in a major key it's almost always IV→iii (4→3) or vii°→I (7→1); combined with hearing "major chord moving to a minor chord a half-step below," there's only one diatonic spot that happens (IV→iii in major; ♭VI→v in minor) — narrows the whole progression fast.
- **Chord quality and chord function ("fourness," "twoness," "fiveness") are each their own trained perception**, built specifically from having transcribed "hundreds and hundreds" of songs — matches [[Scale Degree Ear Training]]'s point that chords are recognized as holistic meta-feelings, not reconstructed live. A ii–V is eventually heard as one fused gesture ("a chunk") rather than two separate chords once this is trained.
- **Sharp-4 in the bass is a specific, learnable landmark** — very commonly signals either a secondary dominant of V ("two-chord," V/V, a Baroque-era-common move) or a ii∅7 (half-diminished ii); hearing sharp-4 narrows the guess to one of those two before any testing starts. Related: the sharp-4-half-diminished chord native to the 4th degree of Lydian mode is a distinct, recognizable "flavor" chord worth training on its own. → [[Diatonic Modes]], [[Secondary Dominants]]
- **A minor ii–V doesn't always resolve to minor** — real songs frequently set up a minor ii–V (which strongly implies a minor destination) and then land on the **major** I instead (and vice versa); keep the ear open to both outcomes rather than assuming the implied resolution is the actual one. Common in jazz and jazz-adjacent pop writing.
- **Expanding chord vocabulary from real music:** when an unfamiliar-but-appealing sound shows up repeatedly across different songs, isolate it, work it out (in one case: a ii chord voiced as a minor 9 whose implied major-3rd-of-the-key color makes it sound almost major), and deliberately drill it until it's a recognized vocabulary item for next time — this is literally how the immediate-recognition category grows over years.
- **First-pass triage:** on a genuinely dense arrangement, it's fine to capture a lead-sheet-level chord+melody sketch on the first pass and leave inner voicing details for a dedicated re-listen later, rather than stalling out trying to catch every note at once.
- **Real songs can feel genuinely ambiguous between relative major and minor** in the moment — the ear can "flip-flop" on which key center it's hearing depending on which chord just played; acknowledge that instability as a real perceptual phenomenon rather than forcing a single premature answer.
— Max Konyi, [[Transcribing Music by Ear in Real-Time Ep.01|Transcribing Music by Ear in Real-Time \| Ep.01]], [[Transcribing Complex Music by Ear in Real-Time Ep.02|Transcribing Complex Music by Ear in Real-Time \| Ep.02]], [[Transcribing Complex Music by Ear in Real-Time Ep.03|Ep.03]]

## The daily practice routine
Four core skills every routine needs to touch: **melodic scale-degree recognition**, **bass-note recognition**, **chord-quality recognition**, and **chord-number/position recognition**.
- **Drone practice** — sing or play long, sustained tones of each scale degree against a held drone; pure familiarity over speed. See [[Scale Degree Ear Training]] for the full 12-degree feeling vocabulary this drills.
- **Melody transcription drill** — pick a simple, familiar song (a nursery rhyme is fine); sing along with the recording, then sing it from memory alone; find the tonic and work out the remaining degrees by feel; verify on an instrument; alternate between listening and singing with the degrees held in mind until it "clicks" — matches the 5-step melody process above almost exactly.
- **Melodic improvisation with conscious degree-tracking** — improvise over a drone or backing track in a known key, but stay consciously aware of which scale degree is being played at every moment, rather than free-noodling; builds the subconscious sound↔label↔finger association during an otherwise fun, low-pressure activity.
- **Chord/progression drill sequence**, run in order on a chosen progression (own or borrowed from a song):
  1. Play just the **bass notes** of the progression and sing along, holding the scale-degree numbers in mind.
  2. Add the full chords back in; keep singing the bass notes while now also attending to each chord's **quality** (major/minor/etc.), naming each one out loud as it passes.
  3. **Quality-swap drill** — keep every chord's position/number the same, but change ONE chord's quality (e.g. turn a minor iii major) and compare against the original; isolates what a quality change alone sounds like with everything else held constant.
  4. **Position-swap drill** — the inverse: keep a chord's quality the same but move it to a different scale-degree position (e.g. swap a minor chord at position 3 for a different minor chord at position 2 or 6); isolates what a position change alone sounds like.
  5. **Transpose the whole exercise to a new key** and repeat it — the point is to notice what stays constant (the numbers, the relationships) versus what changes (the actual pitches), since the numbers are the actual translation layer being trained.
— Max Konyi, [[The Perfect Ear Training Routine]]

## From videos

- **"Clear-instance bias" as a real transcription pitfall, proven with a Melodyne-verified case study**: "Yesterday" (Beatles)'s opening phrase repeats 4 times; the 2 fast/fleeting occurrences are actually sung on different pitches than the 2 slow/clear ones, but nearly every published transcription and cover gets all 4 instances wrong by defaulting to the clearly-heard version. **The mechanism**: when a repeated phrase has both an easy-to-hear and a hard-to-hear occurrence, the ear tends to pattern-match the ambiguous one onto the clear one rather than transcribing each instance independently — worth actively guarding against by re-verifying every repeat of a phrase on its own terms, especially the fast/quiet ones, rather than assuming they match the clearest pass. Companion caution: published sheet music is transcribed by a third party working by ear (not the songwriter, and without pitch-detection tools), and errors copy forward when one transcriber works from an already-wrong prior transcription instead of the source audio — so sheet music is not itself a reliable ground-truth check, only a cross-reference. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Does everyone play Yesterday wrong]]

Named reason this matters: most beginners can't transcribe by ear yet, so they have no independent way to catch a bad tab themselves. **Fix: use a real published book or a dedicated paid/curated source (basebooks.com named) over free crowd-sourced tabs.** Paired with a three-part deliberate-listening habit for building the ear skill this depends on: (1) actively train your ear to focus on the bass part specifically within a mix — not automatic, a trained skill; (2) listen on real headphones/speakers, never a phone's built-in speaker, so the low end is actually audible/felt; (3) deliberately listen to songs with clearly audible, recognizable basslines (BassBuzz supplies a companion 50-song Spotify playlist for exactly this). → [[Steal Like a Bassist — Ear-Training from Known Melodies]] (the applied bass-side technique this listening habit feeds) — BassBuzz, [[20 Tips I Wish I Knew as a Beginner Bassist (Avoid My Dumb Mistakes)]]
- **Steal comping vocabulary the same way soloists steal licks — and steal specifically from piano players, not other guitarists — plus boredom named directly as the actual practice engine.** Peter Bernstein's stated method: "people develop their soloing language by stealing a few licks... it's the same thing with comping, you have to steal comping patterns" — comping vocabulary is built by transcription/absorption exactly like a soloing vocabulary, not invented from theory alone. **Points the transcription target specifically at piano, not guitar**: piano players "have more of an orchestra to work with," the instrument is inherently more percussive, and the best jazz piano compers were "so funky in where they put the stuff and how they phrase" — **named source to transcribe from directly: Hank Jones's comping** ("who cares about the solos, just listen to what's going on there"). Jim Hall, Kenny Burrell, and Wes Montgomery are named as great guitar compers too, but explicitly framed as secondary to the piano tradition as the real template. **The practice-motivation half:** "the biggest motivator is boredom — you practice comping and get sick of your chords... work out another chord, what's another way to get from here to here" — stated as a repeatable habit worked chorus after chorus on a real tune, using boredom with a specific harmonic transition as the direct signal to go find an alternate path through it (a worked instance of exactly this → [[Chord Substitution]] § *the Days of Wine and Roses B♭m-for-E♭7 substitution*). → [[Etude-Based Vocabulary Practice]] (a complementary structured method for absorbing written vocabulary, vs. this ear-led "steal by transcribing" approach) — Peter Bernstein, [[Peter Bernstein Comping Masterclass - Lesson]]
- **Five-step process for learning a tune entirely by ear, in a fixed order, before ever checking a chart:** (1) learn the **melody by ear** from the original recording, or an early version likely reading the real score — connects the melody straight to your ear instead of to a chart, and preserves the freedom to embellish later since you know the plain version; (2) work out the **chords from a great pianist's recorded version** of the tune (Bill Evans, Oscar Peterson, Wynton Kelly named) — pianists tend to state harmony most clearly since delineating chords is literally their job; (3) **listen closely to the bass, especially on downbeats where the harmony shifts** — the bass is usually holding the chord's root while the piano voices its quality; (4) a mentor's trick (Steve Kabalch): **pitch-shift the recording up an octave** so the bass sits in the guitar's own register and becomes much easier to pick out directly; (5) **check your work last** — only once the ear-work is done, cross-reference a chord chart online or in iReal Pro. Doing the heavy lifting by ear first, before checking, both makes the tune easier to actually play and makes it stick in memory longer. → [[The Five Pillars of Jazz Guitar Practice]] § Pillar 5, [[Internalizing a Tune Through Repetition]] (a complementary repetition-driven method for internalizing a tune once you have melody+chords in hand) — Nathan Borton, [Just Practice Like THIS Every Day (For Real Results)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d4fgvSyvv1g)
- **Form-first, top-down transcription: write out the FORM of a tune before any chords, then fill chords in while listening, leaving anything unclear open rather than guessing.** Direct analogy to a 12-bar-blues shortcut: knowing one binary fact (does it go to IV in bar 2 or not) is enough to play a new blues immediately because everything else is shared vocabulary — the same logic scales to a full 32-bar standard: nail the form (how many bars, how many sections, where the halves split) before worrying about any individual chord, note the key once it's clear, then work chords in against the form rather than chord-by-chord from bar 1. **Exploit repetition explicitly** — once a section (e.g. a tune's first half) has been transcribed, a later repeat of the identical section usually just confirms rather than needing to be re-derived from scratch, which is most of the time savings on a form this repetitive. General framing tying it together: analyze/transcribe/learn top-down (big picture first, details filled in after), not bottom-up chord-by-chord — stated as the more efficient way to work in every case, not specific to any one tune. Full worked slot-by-slot form analysis this method produced, across five standards → [[32-Bar Song Form]]. → [[32-Bar Song Form]] — Jens Larsen, [[Jens Larsen - Learn Jazz Standards - Get The Most of What You Already Know]]
- **Sing the notes of a chord as you play it to feel voice-leading motion in real time.** Singing back a chord's individual notes creates an immediate emotional connection ("if you sing, you emote") and, over repeated practice, sharpens the ear for the specific motion of each note between chords — the stronger that felt inter-chord motion, the more nuanced and "high resolution" the resulting harmonic narrative. → [[Guide Tones]] — Jacob Collier (Sol State), [Jacob Collier Q&A: Key Change Hack, Advanced Triplets, Breaking Rules, Ear Training, Music School](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Sa3A72qnFgI)
- **Ear training's real engine is obsessive, active listening to musicians you love, not app drills.** Collier says his own ear training came almost entirely from consciously listening to musicians he liked and working out what they were doing until their musical vocabulary was internalized deeply enough to become reflexive — corroborates this note's practice-routine framing that recognition needs to become instant/gestalt rather than calculated. — Jacob Collier (Sol State), [Jacob Collier Q&A: Key Change Hack, Advanced Triplets, Breaking Rules, Ear Training, Music School](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Sa3A72qnFgI)
