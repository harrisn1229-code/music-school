# How Duda Changed Deadmau5 Kicks FOREVER (10+ years)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3rDduXys5I)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3rDduXys5I)

## Synopsis

Steve Duda (creator of Serum, longtime friend of deadmau5) and deadmau5 independently describe an almost identical philosophy of why short kicks matter — traced back to a specific club night where a long kick turned into an unbroken, exhausting hum on a real sound system. The bigger idea underneath the specific kick-length advice: low end should arrive in intentional *waves*, not as constant presence, and a mix's clarity comes from deciding what to leave OUT as much as what to include.

## Techniques

- **Don't obsess over one element at the expense of the whole song — find "close enough" and move on.** Described sample-auditioning habit: cycle through kick samples fast (skip-skip-skip-boom) because the target sound is already decided mentally before searching even starts; stop as soon as something is close enough rather than exhaustively comparing options. Works because of a clear artistic vision held at both the whole-song level AND the individual-element level — knowing what you're going for in advance is what makes "close enough" fast to recognize. → [[Committing and Printing]]
- **Budget each beat subdivision in actual milliseconds and decide what you can afford to fit in that window.** At 120 BPM a quarter note is 500 ms — treating that as a literal time budget (not an abstract feel) reframes "how long should this sound be" into a concrete constraint question. → [[Gain Staging]]
- **The core mixing question is "what can I afford to get rid of," not "how do I fit everything in."** Not every element can be loud/prominent simultaneously — deliberately keeping some things quiet or short is what makes the remaining loud/long elements read as bigger by contrast; soloing a kick will always make you want it longer, but in the context of a busy bass part that same long kick stops being valuable. "Clarity is what you don't hear more than what you hear" — clarity comes from absence of junk, not more processing. → [[Masking and Carving]]
- **Design low end as intentional waves over time, not constant presence.** The reasoning behind short kicks: if bass/sub is always there, it becomes a static, unchanging hum that stops registering as exciting (a "standing wave") — you need moments WITHOUT bass for the moments WITH bass to read as a wave/impact rather than background noise. Short kick transients exist specifically to create that empty space before the next hit. → [[Bass Design]], [[Kick Design]]
- **A short kick on headphones/a small room can still ring far longer than intended once played on a real club system — factor in room reverberation of low frequencies, not just the sample's own length.** Origin story: a deadmau5 track's kick sounded fine at home but became a wall of undifferentiated 30 Hz hum in a real club, because low-frequency waves propagate and reflect off room surfaces and physically exist far longer than the audio file's own decay — an 8th-note kick might effectively ring almost a full quarter note once room reverberation is accounted for. The practical implication: err shorter than feels necessary on headphones, because the room will always add length back. → [[Referencing and Translation]], [[Kick Design]]
- **No universal "correct" kick length — calibrate against other tracks in your specific genre**, since every genre balances kick vs. bass differently.
- **Genre-level rule: you can have a big kick OR a big bass sound, not both simultaneously.** House favors a prominent, punchy kick with a comparatively modest bass; dubstep inverts it — an overwhelming, massive bass with a kick that doesn't need to carry much low end on its own. → [[Kick Design]], [[Genre Drum Patterns]]
- **Psychoacoustic "fake the size" trick: drench a kick in a big hall reverb BEFORE the real bass enters, so the ear calibrates its sense of "big low end" on the kick alone.** Works best when the kick is the first element heard in an intro, with no actual sub-bass present yet — the reverb-heavy kick primes the listener's expectation of scale; when the real bass finally lands, it reads as enormous by contrast even if the bass patch itself isn't extreme in isolation — described as being "starved of low frequencies" so the eventual payoff feels bigger, like drinking water after being in a desert. A common dubstep-intro move explained mechanically rather than just described. → [[Reverb]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
