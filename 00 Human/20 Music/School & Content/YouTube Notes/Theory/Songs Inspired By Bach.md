# Songs Inspired By Bach

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4E8HUjxroFA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4E8HUjxroFA)

## Synopsis

A survey of pop/rock songs that trace directly back to specific Bach pieces, drawn largely from the songwriters' own interviews — ranging from a barely-audible melodic-contour echo (Paul Simon) to a note-for-note counterpoint lift (Paul McCartney's "Blackbird") to a full sample of a 1960s jazz re-arrangement of a Bach prelude (Jem).

## Techniques

- **A melody's "kicking-off point" doesn't have to survive into the finished song**: Paul Simon started "Bridge Over Troubled Water" from a melodic segment of Bach's chorale "O Sacred Head, Now Wounded," but the finished song barely resembles it — only the melodic *contour* (shape of ups/downs) of one phrase survives. Explicit lesson: using an existing piece as a starting point doesn't obligate the result to sound like the source.
- **The same source material can be borrowed lightly once and directly lifted later, by the same songwriter**: Simon reused the same Bach chorale far more directly in "American Tune" — melody and (new) lyrics set directly over the borrowed tune. Layered irony worth noting as a songwriting-history detail: Bach didn't originate the tune either — he borrowed it from a Paul Gerhardt hymn, which in turn borrowed it from an even earlier Hans Leo Hassler work; Simon's "American Tune" (about European settlers becoming American) is itself a 400-year-old German tune "becoming American," a genuinely on-theme piece of borrowing.
- **Two-voice counterpoint (melody + independent bass line, each a complete melody on its own) directly reused, then expanded**: McCartney and Harrison's teenage party-piece arrangement of Bach's Bourrée in E minor (melody on top, independent bass line underneath, forming harmony purely from the two lines' relationship, no other harmonic support) became the basis for "Blackbird." McCartney's key extension: Bach's original is 2 voices; "Blackbird" is 4 (3 guitar voices + McCartney's own sung melody), adding a pedal-tone middle voice on the root note specifically to glue the outer voices together harmonically. → [[Species Counterpoint]].
- **A full melodic lift with a full genre/meter transplant**: The Beach Boys' "Lady Linda" (Alan Jardine) opens with an unmodified harpsichord rendition of Bach's "Jesu, Joy of Man's Desiring," then shifts into a Beach Boys arrangement that both changes the melody and re-meters the piece from its original compound 9/8 into a straight 4/4 — genre transplant plus meter reduction (compound → simple) as the specific mechanism of "making it theirs."
- **Sampling a re-arrangement of a public-domain classical piece, rather than the "original" itself, changes what actually reaches the listener's ear**: Jem's "They" (2004) samples Bach's Prelude in F minor (*The Well-Tempered Clavier*, Book 2) — but specifically via the Swingle Singers' 1963 jazz vocal arrangement, not a straight orchestral/keyboard recording — meaning the sampled harmonic/rhythmic character is really the 1963 arranger's interpretation layered on top of the 1700s original.
- **A chord-progression lift that diverges after the opening, still counts as a clear influence claim**: Procol Harum's "A Whiter Shade of Pale" (Gary Brooker) shares Bach's Orchestral Suite No. 3 ("Air on the G String")'s exact descending chord progression and long suspended notes for its first two bars, by the songwriter's own account, before going its own way — the borrow only needs to be structurally identifiable, not sustained for the whole piece.
