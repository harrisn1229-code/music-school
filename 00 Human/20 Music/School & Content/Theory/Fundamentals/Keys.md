# Keys

Part of [[Music Theory]]. A **key** = the pitch that feels like "home" in a song, plus the [[Scales|scale]] built on it (major or minor). Play the same melody's scale degrees from a different key's scale and you've transposed it.

## What makes a song sound "in a key"
A song is in C when it gives the listener these clues:
- Phrases start/end on the C (or Cm) chord
- Chord motion follows tonic → subdominant → dominant patterns ([[Chord Progressions]])
- Most notes come from the C major (or minor) scale
- The melody emphasizes C at important points

Real songs weaken some of these on purpose — remove too many and the key feeling dissolves.

## Relative major & minor
- Pairs that share the **exact same pitches** (and the same [[Key Signatures|key signature]]): C major ↔ A minor, G ↔ Em, D ↔ Bm, A ↔ F♯m, E ↔ C♯m, B ↔ G♯m, F♯ ↔ D♯m, G♭ ↔ E♭m, D♭ ↔ B♭m, A♭ ↔ Fm, E♭ ↔ Cm, B♭ ↔ Gm, F ↔ Dm
- Relative minor = 2 scale degrees DOWN from the major tonic; relative major = 2 degrees UP
- Songs commonly drift between relative keys section-to-section (they share all diatonic chords — see [[Chords by Key]]); the VIm chord as temporary home = instant relative-minor feel

## Parallel major & minor
- Same tonic, different scale: C major vs C minor
- Mixed by **borrowing chords**: a ♭III (E♭) appearing in a C-major song is borrowed from C minor. Common borrowed chords: ♭III, ♭VI, ♭VII, IVm
- See [[Chord Progressions]] for how borrowed chords function

## Identifying / choosing keys
- [[Key Signatures]] narrows it to a major/minor pair; the actual chords + melody decide which
- [[Circle of Fifths]] shows how closely two keys are related (neighbors share 6 of 7 notes)
- Full chord menu per key: [[Chords by Key]]
- **By ear:** find the tonic (imagine the music resolving to one note, or find the bass note of the tonic chord), then test major vs. minor by feel or by playing the minor/major 3rd above the tonic against the track — full method, common mistakes (relative-key confusion, assuming first/last chord = tonic) on [[Transcribing and Playing by Ear]]. — Max Konyi, [[Find the Key of Any Song in 30 Seconds (By Ear)]]

## From videos

- **Tonal vs. atonal music, and how far a key can bend before it stops reading as "in key"** — a piece is tonal when one note functions as fixed "home" (chords/melody/bassline all relate back to it); atonal music deliberately avoids letting any single note read as more home than another, which is why it's less immediately relatable despite using the same 12 notes. A song can include non-diatonic (out-of-scale) notes and still clearly stay "in" a key, as long as the root still reads as home and its major/minor quality stays clear — the safe default is still writing from the scale, but the occasional chromatic note doesn't break the key feeling by itself. → [[Borrowed Chords]] — Max Konyi, [What Does It Mean To Be "In a Key"?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=31EWnCx9JkQ)
