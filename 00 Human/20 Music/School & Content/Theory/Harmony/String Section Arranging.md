# String Section Arranging

How to write chord progressions and lines for a violin-family string section (orchestral or a quartet), and the practical instrument facts that shape it.

## The instruments

The violin family has four members, overlapping in range but each covering a different band of pitch: violin (highest), viola (middle), cello (near the bass end), double bass (lowest). All four can play middle C. The cello, viola, and violin are each tuned in fifths (four strings); the double bass is tuned in fourths (E-A-D-G) — the same tuning as an electric bass, which is effectively an electric double bass.

**An orchestra treats this as five voices, not four**: bass, cello, viola, and violins split into two independent groups (first and second violins) — the only member of the family that reaches the highest treble notes, so having two independent violin parts up there (rather than one violin group plus an extra low voice) is more useful, especially since the melody usually lives in that register.

**A string quartet mirrors that same logic at one-player-per-part scale**: two violins, one viola, one cello — no double bass. The reasoning is identical to the orchestral case: two violins are more valuable than an extra bass voice, especially with the cello already covering the bass role.

## Writing for them as monophonic voices

Each string instrument is written as if it can only play one note at a time (true multi-note playing — double stopping — exists but isn't the default writing approach). So a chord that a guitar could play on one instrument has to be **shared out across the different players/sections** — one note of the chord per instrument/section.

**How to voice the chord — worked from Samuel Barber's *Adagio for Strings*:**
- **Bass gets the root**, same convention as pop/rock bass — an E♭ minor 7 chord puts E♭ in the double bass, or the music won't feel grounded.
- **The double bass is a transposing instrument, sounding an octave below what's written** — historically it existed specifically to double the cello part an octave down (hence the name), and even where it now has its own independent part, cello and bass playing the same root note (an octave apart) is still common.
- **A section can be split into subgroups (*divisi*) to voice more notes than there are literal sections** — Barber splits the cellos into two, and the second violins into two, effectively getting three violin-family voicing slots out of what's nominally two sections; notated either as separate staves or one stave marked "div." (divisi) so players know half the section takes the lower note, half the upper.
- **The viola uses alto clef**, the one common orchestral instrument that does — middle C sits in the middle of both the viola's range and the alto clef itself (the clef symbol is literally a stylized "C" marking that line). Cello/bass use bass clef (for ranges mostly below middle C); violins use treble clef (mostly above).
- **First violins typically carry the melody, sitting on top of the rest as an accompanying texture** — this is *melody-dominated homophony*: every other voice moves together to form one blended harmonic body, while the top line stands out as the tune, functionally the same relationship as a lead vocal over a pop band's accompaniment.

## Voice leading the progression

Chords are the *vertical* interaction between the instruments' notes; **voice leading is the horizontal concern** — how smoothly each individual instrument's line moves from chord to chord. The goal is to minimize the distance each part travels between consecutive notes, rather than letting any instrument leap around its range. In the Barber example, each voice is given the closest available note of the next chord to what it just played, so the part moves stepwise/smoothly rather than jumping. See [[Voice Leading]] for the general principle this applies.

## Playing techniques worth knowing when writing

- **Arco** (Italian for "bow") — the default, bowed articulation.
- **Pizzicato** (Italian for "pinched") — plucked with the fingers; the default technique for a double bass in a non-classical setting (e.g. a jazz trio, where the instrument is usually called "upright bass" rather than "double bass," same instrument).
- **Vibrato** — a default expressive technique added by the player (subtle pitch fluctuation via finger movement on the neck), especially on longer sustained notes.
