# Reading Notation on Guitar

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Notation]]. **Why guitar is genuinely harder to read on than piano, and what the notation actually asks of you.** The symbol system itself is in [[Notation]]; this is the instrument-specific layer.

---

## 1. Guitar sounds an octave below what's written

Guitar is a **transposing instrument**. Written middle C sounds as the C an octave *below* middle C. The whole instrument is printed an octave high so it fits comfortably on a treble staff instead of crawling all over the grand staff.

Engravers sometimes mark this with a small **8 beneath the treble clef** (the `8vb` clef). **Modern scores usually leave it off** and assume the player knows — so the absence of the 8 tells you nothing. Bass guitar works the same way in bass clef.

This only bites when you're reading **against** something — matching a written piano part, checking a DAW's score view, or writing a part for someone else. Playing solo from a guitar chart, it's invisible.

Guitar's written range in standard tuning runs from **E below the treble staff** (open 6th string) up past the staff into `8va` territory at the top of the neck. Anything above about the 12th fret on the 1st string is printed with `8va` to keep the ledger lines sane.

## 2. The real problem: one pitch, five places

On piano, middle C is one key. On guitar, **the same written pitch exists in up to five or six places** on the fretboard, each with a different fingering, a different tone, and a different set of notes reachable next.

That means reading on guitar is not a pitch→note problem, it's a pitch→**position** problem. The notation does not tell you where to play it. You decide, and you decide *ahead* of the notes.

**This is exactly what the position system exists to solve.** [[Guitar Positions]] and the twelve `Position NN` pages define a fixed four-fret window with a fixed finger-per-fret assignment. Inside a position, every written note has one answer. Reading becomes possible because the ambiguity is removed before you start.

Leavitt's whole Volume 1 is first position and nothing else for 24 pages, for exactly this reason — see [[A Modern Method for Guitar - Enrichment]].

## 3. Position and fingering marks

| Mark | Means |
|---|---|
| **Roman numeral** (V, VII, IX…) | Play in that **position** — index finger at that fret |
| **Circled number** ①–④ | **Fretting-hand** finger: 1 index, 2 middle, 3 ring, 4 pinky |
| **Plain number beside a notehead** | Same thing in most guitar engraving |
| **p i m a c** | **Picking-hand** fingers (classical): thumb, index, middle, ring, **pinky** |
| **Circled number on a string** ⑥–① | Which **string** to play it on — 6 is low E |
| **⊓ / ∨** | Pick **downstroke** / **upstroke** |
| **C / CVII** | Full **barre** at that fret (half-barre often `½C`) |
| **Roman numeral, no C/B prefix** (e.g. `V2`) | Pure **position marker**, not a barre instruction — tells you which of several playable positions/fret-locations the tab assumes for that shape (useful when the same chord is reachable in more than one place on the neck) |
| **8va** | Play an octave higher than printed |

String numbering runs **6 → 1, low to high**: E A D G B E. Fret numbering starts at the nut. The two systems get confused constantly — a "3" could be third finger, third string or third fret depending on where it's printed, so read its *position on the page*, not the digit.

**Why p-i-m-a-c doesn't map 1:1 onto English finger names:** the letters come from the Spanish names for the picking-hand fingers — *pulgar* (thumb), *índice* (index), *medio* (middle), *anular* (ring), *meñique* (pinky) — so index/middle happen to line up with their English initials (i, m) but thumb and ring don't (p, a), and the pinky is **c**, not "p" for pinky. A printed "c" above a note in a chord frame or tab means the pinky plays it.

**A bracket over specific strings (not always the top or bottom half) marks a partial/half barre** — e.g. "half CV" over strings 4-3-2 rather than the full 6-string barre implied by a plain "CV." The bracket shows exactly which strings are covered; don't assume it's a literal top-half or bottom-half split. — Jared, [[Georgia on My Mind Fingerstyle Guitar Tutorial (With Sheet Music & Tab)]]

## 4. Chord frames (chord boxes)

The grid diagram above a chord symbol. Joe Pass states the convention plainly on p.2 of [[Joe Pass Guitar Chords - Enrichment|Guitar Chords]], and it's near-universal:

- **Vertical lines = strings**, low E on the left. **Horizontal lines = frets.**
- A **heavy line across the top** = the **nut** (open position). No heavy line + a **Roman numeral to the right** = start at that fret instead.
- **`x` above a string** = muted, not played. **`o`** = played open.
- **Numbers in circles** = fingering. A **curved line across several strings** = a barre with that finger.
- Note names printed **under** the diagram spell the actual voicing, low to high — this is the most useful part of a chord box and the most ignored.

> **Play only the strings indicated.** Joe Pass is explicit that some of his voicings sound wrong if you fill in the muted strings. A chord box is a precise instruction, not a suggestion of a region.

## 5. Slash / rhythm notation

Diagonal slashes on the staff with chord symbols above: **play the chord, one strum per slash**, rhythm as written, voicing up to you. This is how comping and rhythm-guitar parts are notated, and it's what most real-world charts hand you.

Leavitt introduces it on p.11 of Volume 1 and keeps a "Rhythm Accompaniment" thread running alongside the reading thread the whole way through (p.11, p.17, p.24) — chord symbols, then bass-note-and-chord patterns, then chord diagrams.

A **rhythmic slash with a stem and flags** specifies the rhythm exactly. A plain slash means "keep time, your call."

## 6. Tab vs standard notation

| | Tab | Standard |
|---|---|---|
| Tells you | **Where** to put your fingers | **What pitch**, and how long |
| Rhythm | Often absent or approximate | Exact |
| Transposes | No — it's tied to one tuning | Yes, freely |
| Readable by | Guitarists | Everyone |
| Teaches you the fretboard | No | Yes, forced |

Tab is faster to *use* and teaches you nothing. Standard notation is slower to learn and is the only one that makes you know where the notes are. **Combined tab+standard systems** (the modern default in published guitar books) let you take the rhythm from the top staff and the position from the bottom — useful, but it's also the easiest way to never actually learn to read, because the eye drops to the tab under pressure.

None of the three method books in this vault use tab. [[Joe Pass Guitar Chords - Enrichment|Joe Pass Guitar Chords]] gives standard notation *and* a chord box for every voicing; [[Joe Pass Guitar Style - Enrichment|Guitar Style]] and [[A Modern Method for Guitar - Enrichment|A Modern Method]] are standard-notation only.

## 7. What makes guitar reading break down

- **Position choice, not note recognition.** Scan a phrase for its highest and lowest note *before* playing, and pick a position that contains both. Deciding mid-phrase is what causes the stall.
- **Looking at the fretboard.** Guitar tempts you to look down in a way piano doesn't. Reading requires eyes on the page, which means the position window has to be secure by feel.
- **Chords read vertically.** A four-note stack is one object, not four notes — recognise the *shape* against the chord symbol rather than spelling it out.
- **Rhythm, as always.** The most common failure point in reading of any kind. See [[Sight Reading]].

## 7a. The one-spot-per-note drill (a training shortcut)

A deliberately narrower training method for the exact problem in §2 — worth running alongside (not instead of) the full position system. Piano removes the pitch→position decision entirely: one written pitch, one key. Guitar doesn't have that luxury, but you can manufacture it: **pick one specific fret/string location for each note name, in one working register, and commit to playing that note there every single time it shows up in any piece of sheet music** — even when it's reachable elsewhere (an open string, a different position). No exceptions, so there's zero decision-making left when the note appears mid-phrase.

- **Sequence pitch before rhythm, strictly.** First pass: ignore rhythm completely, just quiz yourself — "where's that note, where's that note" — jumping from notehead straight to the fixed fretboard spot as fast as possible, like a flashcard drill. Only once the pitch-jump feels automatic, go back and layer rhythm on top. Don't try to solve both at once while the pitch map is still new.
- **Starts in the middle of the neck, not open position, on purpose.** Most method books start you in open/first position and work outward — which can take years to reach fluent reading further up the neck (Leavitt's own Volume 1 alone is 24 pages of first position only, see §2 above). This drill skips that ladder: build the one-spot map anywhere on the neck you like, the point is committing to a single fixed answer per pitch, not which register you pick.
- **Practice on real lead sheets, not exercises** — actual real-book melodies (demoed on Pat Metheny's "Bright Size Life" and "What a Wonderful World"), and for a denser workout once the basic drill is easy, the **Charlie Parker Omnibook** (dense, chromatic, transcribed improvised lines, not just heads).
- Claimed payoff: ~2 weeks of this drilling to go from stuck to "grab any tune and start plunking out the melody," versus a much longer position-by-position method-book trajectory to reach the same point in the middle of the neck.

— Jared Borkowski, [[Shortcut to Reading Sheet Music for Guitar - Read Guitar Lead Sheets]]
