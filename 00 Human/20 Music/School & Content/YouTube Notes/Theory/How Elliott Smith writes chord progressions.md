# How Elliott Smith writes chord progressions

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HmcSWQ3CZYg)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HmcSWQ3CZYg)

## Synopsis

A signature-technique breakdown, built from listening through Elliott Smith's whole catalog — two recurring harmonic fingerprints: heavy use of the "major II chord" (borrowed from Lydian) and a broader, more constant blending of major and minor within the same song than most songwriters use.

## Techniques

- **The major-II chord, quantified across the catalog**: 40% of all Elliott Smith songs use a MAJOR ii chord (e.g. D major instead of the diatonic D minor in the key of C) — rising to 64.5% of his major-key songs specifically. Common pattern: I → major-II → IV ("Ballad of Big Nothing," "Speed Trials," "Clementine," "Coming Up Roses") — starting on the tonic, moving to the bright/unusual major-II, then resolving the raised note back down to the regular IV, giving a felt "release" as the borrowed brightness settles back into the diatonic key. Variant: major-II → minor-ii ("2:45 AM") — same non-diatonic-to-diatonic contrast, just resolving to the minor two-chord instead of IV.
- **Two valid analytical labels for the same chord, with a stated preference.** The major II can be called a **secondary dominant** (it's V/V — the dominant of the dominant). It can also be called **modal interchange/mixture** (borrowed from the parallel Lydian scale, where the 2nd-degree chord is naturally major). The video argues modal interchange is the more accurate label here specifically because it connects to Smith's SECOND major technique below — it isn't an isolated borrowed chord, it's one instance of a much broader modal-blending habit.
- **Bar-to-bar major/minor ambiguity as a whole-song technique, not just one borrowed chord.** "LA" is nominally in E, but shifts between E major and E minor freely enough that it reads as genuinely both at once: opens on an ambiguous E5 power chord (no 3rd to confirm major or minor), moves to F minor (from E major), then E minor7 (from E minor), then C major7 (from E minor) — before the chorus finally resolves to E major. "Son of Sam" does the same at a larger structural scale: opens clearly in A minor, but the verse doesn't resolve back to Am as expected, later pivoting back to it via an F chord — the song genuinely swings between A major and A minor rather than settling in one.
- **Attributed influence lineage**: this modal-mixture-heavy style is described as shared with Beatles and Radiohead songwriting, and as a direct influence on Phoebe Bridgers (a stated Elliott Smith fan), per a separate video by Brian Krock the host credits for identifying the same "Elliott Smith chord."
