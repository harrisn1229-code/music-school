# how Kanye West changed sampling FOREVER!?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AwtJV33IWPA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AwtJV33IWPA)

## Synopsis

loner surveys the history of sampling through three lenses: Kanye West's early "soul chop" sound (*The College Dropout* / *Late Registration* era), J Dilla's swing/quantization technique (Kanye's own named inspiration), and Drake/40's much more minimalist modern approach — recreating a representative flip for each along the way, using the sponsor plugin/sample bundle "Layers Legacy" as the sound source throughout. The most valuable material here is genuinely technical rather than a plugin ad: a concrete, step-numbered J Dilla swing recipe (rushed snare, heavily-swung-but-perfectly-quantized hats, on-grid kick/open-hat) built around debunking the popular "Dilla just didn't quantize" myth — he actually applied *different levels of quantization to different drum elements* to get the unique bounce — plus Kanye's specific "chop around the drums" space technique from "All Falls Down," his vocal-chop-plus-James-Brown habit, and a clear frequency/emphasis contrast between Kanye/Dilla's meticulous multi-element chopping and Drake/40's "one or two key elements" minimalism (illustrated with "Pound Cake" and "Hotline Bling"). The closing segment on Kanye building a whole new beat from someone else's drums-and-all loop, versus building a second layer from a different intro sample entirely, is a good worked example of "the beat itself can be the sample."

## Techniques

**Recording-limitation framing (why classic soul samples sound the way they do):**
- **The "vintage" sound of classic soul samples is a direct product of the recording limitations of that era, not a mixing choice — recreate it by imposing the same limitations, not by reaching for a "vintage" plugin.** Source studios had very limited mixing tracks, so certain elements had to be recorded in mono with one mic, audio went to tape, and there were no dedicated reverb/delay units the way there are now. Recreated recipe used across the whole video: build each layer (piano, bass, vocal, strings) individually, then push each one through a single room reverb rather than the layered modern effects chains used elsewhere in the same video — the constraint (one mono-feeling reverb per layer, nothing else) *is* the vintage character. → [[Reverb]], [[Ambient Texture and Field Recording]], [[Sampling and Sound Selection]] *(general starting point — exact reverb settings not given)*

**Kanye-style soul-chop layer recipe (recreated, not a real Kanye session — but a genuine, reusable build order):**
- **Piano first, vintage-warped, then room reverb.** Started the sample with a "Vintage Upright Warped" preset piano (Layers Legacy), paired with a MIDI performance, then ran it through a room reverb to sell "recorded in a small vintage studio." → [[BED Slot]], [[Reverb]]
- **Acoustic bass as melody, not just low end — write the runs/glides in.** Added an acoustic bass one-shot; called out that in 70s soul the bassline itself often carried the melody, so deliberately write extra passing notes, runs, and glides into the bass part rather than a plain root-note line. → [[BASS Slot]], [[Bassline Writing]], [[Bass Design]]
- **Two distinct vocal-sample moves: sample lyrics to seed song ideas, or sample a pure vocal riff.** Kanye's catalog shows both: sampling actual sung lyrics and building his own song's lyrical ideas off them, *and* separately sampling a wordless vocal riff purely for its melodic/textural value (used here: a riff pulled from Layers Legacy). → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]], [[Melody Writing]]
- **Pan string accents to different sides for width, rather than centering every layered element.** Added string accents/phrases as the final layer and explicitly panned them across the stereo field for a wide feeling. → [[Stereo Field]], [[Width and Stereo]]

**J Dilla swing/quantization technique — the headline extraction, with exact steps:**
- **Debunked misconception: Dilla did NOT simply skip quantization — he applied *different quantization levels to different drum elements* to get his signature bounce.** This is the core insight the whole segment is built around; the popular "he just didn't quantize" explanation is wrong, and the actual mechanism is asymmetric, per-element quantization rather than a single global "off" switch. → [[Rhythm and Groove]], [[Pocket and Microtiming]]
- **Step 1 — the "rushed" snare:** start from a basic snare pattern sitting perfectly on-grid, select every snare hit, and move the whole selection **left about 6 steps** (i.e., early/rushed relative to the beat) — the opposite direction from the usual "lay it back" hip-hop swing move. *(general starting point — "6 steps" as stated in the source; step size depends on your grid resolution, confirm 16th vs. 32nd before applying)* → [[Rhythm and Groove]], [[Snare Design]]
- **Step 2 — the swung hi-hats, opposite direction from the snare:** the hi-hat notes are perfectly quantized to start, then pushed **back** (late) with swing, "and quite a lot" — the explicit instruction is to push further than feels natural. The snare landing slightly early + the hats landing noticeably late (in opposite directions from each other) is what produces the unique bounce — not either offset alone. → [[Rhythm and Groove]]
- **Step 3 — kick and open hat stay on the grid.** A simple open hat and kick pattern was added and left close to fully quantized, functioning as the stable anchor the rushed snare and swung hats work against. → [[Rhythm and Groove]], [[Kick Design]]

**Kanye chopping technique:**
- **"Chop around the drums" — use silence/space in the chop pattern itself as a rhythmic device, not just a byproduct of where the source has gaps.** Cited from "All Falls Down": Kanye chopped the sample *around* the drums, meaning the chop pattern's gaps line up with the drum hits, creating a new bounce from the interplay of sample-silence and drum-hit rather than from the chop's pitches/timbres alone. → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **Vocal chops specifically sampling James Brown, "countless times," with the gaps filled by a different instrument layer, not silence.** When a vocal-chop section left rhythmic gaps, Kanye filled them with an acoustic bass part plus a *different* hi-hat pattern from the surrounding beat — the fill is a second layer, not a repeat of the existing groove. → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]], [[Rap Vocal Production]]

**Drake / 40-style modern minimalism (contrast case):**
- **The core stylistic split: Kanye/Dilla-era chopping is meticulous and multi-element; Drake's approach is to nail one or two key elements and leave the rest sparse.** Named as the first, most obvious difference on close listening — worth treating as a genre/era dial: more elements + more chopping reads "classic soul sample," fewer elements done really well reads "modern." → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **"Pound Cake"-style vocal treatment: a wet vocal phrase pushed through heavy reverb AND delay (not the vintage single-room-reverb approach above), because the modern era has fancier reverb/delay units than the soul-sample sources did.** Recreated recipe: grab a wet vocal phrase (Layers Legacy), add a heavy reverb plus a delay on top, then lay ambient chords underneath (Retro Roads "Room" preset) rather than a busy multi-layer arrangement — three moves total, deliberately restrained. → [[Reverb]], [[Delay]] *(general starting point — no exact reverb/delay decay or time values given)*
- **"Pound Cake" drums are radically minimal: just kick, snare, and a very subtle bassline underneath** — called out explicitly as "so simple," reinforcing the one-or-two-key-elements framing above; the drums exist to support the vocal sample, not to compete with it. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]], [[Kick Design]]
- **"Hotline Bling"'s three attention-grabbing elements, each with its own recipe:** (1) the **stabby key sound** — Retro Roads "Warped" preset (more crunchy/detuned-sounding than the plain vintage preset), run through an amp, "which a lot of instruments will run through" for that character; (2) the **percussion loop** — a bunch of percussion one-shots from the Layers Legacy vintage drum kit, layered into a loop; (3) glue — a simple room reverb plus a compressor across the percussion layer "to bring them all together." → [[Compression]], [[Reverb]], [[Sound Selection Guide]] *(general starting point — no compressor ratio/threshold given, described as a glue/cohesion move)*

**Kanye "the sample becomes the beat itself" — closing worked example (two different techniques on two different samples, same source track family):**
- **Building a NEW beat's melodic layer from a different intro sample than the main sample, using chord inversions for melody:** for the melodic layer, used a piano (Layers Legacy "Vintage Upper Room" preset) plus a bass one-shot, and specifically used **high notes to create inversions of the underlying chords** as a way to generate melody and rhythm at once — reasoning given: the real players on the original record "would never just play a simple one-chord-per-bar progression," so inversions (not new chords) are how to add movement without breaking the harmony. → [[Melody Writing]], [[Chord]]
- **Deliberately staying in a major scale for a "hopeful" melodic feeling** — called out as a recurring Kanye preference, not a one-off; a usable rule of thumb when a flip needs to read as uplifting rather than moody. → [[Melody Writing]], [[Scale]]
- **Reuse the same vintage-room-reverb vocal-phrase move from the soul-chop recipe above** — added another vocal phrase (Layers Legacy) processed with room reverb again, specifically to keep the "vintage, washed-out" character consistent across the two different sample layers in the same beat. → [[Reverb]]
- **When the source track's OWN drums are usable, take the whole loop as the beat — don't reprogram from scratch.** For the main/beat sample (as opposed to the melodic intro layer above), Kanye "literally took it as the beat itself," drums and all — the lesson: check whether the source loop's own groove is already usable before defaulting to chopping it apart and reprogramming.
- **Vintage-beat recreation recipe when you DO want to rebuild the drums by hand:** copy the chords and bass across from the melodic layer, add an organ one-shot, record a guitar part through a heavily distorted amp preset, then build vintage-style drums from a percussion loop (Layers Legacy) plus two hats and a snare, finishing with **two kick samples layered together** rather than one. → [[Layering and Stacking]], [[Kick Design]], [[Sound Selection Guide]]

**Closing takeaways (stated directly):**
- Classic soul samples sound vintage because of the *limitations* of the era's recording tech — lean into similar limitations deliberately rather than faking the sound with a "vintage" plugin preset alone.
- Modern (Drake-style) sampling is less about layering and more about isolating two or three elements that actually grab the listener.
- For a more experimental/outside-the-box flip: try combining two completely unrelated samples using a scale different from the one you'd normally reach for. → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]]
