# Tempo Changes and Rubato

**What it is:** deliberately varying tempo within a song — a permanent shift up or down, a temporary detour and return, or a continuous free-floating rubato feel — as an expressive/arrangement tool most modern click-track production quietly forecloses.

## Why it's underused
A grid-locked DAW workflow (Pro Tools/Logic/Ableton to a fixed click) makes editing easier but makes tempo a non-decision by default. A consistent tempo isn't wrong — it gives groove and stability — but treating tempo as fixed-by-default means skipping an entire expressive dimension without ever choosing to.

## Shift shapes
- **Permanent speed-up** — the tempo jumps or ramps once and stays there for the rest of the song, usually as a late-song energy injection. "Baba O'Riley" (The Who): 118→173 BPM at 3:21, "like a gear shift," then a further gradual increase into the abrupt ending. "Proud Mary" (Tina Turner's version, vs. Creedence Clearwater Revival's steady original): 92→171 BPM roughly halfway, explicitly announced in the intro ("we're gonna do the beginning easy... then we're gonna do it rough").
- **Metric-modulation halving trick — a tempo change that doesn't feel like one**: "Come On Eileen" (Dexys Midnight Runners) climbs 110→240 BPM, then the final chorus lands at ~120 BPM — which is exactly half of 240, so what was the quarter note becomes the eighth note. The pulse the ear was tracking barely changes even though the metronome number drops by half — reframe a big tempo drop as a subdivision change, not a new tempo, and the transition reads as seamless.
- **Fast intro, slow body** — "Take Me Out" (Franz Ferdinand): opens at 143 BPM, decelerates over the first ~45 seconds down to 107 BPM for the rest of the song — deliberately reversed from the usual "verse slow, chorus fast" intuition (the band found verses worked better fast and choruses better slow, so relocated all the fast material to the front as an intro rather than splitting it verse-by-verse). "We Are Young" (fun.): abrupt 116→91 BPM drop ~40 seconds in, stays slow for the rest.
- **Verse/chorus tempo alternation** — a temporary jump for the chorus, back to the verse tempo after. "Prayer of the Refugee" (Rise Against): 151→170ish BPM at the chorus, back to 151 for the verse — a rhythmic break bridges the drop so the faster section can end cleanly. "Welcome to the Black Parade" (My Chemical Romance): 75 BPM intro → 194 BPM main section → 88 BPM (not all the way back to 75) for the next section — three distinct tempos, not a simple two-way toggle. "Good Vibrations" (Beach Boys): 153→132→153, a full there-and-back. "Paranoid Android" (Radiohead): 82→63→82, the drop reserved for the song's most solemn section.
- **Bookending contrasting sections** — an abrupt tempo change reads as "a new chapter starting," which makes it a natural tool for episodic, multi-section songs. "Bohemian Rhapsody": 71→~beats-per-minute jump at the ballad→operatic section boundary, then a gradual 139→72 descent into the outro. "Scenes from an Italian Restaurant" (Billy Joel): 72→66→97, then back to the opening tempo to reprise the intro material — four sections, each with its own tempo identity.
- **A subtle few-BPM dip as a lyric/anticipation device** — "Tiny Dancer" (Elton John): the tempo eases by only ~4 BPM just before the chorus, both word-painting the lyric ("softly, slowly") and building anticipation for the long-withheld hook. → [[Musical Form]] for the companion AABA-verse device this same song uses.

## Rubato — continuous, not stepped
**Rubato** ("to rob") is tempo/timing that swells and breathes expressively within a phrase rather than jumping between fixed values — time is "stolen" from one beat and given to another. Common in classical performance practice (a rigid-tempo reading of Chopin's Waltz in A minor sounds mechanical; a rubato reading of the same piece, phrase-shaped, reads as genuinely emotional) but not limited to it — Thom Yorke's solo piano playing on "Dawn Chorus" lets tempo ebb and flow take to take, a pop/electronic example of the same idea.

## The click-track tradeoff
Recording to a fixed click (the industry-standard default in Pro Tools/Logic/Ableton) makes edits, comping, and arrangement changes far easier — nothing here argues against that as a default workflow choice. But grid-locked recording makes genuine tempo *rubato* — the small, unplanned, performance-driven fluctuations a human take naturally has — effectively impossible to capture, because everything is quantized to a fixed pulse before it's even played. "Bohemian Rhapsody" was tracked with no click at all — bass/piano/drums recorded together in one room locked only to each other's internal sense of time — which is what let the take's tempo breathe rather than sit dead-flat; getting a "perfect" take that way costs more studio hours, but the tempo movement that results is baked permanently into the recording. Pre-1980s recordings often show audible tempo drift that reads as unplanned energy rather than a compositional decision — "You Can't Always Get What You Want" (Rolling Stones) gradually speeds up across its outro, almost certainly the band's live energy bleeding into the take rather than a written tempo map.
