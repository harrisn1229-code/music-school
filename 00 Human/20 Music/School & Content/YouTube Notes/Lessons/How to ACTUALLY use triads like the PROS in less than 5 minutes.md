# How to ACTUALLY use triads like the PROS in less than 5 minutes

## Synopsis
Jesse Glick's "5 Minute Monday" lesson on a beginner-friendly, chord-shape-first way into triads — deliberately positioned as an alternative to rote triad-shape memorization drills ("triads are incredibly boring to practice, especially if you practice them like this"). Rather than teaching triads as abstract root-3rd-5th shapes to be drilled up and down the neck, the lesson starts from **chords Nate already knows** (open D, the 5th-fret D-shape, the barre D at the 5th fret/D-shape-with-full-bar) and shows how each one already contains a triad — strip the doubled root and you're left with a three-note triad shape. From there, the core technique is a **one-note-at-a-time relocation move**: take the highest note currently being played, find the nearest instance of that same pitch on the next unplayed string, and move it there — repeating the move string by string walks the triad through all its inversions/voicings without ever having to think in scale-degree terms. The lesson closes by applying the whole idea musically: taking a common pop progression (D-A-Bm-G, a I-V-vi-IV) and picking one triad shape per chord to play the progression as a sequence of small triad grips rather than full chords, plus a one-line minor-triad conversion (drop the major 3rd down a fret). Short, practical, and aimed at players who already hold some open/barre chord shapes but haven't connected them to "triads" as a concept yet — a beginner on-ramp that sits directly alongside the vault's more advanced triad-position and voice-leading material.

## What's taught
- **Any triad is hiding inside a chord shape you already know.** Open D major (x x 0 2 3 2) already has the D major triad (D, F♯, A) doubled across its top strings — remove the doubled root note (the open D string's duplicate) and what's left on the top three strings **is** the triad. *(00:01:30 area, per transcript flow)*
- **The core relocation technique — move the highest note to the nearest matching pitch on the next string over:** starting from the open-D-derived triad, take the current highest note (F♯ on the B string, 2nd fret), remove it, and find the nearest F♯ on the next string that isn't currently being played (D string, 4th fret) — the result is a new D major triad/inversion using two held notes plus the relocated one. **Repeat the exact same move** on the new highest note each time (D note under the middle finger → find the nearest D on the A string, 3rd fret; then the A note under the index finger → find the nearest A on the low E string) to walk through **four total voicings of the same D major triad**, moving one note at a time, never re-deriving the shape from scratch.
- **The same relocation move generalizes to any known chord shape**, not just open D — worked a second time starting from the 5th-fret D-chord shape (using the borrowed A note on the low E string as the anchor), producing its own chain of triad voicings by the identical "take the highest note, find its nearest match on the next string" process; and a third time starting from the full barre D shape at the 5th fret (top three strings' triad, then walking the same relocation down through the remaining strings). All three starting shapes converge on physically identical or closely related triad voicings once fully walked out — demonstrating that the method, not the starting shape, is what matters.
- **Minor conversion: drop the major 3rd one fret toward the nut.** To turn any of these D major triad voicings into D minor, find the note that's the triad's 3rd (F♯) and move it down a single fret (F♯→F) — every other note in the shape stays put. Demonstrated on two different voicings from the D-major chain to confirm the move is shape-independent, not a one-off trick for a single fingering.
- **Musical application — one triad grip per chord of a progression.** Worked on a common pop progression **D – A – Bm – G** (I-V-vi-IV in D major): instead of playing full chord voicings, pick one small triad shape per chord and play through the progression as a run of triad grips, demonstrated both as a straight chord-to-chord comp and as a version using different triad voicings/inversions for the same progression to vary the sound — the practical payoff of having multiple triad voicings on tap for a single chord shape, rather than a fixed grip you have to jump to.
- **Framing/practice philosophy:** the whole method is offered as a way to make triads *usable* rather than just theoretically understood — start from a shape already under the fingers, generate the rest of that triad's voicings by ear/feel via the one-note relocation move, and use whichever voicing is closest to the previous chord when soloing/comping over a progression, rather than committing every triad shape to rote memory first.
