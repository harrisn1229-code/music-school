# Quartal Harmony - all about quartal chords on guitar

## Synopsis

A 17-minute theory lesson from Jared Borkowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared), the 24th entry in his chord-and-harmony series, entirely devoted to **quartal harmony** — chords built by stacking 4ths instead of 3rds. He defines the concept against ordinary "tertiary" (stacked-3rds) harmony, walks through fretboard-mapped 3- and 4-note quartal chord shapes built off the C major scale, then pivots to modal harmony as quartal harmony's natural home: a tonal center with every other scale degree treated as equal, no harmonic motion to track, which is why quartal voicings (neutral, non-committal to any one chord quality) suit it better than tertiary chords do. He grounds this historically (Stravinsky/Ravel/Debussy/Schoenberg's 20th-century turn away from functional harmony) and then in the canonical real-world example: Miles Davis's "So What" (*Kind of Blue*, 1959), the Bill Evans quartal comping voicing, and why the tune's Dm11 lead-sheet symbol is really just functional-harmony vocabulary approximating a D Dorian modal sound. The most reusable material for someone who isn't planning to play modal jazz specifically is the back half: **using quartal shapes as a reharmonization/color device over ordinary tertian chords** — harmonizing the minor pentatonic scale in stacked 4ths to reharmonize any minor chord, the demonstration that one fixed quartal shape reads as eight different named tertian chords depending purely on which note you treat as the root, and two inversions of a quartal pentatonic shape that produce genuinely new, still-usable colors from the identical notes. ## What's taught

- **Definition: quartal chords stack 4ths instead of 3rds.** Ordinary ("tertiary") chords take 1-3-5(-7) of a scale; quartal chords take 1-4-7(-…) — same "build off every scale degree" method, different stacking interval. Demonstrated building 3-note quartal chords through C major on strings 5-4-3, and 4-note quartal chords through C major on the middle 4 strings, contrasted against the familiar-sounding tertiary triads built the same way on the same strings. → [[Quartal Chord Voicings]], [[Chords]]
- **Why it sounds "out there": quartal chords don't name a function.** A stack of 3rds implies a chord quality and therefore a harmonic function/resolution; a stack of 4ths states a set of notes without committing to major/minor/dominant — more open, ambiguous, dissonant to ears trained on tertiary harmony.
- **Modal harmony defined and tied to quartal chords.** A tonal center (the root) with every other scale degree treated as equal — nothing resolves, no harmonic motion to track, which is what makes a mode "jam-out heaven": no wrong notes to worry about because there's no motion being violated. Using tertiary chords in that context smuggles back in a sense of function; quartal chords stay neutral, which is why they're the natural vocabulary for modal playing. → [[Modal Jazz]]
- **Historical grounding.** Tertiary/functional harmony dominated Western classical music through the 19th century. In the 20th century, Stravinsky, Ravel, Debussy, Schoenberg and others deliberately started stacking voices in 4ths specifically to escape the sense of a traditional key center — more open, ambiguous, dissonant sounds as a deliberate goal. → [[20th-Century Harmony]]
- **The "So What" chord, fully worked (Miles Davis, *Kind of Blue*, 1959; Bill Evans, piano).** The tune sits in D Dorian — D is the tonal center, every other D Dorian note equally fair game. Evans's signature comping voicing between melody phrases: a stack of **three perfect 4ths with a major 3rd on top** — E-A-D-G-B bottom to top — not purely quartal because of that top major 3rd, but the canonical jazz reference point for "the quartal chord." Move the top note (B, high E string) down an octave to the low E string at the same fret and the five notes reorder into a **genuine 5-note stack of pure perfect 4ths** — identical pitches, different voicing. → [[Quartal Chord Voicings]], [[Modal Jazz]]
- **Dm11 as a functional-harmony approximation of a modal sound.** Lead sheets for "So What" typically show Dm11 held for 16 bars. Derivation: take Dm7, replace the 5th with the 11 (the 4, a whole step above the 5) → the resulting shape is one of the stacked-4ths quartal voicings. The more accurate description is "playing in D Dorian," not "playing over Dm11" — the chord symbol is functional-harmony vocabulary reaching for a modal sound it can't name directly.
- **3-note quartal chord shapes mapped through D Dorian on strings 2-3-4** — the practical comping/improvising vocabulary for a "So What"-style vamp: use the top note of each shape as part of a melodic line, comp with the shapes under a soloist, or arpeggiate them for melodic content.
- **Reharmonization trick: harmonize the minor pentatonic scale in stacked 4ths, to color any minor chord (not just modal tunes).** Take the minor pentatonic scale on one string (worked in E minor along the 2nd string) and stack two perfect 4ths below each scale tone — the same physical shape repeats at every scale degree. This introduces a **natural 6** into the sound, functionally treating the chord as Dorian even when it isn't officially Dorian — explicitly framed as fine to do regardless. Slide the same shape to any root to reharmonize any minor/minor-7 chord — worked over both E minor and A minor. A drone/pedal root note underneath keeps the ear anchored while the moving quartal shapes float on top. → [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]
- **Chromatic connector via the blues note.** The same quartal shape, built off the ♭5/blues note of the pentatonic scale, works as a passing/connecting voicing between the diatonic pentatonic-harmonized shapes.
- **General-purpose version: quartal shapes over any chord type.** Map quartal shapes off the scale a given chord actually belongs to (e.g. quartal shapes over a G7 pull from the C major scale) — same reharmonization approach as the minor-chord case, generalized.
- **One quartal shape, eight different tertian chord identities depending purely on assumed root.** A single fixed 3-note quartal shape reads as: minor 11 (bottom note as root) → ♭ major 7♯11 → sus4 → 6/9 → major 13 → 7sus4 (or minor 11) → sus2 → 6/9 (register up) as the assumed root moves up through the shape's other notes — the physical grip never changes, only which note the ear treats as the root. A direct crossover with the "one shape, many chord identities" rootless-voicing principle. Stringing several of these root-reinterpretations together reads as a coherent, ambiguous chord progression — exactly the non-committal quality that makes quartal shapes suit modal contexts. → [[Quartal Chord Voicings]], [[Chord Voicings]]
- **Two inversions of a quartal pentatonic shape, each producing a genuinely new usable color from identical notes.** (1) Move the bottom note up an octave → no longer a pure quartal stack, but the same pitch classes and therefore usable in the exact same context; following E minor pentatonic along the 2nd string with this inversion gives a whole-step interval on top, described as "beautiful." (2) Move the top note down an octave instead → another new shape from the same notes, followed along the 5th string, called out as a personal favorite. → [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]
