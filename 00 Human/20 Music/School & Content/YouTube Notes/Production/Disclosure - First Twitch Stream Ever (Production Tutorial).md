## Synopsis

Guy Lawrence's (Disclosure) very first Twitch stream — a studio tour + live beat-making session recorded from his home studio just before the *ENERGY* album release (Aug 2020). Unlike the later, more focused production-breakdown streams, this one is a loose "welcome to the studio" hang: he walks through his hardware (Roland Boutique series, Novation Peak, vintage Juno-106/Andromeda/Sub Phatty, monitor chain), explains his Logic template philosophy (a pre-built 81-track "studio already plugged in" session rather than starting from blank), talks through his signature effects chains (H-Delay, Valhalla reverbs, Logic Chorus + Roto Cab for the classic "Disclosure sound"), makes a rough beat live using the TB-303/SH-101/Dilla-style samples, then opens the actual "My Hi" session (the ENERGY-era single) to walk through its real drum-bus and bass-track processing chains. Heavy on workflow/mindset advice (keep writing through blocks, draw inspiration freely, don't over-perfect swing early) alongside genuinely concrete plugin/hardware technique.

## Techniques

**Studio setup / hardware**
- Roland Boutique series (SH-101, D-05, SE-02) chosen specifically because they're USB-powered (audio + power + MIDI over one USB cable) — zero patching friction for capturing a fast idea, unlike deep-menu hardware synths that get skipped because "you forget" while hunting through submenus.
- SE-02 is the one genuinely analog unit in that stack (Moog-derived dual-oscillator) — most flexible-sounding but also the fiddliest to dial in.
- Novation Peak: digital oscillators run through an analog filter/distortion stage — described as "a Juno and a Moog smashed together." Was surprised digital oscillators could sound this good; assumed digital = worse before using it.
- TR-8S (808-style drum machine) and TB-03 (303 reissue) sit right at hand as the "always-on" sketch tools — described as an essential part of the Disclosure setup even when the literal recorded sounds don't make the final track, because the *ideas* that start there lead somewhere.
- Vintage rack: Juno-106 (inherited from his dad's old touring gear), Sub Phatty, Alesis Andromeda (rarely used — too menu-deep/complex despite being a genuinely unique 12-16-voice-poly synth with per-voice outs).

**Monitoring chain**
- Neumann KH 310 (with sub) — wide-spectrum reference monitors; without the sub you can't push them loud (they "fart"/distort). Possibly mastering-grade monitors due to their flat wide response.
- Yamaha NS10s (through a Bryston amp, needs 300W+ to drive properly) — deliberately harsh/midrange-forward "tool" monitors, not pleasant listening; if a mix sounds good quiet on NS10s, switching to the big monitors makes everything "come to life," similar to un-muting the bass when DJing.
- Avantone (self-powered) — even more extreme midrange focus than NS10s, used near the end of a mix as a final-day-only reference; also useful as a mono check (many people use them that way).
- Room treatment tip: in an untreated/small room, physically move your mixing chair around while playing a bass-heavy reference to find the room's actual bass "sweet spot" (every room has dead spots and boomy hot spots) — commit to that seating position rather than assuming a fixed spot works everywhere.

**Logic template philosophy**
- Runs a pre-built 81-track template ("a studio already plugged in and ready to use") rather than starting from a blank session — organized by color/section (all drums together, all synths together, all bass together) so any idea can start instantly without hunting for a track or remembering signal routing.
- Keeps a vocal "scratch bus" pre-loaded with a standard chain (Tube-Tech-style compressor, Pro-Q 3 with rumble removed, a de-esser, H-Delay, Valhalla reverb) ready before a singer even arrives — the same "ready to go, no patching" philosophy applied to sessions with vocalists, which led him to apply the identical idea to his own instrumental template.
- Uses Ableton (ReWired into Logic) specifically for sampling/audio-warping — cites "Expressing What Matters"'s choppy sampled sections as something achievable in Ableton's warp engine that he couldn't replicate in Logic. Stays in Logic for everything else purely because of 20+ years of instinctive keyboard-shortcut/plugin-location muscle memory.
- **ReWire quirk:** always starts a ReWired Ableton-fed session at bar 9, not bar 0/1 — starting at bar 1 produces a bad noise/glitch on transport start with this ReWire setup.
- Runs a permanent tape-hiss + vinyl-crackle bus from the start of every session, kept low in the background — his stated reasoning: none of his favorite records feel "finished" without at least some hiss/grit, so he builds it in from the first idea rather than adding it at the end.

**Signature effects chains**
- The "classic Disclosure sound" on a Juno pad: dry signal down the middle + a bus with (1) Logic's stock Chorus (not an exotic third-party chorus — he's explicit that people often assume it's something fancier) then (2) Logic's Roto Cab (rotary-speaker emulation) for an organ-like ~1–5kHz lift. Two stock Logic effects, used deliberately/simply.
- Go-to synth-bus delay: H-Delay (Soundtoys) at ~80% feedback, lows cut/highs kept so the repeats "swish" rather than muddy, modulation rate around 4 (matched to feel).
- ValhallaDelay is the reach for more experimental/pitchy delay effects (reverse/pitch-shifted feedback) vs. H-Delay's cleaner tape-style repeats.
- ValhallaVintageVerb, Concert Hall + 1980s Color ("bright hall") is the default reverb reach — describes himself as "more of an 80s reverb guy than a 70s guy," saving the 1970s Color's deader/darker top end for something meant to sit further back (e.g. behind a vocal rather than a lead synth). Cites George Michael's "Jesus to a Child" as the reference point for how far an artist (not an engineer) can push vocal reverb.
- UAD EMT plate reverb is the "go-to emergency send" — subtle, works on vocals, group vocals, snare, or anything needing a quick reverb without much thought; also likes Logic's built-in spring-reverb emulation specifically for plucky guitar. Notes that Logic's Space Designer (used on the first two Disclosure albums) now sounds slightly digital/artifact-y to his ears in retrospect.

**Kick synthesis**
- Uses a two-layer kick-synth plugin (one layer for an actual sample, one layer that's a synthesized "shaper") as his current go-to — notes Logic's own stock equivalent does a similar job with less fine control.

**Sampling / drum programming**
- Builds beats from J Dilla-style chopped drum samples (his own collected library, not a specific commercial pack) plus Battery (NI) for triggering hats/claps.
- Uses Splice for scratch percussion (shakers, tambourines) to get an idea moving fast in a session, then plans to re-record that percussion with a real mic later if the track is a keeper — the sample is a placeholder, not the final part.
- Runs a de-reverb tool (cites a cheap/free Native Instruments one; the owned equivalent is [[RX De-reverb]]) on percussion one-shots first to strip baked-in room tone before adding whatever character is actually wanted.

**Sidechain / manual ducking**
- Doesn't use dedicated sidechain-ducker plugins — instead sends a plain 4-on-the-floor noise/click trigger to a bus and keys a normal compressor off it, hand-tuning the compressor's own attack/release to shape the pump. More manual than a one-knob ducker, but full control over the actual gain-reduction curve.
- Flags a real UAD workflow pain point: because UAD plugins process outboard on the Apollo/Satellite hardware (which is the whole point — it's off your CPU), there's real round-trip latency, so a sidechain trigger placed after a stack of UAD plugins has to be manually nudged/delayed to land back in time. Says he asked UA support directly and nobody had a clean fix as of this stream.

**"My Hi" (ENERGY single) walkthrough — real chain breakdown**
- Session built from a cleared garage drum loop (Jeremy [Sylvester]) as the rhythmic foundation, layered with his own recorded rim, clap, and two kicks (a sub kick for weight, a second kick for punch) on top of the loop rather than replacing it.
- Drum bus chain: API-2500 driven hard for an aggressive transient slam at the entry point, tamed via the plugin's own Mix knob → tape saturation (a GP9-style setting, lows rolled off, driven hard specifically for how it "squashes the hi-hats" against every kick hit) → Oxford Inflator (touching the red, A/B'ing the Clip button on/off for two different saturation flavors) → a light corrective master-style EQ cut → a limiter for a few dB of natural-sounding overdrive with headroom left for the master bus.
- Bass/vocal chain: Monarch (u-he) sketched the bassline, then Amino Sekou's vocal *and* the bass were both run through a hardware Distressor pushed to full red for heavy, intentional distortion — the same extreme-hardware-drive philosophy applied to two different sources on the same record — then EQ, then EchoBoy for movement.
- Mastered by Stuart Hawkes at Metropolis Studios, London (also mastered *Settle* and several Disclosure EPs).

**Mindset / process**
- Made ~200 songs to land the 11 that became *ENERGY* — the explicit advice for creative blocks is "just keep writing," including songs you don't like; finish and save them rather than stopping mid-idea.
- On developing an original sound: everyone starts by copying/drawing inspiration (cites a school lesson comparing a Mozart melody to a "sampled" Beethoven passage) — originality comes from which influences you gravitate to, not from avoiding influence entirely.
- Don't over-fix MIDI swing/timing during the initial writing session, especially when working with a collaborator — capture the idea first, tighten timing/swing later if the track actually needs it.
- Genre-hopping (hip-hop side projects, house/garage edits) between "main lane" sessions is presented as a deliberate way to avoid staleness — reset with something unfamiliar so returning to the main sound feels fresh rather than routine.
