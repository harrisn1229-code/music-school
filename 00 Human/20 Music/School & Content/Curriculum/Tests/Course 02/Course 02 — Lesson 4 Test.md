> **Q1.** **A melody note that isn't in the chord underneath it**
> *Also accepted:* non-chord tone
>
> **Q2.** **Approached by step and left by step, continuing in the same direction**
> The direction is the part that distinguishes it from a neighbour tone, which comes back the way it went.
>
> **Q3.** **Because they're what a melody has that an arpeggio doesn't**
> A line made only of chord tones outlines the harmony. The notes between the chord tones are where the line becomes a line.
>
> **Q4.** **Tonicization points at a chord briefly; modulation relocates the tonic and confirms it**
> The difference is whether the ear resets its sense of home. Tonicization borrows the gesture without the commitment.
>
> **Q5.** **How long you stay, and whether you cadence there**
> *Also accepted:* duration and confirmation
> Both together — a long stay with no cadence still reads as wandering, and a quick cadence in passing doesn't reset the ear.
>
> **Q6.** **A chord diatonic to both keys, so it can be heard either way**
> That dual membership is the whole mechanism — it lets the listener reinterpret without noticing a seam.
>
> **Q7.** **Approach it in the old key · reinterpret it · cadence in the new one**
> The cadence is what makes the new key stick rather than sounding like a visit.
>
> **Q8.** **vi in C and ii in G — it belongs to both keys, and it's a natural pre-dominant in the new one**
> Landing on ii in G sets up the D7–G cadence that confirms the modulation, so the pivot does double duty.
>
> **Q9.** **It has the gesture of a modulation but not the confirmation, and duration is doing the arguing**
> *Rubric (5):* says V/V–V supplies a dominant aimed at the new tonic, which is the tonicizing gesture (2) · says there's no cadence *in* the new key, only arrival on its dominant (2) · says the deciding test is whether what follows confirms the new tonic or returns home (1)
> V/V–V puts a real dominant in front of V and arrives there, which is exactly what tonicization sounds like. What's missing is the confirming cadence: you land on the dominant, not on a new tonic that's been established as home. Four bars is long enough for the ear to start treating V as a centre, which is why it's borderline rather than clear-cut. The decider is what happens next — if the passage cadences in the new key and stays, it modulated; if it slides back to the original tonic, the whole thing was an extended tonicization.
>
> **Q10.** **They create moments where the melody disagrees with the harmony and then resolves**
> *Rubric (5):* says chord tones alone agree with the harmony at every point (2) · says non-harmonic tones introduce momentary dissonance (2) · says the resolution of that dissonance is what produces melodic motion or interest (1)
> A melody built only from chord tones is consonant on every note, so nothing is ever unsettled and there's nothing to resolve — that's precisely what makes it read as an arpeggio rather than a tune. Non-harmonic tones put the line briefly at odds with what's underneath it, and the step out of that friction into a chord tone is the smallest unit of melodic motion. The interest isn't in the dissonance itself; it's in the pattern of leaving and returning.
