# The Practice Method That Makes Failure Impossible

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xGDyoa1Ablw)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xGDyoa1Ablw)

## Synopsis

The 6th entry in Jared's ("Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared," soundguitarlessons.com) "sound practice log" vlog series, where he exposes his own practice process on camera. The whole video is a single, well-developed method — what Jared calls the "check-off method" — for learning a piece of written repertoire (classical, fingerstyle, solo guitar, or any fully-notated melody) so thoroughly that mistakes become close to impossible under pressure. He frames it with an origin story: a last-minute, high-stakes gig (playing guitar on Stuart Copeland's reimagined *Ben Hur* score with the Seattle Rock Orchestra, Copeland himself on drums, only a few weeks' notice) that he says he only pulled off by practicing exclusively this way. The method itself is a **pyramid combination order** — build a passage up from single measures into pairs, pairs into groups of four, fours into eights — gated at every step by a **clean-reps-in-a-row streak**, not a raw repetition count, tracked either on the printed sheet music in pencil or in a spreadsheet, with a phone app used as a rep counter. He demonstrates it live on the opening of Bach's Bourrée in E minor (Lute Suite, BWV 996), his own solo-guitar arrangement. This is a genuinely concrete, actionable methodology video, not a motivational one — the "make failure impossible, not just minimize mistakes" framing is a real operating principle behind the specific mechanics (escalating rep-streak targets, checking segments off to force attention onto weak spots), not just a title hook. It shares its channel and its clean-reps-in-a-row gating mechanic with the previously-enriched [[Backward Chaining Practice]] (from a different video, "Piece #6" of Jared's beginner classical series), but the combination order here is a distinct, more elaborate system — forward pyramid pairing rather than backward one-measure-at-a-time chaining — so it gets its own technique note rather than being folded into that one.

## What's taught

- **The check-off method, full pyramid order** (worked on an 8-measure passage): measure 1 alone → measure 2 alone → measures 1+2 together → measure 3 alone → measure 4 alone → measures 3+4 together → measures 1–4 together → measure 5 alone → measure 6 alone → measures 5+6 together → measure 7 alone → measure 8 alone → measures 7+8 together → measures 5–8 together → measures 1–8 (the whole passage) together. Generalizes past 8 measures by continuing the same doubling pattern. Full extraction on [[The Check-Off Method for Repertoire Practice]].
- **Segment boundaries follow phrasing, not strictly the barline** — a segment that opens on a pickup beat is always landed on beat 1 of the *next* measure, not cut exactly at the bar line.
- **The gate is a clean-reps-in-a-row streak, not a rep total.** Demoed baseline: 3 correct in a row; a wrong note resets the count to zero, even on the last rep. For higher-stakes material, push the target well above 3 — Jared names wanting counts up to **10 in a row** for real assurance, and describes deliberately wanting to see it fail near the top of a high count (confirmation it's not actually secure yet) rather than "accidentally" succeeding at a low count and getting false confidence from a lucky streak.
- **"Make failure impossible" as the explicit operating principle**, not just the video title: the goal is stated as not merely minimizing mistakes but eliminating the possibility of them — over-program the technical execution so it needs zero conscious attention during a real performance, freeing full attention for musical expression, dynamics, and interpretation instead.
- **Tracking, two interchangeable forms:** (1) pencil brackets + checkboxes drawn directly onto the printed sheet music around each measure grouping — Jared's method "for years"; (2) a spreadsheet with one row per segment, which scales to holding an entire repertoire list across multiple pieces in one place rather than separate marked-up scores per piece.
- **Rep-counter app named: Multi Timer** — used as a simple tap counter once the target streak count goes past what's easy to track by feel (e.g. approaching 10); the same app also runs his overall session timer and a break timer to keep rest periods from running long and derailing the schedule.
- **No metronome used during this drilling**, deliberately, even on a fairly metronomic Bach passage — kept out specifically to leave room for expressive, non-mechanical timing during the check-off reps.
- **The "shiny spot" anti-bias case for why this beats unstructured practice:** left to their own devices, players default to replaying whatever's already comfortable (his analogy: cleaning a bathroom and gravitating to the one already-sparkling spot instead of the genuinely dirty one) — producing a passage where some measures get drilled a hundred times and others four, with the real trouble spots the least rehearsed of all. Checking a segment off removes it from the rotation, forcing attention onto whatever's still unchecked — the actual weak material.
- **Refresh is structurally built in, not a separate maintenance step.** Because the later pyramid stages recombine already-checked-off smaller units (e.g. drilling measures 1–4 together necessarily replays 1, 2, 3, and 4 again), earlier segments get naturally re-exposed as the piece is assembled, without a dedicated spaced-repetition pass added on top.
- **Judgment call on strictness:** whether a given rep "counts" toward the streak is left to the player's own standard — Jared counts one take despite a minor tone issue on a single note, showing the bar is "honest streak against your own chosen strictness," not a rigid note-perfect requirement.
- **Origin story / use case framing:** developed under real deadline pressure roughly 10 years prior — a few weeks' notice to learn a hard, full-orchestra guitar part (Stuart Copeland's *Ben Hur* score, Seattle Rock Orchestra, Copeland on drums) while already overcommitted — credited as the specific reason that performance came together. Positioned as a "secret weapon" for compressed prep time, but usable any time, not deadline-specific.
- **Repertoire tracking context (secondary, organizational):** Jared briefly shows his own repertoire-tracking spreadsheet (a "classical repertoire" category/homepage with pieces listed and their check-off segments to the right) — noted as an evolving personal system he's actively experimenting with, not a finished template to copy exactly.
- **Piece used for the demo:** Bach's Bourrée in E minor from the Lute Suite in E minor (BWV 996), Jared's own solo-guitar fingering/arrangement, changed recently on one note (a fingering shift from 2 to 3 on a specific note for better ergonomics/less tension, still not fully comfortable at demo time).
