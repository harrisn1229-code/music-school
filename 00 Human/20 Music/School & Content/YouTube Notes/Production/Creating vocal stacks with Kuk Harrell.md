# Creating vocal stacks with Kuk Harrell

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pmDJ4uJPXx4)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pmDJ4uJPXx4)

## Synopsis

A Mix with the Masters walkthrough of Grammy-winning vocal producer Kuk Harrell (Justin Bieber, Rihanna, Beyoncé, Chris Brown) building a stacked background-vocal section live, working from a real second-verse example. Harrell narrates the session step by step — panning the first stack immediately as it's tracked so he can judge it in mono, keeping every stack's level identical to the one before it so he can honestly compare the blend, the "always four, never two" stacking rule, and a specific soft-delivery direction ("give it to me sexy") for a unison layer sitting under an already-powerful lead. He also lays out his simplicity philosophy for FX — one reverb/delay/doubler/EQ/compressor/"driver" chain reused across the entire record's stacks rather than reinvented per line — and closes with the vocal-producer judgment side of the job: harmonies created live and by feel rather than written out, needing both a "toolbox of audio colors" and real music-theory knowledge, and the discipline to know when a record needs elaborate stacks versus none at all (he cites most Rihanna records as staying single-lead). Short and dense — a companion piece to the channel's separate 5:32 Harrell breakdown ([[Radio-ready vocal effects with Kuk Harrell]]), which covers the lead-vocal tracking chain rather than the stack-building process this video documents.

## Techniques

- **Pan a stack hard L/R the instant it's tracked, and judge the FIRST stack in mono before anything else.** Harrell tracks the first stacked take, immediately pans it hard, and specifically listens for tightness *in mono* first ("I'll start panning left and right away so I can tell if it's tight in mono... it's definitely there, so it's so tight"). The panned image only comes after the mono check confirms the take is genuinely tight to the lead — panning wide is what exposes looseness that reads as "fine" in mono but "barely there" once split. → [[1 Double Vocal Track]], [[Width and Stereo]], [[Latency-Free Tracking]]
- **Keep every additional stack's level EXACTLY the same as the one before it while tracking, specifically so you can judge whether the vibe is matching.** "I keep the levels exactly the same because if I start pulling down what they did now I can't really tell if the vibe is matching." Level-matching during the tracking pass — not after — is what makes an honest A/B between stacks possible; pulling one down early hides a blend problem instead of catching it. → [[1 Double Vocal Track]], [[Vocal Comping and Takes]]
- **Stacks always come in an even number — specifically four, never two.** "If you stack something you always want to do four, don't do two — because if you do two you've just got three there and it really doesn't create anything special. So always just do an even number of four." Two takes plus the lead reads as three competing voices; four becomes texture rather than a countable extra voice. → [[1 Double Vocal Track]], [[1 Backup Vocal Track]], [[Layering and Stacking]]
- **Blend later stack layers down in level for balance, rather than matching the first two at full strength.** The third/fourth notes in the stack ("this particular note is blended down just a little bit because [they] have to be balanced properly") sit under the first pair once the mono-check/level-match phase (above) has confirmed they're in tune and lined up — the level-matching discipline is for judging the blend, not the final print level. → [[1 Harmony Vocal Track]]
- **"Give it to me sexy" — the specific soft-delivery direction for a unison layer under an already-powerful lead.** "If the lead is powerful, most of the time it is... I wanted to have some layer and some sweet texture to it, I just simply have them sing that unison part a little bit softer — my term is usually 'now give it to me sexy.'" The softer unison sits as texture "not taking up too much space," and because it was tracked rather than just turned down, he can push its level back up later if he wants more of that specific note. A concrete director's line for the general "harmony/double stays under the lead" rule already on [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]]. → [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]], [[Vocal Mic Technique]]
- **A single reused FX recipe for every stack on the entire record — reverb, delay, a little doubler, EQ, compressor, then "the driver."** "There's no magic here, there's nothing different when it comes to the background vocals — remember, simplicity is key. So once I start... getting my EQ and everything set, it's pretty much the same for the entire record — all my stacks, exact same reverb, delay, little bit of doubler, and then EQ, compressor, and then just the driver." *("The driver" is not otherwise identified on camera or in captions — flagged as unconfirmed; likely a saturation/drive stage, not matched to a specific owned or wishlist plugin.)* Same "dial it in once, reuse it everywhere" philosophy as Harrell's tracking-chain video ([[Radio-ready vocal effects with Kuk Harrell]]), applied here to the whole stack bus rather than just the lead. → [[1 Backup Vocal Track]], [[1 Harmony Vocal Track]], [[Reverb]] (effect), [[Delay]] (effect)
- **Pull a vocal moment straight from the demo instead of re-cutting it, specifically because it already sounds right — compressed, pushed, and vocoder-like.** On one line in the second verse: "we realized that we love how that sounded in the demo, so we pulled that in from the demo — him doing [it] — but it's really compressed, it's really pushed, and it sounds like it has vocoder on it as well." A take-selection call, not a processing one: the demo's baked-in character (heavy compression + pitch artifacts reading as vocoder) was the reason to keep it rather than replace it with a "clean" re-tracked pass. → [[Vocal Comping and Takes]]
- **Layer a high ad-lib alongside block harmonies in the same section, as two distinct colors rather than one thickened part.** The second verse's extra layers: block harmonies (tracked per the four-stack rule above) plus "a high ad lib" added on top — two different jobs (harmonic fill vs. energy/response) stacked into the same few bars. → [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]]
- **Write two harmony lines that deliberately CROSS against each other rather than doubling the same phrase — the interplay itself is the effect.** "This high harmony, 'easy to let me go,' and then that also goes with 'easy to let go' — so you got two parts that are running against each other, and it just creates great magic." Two harmony lines carrying slightly different words/timing landing against each other, rather than two takes of the same line, is what's doing the work here — not extra processing. → [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]], [[Melody Writing]]
- **Harmonies are never written out — they're improvised live, on the spot, by feel, which is exactly what turns a demo into a record.** "None of these things are written out... I don't listen to it and write stuff down, because you don't have time for that — I go on all feeling. That's why it's so important to have a toolbox of audio colors, and that you know music theory... you know what harmonies work together, when things are in the right key, when things are in the wrong key." He frames this explicitly as the vocal producer's value-add over the demo: "there really weren't any harmonies there — that's the extra thing... that I bring to the demo that makes the demo a record." Speed matters too: "the artist does not have time to stand in a booth while you listen to something and go 'wait a second, let me figure out something' — these things literally come out of me at all times." → [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]]
- **The judgment call on WHETHER a record needs harmonies at all is as important as how to build them — compared directly to a football coach calling a play.** "You have to know when to make a call... you have to know if this is a record that calls for complicated harmonies or just simple harmonies — because if it's like a young pop record, no harmonies at all... most of the records that we do with Rihanna are very simple." He names the goal directly: "it's all about showing the vocalist['s] tone [and] their special ability, and not how many tricky harmonies I can come up with" — restraint as a deliberate choice, not a fallback. Ties into this vault's existing "further from the lyric = more texture" governing rule on [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]] by extending it one level up: sometimes the right amount of stacking is none. → [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
