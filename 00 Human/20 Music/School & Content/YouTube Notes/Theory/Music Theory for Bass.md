# Music Theory for Bass

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GkQGceZynxg)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GkQGceZynxg)

## Synopsis
A "mega dump" survey of essentially the whole beginner/intermediate theory curriculum, taught explicitly through the lens of "use it immediately on the bass or it doesn't count" — the host frames his own college theory classes as a cautionary tale (classmates who memorized rules like figured bass and cadence names without ever applying them never actually learned the material) and states his method up front: every new concept gets picked up and played on the instrument the moment it's introduced. The video moves fast through notes/pitches → the musical alphabet and fretboard navigation → generic vs. specific intervals → scales → chords/triads and arpeggios → chord progressions and diatonic numbering → rhythm (beat/tempo/time signature/note values) → song form, consistently translating each abstract concept into "what shape does this make on the bass neck." Nothing here is deep on any single topic by design (the host repeatedly defers depth to a paid course and a free PDF), and every topic already has a dedicated, much deeper reference page in this vault's [[Music Theory]] library — the video's real value-add is the **bass-first framing**: intervals become fretboard shapes ("box shapes") you can navigate without knowing interval names, chords become arpeggios you outline one note at a time instead of full grips, and the musical alphabet becomes a literal fret-by-fret map starting from the four open strings. Good orientation video for a bass beginner; low new-information density for anyone who has already read the theory library, but the bass-specific translations are worth capturing since nothing in the vault currently documents them.

## What's taught

**Notes & the musical alphabet**
- A note = a sound made by a vibrating string; how fast the air vibrates determines pitch (slow = low, fast = high) — plucking a string, doubling the vibration speed produces a note that sounds like "the same note," just higher: this special relationship is the **octave**, and it's what turns an infinite range of possible pitches into a manageable, limited note set
- Western music uses a **12-note alphabet**: the first 7 letters (A B C D E F G) name the naturals, and the remaining 5 notes are named by calling them sharp or flat relative to a neighboring natural — forward through the alphabet = higher pitch, backward = lower
- **Enharmonics**: the same pitch can have two valid names (F♯ = G♭) — which name is "correct" depends on the song's key/context, not personal preference (matches [[Notes & Pitches]]'s existing enharmonic-equivalents point)
- **Bass fretboard navigation via the alphabet**: start from a few reference notes — the four open strings, **E A D G** — then move up one fret at a time to walk the musical alphabet in order: A, A♯, B, C, C♯, D, D♯, E, F, F♯, G, G♯, A. Framed explicitly as "having a map" for a fretboard that's otherwise "daunting and mysterious." Practical payoff stated directly: if a bandmate calls out a progression by letter name ("G to E to C"), you can find and play those notes immediately using the alphabet map alone, with no other theory required
- Free companion PDF (linked in the video description) with practice resources for the alphabet and everything below

**Intervals**
- **Generic intervals** — count the letter-name distance only: A→B = a 2nd, A→C = a 3rd, A→D = a 4th... up through a 7th, and A→A = an 8th (called an octave)
- **Specific intervals** — count the exact number of half-steps (one fret / one piano key) between two notes: A→C is 3 half-steps, A→C♯ is 4 half-steps — this precision is what lets you tell A→C (a **minor third**) apart from A→C♯ (a **major third**) even though both are generically "a third." A→E is a **perfect fifth**; A→E♭ is a **diminished fifth**. Matches this vault's existing quality-naming rules in [[Intervals]]
- **The bass-specific shortcut: intervals as fretboard shapes.** Any interval played on the bass makes a visual pattern — a fixed number of frets and strings apart — so the neck itself functions as an interval ruler without needing to know the theory names. The video's example: the **"box shape"** used by "millions of bassists who don't know any interval names" to play a perfect-fifth / minor-third / major-second pattern purely by shape recognition. Explicitly stated as a legitimate, widely-used alternative to learning interval names — many working bassists navigate by shape, not by interval ID

**Scales**
- A scale limits the 12-note alphabet down to a smaller, curated "palette" so a song doesn't sound like chaotic note-soup — analogized to a painter choosing a limited color palette rather than every color at once
- Named scales covered, roughly in order of how common they are: **major**, **natural minor**, **major pentatonic**, **minor pentatonic**, **blues scale** — "most people stop there." Deeper end (named but explicitly flagged as not-right-now material): all 7 **modes of the major scale** (Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, Locrian), the **whole-tone scale**, **diminished scales**, **harmonic minor** and its modes, **melodic minor** and its modes. Stated advice: learn major and minor first, don't try to absorb all of the above at once
- **Movable major-scale box shape on bass**: learn one shape, plant it on whichever note you want as the root (e.g. start on C for a C major scale), and you can play along with any song in that key
- **Ear application, scale-degree framing**: once a scale is genuinely "in your ear," a bassline reads as its scale-degree pattern rather than a memorized sequence of notes — cited examples: Gorillaz' "Feel Good Inc." bassline as the 1st/2nd/3rd (etc.) degrees of the **E minor** scale shape; The Jackson 5's "I Want You Back" bassline as octave-7-6-5-4-3-2-5 of a major scale

**Chords, triads & arpeggios**
- A chord = a scale-derived stack of notes sounded together, built by **skipping every other note** of a scale: playing degrees 1-3-5 of a major scale (skipping 2, 4) gives a **major triad**; the same 1-3-5 skip on a minor scale gives a **minor triad**. Major and minor triads are stated as "by far the most common chords in the music we all listen to" — cited examples that use only major/minor chords: The Beatles "I Want to Hold Your Hand," Coldplay "Yellow," Fleetwood Mac "The Chain"
- **Bassists mostly don't play full chords** — playing several notes of a chord together on bass "makes a bunch of mud that doesn't work in the average song." Exceptions named: Peter Hook, Marcus Miller do play chords on bass, but that's the exception, not the norm. Standard bass practice instead: play the same chord notes **one at a time** — an **arpeggio** — choosing from the guitar/keyboard's chord tones to build a bassline that fits
- **Two triad shapes cover most bass playing**: the **major triad shape** (grabbed from the 1-3-5 of the major scale shape already learned) works over any major chord; the **minor triad shape** (1-3-5 of the minor scale shape) works over any minor chord. Workflow: find the chord's root via the alphabet/fretboard map, then lay the matching major- or minor-triad shape over that root

**Chord progressions & diatonic numbering**
- A **chord progression** is how chords move through a song; the chord built on the scale's root/tonic note is the song's "home base" and establishes the key — other chords are the "journey" away from and back to that home base
- **Diatonic chords**: every major or minor scale has 7 notes, and the same 1-3-5-skip trick used for triads can build a chord starting on *any* of those 7 scale notes, giving 7 diatonic chords total, all built from in-key notes only
- **Nashville numbers**: label each diatonic chord by which scale degree it starts on — the chord built on the 1st scale degree is "the one chord," on the 2nd degree "the two chord," and so on through all 7. Framed as the tool for spotting the patterns behind "90% or more of popular songs"
- Worked song examples in Nashville numbers: Journey's "Don't Stop Believin'" = **1-5-6-4**; The Who's "Baba O'Riley" = **1-5-4**

**Rhythm**
- **Beat** = the song's repeating pulse; **tempo** = how fast the beats happen, measured in **BPM** (beats per minute); a group of beats = a **bar/measure**
- **Time signature**: top number = beats per bar, bottom number = which note value equals one beat. **4/4** = four beats per bar, quarter note = one beat
- **Note-value durations** (in 4/4): whole note = 4 beats; half note = 2 beats; quarter note = 1 beat; eighth note = half a beat (counted "1 and 2 and…"); eighth-note triplet = a third of a beat (counted "1 and-a 2 and-a…"); sixteenth note = a quarter of a beat (counted "1 e and a 2 e and a…"). Framed as learnable either by reading notation or by ear/feel from the counted syllables alone

**Song form**
- **Form** = the rhythm of when a song changes sections (verse → chorus → bridge), zoomed out from beat-level rhythm to section-level rhythm — "feeling where you are in the song so you don't get lost"
- Three named forms cited as extremely common: the **12-bar blues** (a repeating form used across thousands of blues-descended songs); the **AABA** form (a jazz-standard structure: a main A section that plays and repeats, a contrasting B section, then a return to A); the standard pop/rock **verse-chorus-verse-chorus-bridge-chorus-chorus** layout
- Stated payoff: the more familiar you become with these common forms, the more you can "feel" your place in an unfamiliar song and stay oriented without getting lost

**Meta / teaching philosophy**
- Central thesis: theory only sticks when it's applied on the instrument immediately after being taught, not studied abstractly — compared explicitly to studying a spoken language for years without ever speaking it to anyone
- Self-promotional plug (not a technique, noted for completeness): BassBuzz's "Beginner to Badass" course teaches theory "100% integrated with music" (every lesson also builds technique/musicianship on real songs), and a separate, more targeted video ("music theory you actually need") splits theory into 5 levels from casual jammer to full mastery, aimed at helping a viewer skip content they don't personally need yet
