# Half-Step Pentatonic Shift Over Moving Changes

A lighter-weight sibling to [[Single-Note Pentatonic Substitutions Over Changes]] — that page swaps one note within a static pentatonic box as the chord changes; this page instead **slides the whole box (or arpeggio shape) up a half step**, timed to land exactly when the harmony itself moves up a half step. Worked over the [[🎵 TADD DAMERON - Lady Bird|Lady Bird]] changes, where — per Jens Larsen — "a lot of the harmony tends to move up" a half step repeatedly through the form, which is what makes the device recur so often in this particular tune.

## The core move: pick the "color" pentatonic specifically because its relative sits one fret from where you're headed

Over Cmaj7, **E minor pentatonic** is a common "10th line" color choice (built off the major 7th's — or more precisely a third above the root's — relative minor). The reason it's chosen *here*, specifically, isn't just that it sounds good over the tonic: its relative, **F minor pentatonic**, sits exactly a half step up on the fretboard — which happens to be the next chord (Fm7). So the same movable pentatonic shape just slides one fret when the chord changes; there's no new box to learn, no arpeggio to switch to mid-phrase. The line keeps moving in **its own logic** (a half step up) rather than tracking the chord change note-for-note, which reads as a mild counterpoint against the bass — it doesn't sound like the melody is "just laying there" restating the harmony.

## Generalizes beyond pentatonic boxes: half-step-planed arpeggios

The same device shows up in the source video as a **half-step-up arpeggio plane**, not just a pentatonic shift: over a B♭7-to-Cmaj7 move, playing a **D♭ major 7 arpeggio** (a half step above the target Cmaj7) resolves down cleanly onto the real chord. The stated mental model matters: even while playing notes that spell a D♭maj7 shape over a chord that isn't D♭, the player is still *hearing/tracking the real underlying chord* the whole time — the half-step-up shape is a **melodic observation** (an interval relationship that happens to produce good target notes), not a conscious reharmonization. This distinction is worth keeping explicit: you're not "thinking in D♭," you're borrowing D♭'s shape as a source of notes that resolve well.

## Why the underlying pentatonic/arpeggio choice works: shared chord tones, not coincidence

The reason the "10th line" pentatonic (built a third above the chord) sits comfortably over the tonic chord in the first place is ordinary chord-scale overlap — see [[Single-Note Pentatonic Substitutions Over Changes]] for the same logic worked from the opposite direction (starting from the tonic's own pentatonic and adding one note per chord change, rather than starting from a related pentatonic and shifting the whole thing). The half-step-shift device here is really a special case: it only works cleanly when the *next* chord happens to sit a half step above the *current* color choice's relative — so identifying which pentatonic/arpeggio has that lucky half-step relationship to the next chord is the actual skill, not a fixed formula that applies everywhere.

## Caution: don't lean on it as a crutch

Stated directly in the source: overusing the half-step-shift move on its own gets "boring" fast — it's a strong, recognizable device precisely because it's simple, which means repeating it too often flattens a solo into one trick. Mix it with other connective material (chord-tone targeting, diatonic passing tones) rather than making every chord change a half-step slide.

## Practice method

1. Find a chord change in the tune that moves by a half step (or where a *related* scale/arpeggio a half step away from your current material lines up with the next chord).
2. Identify the movable box/arpeggio shape you're already using and confirm it slides cleanly — same fingering, one fret up.
3. Play slowly in tempo, landing the shift exactly on the chord change downbeat, and listen for the counterpoint effect against the bass rather than a literal chord-tone restatement.
4. Deliberately limit how often you use it in a single chorus — alternate with other target-note or diatonic connective devices so it stays a surprise rather than a tic.
