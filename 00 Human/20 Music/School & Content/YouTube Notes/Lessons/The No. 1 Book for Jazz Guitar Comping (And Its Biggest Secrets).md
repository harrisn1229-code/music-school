# The #1 Book for Jazz Guitar Comping (And Its Biggest Secrets)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Q_veiwqoXwM)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Q_veiwqoXwM)

## Synopsis
Nathan Borton breaks down Barry Galbraith's *Comping Etudes* — a jazz-guitar comping method book built from real, playable etudes rather than abstract theory, with accompanying audio of Galbraith comping the etudes alongside bassist Milt Hinton — and pulls out what he considers the book's two biggest secrets. The first is a reharmonization device he names "multifunctional voicings": a single memorized chord shape (a min7♭5, a half-diminished, a maj7♯5, or a dominant 9 grip) is played starting from different chord tones of a target dominant chord to produce entirely different harmonic functions from the exact same fingering — altered dominants, dominant 9s, and even a tritone-substitute reuse of a familiar shape. The second is rhythmic: the Charleston rhythm, the backbone of a huge amount of jazz comping, shown not just in its textbook beat-1 form but displaced to start on beat 2, the "and" of beat 1, or beat 3, and combined freely with quarter and half notes. Borton then works through three increasingly dense 2-5-1/blues comping lines straight from the book that chain both ideas together, before closing with a four-part practice method (play-then-analyze, play along with the recordings, one etude at a time over months, and drill with rhythm apps) for actually internalizing material like this. Short (11 minutes) but dense and fully transcript-legible — a spoken lesson, no music-garbled sections. Most valuable as a concrete, reusable comping-efficiency trick (one shape → several altered-dominant colors) rather than a general theory survey.

## What's taught
- **Multifunctional voicings — the core reharmonization concept**: one chord shape, played off different chord tones, implies different chords/functions. Full derivation, all four worked shapes (min7♭5, half-diminished, maj7♯5, tritone-sub dominant 9), and the three chained 2-5-1/blues comping lines → [[Multifunctional Voicings]].
  - Fm7♭5 shape: off its own root = plain m7♭5; off the ♭7 of a dominant = a highly altered voicing for that dominant (worked over G7); off the 3rd of a dominant = a dominant 9 voicing *(general rule stated, not tied to one specific root in this example)*.
  - Half-diminished shape: off root = plain half-diminished; off the ♭7 of the dominant (worked as A♭, the ♭7 of B♭7) = B♭7-altered, resolving to I.
  - A♭maj7♯5 shape: off root = plain maj7♯5; off the 3rd of a dominant = another altered-dominant color.
  - A familiar dominant-9 shape (fingered as if rooted on B♭) reused rooted a tritone away to voice an altered E7 (E7♯9♯5) — the tritone-substitute application of the same "reuse one shape at a new root" logic.
  - Two recurring resolution voicings worth remembering as go-to shapes on their own: a **quartal voicing off G major built off the 3rd** (line 1's resolution), and an **open-string A minor 9** (used to resolve both line 2 and the minor-blues line) — the open-string A minor 9 shows up twice, confirming it's a stock Galbraith resolution shape, not a one-off.
- **Rhythm — the Charleston, displaced.** The Charleston (traditionally struck on beat 1) is shown starting on beat 2, the "and" of beat 1, or beat 3, freely combined with quarter and half notes — "adding surprise and propulsion." → folded into [[Jazz Comping — Rhythmic Motif and Walking Bassline]] (the existing note already documents the fixed beat-1/and-of-2 Charleston form from other sources; this adds the displaced-starting-point variation).
- **Three worked 2-5-1/blues comping lines from the book** chaining the voicing and rhythm ideas together (Charleston-on-beat-3 + half-diminished-off-♭7 of D7 → quartal-off-G resolution; a second line adding the A♭maj7♯5-off-3rd device, resolving to open-string Am9; the minor-blues etude's two V-I ideas, the second being the tritone-substitute B♭9-as-altered-E7 device) — full breakdown on [[Multifunctional Voicings]].
- **Practice method — how to actually learn from an etude book like this** (four numbered steps in the source): (1) treat the etude like a transcription — play and memorize it precisely *before* reading/analyzing the chord symbols, deconstruct only afterward; (2) play along with the book's own recordings (Galbraith + Milt Hinton) until locked in with the rhythm section, not just correct on the page; (3) work only one etude at a time, often for months, treating it as deep assimilation rather than a race; (4) drill with a dedicated rhythm app (named: **Drum Genius, Pro Metronome**) rather than a plain metronome, to isolate and reinforce the rhythmic concepts specifically. Full write-up → [[Etude-Based Vocabulary Practice]].
