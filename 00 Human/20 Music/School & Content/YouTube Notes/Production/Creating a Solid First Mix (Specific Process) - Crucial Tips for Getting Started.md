# Creating a Solid First Mix (Specific Process) - Crucial Tips for Getting Started

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dXBNtKHt2r0)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dXBNtKHt2r0)

## Synopsis

Max Konyi walks through one complete, ordered first-mix pass on a finished arrangement (Studio One, but every move maps cleanly to Ableton stock devices): a volume balance done at low listening volume and in strict order of importance, panning for space and stereo balance, reverb/delay placement, and a final multi-listen QA pass before export. He's explicit this is *one* method among many (mix-as-you-compose, mix-only-after-everything's-written, hire it out), not a universal recipe. The most transferable, concrete ideas: mixing quietly first because loud listening distorts perceived frequency balance; working outward from a single "most important element" with a fixed reference point (drums) rather than eyeballing all faders at once; the fact that fader/pan moves silently get overridden on any track carrying automation, which needs an insert-gain workaround; and a simple rule of thumb for which sources reverb sits well on (sustained notes) versus which reveal it too obviously (short/plucked notes).

## Techniques

- **Do the volume balance pass at low listening volume, then spot-check louder afterward** — turning the monitors up changes how your ears perceive the balance between frequencies (quiet-sounding elements can seem fine, or already-fine elements can seem too loud, purely because of the volume you're listening at), so a volume-balance pass should happen quiet, with occasional turns-up/back-down to confirm the balance still holds louder. → [[Gain Staging]]
- **Loop the main section of the song before balancing, then loop other distinct sections in turn** — set the loop bracket around the song's main/most representative section first and do the bulk of the balance there; once that's solid, move the loop to a different section with different instrumentation (a guitar/piano-heavy passage in the example) and balance those newly-introduced elements against what's already set. Don't try to balance the whole song at once from the top.
- **Balance in strict order of importance, using drums as your fixed reference point** — before touching faders, do a mental scan of every element and rank them: identify the single MOST important element in the song (here, a lead flute carrying the melody) and make sure it reads clearly above everything else; then work outward through the next-most-important elements one at a time, always checking each new element against the one above it in the ranking rather than against the whole mix at once. Drums are treated as a stable reference point everything else gets balanced relative to, rather than being re-judged each pass. → [[Gain Staging]]
- **A track with volume automation ignores fader moves — use an insert gain device instead of the fader** — if a track already has volume automation drawn on it, moving that track's fader during a balance pass has no lasting effect: on playback the automation snaps the level right back to whatever it says, because the automation (not the fader position) is what's actually driving the track's level. To adjust the *overall* level of an automated track without erasing or fighting the automation, insert a gain-only utility device (Studio One's "Mix Tool"; the Ableton stock equivalent is [[Utility]]'s Gain knob) ahead of/instead of the fader — this becomes the new volume control for that track, leaving the drawn automation curve untouched underneath it. Same applies to pan automation: an automated pan won't hold a manual pan-knob move either — the fix there is editing the automation itself, not fighting it via the knob. → [[Gain Staging]], [[Automation]], [[Utility]]
- **Check one-shot, non-looping ear-candy elements (sweeps, reverse hits) separately from the main balance pass** — small one-off elements that don't repeat with the main loop (a riser sweep, a reversed piano hit) are easy to lose track of during a loop-based balance pass since they only play once; do a dedicated check listening specifically for those, confirming each one sits at the right level rather than assuming the main pass caught them.
- **Panning reads as a volume increase — expect to re-balance levels after panning, not just before** — once every element sits dead center, they're all competing for the same space and volume; the moment something gets panned even partially off-center it suddenly reads as louder/clearer purely because it's no longer sharing the exact same position with everything else — so a second pass at trimming individual levels after panning is normal and expected, not a sign the first balance pass was wrong. → [[Width and Stereo]]
- **Use partial pan positions more often than full hard-panning, and reserve hard-panning as a deliberate special case** — "hard panning" = sending an element fully to one side; it's available and sometimes exactly right, but panning most elements only partway (leaving some signal audible in the opposite ear) reads as more natural for most material — audition both and let the part decide, rather than defaulting to 100%.
- **Keep the lead melody, kick, snare, and bass centered — and keep thick harmonic elements (chords/pads) centered too, or the mix reads lopsided** — the lead melody and the core rhythm-section anchors (kick, snare, bass) should generally stay dead center so they read as the fixed focus of the mix. A large, thick-sounding harmonic element (a chord/pad part) is a special case worth calling out separately from thin single-note parts: pan it hard to one side and the *opposite* side of the mix can start to feel hollow/empty by comparison, because that element was filling a lot of perceptual space on its own — keep wide/thick elements closer to center so they fill both sides roughly evenly, and save hard panning for thinner, less space-filling parts. → [[Width and Stereo]]
- **Order reverb and delay as delay-into-reverb, not reverb-into-delay** — when both effects sit on the same element, the delay should be earlier in the signal chain than the reverb (the echoes should "enter the room" rather than the room echoing) — an insert order that can be fixed after the fact by dragging the devices to reorder them if they were built the wrong way round. *(Confirms the existing house convention — see [[Depth and Space]].)*
- **Be cautious with reverb/delay on bass and drums; everything else is fair game to experiment on** — the two elements most likely to suffer from added space are the bass and the drums (loses low-end focus/punch); everything else in the mix is open territory to try different reverb and delay types on and see what works. *(Confirms the existing house convention — see [[Depth and Space]].)*
- **Sustained, held notes blend into reverb more naturally than short, plucked notes — a rule of thumb for choosing what gets a reverb send** — a part that holds long notes (a legato guitar line, a piano part with sustain) absorbs reverb smoothly, expanding into the space and changing subtly over time without drawing attention to the effect itself, unless you deliberately stop/start the transport to expose it. A part made of short, quickly-decaying notes reveals the reverb's presence much more distinctly by comparison, since there's more silence between hits for the tail to be heard in isolation. Use this as a first filter for which parts are strong reverb candidates versus parts that need a lighter touch or a different depth cue instead. → [[Depth and Space]]
- **A/B every insert on/off ("super dry") before committing, section by section** — after dialing in reverb/delay per element in one section, toggle the inserts off entirely to hear the fully dry state, confirm the wet version is actually an improvement, then repeat the reverb/delay pass on the next distinct section of the song rather than assuming settings from one section transfer cleanly to another.
- **Do a final multi-pass, multi-system, multi-volume listen-through hunting for anything that "jumps out"** — once volume, panning, and reverb/delay are set, listen to the whole song start to finish several times, deliberately switching listening volume (including quiet) and playback system (headphones, computer speakers) between passes, since different volumes/systems expose different problems. The specific thing you're listening for is any single element that suddenly feels too loud/wrong relative to everything else — when one jumps out, the two available fixes are pulling its volume down, or pushing it further back in depth with reverb so it "sinks backward" into the mix instead of sitting on top of it. Expect to get sick of the song by the end of this process — that's a normal side effect of the repetition, not a sign something's wrong. → [[Referencing and Translation]], [[Depth and Space]]
- **Use incremental Save As versions before any risky experimental move** — before trying something you're unsure about (a big processing change, a structural edit), save the project under a new incrementing name (e.g. "Song Name V2") rather than overwriting the working file — if the experiment goes badly, the previous version is still intact to fall back to, so nothing is risked by trying it.
- **The mix is considered done when a full listen-through leaves you completely satisfied** — the endpoint of this process isn't a checklist, it's a final full playthrough with no notes; once that's reached, the song is ready to export (bounce to an MP3/audio file).
