Where the sibling videos are about singing scale degrees/solfège against a known reference (melodic, tonal-context-first), this one is squarely **harmonic ear training** — pulling individual notes out of *simultaneous* sound, the skill Jared says guitar's fretted, look-don't-listen design actively suppresses relative to fretless/bowed instruments. ## What's taught

- **Why guitarists specifically have underdeveloped ears — the fretted-instrument argument.** The brain only builds a skill it's forced to use; guitar's frets let you find/verify a note by looking at your hand instead of listening for it, so guitarists can go years without ever being forced to identify a pitch by ear. Contrast: violin/cello/upright bass players must intonate by ear from their very first lesson (no frets to look at), so string players who don't play guitar often have a better harmonic ear than guitarists who've played for decades, purely from that structural difference — not talent. Jared's own anecdote: a double-bass player in his gypsy jazz group (classically trained, played mandolin and other fretted instruments too) said his own ear was "horrible" until he switched to fretless upright bass, which forced him to start listening for the first time.
- **The two kinds of ear training this channel draws a hard line between (stated explicitly here):**
  - **Harmonic ear training** — hearing *multiple simultaneous* notes and separating/identifying one from the mass (this video's entire focus).
  - **Melodic ear training** — hearing single notes happening one after another and judging the distance between them (the [[Scale Degree Ear Training]] / solfège-singing side, covered in the sibling videos).
- **Exercise 1 — The chord-tone isolation test (unstructured, "fun test" version):**
  1. Pick a chord (G, D, C, Am all demonstrated) and **strum it quickly** — explicitly *not* slowly/broken, because hearing the notes ring out individually defeats the point; the test is separating a genuinely simultaneous sound.
  2. **Before playing, silently pick one string/note to try to isolate** (e.g. "the note on the third string") — decide the target ahead of time rather than picking whatever happens to jump out.
  3. Strum the full chord, then try to isolate and **sing** just that one note.
  4. **Check** by playing that string alone right after singing — if you were off, you'll hear it immediately.
  5. **Expect total failure at first — that's the correct, useful outcome**, not a sign of a bad ear: "this is good, this is normal... the brain starting to listen in a way that it hasn't had to before."
  6. **If you genuinely can't isolate the note at all, give yourself the answer** (play the target note alone first), then go back into the full chord and try to hear it *in there* now that you know what you're listening for — repeat this back-and-forth (play target → try in chord → play target again) until it starts popping out.
  7. **Middle notes in a chord are the hardest** — top and bottom notes are comparatively easy to isolate; middle voices get buried. Practice hunting for middle notes specifically once outer notes are solid.
  8. **A note "not being heard" doesn't mean it's inaudible — it means attention hasn't been trained to grab onto it.** Jared's own description of the shift: one note ("the loudest," in his G-chord example, the open G string) dominates attention by default; learning to consciously push past the dominant note and isolate a *different*, targeted one is the actual skill — he describes the moment it clicks as feeling like "a whole other dimension," a genuinely different subjective experience of the same chord.
  9. **Extend past single notes once comfortable**: try isolating notes low-to-high, high-to-low, or any specific note anywhere in the voicing; apply the same test to a real song you're playing — pause and ask "can I hear the individual notes in this chord?"
  10. **No fixed practice-time requirement** — Jared frames this as something to run as a quick gut-check "every so often" while playing, not necessarily a scheduled drill (contrast Exercise 2 below, which he does recommend running as deliberate, scheduled practice).
- **The motivating anecdote — why this matters beyond ear-training-for-its-own-sake:** Jared discovered (via arpeggiating a barre chord he was playing, not by ear in the moment) that a chord tone he assumed was present in his own D7-shaped barre chord voicing simply wasn't sounding — he hadn't noticed by ear at all, only by accident. Framed as a common, unnoticed failure mode: guitarists often play a barre-chord shape assuming every note is doing its job, when the chord's actual defining/color tone (the one that gives it its specific quality) isn't even sounding — worth deliberately checking barre-chord voicings this way, not just trusting the shape.
- **Exercise 2 — The structured interval ladder (methodical, recommended as scheduled practice, ~5 min/session):**
  1. Work through **every interval type in ascending order**: half-step (minor 2nd) → whole step (major 2nd) → minor 3rd → major 3rd → perfect 4th → (implicitly continuing up through the full interval set, same order as [[Interval Ladder]]).
  2. For each interval: **first drill the physical shape** until comfortable (e.g. half-step = 5 frets total on one string-pair, one fret smaller when crossing the B string — the same B-string-breaks-the-pattern fact documented on [[Interval Ladder]]), **then play it in random neck positions and try to separate + sing both notes** (low one, then high one), then **check each by playing it alone**.
  3. **Octave-transposition tip for singing high positions**: if the two notes are out of your vocal range, sing the *pitch class* down an octave rather than skipping the exercise — same note name, comfortable register, still counts.
  4. **Milestone/gate: 10 correct in a row before advancing to the next interval type.** A miss resets the count to zero — Jared is explicit this is a real bar, not a vague "practice until it feels done." This is the same milestone-gate philosophy documented generally on [[The Practice Priority Pyramid]] (Jared's own framework, from a later video), applied here specifically to ear training years before that framework video was made.
  5. **Explicitly framed as slow, months-long progress** — compared directly to physical fitness ("getting your ears in shape... it takes months"), not a quick fix; 5 minutes a day or per session is enough if it's consistent.
  6. **Two side benefits named**: (a) the physical drilling doubles as fretboard/interval-shape practice (same shapes as [[Interval Ladder]], just with an ear-training layer added on top rather than shape-memorization alone); (b) real-world listening starts to feel "more three-dimensional" as individual notes inside recorded music start popping out, even before formal interval-naming knowledge is layered on top.
  7. **Extending past two notes**: once the full two-note interval ladder is solid, apply the identical isolate-and-sing method to **three-note structures** — any 7th-chord shape, or a plain 1-3-5 triad — broken apart note by note; Jared flags this as a likely separate future video (not covered in depth here).
