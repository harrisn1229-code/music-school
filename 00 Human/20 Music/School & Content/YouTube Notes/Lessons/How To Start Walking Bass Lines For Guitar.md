# How To Start Walking Bass Lines For Guitar

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=r2X0Cvf7IdI)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=r2X0Cvf7IdI)

## Synopsis

An 11-minute guest-lesson swap: this video is hosted on **Nathan Borton's** channel but taught by **Jared Borkowski** of Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared (Borton returns the favor with a guest lesson on Jared's channel, referenced but not captured here). It's a tightly scoped, step-by-step build of a solo-guitar walking-bassline-while-comping technique, aimed specifically at the case where chords change **every two beats** — a harmonic rhythm common enough (turnarounds, jazz blues, rhythm-changes-style movement) that Jared frames this two-beat case as the natural first phase of a larger series. The method is deliberately incremental: start with plain shell-voicing dominant 7ths moved through the circle of fourths, layer in tritone substitution (first as a full chord, then reduced to just its bass note — which is the moment the line starts to sound like an actual walking bass), then add rhythmic variety via three named "chord punches" (sustain, short up punch, double punch), then add variety to *which* note connects one chord's bass to the next (half step below, whole step above, a two-note chromatic run down from a whole step above). The throughline mirrors a practice-sequencing idea already documented elsewhere in this vault: drill each new variable in isolation first ("very exercise-y," "one-dimensional... a great way to practice"), then mix by ear once each piece is solid. Jared explicitly scopes what he *didn't* cover — non-dominant chord qualities (ii-V half-diminished-to-altered), a full I-vi-ii-V progression, one-chord vamps, and one-chord-per-measure harmonic rhythm — as material for a follow-up video, and closes with a direct real-world application: drop this walking-bassline treatment into a jazz-blues turnaround (worked over E♭7–B♭7–G7–Cm–F7) and return to normal comping afterward.

## What's taught

- **Step 1 — shell-voicing dominant 7ths through the circle of fourths, as the harmonic skeleton before any rhythm/bassline work.** Root-3rd-♭7 shell voicing (see [[Chord Voicings]] §*Which notes actually matter*) on G7, then the same shape moved down a fourth each time: C7, F7, B♭7, E♭7, A♭7, D♭7 — alternating between two shell-voicing shapes (one skips the A string) as the roots cycle. *(General starting point: loop the full circle of fourths on shell voicings alone before adding anything below.)*
- **Step 2 — add the tritone substitution of each dominant 7th as a full second chord, landing a half step above the target.** G7 → its tritone sub (D♭7, a half step above the *next* target C7) → C7 → its tritone sub (a half step above F7) → F7, and so on around the circle. Framed simply as "add a half step above each one" rather than by interval-math — same underlying mechanism as [[Chord Substitution]] §*2. Tritone substitution*, but used here as a **rhythmic/motion device** rather than a harmonic reharmonization choice.
- **Step 3 — strip the tritone-sub chord down to just its bass note (root only), which is the moment this becomes a walking bassline rather than a chord substitution exercise.** Same half-step-above-the-target note, but played alone instead of as a full chord: root, half-step-above approach note, next root, half-step-above approach note... "already sounds like a walking bass line" even before any of the rhythm variety below is added. → [[Chord Substitution]], [[Chromatic Bass Lines]]
- **Step 4 — the three "chord punches" (Jared's own term), rhythmic placement options for *when* the chord itself sounds against the moving bass note:**
  - **Sustain** — the chord lands on the downbeat where the harmony changes and is held as long as possible before the next change cuts it off; slight overlap with the bass note moving underneath is fine and "makes it sound like two separate parts." This is the plain version already built in steps 1-3.
  - **Short up punch** — instead of the sustained downbeat chord, the chord is struck **very short and staccato** on the **"and" of beat 1** (or the "and" of beat 3, wherever the next chord change is), i.e. displaced *off* the downbeat and cut immediately. Named as the ingredient that "starts to really bring this to life."
  - **Double punch** — combine the two: strike the chord **on the beat the harmony changes** *and* add the short staccato up-punch on the following "and," within the same bar.
  - **Sequencing:** drill all three in isolation first (deliberately "one-dimensional... a great way to practice"), *then* mix the three by ear/intuition through the same progression rather than committing to one — the same "lock in the plain version, then vary by ear" order already documented on [[Jazz Comping — Rhythmic Motif and Walking Bassline]]. Explicitly named as the first exercise in this whole method that asks for improvisatory judgment rather than a fixed instruction.
- **Step 5 — connecting-note options for *how* the bass approaches the next chord's root, layered on top of the chord-punch rhythm choices:**
  - **Half step above** (the tritone-sub-derived approach from steps 2-3) — the default built up through the lesson.
  - **Half step below** — stated to work "pretty much always... on any chord," with "the same effect" as the half-step-above version; drill the whole progression with every approach note coming from below instead of above.
  - **Combine directions across the progression** (some approaches from above, some from below) — described as where the line "really starts to be expressive and cool and kind of in the moment," once both directions are separately solid.
  - **Whole step above** — "a little more awkward physically" and has less pull/momentum into the target than the half-step version, but worth having in the vocabulary for variety, specifically because relying only on half-step approaches gets stale.
  - **Whole step above, connected by two eighth notes** — whole step above the target, then down a half step, then to the chord: a three-note descending run (whole-step-above → half-step-above → root) instead of a single approach note, giving a denser eighth-note bass line for that beat.
  - **Whole step below / chromatic approach from a whole step below** — flagged as working especially well when the *target* chord is minor (as opposed to the dominant 7ths used throughout the worked examples).
  - **Sequencing again:** work through each approach-note option in isolation, then mix and match connecting-note choices *together with* the chord-punch choices from step 4 for the fullest version of the technique.
- **Explicitly scoped-out extensions, named as follow-up material rather than covered here — useful as a worklist for a later video/practice session:** applying the same method to **non-dominant chord qualities** (a minor 2-5 — half-diminished ii to an altered V7 — instead of straight dominant 7ths); working it over a **full diatonic progression** like a I-vi-ii-V (given as C major – A minor 7 – D minor 7 – G, "that's when it's gonna start feeling kind of like you're playing a song," since I-vi-ii-V shows up in "tons of songs"); a **vamp on a single chord for four bars**; and **harmonic rhythm of one chord per measure** (rather than the two-beats-per-chord case this whole lesson is built around) — each named as needing "different approaches and exercises."
- **Real-world application: drop the technique into a jazz-blues turnaround, then return to normal comping.** Because the whole method targets a two-beats-per-chord harmonic rhythm, the natural place to deploy it is wherever a tune's harmonic rhythm briefly speeds up to that rate — a turnaround being the clearest, most common case. Worked over a full turnaround progression, **E♭7 – B♭7 – G7 – Cm – F7**: "when that turnaround happens... if you throw those walking bass lines in it'll sound awesome," then drop back out of the technique into whatever other comping approach was running before it. Framed as creating "a little momentum, variety" at exactly the moment in a tune where it's most useful, rather than as a technique to run continuously. → [[Turnarounds]]
