# 4 Songs you didn't notice change key

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FTEDBIs8rAA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FTEDBIs8rAA)

## Synopsis

Subtle verse-to-chorus modulations that don't register consciously — with the single clearest reusable principle in Bennett's whole modulation coverage: shared-note count between two keys directly predicts how obvious or smooth a key change will sound.

## Techniques

- **The core quantitative rule: shared-note count predicts modulation smoothness.** Two keys sharing most of their notes → smooth, easy-to-miss key change. Two keys sharing few notes → stark, obvious shift, regardless of how close the tonics are on the keyboard. Worked contrast pair: "Message in a Bottle" (The Police) moves C minor → A major, sharing 6 of 7 notes — one of the smoothest key changes possible. "Love on Top" (Beyoncé) moves C major → D♭ major, only a semitone apart on the keyboard, but sharing only 2 of 7 notes (C major has no sharps/flats; D♭ major has five flats) — a stark, obvious jump despite the tiny physical distance between the tonics.
- **Non-relative-key choice for a deliberate "lift"**: "Zephyr Song" (Red Hot Chili Peppers) verse in A minor, chorus in D major — the "obvious" choice would have been the relative major, C major, which would keep the song in one key throughout. Choosing D major instead (a genuine modulation) gives the chorus a noticeable boost/lift that staying in C wouldn't have produced — deliberately picking a non-relative key change specifically for the energy jump.
- **A pivot chord easing a distant key change**: "Africa" (Toto) moves B major (verse) → F♯ minor (chorus) — normally a fairly obvious shift — via an A major chord that doesn't belong diatonically in B major but does belong in F♯ minor, smoothing the transition.
- **Modulation as a range-management tool, not just a color device**: without the "Message in a Bottle" key change, the pre-chorus vocal melody would have to sit in a noticeably lower, lower-energy range — the modulation exists partly to keep the vocal line in a better register, not purely for harmonic interest.
