# Behind the Track｜'Neverender' by Justice & Tame Impala

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=emP9EWcqss4)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=emP9EWcqss4)

## Synopsis

Another **Mix with the Masters** "Behind the Track" entry, and — like the channel's 070 Shake ("Elephant") and Selena Gomez/benny blanco episodes — closer to a documentary/interview piece than a plugin-by-plugin walkthrough: no screen-share, no on-camera fader moves, mostly Justice (Gaspard Augé and Xavier de Rosnay) and Kevin Parker (Tame Impala) talking through "Neverender" from *Hyperdrama*, intercut with outside voices (French-touch scene commentators, other producers) contextualizing Justice's 30-year influence on the genre. What it lacks in screen-share it makes up for in a handful of genuinely specific, quotable production choices: a single "special" synthesizer that ended up as the source of most of the album's sounds, chord-writing driven by exploring where that synth sounds best across the keyboard, a deliberate "ghost note + short impact + reverb tail" layering trick to make one instrument read as a whole band, Melodyne's Resynthesize mode used specifically for its "digital and futuristic" (not corrective) texture on vocals/synths/pianos, live drums recorded on an actual beer bottle in place of a triangle, and a stated aesthetic philosophy — sample/chop/re-pitch specifically because the ear recognizes "not the real thing" and responds to that as exciting, not as a flaw to hide. The rest is collaboration lore (how the Justice/Kevin Parker pairing came together, why the vocals were recorded live and simply in one room at Conway in LA) and editorial framing of the "Justice sound" (distortion, compression, loudness, but still harmonically emotional — prog-rock/70s-French-soundtrack vibes) that stays mostly qualitative rather than settings-level.

## Techniques

- **A single "discovered" synthesizer became the primary sound source for the whole album, and its own timbre dictated which chords got written where on the keyboard.** Gaspard: "we discovered this synthesizer when I was doing my solo record. It had such a special quality that we ended up buying one... it's always the sound that kind of dictates the writing in some ways, depending on the place on the keyboard where it sounds the best, you're going to find different chords. Most of the sounds of Hyperdrama comes from this synthesizer. That's the main synth of Neverender." The synth isn't named on camera, but context (Justice, French touch, the described character) plus context clues elsewhere in the video point to a **PPG Wave** — later in the video the drums are described as "the same PPG, but played, instead of being sampled and re-sequenced," confirming a PPG unit is central to the record's sound. Reusable songwriting lesson: rather than write chords in the abstract and then find a patch for them, explore a single characterful instrument's register/timbre first and let where it "sounds best" on the keyboard suggest the chord voicings. → [[Melody Writing]] — Justice x Kevin Parker (Mix with the Masters), [Behind the Track｜'Neverender' by Justice & Tame Impala](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=emP9EWcqss4)
- **A three-part layering recipe to make ONE synth instrument read as a whole band playing together: a ghost note of the chord just before/after the main hit, one short percussive "impact" of the chord, and a separate reverb-tail layer that comes back in after.** "On the first demo we were trying to make one synth sound like if it was a whole band playing together... every time there's a chord playing you're going to have like a ghost note of the chord just before or after, or you're going to have one impact of the chord that's going to be short, and then you're going to have the reverb coming back... so it gives like a movement of a whole band playing together just with one instrument." Three distinct roles stacked from ONE source rather than three different instruments: (1) a quiet pre/post-echo "ghost" hit for anticipation/decay, (2) a short, punchy transient impact for the actual downbeat, (3) a longer reverb-tail return that reads as a separate "player" filling the space after the chord. → [[Layering and Stacking]], [[Reverb]] (effect), [[5 Inst & Synth/1 Synth Bed Track|Synth Bed Track]] — Justice x Kevin Parker (Mix with the Masters), [Behind the Track｜'Neverender' by Justice & Tame Impala](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=emP9EWcqss4)
- **Melodyne's Resynthesize mode used deliberately for its "digital and futuristic" character, not for its intended pitch-correction job.** "We used Melodyne a lot, so of course it's a tool made to fix vocals tuning and everything. And we used the Resynthesize part of the plugin. And what we love about it is that it gives things like a very digital and futuristic texture. And we wanted things to sound a bit unnatural on this record. Not unnatural, but supernatural." A corrective tool repurposed as a texture/sound-design source — worth trying on vocals, synths, or piano parts specifically for the artifact character Resynthesize introduces, not for cleanup. → [[Melodyne]], [[Pitch and Formant]] (effect) — Justice x Kevin Parker (Mix with the Masters), [Behind the Track｜'Neverender' by Justice & Tame Impala](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=emP9EWcqss4)
- **Named aesthetic philosophy: chop, re-pitch, and stitch together audio in ways that are "impossible to play" — deliberately, because the ear recognizing "not the real thing" is the exciting part, not a flaw to hide.** "You take music or sound... that you record with an instrument, and you start chopping it up and doing things to it that the ear knows is not the real thing... when you take vocals and chop them up, when you take synthesizers or pianos and re-pitch them and stitch them together in a way that is impossible to play — as listeners, we respond to that in a different way. We can hear that things are chopping instantly, because it sounds unnatural — it's exciting. That's the holy grail: finding a sweet spot between something that's real and expressive, and then fucking with it in a way that's kind of alien or robot." A framing principle more than a setting: push chopping/re-pitching far enough that it reads as intentionally artificial rather than trying to hide the seams. → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]], [[Pitch and Formant]] (effect) — Justice x Kevin Parker (Mix with the Masters), [Behind the Track｜'Neverender' by Justice & Tame Impala](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=emP9EWcqss4)
- **Live drummer Vincent Taeger played a part on an actual glass beer bottle instead of the demo's triangle, at his own suggestion.** "Every time you hear live drums on this record, it's Vincent Taeger playing. On the demo, we made this with a triangle. And when we played it to him, we asked him 'can you play the triangle part' — he listened to this part, and he told us: 'no, we're going to make it with a bottle.' So he asked for a beer to be served to him. So we gave him a beer, and once he drank it, he played it — so that's this part." A found-object percussion substitution suggested by the player, not the producers — worth remembering as a general move: when a placeholder demo sound (triangle, shaker, etc.) is close but not quite right, try an adjacent real-world object rather than only auditioning more samples/synth patches. → [[Sound Selection Guide]] — Justice x Kevin Parker (Mix with the Masters), [Behind the Track｜'Neverender' by Justice & Tame Impala](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=emP9EWcqss4)
- **Drums are sampled and re-sequenced rather than played straight through, and that's named as the reason the track feels "moving" and "enveloping" instead of merely traditional.** "Even if we had, like, just the drums more natural, the same PPG, but played, instead of being sampled and re-sequenced, and Kevin singing more natural on top of it, it would be the exact same song. But it wouldn't give the same kind of impression of that thing that's moving, and the sound a bit enveloping you." The chord progression and melody could stay identical — it's specifically the sampling+re-sequencing of the drum part (chopping a played performance into pieces and rearranging it) that supplies the "moving"/enveloping quality a straight live take wouldn't have. → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]], [[Genre Drum Patterns]] — Justice x Kevin Parker (Mix with the Masters), [Behind the Track｜'Neverender' by Justice & Tame Impala](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=emP9EWcqss4)
- **Kevin Parker's vocal for this collaboration was recorded live, simply, in one room, in a single pass — a deliberate contrast to his usual heavily-layered solo Tame Impala vocal treatment.** "The vocals are very simple. We recorded them in Conway in Los Angeles. And as soon as we were all together back in the room, and he was in the couch holding the mic like this, he sounded like an angel — he was singing everything perfectly." Justice frame the whole collaboration around being in the same physical room and "rebounding on each other's musical proposition" rather than trading files remotely. *(No mic model, preamp, or processing chain named — logged qualitatively.)* → [[Tame Impala Vocal Chain]], [[Vocal Mic Technique]] — Justice x Kevin Parker (Mix with the Masters), [Behind the Track｜'Neverender' by Justice & Tame Impala](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=emP9EWcqss4)
- **"Justice sound" characterized by outside commentators as distortion, compression, and loudness that still reads as harmonically emotional — described as a mix of heavy-metal aggression and epic 70s-French-film-soundtrack sweep, built from the same drum kit reused across most songs.** "There is this distorted sound, the compression, the loudness, but at the same time it's very emotional with the Justice chords. So it's a mix of heavy metal vibes and epic sounds that reminds us of some iconic French soundtracks from the 70s." Also: "I don't think there is a Justice recipe in terms of sound, but there is definitely something going with their use of drums — they have the same drum kit on most of the songs." This stays editorial/qualitative — no drive amount, compression ratio, or kit name given — logged on this page only as scene-setting rather than routed to a specific settings-level note. *(general aesthetic characterization, not actionable)*
- **Songwriting starting point: two chord progressions written on the main synth ARE the track — the arrangement and production build outward from harmony first, not a beat or a sample.** "As soon as we wrote the chord and started organizing the music..." — Justice's process for this song began from the two chord progressions on the discovered synth, with everything else (drums, vocal, texture) built to support that harmonic core. Ties to the closing sentiment: "the thing that keeps us spending so much time on the track is the harmonic sensation and the emotion that we still like" — harmony as the thing they protect above all else through the whole build. → [[Melody Writing]]
