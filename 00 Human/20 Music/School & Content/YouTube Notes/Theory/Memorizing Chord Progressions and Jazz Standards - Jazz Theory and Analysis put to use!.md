# Memorizing Chord Progressions and Jazz Standards - Jazz Theory and Analysis put to use!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZJf8f_pZxKg)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZJf8f_pZxKg)

## Synopsis

A Patreon-question video from Jens Larsen answering a direct request: "I tend to memorize the heads far more effectively than the chords — any advice for internalizing charts?" His answer is a memorization *method*, not a practice-drill list — treat a standard's chord chart the way you'd treat a phone number (country code / area code / rest of the digits), grouping raw chord symbols into functional chunks so there are dramatically fewer "things" to hold in memory. He states the case for memorizing at all first (reading costs energy that should go into playing and into watching/interacting with the other musicians on stage), then spends the bulk of the video doing a full worked chunking analysis of Harry Warren's **"There'll Never Be Another You"** (key of E♭, 32-bar ABAC form) live on a whiteboard, using the chunking method to reduce ~20+ individual chord symbols down to about 6–7 memorable functional units. Along the way he repeatedly cross-references the same devices' appearance, in the same structural slot, across other standards ("It Could Happen to You," "But Not for Me," "Embraceable You," "Take the A Train," "Pennies from Heaven," "I Remember You," "You Do Something to Me") — the explicit point being that once you've chunked one standard this way, you've functionally learned a big piece of the next ten. Closes with a "theory is a language, not a dictionary" framing: the goal is to connect the chunks to what you *hear*, not just what you can label, and playing familiar tunes in multiple keys / comping for vocalists is offered as a way to build that transfer. No production content — pure theory/memorization-methodology lesson, and a particularly strong companion to the vault's other Jens Larsen "There'll Never Be Another You" analysis ([[Secondary Dominants - What You Want To Know]]) and its five-tune comparative ABAC breakdown ([[Jens Larsen - Learn Jazz Standards - Get The Most of What You Already Know]]) — this video adds a sixth worked example of the same 32-bar-form slot devices, on a tune not yet analyzed elsewhere in the vault, framed specifically for memorization rather than harmonic labeling.

## What's taught

- **Why memorize a standard at all.** Reading costs attention that would otherwise go to playing; more importantly, reading means you can't look at the other musicians on the stand — internalizing the tune frees you to actually interact/react with the band in real time, not just execute your own part *(~0:00–1:30)*.
- **The core method: chunk chords into functional groups instead of memorizing them as a flat row of symbols — the phone-number analogy.** A phone number isn't memorized digit by digit; it's memorized as country code + area code + the rest, because that structure means there's genuinely less to hold in memory. Apply the same idea to a chord chart: identify the **form** (how many bars, how many sections, where they repeat), then within each section identify **cadences and small functional units** (a ii–V, a turnaround, a IV-minor move) and memorize those as single "things," not as separate chords. Once you know the form, some material only needs to be memorized once because it repeats *(~1:30–3:15)*.
- **Full worked chunking analysis of "There'll Never Be Another You" (Harry Warren, key E♭ major, 32-bar ABAC form: A 8 bars, B 8 bars, A repeats 8 bars, C 8 bars).** Because it's ABAC, the first A only needs to be memorized once — B and C additionally share their first three bars, so even the "different" second half of the form is mostly the same shape twice with a different ending *(~3:15–4:30)*.
- **A section, chunked into 3 units instead of 6 individual chords:** (1) the tonic, Eflatmaj7 for two bars; (2) **a secondary ii–V to the 6th degree** — Dø7–G7 → Cm — thought of and named as one unit, "a 2-5 to six," not two separate chords → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Secondary Dominants]]; (3) **a secondary ii–V to the 4th degree** — B♭m7–E♭7 → A♭ — same chunking logic, "a 2-5 to four." Flagged as a very common device even though B♭m7/E♭7 aren't diatonic to E♭ — cadences to the IV are common in this form, cross-referenced against "It Could Happen to You" and "But Not for Me" using the identical move → [[32-Bar Song Form]] *(~4:30–7:00)*.
- **B section, chunked into 4 units.** Opens on the **IV chord (A♭)** — flagged as a very common slot-device for ABAC-type 32-bar forms specifically, cross-referenced against the same slot in "It Could Happen to You" and "But Not for Me." Then: (2) **IV → IV-minor → I** — the D♭7 here is read as "some sort of four minor chord... really just an A♭ minor 6 with D♭ in the bass," i.e. a backdoor-dominant-flavored realization of the same borrowed-subdominant move, resolving back to I → [[32-Bar Song Form]], [[Borrowed Chords]]; (3) the **first half of an ABAC form never has a real ending** — it has to keep the song moving rather than resolve home, because the second half needs somewhere to launch from; (4) **"the dominant of the dominant" (V7/V, "5 of 5") sitting for two bars**, sometimes preceded by its own ii, then a plain **ii** chord that opens back into the top of the next A section rather than cadencing to I → [[Secondary Dominants]] *(~7:00–11:30)*.
- **C section, first three bars identical to B (IV, IV-minor→I), because B and C share their opening material — one of the reasons the ABAC form is efficient to memorize.** Then: **Dø7 (half-diminished) cadencing to G minor** — identified as historically **a ♯4-diminished chord that got reharmonized into a minor ii–V once bebop players started turning ♯4°-type chords into ii–V's instead of leaving them as bare diminished chords**, the same underlying chord/relationship as the "Stella By Starlight" ♯4-diminished device documented elsewhere in the vault. Larsen specifically credits **"Embraceable You"** as (possibly) one of the *first* standards where bebop players made this diminished→ii–V substitution — turning the raw G♭ diminished into a half-diminished→E7-type dominant resolution — flagged with an explicit "I could be wrong, correct me if not" caveat → [[Diminished Chords]] *(~11:30–14:00)*.
- **The song's closing stretch, reduced from a wall of chord symbols to two large functional chunks plus a small turnaround tag.** The final B♭7 before the repeat is flagged as "just there to go back to the beginning" — memorize it as a turnaround tag, don't treat it as part of the main analysis. The actual ending: **two bars of tonic (I)**, needing to reach a ii–V cadence, filled in with a **tritone-dominant "3" chord (V7/iii, an auxiliary dominant to the ii)** — described as basically **two bars of E♭ major decorated with extra color chords**, since on the strong beats the harmony is really just **I (E♭) then ii (Gm, itself tonic-function in E♭)**, with the A♭7 and C7 in between functioning as passing embellishments rather than structural harmony → [[Chord Progressions]], [[The ii-V-I]] *(~14:00–18:30)*.
- **The full chunk map, restated as four bracket-groups spanning the last third of the form** (the practical payoff of the whole method): (1) a 4-bar chunk = **cadence to C minor**; (2) a 4-bar chunk = **2-5-1 to the 6th degree**; (3) a 4-bar chunk = **"4-minor back to 1," reusable across songs in this form**; (4) a 4-bar chunk = **the "5 of 5 to 2-5" progression**, called out as *extremely* common at this exact position in ABAC-type forms — and independently, at the **end of the bridge** in AABA forms too, cross-referenced against Duke Ellington's bridges generally (his example: "Take the A Train," while flagging that tune isn't strictly an Ellington-composed bridge) and "Pennies from Heaven" *(~18:30–20:30)*.
- **The 2-bar "…to the 2nd degree" turnaround-flavored progression (E♭–A♭7–Gm–C7) generalized across other standards, with named variant substitutions:** "Pennies from Heaven" uses a **G♭ diminished** in place of the plain C7 in the same slot (same function, different color); the same shape shows up in Hank Mobley's playing essentially as "I, then a diminished chord serving as a dominant into the two chord"; "It Could Happen to You" uses a close variant of the identical progression → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Chord Progressions]] *(~20:30–21:30)*.
- **The ♯4-diminished-to-minor-ii–V device (from the C section, above) independently corroborated in two more standards, same structural placement (resolving back to I):** **"I Remember You"** (a different form, ABA, but the device sits in the same functional spot) and **"You Do Something to Me"** (Cole Porter) — both "harmonized in both places with a minor 2-5," reinforcing the chunk as a genuinely transferable, position-predictable unit rather than a one-off in this tune → [[Diminished Chords]] *(~21:30–22:15)*.
- **Closing framing: theory terms are a language, not a dictionary to memorize.** Explicitly declines to make a video cataloging every possible small progression in isolation — the payoff comes from hearing a chunk in context inside real tunes, the same reason nobody learns a language by memorizing a dictionary. Recommends **playing already-known standards in different keys** and **comping for vocalists** as concrete ways to build the "hear it coming" fluency the chunking method depends on, rather than relying on chunking as a purely intellectual/visual memorization trick *(~22:15–24:52)*.
