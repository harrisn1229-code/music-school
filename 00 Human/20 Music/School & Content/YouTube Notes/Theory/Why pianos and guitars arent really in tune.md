# Why pianos and guitars aren't really in tune (just intonation vs 12TET)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7JhVcGtT8z4)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7JhVcGtT8z4)

## Synopsis

A deep dive into why fixed-tuning instruments (piano, guitar) can never be perfectly in tune, why that's mathematically unavoidable rather than a tuning skill problem, how instruments without fixed pitch (voice, unfretted strings) work around it in real time, and concrete production examples of musicians deliberately exploiting just intonation in the studio.

## Techniques

- **Why a fixed-tuning instrument can't be perfectly in tune, mechanically:** just intonation requires every interval to hit a pure small-integer frequency ratio relative to its OWN root (perfect 5th = 3:2, major 3rd = 5:4, derived from the harmonic/overtone series — doubling the fundamental gives the octave, tripling gives the 5th, and so on). Tune a piano's intervals purely relative to A, and A–C♯ and A–E are perfect — but B–F♯ (a fifth not built directly off A) is now measurably off, because the pure ratios don't compose consistently across the whole keyboard. There is no single fixed tuning that makes every interval simultaneously pure; a fixed instrument can only approximate.
- **12-TET is the standard compromise: only the octave is kept pure (2:1), and the rest of the octave is divided into 12 mathematically equal steps** — every key becomes equally (im)pure instead of some keys being pure and others badly out. Concrete cents data from this video: 4ths/5ths are only ~2 cents sharp/flat of pure (inaudible); the major 3rd is 14 cents sharp of pure (audible, and the interval most listeners would flag if made aware of it) — yet the tritone, further from pure than the 3rd, isn't noticed as "wrong" because it's expected to sound tense/unstable anyway. General principle: how noticeable an out-of-tune interval is depends on how consonant the ear expects it to be, not just the raw cents deviation.
- **Ears normalize to whatever tuning system they're raised on** — a lifetime of 12-TET exposure makes justly-tuned intervals sound "uncanny"/unfamiliar on first hearing, even though they're the mathematically purer version of the same interval.
- **Non-fixed-pitch instruments (violin, cello, voice) retune each note in real time based on harmonic context, rather than committing to one fixed pitch per note name** — a violinist's B♭ is pitched differently depending on what it's stacked against in the moment (e.g. lowering it slightly the second time it appears in a scale passage, even though the same left-hand finger position produced a "correct" B♭ the first time against different harmony). This is presented as one of the genuine technical challenges of fretless/unfixed instruments, made harder still when playing alongside a fixed-tuned instrument like piano, since the two players' tuning philosophies are structurally incompatible in real time.
- **"Beating" as the audible mechanism and tuning tool**: two close-but-not-identical frequencies sounding together produce an audible pulsing/roughness at a rate equal to their frequency difference (400 Hz + 402 Hz beats at 2 Hz; narrowing to 401 Hz halves the beat rate; matching exactly at 400 Hz eliminates it). Practical use, demonstrated via a John Williams scoring-session clip: a conductor/producer can literally instruct a choir or ensemble to "listen for the beating" and adjust pitch until it disappears, as an audible, real-time tuning target more precise than "sounds about right." Jacob Collier is cited making the same real-time just-intonation adjustment when stacking vocal harmonies, on the stated belief that justly-tuned harmony is more resonant/"inspires more endorphins" than tempered harmony.
- **A concrete studio example of deliberately retuning a fixed instrument for one passage: John Frusciante (Red Hot Chili Peppers) tuned his guitar's A string 14 cents flat for the "Scar Tissue" intro**, specifically to cancel out 12-TET's ~14-cent-sharp major 3rd/10th and produce a genuinely justly-tuned major tenth on the opening interval — only possible because it's a studio recording (he retuned back to standard before the rest of the song); a standard-tuned guitar played along with that intro audibly clashes against the record for exactly this reason. A directly reusable production move: for a single sustained interval that needs to ring especially pure (an intro, a pad, a drone), consider a deliberate few-cents detune on just the note(s) involved rather than accepting the tempered default — cannot be done live on a fixed-fret/fixed-key instrument mid-song, but is free in a DAW/recording context.
- **Software-adjustable tuning exists for fixed/MIDI instruments (mentioned: Entonal Studio, a retuning plugin)** — makes it possible to set a keyboard to just intonation relative to a chosen root, or build custom microtonal tuning tables, and apply that to third-party VST instruments; the video's own experiment (retuning the piano's tuning table per chord change to keep each chord justly tuned) made major chords sound "really pure and clean" but produced awkward results on chromatic melody notes and at the exact moment of a tuning-table switch — a real limitation worth knowing about before trying the same trick, not just a solved problem.
