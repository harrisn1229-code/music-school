# BIGGEST Music Theory MYTH That Will RUIN Your Understanding

## Synopsis
Guitar instructor Daniel Seriff debunks a myth he says he hears constantly from intermediate guitarists: that B♯, E♯, C♭, and F♭ "don't exist" or are just simplified-away duplicates of C, F, B, and E. His counter-argument is structural rather than just definitional — every natural note can be sharped or flatted (that's *all* sharp/flat means: raise or lower by a half-step), so if every other pitch already has two valid names (enharmonic equivalents), there's no principled reason these four wouldn't too. The video's real payload is a set of **four rules for spelling a valid major scale/key** that make the "weird" accidentals not just permissible but *required*: (1) never mix sharps and flats in one key, (2) use all seven letters A–G exactly once, (3) never use two different versions of the same letter in one key, (4) each scale degree must be the next letter of the alphabet. He walks these rules through four worked examples on guitar (low E string, A string) — F♯ major (needs E♯ for its 7th degree), G♭ major (needs C♭ for its 3rd), C♯ major (all seven degrees sharped — a capo'd guitarist lands here from open C-major shapes at the 1st fret), and C♭ major (all seven degrees flatted, enharmonically identical to B major but preferred by musicians who think in flats, e.g. horn players with transposing instruments). The closing frame is the "15 logical major scales" — the complete set of keys once C♯ and C♭ are counted alongside the 13 more familiar ones — and the claim that skipping the four rules is what actually makes triads and 7th chords stop making sense, not the other way around. Ends with an ebook plug (discount code "they lied") that isn't itself instructional content.

## What's taught
- **Enharmonic equivalents exist because sharp/flat are pure half-step operations, not fixed note-name pairs** — ♯ = raise any natural by one half-step (one fret toward the body on guitar), ♭ = lower any natural by one half-step (one fret toward the headstock); since that operation applies uniformly to all seven natural letters, every natural has both a sharped and flatted neighbor-name, including B (→ B♯), E (→ E♯), C (→ C♭), and F (→ F♭) — these aren't exceptions, the "no duplicate names for these four" claim is the actual myth. (~0:00–2:00) → [[Notes & Pitches]]
- **Rule 1 — no mixing sharps and flats in the same major scale/key.** A key is either all-sharp or all-flat, never both. (~2:15)
- **Rule 2 — all seven letters A–G must be present, one of each.** You can't have two Cs and no D, for instance. (~2:20)
- **Rule 3 — never use two different accidental versions of the same letter within one key** — e.g. no G *and* G♭ together, no A *and* A♯ together; doing so "confuses the system." (~2:30)
- **Rule 4 — the next scale degree is always the next letter of the alphabet**, wrapping G back to A — never restate the same letter name for consecutive degrees even when a different spelling would look simpler. (~2:40) → [[Scales]] (new "Spelling rules" section, all four rules written up together)
- **Worked example: F♯ major on the low E string, 2nd fret** — F♯ G♯ A♯ B C♯ D♯ [E♯] F♯ using the W-W-H-W-W-W-H formula; the 7th degree is enharmonically the same pitch as F, but rule 2 already used F for nothing (F♯ is the tonic) and rule 4 demands the letter after D♯ is E, so it must be spelled **E♯**. Compared directly to F major (7th degree = plain E, a half-step below the F tonic) to show the parallel relationship. (~4:00–5:30)
- **Worked example: G♭ major** — G♭ A♭ B♭ [C♭] D♭ E♭ F G♭; the 3rd degree is enharmonically B, but the alphabet already needs a "C" at that position (after B♭) so it's spelled **C♭**, with the explicit reminder that B and C♭ are literally the same pitch under two valid names. Noted that F♯ major and G♭ major are the same key sonically — worth being fluent in both spellings since different collaborators default to different ones. (~5:45–7:00)
- **Practical trigger for landing in C♯ major: a capo.** A guitarist playing open C-major shapes with a capo on the 1st fret is sounding a half-step higher — i.e., playing C♯ major — even though their hands are still forming C-major shapes. (~7:10–7:30)
- **Trick for spelling any all-sharp/all-flat key fast: compare it to the C major scale letter-for-letter and apply the same accidental to every degree.** C major = C D E F G A B (zero accidentals) → C♯ major = C♯ D♯ E♯ F♯ G♯ A♯ B♯ (sharp every degree) → C♭ major = C♭ D♭ E♭ F♭ G♭ A♭ B♭ (flat every degree). Same worked on guitar: C♯ major starting at the 4th fret of the A string, W-W-H-W-W-W-H = C♯ D♯ E♯(needs to be called E, not F♯, because it's the 3rd — the transcript briefly muddles this step but the letter-for-letter C-major-plus-sharp trick resolves it cleanly) F♯ G♯ A♯ B♯ back to C♯. (~7:40–9:30) → [[Scales]] (C♯/C♭ major rows added to the major-scale table)
- **When to prefer C♭ major over its enharmonic twin B major: collaborator-dependent.** Horn players (transposing instruments) tend to think in flats and find flat-heavy keys easier to read/communicate in; violinists/fiddle players tend to default to sharps. A working guitarist should be comfortable thinking through a key either way rather than picking one spelling as "the real one." (~9:40–10:20)
- **The complete key count: 15 "logical" major scales**, not the more commonly cited 12 — the extra three come from enharmonic pairs that both get their own full spelling under the four rules: F♯/G♭, C♯/D♭, and C♭/B. Framed as the payoff for following the rules rather than shortcutting to "12 keys, pick whichever spelling is easiest." (~10:30–11:00) → [[Key Signatures]] (7-sharp C♯ major and 7-flat C♭ major rows added to the key-signature table, which previously stopped at 6 sharps/6 flats)
- **Why this matters beyond scale-spelling: chord/arpeggio construction breaks without correct enharmonic spelling.** Stated directly — if a player doesn't accept B♯/E♯/C♭/F♭ as real notes, triads and 7th chords built in keys like F♯, G♭, C♯, or C♭ major "won't make any sense," because chord tones are pulled from the properly-spelled scale degrees, not from whichever enharmonic name happens to be more familiar. General principle, not a specific chord voicing — no concrete chord-tone example given in this video. (~10:20–10:30, ~10:45–11:00)
