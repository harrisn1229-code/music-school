# Sampling and Sound Selection

How Disclosure actually build tracks: not from scratch synths, but by **auditioning and re-pitching a pile of samples until a vibe assembles** — and choosing sounds for the *feeling* they evoke, not the literal thing they are. This note is the *method*; for specific "good sound for X" picks by role (drum hits, bass, guitar, synths) see the **[[Sound Selection Guide]]**. Companions: [[Sound Palette]], [[TEXTURE Slot]], [[Ambient Texture and Field Recording]], [[RC-20 Retro Color]].

## From videos (recent additions)
- **Why an isolated drum break is the most flexible kind of sample to build on, plus why an a cappella vocal is the most flexible kind of MELODIC sample.** A pure drum beat carries no key or tonality — no chords, no melody — so sampling one doesn't constrain the new track's harmony at all, which is exactly why the Amen break (The Winstons' "Amen, Brother") and the Funky Drummer break (James Brown) are likely the two most-sampled recordings in music history, reused across wildly different genres/keys. Same logic on the melodic side: Fatboy Slim's "Praise You" superimposes an a cappella vocal hook (Camille Yarborough's "Take Yo' Praise") over an unrelated 2-bar piano vamp sampled from, of all things, a 1973 JBL audio-equipment test recording — the vocal being unaccompanied in its original form is precisely what let it be freely re-harmonized under a totally different backing. A single source recording can also supply two DIFFERENT artists two DIFFERENT kinds of sample without conflict: the Isley Brothers' "Footsteps in the Dark" was sampled melodically/harmonically by Ice Cube ("It Was a Good Day"), then 25 years later sampled by Thundercat ("Them Changes") for its drum groove only. → [[Sample Clearance]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[18 Songs That Sample Other Songs]]
- **A 1967 precedent for sampling itself: build a wanted-but-unbuildable sound out of chopped-up existing recordings rather than trying to record it fresh.** George Martin, tasked by John Lennon with creating a circus/fairground atmosphere for "Being for the Benefit of Mr. Kite!," originally wanted a real steam/circus organ recorded live — but programming one authentically would have taken roughly three years, so he instead cut up pre-existing recordings of steam organs and circus sounds and collaged the fragments into the instrumental break. In his own words, decades before digital samplers existed: "my hurdy-gurdy sounds were in fact the forerunner of today's samplers, because all I was doing was taking existing recorded sounds and chopping them up and using them for my own purpose." The generalizable move: when the "real" source is impractical to record or build, found/existing recordings cut and reassembled can substitute — sampling as a workaround for an unbuildable sound, not just a genre aesthetic. → [[27 Beatles songs that George Martin played on]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [27 Beatles songs that George Martin played on](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YDYedISQ2JU)
- **Vary the PROCESSING of a part — not just write a new part — as its own dynamics tool at a structural turn, and use a live-sampling/glitch pedal to freeze a single word inside a lead vocal specifically at the point a section turns.** Atticus Ross: "much like with the sax, if you're [varying] the process as well as the part, you can create dynamics within the piece" — stated directly about a moment building into a track's breakdown, where a rare sampling pedal (called a "teaspoon," transcript-uncertain name) is used to get "one of the words stuck" inside Trent Reznor's vocal, "going into the end section," and separately on a sax line leading into the same moment. The reusable idea: reserve a distinct processing treatment for the specific structural turn rather than running it throughout — the freeze/glitch itself is functioning as an arrangement event, not a texture. Owned-lane equivalent for the freeze: [[Portal]], automated on specifically at the transition. → [[Portal]], [[Automation]], [[Vintage Gear Worklist]] — Mix with the Masters (Atticus Ross), [[Atticus Ross' Production Process]]
- **Keep essentially everything warped as a standing default, not a special-case editing move — "I've been lately keeping everything in Elastic Time."** Emile Haynie applies Pro Tools' Elastic Time/Elastic Audio across synths, samples, and drum loops alike, treating pitch/time flexibility as always-on infrastructure rather than something switched on only when a specific edit calls for it. Owned-lane equivalent: leave Ableton clip **Warp** (Complex/Complex Pro) on by default for melodic/drum material, not just when a stretch is imminently needed — it's the enabling layer under every other elastic-audio move logged on this page and this video. → [[Layering and Stacking]], [[Rhythm and Groove]] — Emile Haynie (Mix with the Masters), [[Arrangement & Processing with Emile Haynie]]
- **The underlying philosophy tying a whole breakdown together: get a genuinely different sound out of material you ALREADY have, rather than always sourcing something new.** Stated across both a melodic pitched-layering pass and a drum-groove pass in the same video — the same synth take recorded once, or the same drum loop already sitting in the session, becomes the raw material for a very different result purely through elastic-audio pitch/time manipulation plus "a lot of simple plugins." Close cousin of this page's existing "process it until unrecognizable" philosophy above, this time framed around reuse of session material specifically rather than dressing up a fresh sample. → [[Rhythm and Groove]] — Emile Haynie (Mix with the Masters), [[Arrangement & Processing with Emile Haynie]]
- **A raw sound — self-recorded, a patch played live, or a purchased sample — is only ever a starting place, never the ceiling; the goal is to process it until it reads as "kind of unrecognizable."** Stated as a core production philosophy, not tied to one specific effect: "the starting place for a sound that you find... that's a great starting place, but it doesn't mean that you can't sort of change it into something kind of unrecognizable... when you take something where you're like 'okay, this is what it looks like when you don't dress it up,' and then you dress it up, it becomes — it takes on so much character and totally changes the way that it actually is." Demonstrated on a plain Output Analog Strings patch, dressed up into cinematic "Hans Zimmer type" strings with five stacked effects (sample delay → clip distortion → [[ValhallaRoom]] → a channel EQ → [[Little AlterBoy]]) — see [[Finneas Drum Sampling Chain]]. The companion workflow habit on the same breakdown: audition a sound library (Battery, in his case) by ear and stop when something "feels really interesting" rather than sound-designing from a blank patch — the transformation happens AFTER selection, not instead of it. → [[ValhallaRoom]], [[Little AlterBoy]], [[Plugin Wishlist]], [[Sound Selection Guide]], [[Finneas Drum Sampling Chain]] — Finneas (Mix with the Masters), [[Creative drum sampling with Finneas]]
- **A composer's own custom sample library, built from real session musicians directly, pitched to them as a trade that would get THEM more work — not as a way to phase them out.** Hans Zimmer, mid-90s London: "if you let me sample you, I will make it so that directors will want more orchestral music, and you will actually get more work out of it" — the better his sampled mock-ups sounded, the more directors got convinced to commission real orchestras, at a moment when orchestral scoring was "thought of as not cool." He kept building it out because commercial libraries of the time "weren't sufficient to what I was hearing in my head," and now runs a two-track pipeline: Spitfire for library work plus a dedicated sample engineer (Claudius Brausewetter) who does phase-alignment on his personal samples specifically — framed as being as important a creative partner as any player. → [[Career & Release]] (Mindset) — Hans Zimmer, [[Hans Zimmer's use of computers and samples in orchestral music]]
- **Pick individual orchestra players the way a guitarist obsesses over pickups/amps — an orchestra is a collection of distinct-sounding individuals, not an interchangeable section, and the room + players change what you write, not just how it's recorded.** "Each individual player's technique, the sound of the instrument... if you go to London, or Paris, or Berlin, they will have different-sounding instruments... you should treat every member of the orchestra the same way." Zimmer writes differently for LA vs. London vs. Paris vs. Vienna sessions because he's writing *for* the specific players and room who'll perform it: "when I write a cello line, I know who I write it for... I see that person in front of me." Cites Berlin and Iceland currently producing distinctly different orchestral character from America/London, driven by the individual players rather than the technology. A version of "audition by feel, not by name" applied to live sources, not sample libraries. → [[Sound Palette]] — Hans Zimmer, [[Hans Zimmer's use of computers and samples in orchestral music]]
- **A full custom orchestral sample library isn't the entry point — a found object, sampled with imagination, is a legitimate one.** "It's okay, you don't have to go out and sample a huge orchestra... that's going to be really expensive. But if you have a rubber band and a cardboard box, and you can think of a really cool way of using that, and sampling that, and making a score out of that" beats buying a library and never learning it — "it's always the guy who really knows these tools who can make amazing music with it." Every project opens with what he calls a "safari of sampling" — hunting/building custom sounds before writing starts, not defaulting to an existing library first. → [[Ambient Texture and Field Recording]], [[Sound Selection Guide]] — Hans Zimmer, [[Hans Zimmer's use of computers and samples in orchestral music]]
- **Live drums recorded, then sampled and re-sequenced rather than played straight through — named as the specific reason a track feels "moving" and "enveloping" instead of merely traditional.** "Even if we had, like, just the drums more natural, the same PPG, but played, instead of being sampled and re-sequenced, and Kevin singing more natural on top of it, it would be the exact same song. But it wouldn't give the same kind of impression of that thing that's moving, and the sound a bit enveloping you." The chord progression and vocal could stay identical — it's specifically chopping a played drum performance into pieces and rearranging it that supplies the extra "moving" quality a straight live take wouldn't have. Paired with a named aesthetic philosophy on the same page: chop/re-pitch/stitch audio deliberately far enough that the ear recognizes "not the real thing," because that recognition reads as exciting rather than as a flaw. → [[Pitch and Formant]], [[Genre Drum Patterns]] — Justice x Kevin Parker (Mix with the Masters), [Behind the Track｜'Neverender' by Justice & Tame Impala](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=emP9EWcqss4)
- **Dig old records for hours as the primary source of inspiration, treating the dig itself as a study of production history — "how people got sounds in whatever era" — rather than just source-hunting for a usable loop.** Young Guru states the digging makes him "a better musician, engineer, producer" independent of whether any given sample gets used; even when the sample itself never survives to the final track, it's what "creates and kicks off and sparks" the idea for the session. His companion calling-card move: **sample around vocal WORDS specifically**, then manipulate what's pulled (speed it up or slow it down) purely to get into a mood, not necessarily to keep the result — a deliberately loose, exploratory first pass before any real composing starts. Worked example: digging turned up Chairman of the Board's 1972 album *Bittersweet*, where "As Much As I Need" caught his ear and sparked the session. → [[Career & Release]] (tool-fluency/toolbox-paradox mindset from the same video) — Young Guru (Mix with the Masters), [Starting a new production with Young Guru](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QDvQ8x0QyFI)
- **Score a film not by imitating the original artist's sound, but by requesting the ACTUAL master multitracks and building new material out of/around them — treating hundreds of stray, unglamorous session stereo files (band warm-ups, chatter, alternate takes) as a sound library, not just the finished songs.** Rather than write a Beach Boys pastiche ("that would have sucked, because you're not going to be the Beach Boys"), Atticus Ross asked Brian Wilson directly for the complete multitrack archive of every album he'd recorded — Wilson sent a box containing masters for every album, including **68 different versions of "Good Vibrations"** alone, plus hundreds of stereo session files (the Wrecking Crew warming up, players noodling) that became raw material to "reach for or play on top of," not just alternate mixes to choose between. Reusable lesson for any project built around an artist's back-catalogue or a found-stem library: go past the finished masters and mine the session ephemera (warm-ups, false starts, chatter) for texture and character the finished takes don't have. → [[Ambient Texture and Field Recording]], [[Sidechaining]] — Atticus Ross (Mix with the Masters), [Behind the Track｜ 'Love & Mercy' – Brian Wilson Tribute](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jTwSMtPakok)
- **Bounce a multi-take vocal stack to one stem, then deliberately filter it thin — leaving room for a future collaborator, not shaping tone.** After bouncing a 3-take (main/high/low) self-recorded vocal stack (mute the other tracks, arm disc-recording on the master, render), the combined stem gets EQ (cut lows + highs) → reverb → a high-pass sweep filter, reasoned directly on-screen: "if you were having that in a sample normally, it'd be hard for the artist to find room to hop on it." A mixing-for-flippability move worth applying to any layered stack (vocal or otherwise) headed for a sample flip. → [[Little AlterBoy]], [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]], [[Auto Filter]], [[90s R&B Sample Chain]] — loner, [how to make your own 90's rnb samples](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PQjIBDgJehQ)
- **Crossfade de-click at reverse/edit transition points, applied to the whole finished sample as the very last step.** After pitching a fully-bounced sample down and reversing a section for variation, a crossfade-mode de-click setting is used specifically at the transition point between forward and reversed audio "so you don't get the little pops" — owned-lane equivalent: a short fade/crossfade drawn at the clip boundary in Ableton's clip editor rather than a hard cut. → [[Pitch and Formant]], [[90s R&B Sample Chain]] — loner, [how to make your own 90's rnb samples](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PQjIBDgJehQ)
- **Fabricate the "sampled from a record" character from scratch with a deliberate heavy dual-end EQ cut + reverb + tremolo pass — a from-scratch simulation recipe, not a sample flip.** Building a Bryson Tiller-style '90s R&B beat entirely from layered VST instruments (no real sample involved): the finished vocal-chop stem gets a heavy EQ cut of BOTH highs and lows, then reverb, then a tremolo panning it side to side — reasoned directly on-screen, "if you're sampling a '90s R&B record, you're going to have to cut loads of the highs out and loads of the lows out, so I did that to make it sound like I'm actually sampling a song." A sharper, more aggressive counterpart to the already-logged "match the age of the processing to the reference era" restraint lesson below (that entry is about a *2000s* reference needing LESS wear; this one is the '90s-and-earlier version, where heavy dual-end EQ cutting IS the era-matching move). → [[EQ]] (effect), [[Reverb]] (effect), [[Modulation]], [[Bryson Tiller Fake-Sample Chord Bed Chain]] — loner, [how to make smooth rnb beats for bryson tiller from scratch (no samples)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZMB_sqF_Y3Q)
- **Write and arrange a chord/vocal bed at a comfortably slow tempo, THEN Warp-stretch the whole thing up to the final tempo once the arrangement is locked — rather than composing at the target tempo from the start.** Referencing Bryson Tiller's "Always Forever" (noticeably faster than the working project), the full chord bed + vocal were written at **80 BPM**, then both the keys and vocal clips were switched to Stretch/Warp mode and the project tempo bumped to **135 BPM** — stated payoff: "it's easy to get a good bounce with the drums" once the material is already living in the faster, stretched pocket. A reusable move whenever the target tempo makes the writing stage feel rushed. → [[Rhythm and Groove]], [[Bryson Tiller Fake-Sample Chord Bed Chain]] — loner, [how to make smooth rnb beats for bryson tiller from scratch (no samples)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZMB_sqF_Y3Q)
- **Structure a sample built for flipping/selling like an actual song — a hook and a verse — rather than a flat loop, so the eventual flipper's arrangement work is already done for them.** "It's always best to structure it how you would with a beat... it gives the producer who's flipping it, they know how to structure it, it's already done for them, so it just saves them a job." Verse-vs-hook is signaled purely through processing (reverse + pitch the mallet and vocal down an octave for the verse — see [[Pitch and Formant]]), not new material. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]], [[Pitch and Formant]], [[Jetsonmade Bouncy Melodic Sample Chain]] — loner, [how jetsonmade makes bouncy/melodic beats for playboi carti & lil keed (from scratch)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OQZbvASWIQE)
- **Bounce a processed vocal stack to one stem, double-time it (stretch to 2x speed), chop to fit, then reverse just the LAST couple of small chopped pieces for a cheap late-phrase variation.** A fast way to generate a warped, faster vocal texture from a slow original take without re-recording; the localized reverse (only the tail, not the whole chop) is the variation move worth reusing on its own. → [[Little AlterBoy]], [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]], [[Jetsonmade Bouncy Melodic Sample Chain]] — loner, [how jetsonmade makes bouncy/melodic beats for playboi carti & lil keed (from scratch)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OQZbvASWIQE)
- **A general declick/degrade pass reached for specifically after heavy chopping, because "for some reason the audio adds clicks" once a sample's been cut up a lot.** *(low confidence — the specific FL Studio tool name is garbled in auto-captions and not reliably identifiable; the general lesson — heavily chopped audio needs a declick pass before it's usable — is the reusable takeaway. Owned-lane options for the same job: a very short fade-in at each chop boundary, or a light limiter/clip-gain pass.)* → [[Jetsonmade Bouncy Melodic Sample Chain]] — loner, [how jetsonmade makes bouncy/melodic beats for playboi carti & lil keed (from scratch)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OQZbvASWIQE)
- **Extract the bass/808 that's already baked into a finished, bounced sample instead of programming a new one — isolate it from the sample audio, then drag the isolated element across the arrangement as the beat's own bassline.** Skips writing a fresh 808 pattern entirely when the sample's own low end already has the right character (here, a heavy-attack bass that also justified skipping a separate kick — see [[Bass Design]], [[Genre Drum Patterns]]). *(FL Studio's audio-isolation feature specifically — owned-lane equivalent: Melodyne-based note extraction, see [[Plugin Wishlist]], or manual resampling/filtering)* → [[Bass Design]], [[Jetsonmade Bouncy Melodic Sample Chain]] — loner, [how jetsonmade makes bouncy/melodic beats for playboi carti & lil keed (from scratch)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OQZbvASWIQE)
- **Pitch a one-shot DOWN deliberately for texture, and chop a key-matched vocal loop so it only plays in the GAPS between chord hits (not continuously), pitching it back to its original pitch once the key-match audition is done.** From the launch video for loner's own **Layers Volume One** kit (151 one-shots, 50 Analog Lab R&B presets, 50 MIDI progressions, 10 samples, 200+ drum sounds — full contents on [[Plugin Wishlist]]): "what i like to do with my one shots is pitch them down, i find they kind of stretch out and they have a really nice texture the lower you go" — a general one-shot move, not kit-specific. Separately, a vocal loop is screened by ear for the closest match to the sample's key (B minor), then chopped so it fills only the space where the chords don't hit, and pitched back down to its natural pitch after being nudged up to check the key match — keeps the loop from running continuously and colliding with the chord layer. *(general starting point — no exact semitone amounts given)* → [[Layering and Stacking]], [[Bass Design]], [[Plugin Wishlist]] — loner, [my step by step process for making crazy r&b beats from scratch (using the best r&b sound kit ever!)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NPn4Jj8kA04)
- **Reverse a chopped vocal section and pitch the reversed copy down a whole semitone specifically to keep it from clashing in frequency with the still-playing, non-reversed main vocal.** A dedicated self-recorded-vocal walkthrough (write chords with the vocal melody already in mind, pick the key for vocal fit, record 3 differently-toned "ooh"-vocalized layers, chain each one differently — see [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]], [[Little AlterBoy]]): once the finished stack is consolidated to one track, a liked section is chopped out, made a unique sample, reversed, and its pitch dropped a semitone — the pitch-down is functional, not decorative, so the new reversed texture layer doesn't fight the main vocal's fundamental when both play together. Companion fix logged the same video: when a double-timed-then-reversed chop played "a little bit off," rather than backing off the stretch, **pitch it up an octave first, then apply half-time** — the half-time stretches the already-pitched material back into a usable groove instead of sounding rushed. Closing move: combine the reverse-chop with a super-heavy ping-pong delay (16th notes, feedback ~93%, drive up, delay's own modulation pulled down — see [[Delay]]) looped across the section, then pitch the WHOLE finished vocal stack down on the master as the literal last step ("things sound better pitched down"). Also notable: wordless "ooh" vocalizing instead of lyrics, stated plainly as a workaround for not being a strong singer — the self-performed complement to this page's existing "ask a singer for oohs/ahs" sourcing tip below. → [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]], [[Vocal Comping and Takes]], [[Vocal Mic Technique]], [[Little AlterBoy]], [[Delay]], [[Pitch and Formant]], [[Melody Writing]] — loner, [how to add your own vocals to your r&b samples (in depth tutorial)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NP27-3YFlDk)
- **Match the AGE of the processing to the age of the reference era — a 2000s sample deliberately gets no tape/wear treatment.** Building a piano sample styled on Ne-Yo's "So Sick" and T-Pain/Chris Brown's "Best Love Song": "normally I'd add some sort of tape effect or something like that, but these types of songs often have really clean sounds. They're not too vintage. Obviously it's from the 2000s, which isn't too long ago, so they're not super worn out and detuned and stuff like that." A direct contrast to loner's usual default of reaching for tape/cassette coloration on nearly every sample — the era itself sets the ceiling on how much wear/character processing belongs on the source. → [[Kontakt 7]], [[2000s R&B Piano Sample Chain]] — loner, [how to make a 2000's r&b sample & flip it into a beat for tory lanez/bryson tiller](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sPqBmZLaDM4)
- **A finished sample's flip-into-a-beat pass: EQ → reverb → stretch/tempo-drop, in that order, to push an already-complete sample further back before drums go under it.** After a full sample (piano + counter-melody + strings + vocal) is finished, the flip starts with a restrained EQ band (some highs, some lows, "not go too crazy"), then a short reverb with the low end cut from the return and Size reduced, then a time-stretch that also drops the tempo (landed around 145 BPM) — three stages, each adding a bit more distance, applied to the WHOLE finished sample rather than to individual layers. → [[EQ]], [[Reverb]] (effect), [[2000s R&B Piano Sample Chain]] — loner, [how to make a 2000's r&b sample & flip it into a beat for tory lanez/bryson tiller](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sPqBmZLaDM4)
- **Deliberately mix a synthy one-shot element into an otherwise all-real-instrument palette, specifically to blend organic and electronic textures in the same beat.** A minimal chill/ambient build stacks Kontakt flute, a free Pianobook violin bank, and a sample-library piano — real-instrument sources throughout — then adds one bell one-shot (from an unnamed kit, auto-captioned "5 and xyz one shot kit") run through heavy delay + medium spring reverb, purely so the beat isn't 100% acoustic-sounding. A cheap, single-element way to keep an organic palette from reading as too "unplugged." → [[Sound Selection Guide]], [[Plugin Wishlist]], [[Frank Ocean Chill Ambient Chain]] — loner, [how to make chill ambient beats for frank ocean (sad/melodic chord technique)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=whI75NJcom8)
- **Melodyne a finished, bounced-and-pitched-down sample to extract its own bass notes, then write a new bass part on top — pitching the WHOLE sample up first if the extracted notes read too low.** Flipping a from-scratch vintage jazz/R&B sample in a fresh project: load the bounced sample, Melodyne it to identify the actual bass notes present (found B♭–C–B♭–C–A♭), then judge whether that register works before writing a new bass line — here the notes read too low, so the entire sample got pitched up 2–3 semitones FIRST, and only then was the new bass (including a couple of gliding/portamento notes) written against the repitched material. - **Identify an odd time signature (6/8) by ear from the DRUMS specifically, not the melody or chords.** loner's stated method for recognizing when a reference track isn't in plain 4/4: listen to how the drums count — PARTYNEXTDOOR's "Joy" groups in threes (one-two-three, one-two-three) rather than fours, the tell for 6/8. Finished with a filter + heavy reverb, forced mono, panned right. → [[Reverb]] (effect), [[EQ]], [[Depth and Space]], [[Sound Selection Guide]] — loner, [making a soulful afrobeats beat for rema & wizkid from scratch (use voice notes to start ideas!?)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=e-KvnLXHAy4)

The result is then processed heavily (low-cut EQ → long always-on reverb → HI-cut → mix-reduced ShaperBox filter) to push it toward an "underwater" character. Reconstructing a Tory Lanez/Chris Brown-style beat. → [[Chord Progressions]], [[Ghz Loh1 3]], [[Shaper]], [[Reverb]], [[Tory Lanez Underwater Synth Chain]] — loner, [how to make melodic r&b beats for tory lanez & chris brown (underwater synth/vocal sample)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7JCup6FVW5o)

## Speed-up sampling
- **Slow source → sped up** is a signature: a slow (e.g. 87 BPM) R&B/soul sample run at the track tempo (136 BPM) gets a fluttery, bright, "chipmunk-adjacent" character that's hard to get any other way. The speed-up *is* the sound.
- **Always key-match.** Pitch every sample to the song key with a key-match sampler (ADSR Sample, Splice Bridge, Ableton warp) so a collage of unrelated sources sits together.
- Ableton's **warping** is the tool of choice for sample-heavy tracks (better than Logic's for this).

## Evocative, not literal
- Build a *mood* from sound choice, not sound effects — Disclosure's "Deeper" feels underwater with **no water SFX at all** (ancient flute, reverse vocals, plucks, distorted reverse-reverb pads). Pick each layer for how it *feels* against the hook; spend the hours auditioning "what sits with this without covering it." → fill the [[Sound Palette]] slots with feeling, then carve.

## Where to actually get samples
- **Trade in the community** (Discord swaps) beats trawling YouTube — you get more interesting material, and pack-makers who nail a sound tend to keep making them (find one you like and depend on them).
- **Movies via PlayPhrase.me** — type a phrase, get clips of people saying it across thousands of films (e.g. an intro vocal hook of the same line said many ways).
- **Keep your ears open in life** — the "When a Fire Starts to Burn" hook was found by accident watching a video, not sample-hunting.
- **Make your own from a great singer** — give a vocalist chords and ask for oohs/ahs (or their favourite song a cappella) and sample *that* (Lianne La Havas → "Simulation"). The most original samples are ones you created.

## Using loops without sounding like everyone
- Prefer **top-loops** (shakers, tambourines, hats) over full kick-hat-snare loops — drums are the most recognisable giveaway of a stock loop.
- **Play your own kick/rhythm in** under a borrowed loop, or chop one-shots out and program your own groove, so the backbone is yours.
- Layer several small textures (an [[Ambient Texture and Field Recording]] bed, a pluck, a reverse hit) under the main element rather than one busy loop.

- **Resynthesis: analyze a sample into short, individually-editable "frames" instead of treating it as fixed audio.** A Synclavier technique — a sampled sound is broken into a sequence of ~5-10 ms frames (one worked example used 29), each frame capturing that instant's harmonic content as its own mini-FM/wavetable patch. Unlike a raw sample, each frame is independently editable — attack time, pitch, harmonic content — "like putty," reshapeable in the MIDDLE of a sound, not just trimmed at the edges; frames can crossfade smoothly into each other or cut directly. **Because it reconstructs harmonic content rather than replaying audio, pitch-shifting a resynthesized frame does NOT slow it down the way pitching down a normal sample does** — a real behavioral difference worth knowing when a part needs to play across a wide pitch range. A frame's harmonics can also be hand-edited via the same coefficient controls used to build a synthesized waveform from scratch (dial in a textbook sawtooth/square/triangle series on a resynthesized vocal frame, for instance), and a single frame can be voiced as multiple detuned/delayed copies to turn one instant of a sample into a sustained, chorused texture. No modern owned-plugin equivalent identified yet — flagged as a genuine gap; closest concept is granular/spectral resynthesis (e.g. in Wavetable's sample-to-wavetable import, though without frame-level harmonic hand-editing). **Practical frame-count budgeting, from a scored-film example**: attack ~3-4 frames, sustain ~7-8 (more, since the ear has time to notice sustained detail), release a handful — a 13-frame flute resynthesis split roughly along those lines. Also demonstrated on real wine glasses (finger-rubbed rim, water-tuned) sampled then resynthesized into a playable, sustained FM instrument for background chords. → [[Vintage Gear Worklist]] — Anthony Marinelli (feat. Kevin Maloney), [[Synclavier Product Specialist Kevin Maloney - Full Interview]], [[Stand By Me - The Sounds I made For The Film]]
