## What's taught

- **Mixing bebop and blues language inside one line (the video's headline idea).** Benson doesn't stay in one vocabulary for a whole phrase — a single line will start with a chord-tone/arpeggio-driven bebop idea and resolve into a blues-scale-flavored idea (or the reverse) without a seam. Early in the solo, over the turnaround from **C7 back to F7**: he opens on C7 with what reads as a **B♭ major-9-type arpeggio/pentatonic idea "on top of" the C7** (i.e. built from the C7's own ♭7, giving upper extensions rather than the plain C7 triad) — sliding up into the C and encircling the B♭ (a chromatic [[Enclosures Through the Scale|enclosure]] around a chord tone) — then, resolving onto F7, drops straight into a **blues double-stop idea**: a sustained D on top over G, A♭, and F underneath — Larsen describes this as "almost Dorian over F7." → [[Bebop Vocabulary]], [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]]
- **A likely-borrowed blues phrase, flagged (not asserted) as coming from Wes Montgomery.** Larsen can't confirm it for certain, but notes the exact same D-over-G/A♭/F double-stop shape appears in Wes Montgomery's playing on a recording of "No Blues," sustained for a whole chorus at the half-note — his best guess is Benson either learned it from that source directly or from wherever Wes himself got it. Framed as a reminder that even a signature-sounding line is often inherited vocabulary, not invented from scratch. *(general observation, not a confirmed attribution — flagged as such in the source.)*
- **From the blues phrase into a plain F-major pentatonic-flavored phrase.** Immediately after the blues double-stop, the line resolves into a straightforward F-major idea: B♭ down to F, then up through the major pentatonic shape hitting the 3rd, 5th, 6th, and root in turn — i.e. minor-blues color first, then a clean major-pentatonic-blues answer right after it, back to back in the same short passage.
- **His favorite device on dominant chords: an arpeggio built from the chord's own 3rd.** Called out explicitly as recurring "again and again" across the whole solo whenever a dominant chord passes — Larsen's clearest example is on the C7 at a turnaround: play the **half-diminished-shaped arpeggio rooted on E (C7's 3rd)** — which, over C7, hands you the 3rd-5th-♭7-9 (E-G-B♭-D) rather than a plain root-position C7 arpeggio — skipping down from the root to the 3rd and back up to the 7th before resolving into the next chord. This is functionally the same "build a chord-tone-and-extension arpeggio off the dominant's own 3rd" device already documented on [[Diminished Chords]] (§ Jack Ruch, full-*diminished*-7-off-the-3rd, giving 3-5-♭7-♭9) — Benson's version here uses the **half-diminished** (m7♭5) shape off the 3rd instead of the full dim7, which lands on the natural 9 rather than the ♭9, a noticeably more "inside"/consonant color than the altered-dominant version already on that page. → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Chord-Tone Soloing]], [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]
- **Thinking in plain triads plus chromatic leading (approach) notes, not a parent scale — the technique Larsen spends the most time on.** Worked over a minor ii-V turnaround into G minor (a pickup figure landing around a half-diminished chord resolving through altered dominant motion into G minor, then on toward C7): rather than running a scale over the changes, Benson treats the underlying chord as a **plain triad** (e.g. G minor 7 heard as just the G-B♭-D triad) and decorates it with **chromatic leading notes into each chord tone** — approach notes from a half step below (or above) landing squarely on 3rd/5th/root in turn. Two concrete instances Larsen plays back: (1) a line starting on the B♭ (3rd of Gm), skipping down to a C♯ purely to set up a chromatic approach into the D (5th of Gm), then two notes leading into the root, then an enclosure around the 3rd of the following C7; (2) a second pass over the same spot starting on an A-minor-triad idea down to its 3rd, then D7 chord tones, landing on Gm's B♭ (3rd) with an encircling approach, and later a similarly-approached line into the 9th of the next chord. → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] (Shapes 1–3, the "approach tone below" / "chord-tone turn" / "enclosure" vocabulary this is a real-recording instance of), [[Bebop Vocabulary]]
- **Why triad-plus-leading-notes produces more interesting lines than scale-running.** Larsen's stated reasoning: because the target is always a triad tone, the ear always resolves somewhere correct — but because you're skipping between triad tones (which are further apart than adjacent scale steps) rather than walking a scale stepwise, the line makes **bigger, less predictable interval jumps** while still landing on a chord tone every time. That combination — surprising motion, guaranteed-correct landings — is framed as a large part of what makes Benson's lines read as more sophisticated than typical scale-based jazz vocabulary. Directly the same underlying logic as [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]'s Step 4 ("outline the chord tones... with musical phrasing") and its Shape 3 enclosure, just demonstrated here on a real recording rather than as a drill.
- **A bebop-cliché blues opening, then call-and-response between two chords.** A later passage: the first bar of the form opens with the standard jazz-blues cliché — the 13th/6th up to the root — followed by a long chromatic climb from the minor 3rd up to the 5th, closed out with a descending bebop-scale-style run back down to the root. The next chord (B♭7) then answers with the **melodic inverse** of that same shape — a call-and-response device where the second phrase is a mirror of the first rather than new unrelated material.
- **Anticipation — landing on the next chord's 3rd before the barline.** On the same passage, Benson resolves onto B♭7's chord tones slightly early, landing on the 3rd of B♭7 ahead of the actual downbeat — a deliberate anticipation rather than a rhythmic accident, adding forward motion into the new chord.
- **A slide used purely for motion on a sustained note**, adding a small pitch bend/slide back and forth on a held note (up to the 5th) rather than leaving it static — a phrasing/touch detail, not a note-choice device. → [[Blues Lead Phrasing and Touch]]
- **Root-and-5th chord tones plus a single leading note to the 3rd, on B♭7** — a sparser version of the triad-plus-leading-note idea above: root, 5th, then a leading note into the 3rd, then back down through the root-5th shape again, rather than outlining every chord tone every time.
- **An F-minor blues cliché reordered, played from the 5th of B♭7.** The final passage worked in the video: a very standard F-minor blues lick (A♭ up to C, down to G, down to F) played over B♭7 — but with the note *order* rearranged from how the cliché is usually played, which Larsen calls out as the simple trick that keeps a well-worn lick sounding fresh: same notes, same underlying "F minor blues over B♭7" logic (the 5th-of-the-chord blues cliché), different sequencing. → [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]]
