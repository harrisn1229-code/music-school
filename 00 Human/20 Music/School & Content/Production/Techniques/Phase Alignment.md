# Phase Alignment

**What it is:** when two mics (or a mic + DI) capture one source, sound arrives at each at different times. Summed, the time offset comb-filters — frequencies cancel and reinforce in a pattern no EQ can fix. Alignment makes multi-mic captures sum *fuller*, not thinner.

## Prevention (at the source)
- **Equal distance rule:** capsules the same distance from the source can't disagree about arrival time.
- **3:1 rule (spaced pairs):** mics ≥3× as far from each other as from the source — the late arrival lands low enough in level that the combing is inaudible ([[1 Acoustic Guitar Track|Acoustic Guitar Track]]).
- **Polarity flip test, every setup:** flip ø on one mic; keep whichever way has **more low end** (bass frequencies cancel first — the low end is your phase meter).
- 🎬 *From video:* on a multi-mic source (a whole drum kit especially), interrogate **every mic pairing independently** rather than applying one universal polarity setting — rack tom vs. floor tom, overheads vs. front/room mic, and stereo pair vs. close mics are each their own relationship and each can prefer a different answer session to session; there's no absolute-right polarity, only what sums fullest for that pairing, that day — Steve Albini, [Optimizing phase relationships with Steve Albini](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DvvLr-Op5yc).

## The pink-noise method (Dan Austin — the pro cab move)
1. Play **pink noise through the amp**.
2. Place mic 1 by ear (headphones in front of the cab).
3. **Flip mic 2's polarity** and physically move it until the summed pair *cancels hardest* — a thin, windy near-silence. Maximum cancellation out-of-phase = perfect alignment in-phase.
4. Flip mic 2 back. The pair is now phase-locked; blend freely. Repeat per extra mic. ([[1 Electric Guitar Amp Track|Electric Guitar Amp Track]].)

## In the box
- **[[Align Delay]]:** nudge sample-level delay on the later mic until the low end is fullest. For mic + DI, the DI always arrives first — delay the DI to the amp mic.
- **Zoom in:** the waveforms show the offset directly; slide the late track back.
- Also a *creative* control: deliberately-not-quite-aligned distant mics = size and room. Alignment is a dial, not a law — but know where zero is before you leave it.

## Where phase bites in mixing
- **Mono check** ([[Utility]] mono / [[Stereo Tool]]): wideners, stereo synths, and spaced pairs can vanish in mono — if an element disappears, its sides are fighting its mid ([[Width Send]]).
- Parallel paths: any manual duplicate routing that skips latency compensation combs against the original.
- Layered samples (kick + 808, snare stacks): flip polarity on one layer and keep the fatter sum — same rule as mics.

## From videos

- **The FFT/Nyquist mechanism behind why zero-latency EQ shifts phase at all, plus an EQ-induced Haas-effect stereo-image warning.** A digital EQ built on the FFT can only represent as many frequency bands as half its block's sample count (Nyquist) — a 4,096-sample block at 44.1 kHz yields ~1 band per 10 Hz, and each band is a **minimum phase filter** that shifts timing/phase around it, worse toward the low end and on high-pass filters specifically — the underlying mechanism behind this note's existing linear-phase/pre-ringing tradeoff entries below. New caution: EQ'ing only one side of an otherwise-identical hard-panned stereo pair (e.g. a doubled guitar) with zero-latency EQ shifts that channel's phase relative to the other, which can trigger the **Haas/precedence effect** and pull the perceived image toward the unprocessed side — worth checking on any element EQ'd per-channel rather than via a linked/mid-side instance. → [[EQ]], [[Width and Stereo]] — Sage Audio, [How EQ Changes Phase](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2_v4VrPkzec)
- **Always check phase between a DI track and its mic'd counterpart on guitar, and flip polarity when they're out of phase — confirmed by ear, not just assumed.** Chris Lord-Alge, on a guitar's DI fuzz layer against its room-mic'd amp signal: "check the phase — okay, it's out of phase, so we flip the phase, that brings all the bottom back... always want to check the phase of DI versus amp and see which works." Once corrected, the DI layer (source/pedal unclear) is credited with adding real "heat" to the combined guitar sound — before the flip it would have been actively cancelling low end against the mic'd signal rather than reinforcing it. → [[1 Electric Guitar Amp Track]], [[Layering and Stacking]] — Mix with the Masters (Chris Lord-Alge), [[Mixing Muse Guitars - Chris Lord-Alge]]
- **Phase-reversal discipline as a standing rule whenever two mics point at each other on the same source, not something checked after the fact.** Sylvia Massy applies it on every opposing mic pair in a drum-kit setup — snare top/bottom, both toms top/bottom: "when two mics are pointing at each other they'll cancel each other out unless you flip that phase." On the MS overhead pair, the side capsule's phase is flipped via a patch-bay cable rather than on the channel strip — found in practice to sound better done that way. → [[Drum Mic Technique]], [[Width and Stereo]] — Sylvia Massy (Mix with the Masters), [[Analog Recording workflow - Sylvia Massy]]
- **Hard distortion on a low-frequency source (kick) visibly/audibly disturbs phase, worth checking after inserting a saturation stage, not just tone.** Tchad Blake, driving a kick through a SansAmp: "it's definitely messing with my face [phase]." → [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[Kick Design]] — Tchad Blake (Mix with the Masters), [[Parallel Distortion - Tchad Blake]]
- **Two separate, deliberate phase-inversion moves inside one reggaeton dembow build — one on a percussion click layer to resolve a clash between stacked samples, one on a doubled parallel-kick layer to fix drift the doubling itself introduced.** In a four-layer dembow click stack, the "classic" four-beat click layer gets its polarity flipped specifically because it clashes with the other click layers once summed (then low-cut to leave them room). Separately, on the kick: after duplicating an already-processed parallel kick layer a second time for extra body (see [[Parallel Processing]], [[Kick Design]]), the two processed copies needed a polarity flip to resolve the phase problem the doubling created — a direct, unsurprising consequence flagged elsewhere on this page (running parallel duplicates concurrently can drift out of phase against each other). *(transcript low-confidence — machine-translated, method past "flip polarity" not recoverable)* → [[Layering and Stacking]], [[Parallel Processing]], [[Kick Design]], [[Genre Drum Patterns]] — Mosty (Mix with the Masters), [[Mosty mixing a reggaeton track]]
- **Deliberately RE-phasing a bass's close mic against its DI to get a different tone on the chorus than the verse — phase used as a creative tonal-shift control, not just an alignment fix.** Nick Launay, mixing a wide-range, slide-heavy melodic bass part: the close mic is split to a second channel that engages specifically on choruses, carrying a **sample-accurate time/phase adjuster** ([[Align Delay]]-equivalent) that shifts the phase relationship between that close mic and the DI (and the other close mic) — nudging phase, not EQ or a different mic, is what changes the tone between sections. **Only possible because the bass was tracked with two sources (DI + close mic) in the first place** — "if I'd just recorded a DI, or just one microphone on the amp, I wouldn't have been able to do this." - **The biggest layering-phase lesson in this note's own "From videos" section, applied to programmed/sampled drum layers rather than mics: a kick, an 808, and a sample break beat each have their own cycle, and mismatched cycles cost you punch even as you're stacking MORE material on top.** Young Guru's plain-language definition, demonstrated on a real Pro Tools session: every wave file has a peak then a slope down — "a cycle" — and if the kick's cycle isn't hitting at the same moment as the 808's, which isn't hitting at the same moment as the break's, they fight instead of reinforcing. **Fix by eye AND by ear, together:** zoom into the waveforms to see exactly where each hit's peak lands, then nudge the 808 and kick in time until all three peaks land at (or very close to) the same moment — **treat the sample break as the fixed anchor** ("my break beat is the thing that has the feel, the human feel in it, it's the thing that I'm trying to emulate") and move the kick/808 to match it, not the reverse. Mute down to a couple of tracks and re-check different sections of the arrangement individually — alignment can drift section to section even within one song. Willing to chop and nudge **every single kick** individually once the session reached Pro Tools, even though it's usually unnecessary if accounted for while programming — "no one cares how long a song took to make... all they do is listen to the song," i.e. the time cost is justified purely by the (inaudible-as-a-technique, audible-as-a-result) payoff. *(general starting point — no exact ms nudge amounts given, described qualitatively as "sliding them over" by eye/ear)* → [[Layering and Stacking]], [[Kick Design]], [[Bass Design]], [[SUB Slot]], [[BASS Slot]] — Young Guru (Mix with the Masters), [[Taking your ideas to the next level with Young Guru]]
- **Absolute polarity (does the kick's transient need to push the speaker cone toward the listener?) probably isn't the audible variable people think it is — the extra inverting amplifier stage it takes to flip polarity is.** The "audience convention" (positive pressure toward the listener, matching what an audience member hears first) is a defensible theory but an arbitrary one — from the drummer's own perspective the air moves *away* first, so enforcing the audience convention is actually an artificial listening experience for the player. Auditioning full mixes both ways rarely produces a preference either way. The likely explanation when a preference *does* show up: in a conventional feedback amplifier, one gain stage inverts polarity, so flipping polarity in a signal path means adding or removing a full stage of amplification/circuitry — what gets heard as "a polarity difference" may really be the sonic signature of that added/removed stage, not polarity itself (stated as an untested hunch, no blind A/B done, but the more likely explanation). → **Within one source, relative polarity between mics is a different story and does matter** — see [[Utility]] for the mic-pairing technique from the same video — Steve Albini, [Optimizing phase relationships with Steve Albini](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DvvLr-Op5yc).
- **Some mics are electronically opposite-polarity straight from the factory or after a repair, with no user error involved — map your mic locker for this and label the offenders.** A demonstrated example: a Russian-made mic wired opposite-polarity from Western-standard mics (possibly extra internal electronics needed to normalize polarity, possibly a different regional standard); summing it with a normal mic produces a built-in subtractive/cancelling effect. Second-hand or repaired/modified mics are a second, unpredictable source of the same issue. Fix: rewire the connector if the circuit allows it; if not, at least document it — "put a sticker on the microphone" — so an out-of-phase mic doesn't get misdiagnosed later as a console or electronics fault. — Steve Albini, [Optimizing phase relationships with Steve Albini](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DvvLr-Op5yc)
- **Running several parallel channel duplicates of the same source simultaneously (each with its own plugin chain) can drift the paths out of phase against each other — worth a dedicated sample-rate/phase-alignment utility in the chain, not just an afterthought.** Building a two-channel parallel vocal duplicate (one channel milder, one driven hard — see [[Parallel Processing]]), the engineer specifically inserted a phase/sample-rate utility plugin (auto-caption "sanzan," name unclear) alongside a limiter, stating its purpose plainly: "to make sure sample rates, sample basing stuff was good, to keep the phase as close as possible — because sometimes it can go out with these guys if they're all running at the same time." A reminder that phase risk isn't only a multi-mic problem — parallel plugin duplicates of one source, run concurrently, can drift too. → [[Parallel Processing]], [[Saturation and Distortion]] — Mix with the Masters (Ben Baptie), [[Julian Casablancas' vocal sound with Ben Baptie]]
- **When linear phase EQ earns its cost, and its main tradeoff (pre-ringing).** Linear phase solves two specific problems, not a general-purpose default: equalizing a **parallel signal** whose original path isn't also filtered (low-latency linear phase avoids phase cancellation when the two paths recombine — see [[Parallel Processing]]), and **high-pass filtering**. At high settings it introduces **pre-ringing** — a pre-echo artifact ahead of transients — though the source's stated opinion is that concerns about its audibility are often overstated in practice. Default for everything else: **zero-latency natural phase**, or **low-latency linear phase** when accuracy without transient smear matters more than zero latency. Complements this note's existing entry below on linear-phase's separate loudness cost (transient smear, worse with more latency) — this is the phase-artifact side of that same tradeoff, not a contradiction of it. → [[Pro-Q 4]], [[EQ]] — Sage Audio, [EQ Masterclass](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iPBnL6mx9c0)
- **Aliasing distortion costs loudness specifically through high-frequency phase cancellation — the mechanical reason oversampling matters on a master, beyond just "cleaner sound."** Undersampled processing generates aliasing distortion, and that aliasing causes **phase cancellation in the high frequencies** — which quietly reduces loudness two ways at once: attenuating important high-frequency content directly, and lowering the overall level through destructive interference between the aliased and original signal. Oversampling reduces the aliasing and therefore this phase-driven loudness loss. Pairs with the separate, unrelated loudness cost of **linear-phase processing** (avoid outside parallel processing — it smears transients, worse with more latency/pre-ringing) — two different mechanisms that both quietly tax a master's loudness if left unmanaged. → [[Clipping and Loudness]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [Mastering Loud But Not Squashed](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YJVE4_CQ9Ts)
- **Phase relationships between layered/duplicated signals change how clipping and compression behave on the combined signal — sometimes by several dB, even with identical processing settings.** Demonstrated: summing a signal with a phase-shifted copy of itself and clipping the sum produced a meaningfully different (~3 dB louder in the demo) result purely from the phase relationship, not from any change to the clipper's settings — worth checking when a layered sound reacts to processing unexpectedly. Stated stance: don't let phase-checking become creative paralysis, but be aware it's a real variable. → [[Clipping and Loudness]] — Fox Stevenson (Sol State), [You Will NEVER Use Clipping The Same Way Again](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rXbMcPeD3Mw)
