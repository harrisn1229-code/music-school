# Behind the Track｜'Modern Girl' by Bleachers

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5Y1PQt5imXo)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5Y1PQt5imXo)

## Synopsis

Another **Mix with the Masters** "Behind the Track" entry, this one Jack Antonoff (Bleachers) walking through 'Modern Girl' solo, filmed at Rue Boyer in Paris. Like the channel's 070 Shake/Elephant episode, it's almost entirely a philosophy piece rather than a settings breakdown — but where that video's philosophy was about restraint (near-zero processing, no comping), this one is about **maximalism in service of a feeling**: capturing the specific, uncontrollable magic of a live show rather than literally recording live. Antonoff states the governing principle directly — "I don't care about how I get the recording. I only care about the feeling of the recording" — and everything else in the video is a concrete example of that. The song's origin is a genuine happy accident: Patrik Berger (co-producer) playing a minimal hat/kick/snare setup through two SM58s in a vocal booth at Esplanade Studios, New Orleans — an accidental "bedroom recording" quality from gear that shouldn't have produced a keeper, but did. Antonoff then tracked a full band to that same raw tempo/feel and waited for the moment the band "forgot they were in the studio" (a specific fill from drummer Evan) — that moment became the literal start of the finished song, not just an inspiration for it. The bass/sub design ("a totally f***ed up bass mixed with a perfectly beautiful sub mixed with a completely deranged arp" — the sub being a Moog run through Waves RBass) and the "bucket" layering approach (stacking full extra takes of fiddle, bass, drums, and sax on top of the core band track, comped from ~8 takes per player) both point at the same idea: pile up contrasting, imperfect elements until the result reads as the chaotic, maximalist feeling of a live show rather than a clean studio recording. The vocal treatment closes the loop — instead of a controlled double/harmony stack, Antonoff kept "singing on top of singing on top of singing," deliberately mixing clean/treated backups with completely wild ones and pitching the whole stack up, specifically to dodge the generic "hey-ho stompy" party-track cliché (his own shorthand for the pitfall, and his own alternate fix for it — turning everyone into a chipmunk — which he deliberately avoided in favor of the more chaotic pile-up). It's a short video with no numbered settings, but the underlying lessons (comp from many takes and grab the accidental keeper moment; build a song's arrangement start-point around where the "magic" actually happens rather than chronologically; stack contrasting vocal treatments instead of a uniform double; use Moog+RBass for a two-part deranged/beautiful sub-bass combo) are all concrete and reusable.

## Techniques

- **"I don't care about how I get the recording. I only care about the feeling of the recording."** The video's stated governing principle — the goal is reproducing the emotional experience of a live show, not a technically clean or literally-live recording. Everything else in the video (the accidental keeper take, the deliberately imperfect layering, the maximalist vocal pile-up) is presented as a direct application of this one rule. *(~0:00–1:00)*
- **An accidental "wrong" setup produced the keeper foundation take.** Co-producer Patrik Berger played a minimal hat/kick/snare setup, miked with (Antonoff recalls) roughly two SM58s, in a vocal booth at Esplanade Studios, New Orleans — described on camera as sounding "like some bedroom recording," i.e. objectively lo-fi/wrong-tool-for-the-job by normal drum-recording standards. That "wrong" raw take is what the whole song was built from — framed as the difference between a technically-competent take ("Moog sounds like a Moog, snare sounds good, cool") and the rare take that actually has "that feeling," likened to "driving on the edge of a cliff and wondering if you're going to go off." → [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **Track a full band to the tempo/feel of that raw keeper take, then wait for the moment the band forgets they're in the studio — and cut the song to start there.** After the core hat/kick/snare take, the full band was tracked playing along to that same tempo. A few minutes in, everyone "forgot they were in the studio" and started playing looser/harder; drummer Evan played a specific fill at that exact moment, which Antonoff identifies as the real magic moment ("I feel him forgetting he's in the studio. I feel the spirit of when my band plays."). That moment was then used as the literal **start of the finished song** — "there's nothing that happens later that isn't happening here" — rather than kept buried mid-arrangement as a high point to build toward. A direct example of arranging FROM the keeper moment rather than TO it. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **A tuned, two-Moog-part sub/bass recipe: a "totally f***ed up bass" + "a perfectly beautiful sub" (Moog through Waves [[RBass]]) + "a completely deranged arp," combined into one composite low-end.** Antonoff names his "one tool to really lift things" as his sub — a Moog run through **Waves RBass** for harmonic sub reinforcement — then layers a deliberately mangled/distorted bass part and a "deranged" arpeggio on top of/around it. The three together ("put the three together and you get a really interesting quality") is the recipe, not any one part alone — a beautiful, controlled sub anchors two much wilder companion layers. *(no specific RBass Frequency/Intensity settings given — qualitative only)* → [[RBass]], [[Bass Design]], [[Layering and Stacking]], [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **Comp from many takes to find the accidental great moment, applied to a live instrumentalist, not just a vocalist.** "What I like to do when I'm recording is just have him play like eight takes and I'll grab moments" — describing fiddle player Bobby Hawk. The same comp-many-takes-for-the-keeper-moment logic this vault already documents for vocals ([[Vocal Comping and Takes]]) applied here to a live instrumental performer. → [[Vocal Comping and Takes]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **The "bucket" layering concept: stack a full extra take of each instrument (a "fiddle bucket," a "bass bucket," a "giant drum bucket," sax) on top of the core band recording, deliberately chasing "beautiful chaos" rather than a clean arrangement.** Described as genuinely emotional to hear come together — "that's the feeling of live... every element is coming together and playing with a kind of maximalism." Rather than one performance per instrument, the finished mix layers multiple simultaneous full performances of the *same* instrument role, embracing the resulting density/imperfection as the point. → [[Layering and Stacking]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **Deliver the vocal with the same maximalist "bucket" logic — many stacked vocal takes ("singing on top of singing on top of singing") mixing clean and wild treatments, then pitched up as a group for surrealism, explicitly to dodge a generic party-track cliché.** Antonoff names the risk directly: a big group-vocal "party track" moment can easily read as generic "hey-ho stompy," and one common fix is pitching everyone up into chipmunk territory. His actual solution avoids that cliché-fix while still using pitch: some vocal layers sung low, some high, some "perfectly treated" (clean, controlled backups), some "completely bananas" (wild, uncontrolled takes) — piled on take after take — with the **whole resulting stack pitched up** afterward for "a level of surrealism," not to make individual voices sound like chipmunks. The chaos comes from the mix of treatments within the stack, not from any single obvious effect. → [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]], [[Pitch and Formant]], [[Layering and Stacking]]
- **"A giant mystery... based on nothing you can control, something just sets you ablaze."** Closing philosophy: the moments that make a song special can't be planned for or scheduled (a pointed dig at "productivity hack" content — the 6am-to-midnight "three days in one day" Instagram post — as fundamentally incompatible with how this kind of creative spark actually works). Framed as a mindset for staying open to accidents (the SM58 drum take, the wrong-note-turned-magic-moment pattern this channel documents elsewhere) rather than engineering them. *(general creative philosophy, logged here only — not a technique with a home elsewhere)*
