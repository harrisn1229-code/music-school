# Why is Strawberry Fields Forever in A half-sharp major?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QgtzOafdoOQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QgtzOafdoOQ)

## Synopsis

The full production history behind "Strawberry Fields Forever"'s famously non-standard pitch center (roughly halfway between A major and B♭ major) — this is the "separate prior video" already referenced in the shorter existing analysis at [[The music theory of "Strawberry Fields Forever"]]. Traces the song through its many studio versions (55 hours of studio time total) to the vari-speed tape-splicing decision that fused two incompatible takes into the released master.

## Techniques

- **Vari-speed as a pre-digital pitch/tempo tool, and its defining constraint**: playing tape back at a different speed than it was recorded raises or lowers pitch — but because pitch and tempo are physically locked together on tape (unlike modern software, which can change them independently), speeding up ALWAYS raises both pitch and tempo together, and slowing down always lowers both together. Every technique below is a consequence of working around that single constraint.
- **Splicing two takes recorded in genuinely different keys AND tempos into one continuous track, by vari-speeding each take to meet in the middle rather than matching one to the other.** Take 26 (faster, in C major) and Take 7 (slower, previously vari-speed-drifted to ~57 cents flat of B♭) needed to become one continuous recording. Because there was no single vari-speed setting that would make both takes match in BOTH key and tempo simultaneously, the engineers prioritized matching KEY over matching tempo: slowing Take 26 by 11.5% brought it from C major down to ~12 cents flat of B♭ (closely matching Take 7's pitch), even though this left Take 26's tempo (~99 BPM) still faster than Take 7's (~91 BPM, nudged very slightly up to narrow the gap further). The result is a genuinely non-standard, in-between pitch center across the whole track — not a stylistic choice, a byproduct of solving a splice-compatibility problem with only one adjustable variable available.
- **Hiding an unavoidable tempo mismatch by placing the edit point at a natural musical pause.** Even after vari-speeding both takes toward each other, a small tempo drift remained (91→99 BPM) — audible only if the two takes are manually resynced against each other, but functionally undetectable in the finished mix because the splice point was deliberately placed where the music had already paused, masking the discontinuity. Confirmed effective by test: John Lennon didn't notice the edit had happened when the producers played him the result, deliberately positioning themselves to block his view of the tape machine during playback.
- **A physical/practical footnote worth keeping for historical grounding**: the actual splice was executed by physically cutting the analog tape with a blade and rejoining it with adhesive tape — the "seamless" edit was a literal razor-blade edit, not a metaphor.
- **Bonus device, same song: use a fade-out immediately followed by a fade-in to conceal an unwanted passage without cutting it entirely.** The released master fades out, pauses, then fades back in at the song's end specifically to skip over a percussion passage from the vari-speeded Take 26 that had timing/sync issues too audible to leave in — while still preserving the material both before and after that flawed section, rather than editing the flawed bars out entirely (which would have left an audible splice).
- **A methodology note on conflicting historical sources, useful as a general research caution**: published accounts (Ian MacDonald's *Revolution in the Head*, Mark Cunningham's *Good Vibrations*) describe the two takes as a semitone or a full tone apart, while the presenter's own direct chromatic-tuner measurement of the publicly released takes finds ~57 cents (closer to a tone). Plausible resolution offered: George Martin's own quoted figure likely describes how far EACH take needed to move to meet in the middle, not the total distance between them — a wording ambiguity that may have propagated into later secondary sources without them checking the primary audio.
