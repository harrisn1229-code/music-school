# Is Your Improvisation Practice a Waste of Time? (And How to Fix That)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0WYeuCUnX-Q)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0WYeuCUnX-Q)

## Synopsis

Jens Larsen's answer to a specific practice-room failure mode: scales-and-exercises practice is easy to structure into a routine with visible progress, but *soloing* practice is often just unstructured noodling with no goal and no way to tell if you're actually getting better. His fix, demonstrated by playing two full solos back-to-back over the same tune (the jazz standard "I Want to Be Happy," a tonic-diminished-turnaround form he'd never played before, learned on the spot from a bandstand call), is to give soloing practice an explicit, nameable goal — melodicism, specifically **motivic development** — instead of "play more licks." The first solo is deliberately the *unstructured* baseline: connect the harmony competently with arpeggios and target notes, staying in one position, but with no larger melodic thread — musical, but reading as "a bunch of licks next to each other," not a solo. The second solo is the fix in action: one melodic idea (a 2–3 note motif) is stated, then developed continuously through the A section by changing its rhythm, its ending, and its starting position on the neck, rather than restarting from scratch on each chord. Larsen narrates both solos bar-by-bar, naming exactly which arpeggio/target-note device is happening on each chord and, in the second solo, exactly how the motif is being varied and why a fresh idea is introduced at the bridge. The most transferable single idea in the video is procedural rather than theoretical: after every phrase you play, *listen to what you just played* and ask "what should come next" — thinking in terms of the sound/shape you want to hear, not in note names or scale choices — which is what actually produces continuity between phrases instead of a new lick per chord.

## What's taught

**The core critique (the "waste of time" problem):**
- Scales/exercises are easy to turn into a measurable practice routine; soloing practice usually isn't, and without a goal it becomes noodling that never visibly improves.
- **The fix: give soloing practice an explicit, nameable goal.** Larsen's chosen goal is melodicism, and he narrows that immediately to one drillable mechanism — motivic development — rather than leaving "be more melodic" as a vague aspiration.
- **Diagnostic for "not a solo, just licks":** if every chord gets its own separate idea with no thread connecting them, "that's not a solo, that's just a bunch of licks next to each other." That is the failure state the whole method is built to fix.

**Pre-requisite: have a strong harmonic overview of the tune before you solo on it.** Before playing a note, Larsen has the harmony explained to him in functional-analysis shorthand rather than a raw chord chart: "tonic diminished, ii–V, ii–V turnaround," and the bridge as "ii–V, ii–IV, back to the dominant, then a 3-6-2-5 with the dominant being a ♭6 and the 5♭9." Framing a tune this way (as harmonic devices/progressions, not a flat chord-by-chord list) is what lets him improvise convincingly on a form he'd never played, cold, at tempo — the same "analyze as functional devices, not isolated chords" principle documented on [[The Five Pillars of Jazz Guitar Practice]] Pillar 4. Companion video referenced directly for the full method of simplifying a chord progression this way (including how Pat Martino and Barry Harris each approach it): linked at the end of this video (title not resolved in the transcript — flag for a future enrichment pass if it surfaces).

**Solo 1 — harmony-connecting baseline (deliberately the "before" example):**
- Stay in one guitar position and lean on arpeggios that spell out each chord directly: F major 7 arpeggio over the tonic.
- **Target the minimum notes that actually change between adjacent chords, rather than re-deriving a whole new scale/arpeggio.** Concrete worked example: F major7 → F♯ diminished — only two notes move (F→F♯, E→E♭); play exactly those two notes on the diminished chord to make the connection audible. Same logic on G minor7 → C7: only one note changes (F→E), so that's the note placed on beat 1 of the C7 bar. A third instance on the turnaround: target the ♭6 (E♭) over the D7.
- Switch arpeggio source per chord as needed (e.g. the B♭ major 7 arpeggio, built from the 3rd of the chord in play, on the next chord change) rather than only ever using the tonic arpeggio.
- Genre-flavor injection while still following the form: current listening (the first Jim Hall Trio album) surfaces as extra rhythmic phrasing on the tonic chord — cited as a concrete example of how what you're listening to leaks into what comes out under your fingers even when you're not consciously quoting it.
- **Bridge = deliberate gear change.** Because the harmony stops cycling around the tonic and moves somewhere else (to B♭), the *solo* has to change with it: switch from the swing/rhythmic phrasing used on the A sections into more clearly Bebop lines (triplets, arpeggios), move into a higher register, and favor longer sustained notes over short rhythmic ones — a bundle of contrasts applied together specifically because the harmony itself just made a bigger move.
- Last A: register/vocabulary shift again — lean on F major 6 / F major pentatonic (a lighter substitute for the F major7 arpeggio used in the A sections) partly because it doubles as a way into the diminished-chord connection (same E♭ target-note logic as above) and partly for a bluesier color; stays in the high register established at the bridge rather than resetting back down.
- Reviewing this solo afterward: audible hesitation/an "emergency exit" phrase where the harmony wasn't yet fully internalized — flagged honestly as a normal artifact of soloing on unfamiliar changes in real time, not something to edit out of the lesson.

**Solo 2 — motivic development as the drill (the actual fix, worked start to finish):**
- **Method: state a short motif (2–3 notes is enough — don't need 8–10 notes to move through changes; more notes means more to recalculate at each chord), then develop it continuously through the form** instead of starting a new idea on every chord. First motif in this solo moves by shifting just its two lowest notes down (to F♯ and E♭) to track the F major7 → F♯ diminished change — same target-note logic as Solo 1, but now framed as motif *tracking* rather than a one-off arpeggio connection.
- **Concrete visual-shape trick for moving a motif through changes: think of the motif as a movable shape on the fretboard**, and reuse that shape (transposed/adjusted) as the harmony moves, rather than recalculating note names chord by chord. Explicitly called "incredibly practical... on the guitar."
- **A motif has to be varied, not just repeated verbatim, or it goes robotic/predictable/boring.** Two concrete, independently-usable ways to vary a repeated motif, drilled here in the same phrase: (1) **change the rhythm** — same or similar notes, different rhythmic placement; (2) **change how the phrase ends** — vary the closing gesture rather than resolving the motif the same way every time. Larsen does both in the same solo, sometimes together, sometimes rhythm-only or ending-only.
- **A motif can drift structurally too** — later restatements can move to "an artistic representation" of the original shape (same underlying melodic contour, no longer literally derivable from the first statement's visual pattern) rather than a strict transposition — signals the motif is being genuinely developed, not just copy-pasted at new pitches.
- **The turnaround at the end of the first A resolves the developed motif into a straight Bebop line** — an explicit "and now switch modes" move once the motivic material has run its course for that section, not a rule that the whole solo must stay motif-based.
- **Balance principle, stated as the umbrella goal:** deliberately vary approach across a solo — blues phrases, Bebop lines, swing/rhythmic phrases, short notes vs. long notes — because staying in one mode the whole time gets boring; "you need to listen to what you're doing all the time" and consciously reach for something different.
- **Bridge, second solo:** busier/more active than the bridge of Solo 1's tune-model comparison (Larsen contrasts with "Star Dust," where the bridge stays comparatively static) — wider intervals, more notes, plus a specific worked device: a **pedal-point idea over the B♭ major 7** (repeating a fixed note against moving harmony underneath it), then a second motif developed via a Trill, which he explicitly attributes to "feeling more free to use trills" in the second pass specifically because motivic development had already been the main focus and this was a chance to layer in a different color.
- **Last A of Solo 2 — an honest "it didn't come out the way I wanted" moment, kept in the lesson on purpose:** a motif built on the F6/F major-pentatonic idea (same E♭-for-diminished-connection logic as Solo 1) that Larsen judges afterward as a good motif that just didn't land where he intended, giving a ♭9 color on the C7 he didn't plan for — presented as normal, not a mistake to hide, and folds into an "and then it just goes into a Bebop line" recovery, similar in spirit to [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]'s "deliberately getting ugly to build confidence" recovery drill.

**The transferable practice instruction (the actual daily drill to take away):**
- **After playing a phrase, listen back to what you just played and ask "what should come next?"** — explicitly **not** a note-name or scale-name decision ("now I want to hear F♯ diminished, or A♭ minor pentatonic" is called out directly as *not* how this works). Think in terms of the sound/shape you want to hear next, then find it on the instrument — described as genuinely different from most of what gets practiced and something that takes deliberate getting-used-to.
- **Concrete drill instruction: take just the first few bars of a tune, state a simple motif, then keep changing how you end the phrase and the order of the notes** — explicitly named as "really useful" exercises for building the "listen → predict → play" loop above, independent of any specific tune.
- Call-and-response as a structural device: treat one motif's statement as a "call," and treat the way it's developed/varied immediately after as the "response" — an explicit naming of the ordinary call-and-response principle as a tool for motivic continuity specifically.
- **Don't over-develop a single motif for the whole solo.** Contrast/renewal matters as much as continuity — "you don't want to stick to a motif too long," so plan explicit conclusions and pivots to a new idea (the video's own bridge transitions are the worked examples of this).
