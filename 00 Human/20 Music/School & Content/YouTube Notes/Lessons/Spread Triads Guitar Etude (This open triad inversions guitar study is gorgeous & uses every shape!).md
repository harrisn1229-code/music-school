# Spread Triads Guitar Etude (This open triad inversions guitar study is gorgeous & uses every shape!)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EoCkiaF00Go)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EoCkiaF00Go)

## Synopsis
A single self-composed etude, not a technique roundup — Jared (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared) walks through a spread-triad ("open position voicing") study he wrote that harmonizes the entire key of C major in spread triads and chains all seven diatonic triads together using **minimal voice leading**: every chord change moves exactly one note, by a half or whole step, nothing else. The etude covers **every diatonic triad in the key** (C, Dm, Em, F, G, Am, Bdim) **and every inversion of each**, purely as a side effect of always taking the closest available spread-triad shape for the next chord in a third-based chain (C→Em→G→Bdim→Dm→F→Am, repeating), walking down the neck one shape at a time. It's framed explicitly as a fretboard/theory/voice-leading study as much as a piece to play — Jared spends real time on the classical figured-bass (Roman numeral) analysis of what's happening (root position / 6/4 / 6, i.e. root/5th/3rd in the bass) and on how to practice it analytically, not just by rote. This channel already has one other video in the vault ([[Gorgeous Classical Progression - Milonga Guitar Chord Theory Analysis| Milonga Guitar Chord Theory Analysis]], cited on [[Chord Inversions]]) using the same "closest-shape voice leading" logic on a different (fingerstyle-classical) piece — this video is the same author applying the identical principle as a deliberately-constructed pedagogical etude covering an entire diatonic key's spread triads, rather than analyzing an existing composition.

## What's taught

**Spread triad = "open position voicing"** — take a close-position triad (three notes stacked on adjacent strings/scale-tones, e.g. D minor root-♭3-5 = D-F-A fretted pinky/5th-fret-A-string, middle-finger/3rd-fret-D-string, index/2nd-fret-G-string) and move the **middle note up an octave**. The result is the same three notes reordered bottom-to-top as **1-5-♭3** instead of 1-♭3-5 — an "open," harp-like sound with a string skip in the middle, same underlying chord. Terminology note: "open position" here is music-theory/composition jargon for this spread voicing, **not** the guitar-specific sense of "open position" (playing near the nut using open strings) — don't conflate the two. Same concept, same physical shape-family, as the vault's existing [[Open Voice Triads for Soloing]] (Jens Larsen) — that page's soloing exercises and this etude are two different applications of identical spread/open-voice triad shapes.

**The etude's construction rule — a third-chain through the whole diatonic key:**
1. Start on the tonic triad (C major) in a spread voicing, high on the neck.
2. Move to the next diatonic triad **a third up** — C → E minor → G major → B diminished → D minor → F major → A minor → (back to C, repeating the cycle) — the full seven-triad diatonic third-chain, walked twice as the etude descends the neck.
3. **Every single chord change moves exactly one note of the three-note voicing**, by a half step or a whole step — never more than one voice, never more than a step. Which of the three voices moves (top/middle/bottom of the three-note spread shape) rotates through the voicing in a repeating pattern as the chain proceeds ("top string, middle string, bottom string, top string, middle string, bottom string…").
4. The whole etude is one continuous descent down the fretboard, chord by chord, until it reaches the lowest playable position, then cadences home: **G7 → C** closes it (the only point in the piece where a real dominant-7 chord, not a triad, appears — an explicit "how to end a triad study with a real cadence" move).
5. Because the chain touches every diatonic triad and the "closest voicing" rule forces a different inversion each time, **the etude ends up covering every diatonic triad of the key in every one of its inversions** — full coverage of the key's spread-triad vocabulary, achieved as a byproduct of the voice-leading rule rather than by deliberately drilling each shape in turn.

**Figured-bass / Roman-numeral literacy, taught in passing:** the on-screen analysis uses classical figured-bass shorthand — plain Roman numeral = root position (root in the bass); "6/4" = second inversion (5th in the bass — read as "6 and 4," the interval sizes from the bass note up to the other two voices once collapsed to close position: a 6th and a 4th); "6" (short for "6/3," the 3 is dropped by convention) = first inversion (3rd in the bass). Applied to the etude's own chain: C (root position) → Em (6/4, 5th in bass) → G (6, 3rd in bass) → repeat pattern for the next group of three triads. **The inversion pattern itself repeats in a fixed root→6/4→6 cadence every three chords**, cycling against the seven-chord third-chain (a 3-against-7 relationship) so the phase between "which triad" and "which inversion" shifts as the chain repeats through the full key.

**How to practice it (Jared's own stated method, not just play-through):**
- **Stage 1 — play it off the page first.** Read the tab/notation as written, get it under the fingers and into the ear before doing any theoretical analysis — don't try to reason your way through it cold.
- **Stage 2 — go back and name the anatomy of each chord.** For every voicing: which chord tone is in the bass (root/3rd/5th)? What's the resulting inversion (root position/1st/2nd)? What's about to move to reach the next chord in the chain?
- **Stage 3 — internalize it structurally, not by rote.** The goal state: standing on any chord in the chain (say D minor) and being able to reason it out live — "it goes up a third in the key to F major, and I know the middle voice moves, so that's the shape" — rather than having memorized a finger sequence with no theoretical map underneath it.
- **Explicit practice-philosophy point (general, not etude-specific):** this kind of slow, low-note-count, analytical "headscratching" practice — figuring out *why* a voicing is what it is rather than just executing it — is called out as some of the most potent practice available for building theoretical/fretboard structural understanding, as distinct from high-repetition technical practice (needed for playing tunes/building chops). Pick the practice mode that matches the goal: reps for technique, slow analysis for internalized structure.
- **A generative drill that works on any spread-triad material, not just this etude:** grab *any* spread-triad voicing at random (from the middle of this etude or elsewhere) and work out, by ear and by theory, the single note that has to move a half or whole step to reach the next diatonic chord up a third. This is literally how Jared says he composed the piece — he noticed the one-note connection between two spread triads and then worked out the whole diatonic chain from that single observation.

**What's not fully recoverable from captions:** the video's on-screen tab/notation carries the complete note-for-note voicings and exact fret numbers for the whole descending sequence; the spoken track narrates *which voice moves and by how much* at each step but doesn't verbalize every fret number for the full ~20-chord descent (only the opening D minor close-position shape gets explicit frets: pinky 5th fret/A string, middle finger 3rd fret/D string, index 2nd fret/G string). The construction rule above (third-chain, one-note moves, rotating voice, root→6/4→6 inversion pattern, G7-C cadence ending) is fully captured and is enough to reconstruct/improvise the etude on the fretboard by ear following the rule; the exact tab is sold separately via the creator's paid weekly-lesson membership (soundguitarlessons.com/join) — not reproduced here.
