# Roland Juno-106

## What it is
Roland's affordable 6-voice polysynth — one DCO per voice, one envelope, one filter, and the most beloved chorus circuit ever built. It was the "starter polysynth" of the 80s and has never left fashion since: house stabs, synthwave beds, dream-pop pads, and half of modern bedroom production speak Juno.

## What makes the sound
- **DCOs (digitally-clocked analog oscillators)** — rock-stable tuning, unlike a drifting Jupiter/Prophet. **Why**: all 6 voices share one common clock and stay perfectly phase-locked to each other — play the same note on all 6 voices and there's zero beating between them, unlike a true free-running analog oscillator design (Prophet-5) where identical notes across voices drift slightly and beat. One oscillator per voice *should* sound thin, which is why…
- **…the Bucket-brigade chorus (I/II/I+II) is the actual instrument.** The famously noisy BBD stereo chorus splits and detunes the mono voice into a wide, glassy ensemble. Juno pad = simple saw + chorus. That hiss is authentic → [[TAL-Chorus-LX]] exists purely to clone it.
- **A single very musical analog lowpass** (self-resonant, with that specific squelchy sweep) + **HPF slider** for instantly cleaning boom out of pads — the "Juno bass to bed" workflow is built into the panel → [[BED Slot]], [[BASS Slot]].
- **PWM on the DCO** gives shimmering motion without tuning chaos; sub-oscillator slider adds one-knob weight.
- Six voices force economical writing: stabs, pads, and basses, all committed and mix-ready.
- **The filter is a Roland IR3109 chip** — the same chip used in the Jupiter-8 and Juno-6/60, which is why all four synths share a family-recognizable low-pass character despite different oscillator sections. Single shared envelope generator drives both VCA and VCF (a deliberate simplification vs. the Prophet-5's per-section envelopes) — fewer knobs, but any envelope change affects amp and filter together. HPF is a stepped (not continuous/voltage-controllable) global control, and its "0" position is actually a slight bass boost rather than true bypass.
- **Digital pitch bend is exact** — bends a precise octave up/down, unlike the earlier all-analog Juno-6/60 bender, which drifts unpredictably. One of the concrete upgrades the 106 made over its own siblings, alongside added MIDI and refined patch management.

## Digital versions
- **Owned:** [[Model 84 Polyphonic Synthesizer]] — Softube's Model 84 *is* a Juno-106 emulation (DCO, filter, and chorus modeled; the house/synthwave workhorse in the collection); [[TAL-Chorus-LX]] — free clone of the Juno chorus, drop on anything to Juno-ify it; [[Drift]] and [[Analog]] approximate the architecture in stock Ableton.
- **Paid:** TAL-U-NO-LX (reference-grade emulation), Roland Cloud Juno-106 (official), Arturia Jun-6 V (Juno-6/60 sibling).
- **Free:** [[TAL-Chorus-LX]]; TAL-U-NO-62 (older free TAL Juno).

## Techniques

- **An entire song's synth part built on this specific synth purely because it was the only one available that day — constraint as the direct source of the song's identity.** Arlo Parks names the Juno-106 as her must-have gear for *Ambiguous Desire*, citing "Two-Sided": "the limitations of only having this one synth and it feeling exciting in the moment is what made my favorite song on the record." Baird notes it wasn't even his own unit — borrowed from a friend specifically because reference tracks for the album had that sound. → [[Arlo Parks Vocal Chain]] — Arlo Parks & Baird (Tape Notes), [[TN 183 Arlo Parks & Baird - Full Video Episode]]
- **Another unidentified-model "the Juno" sighting — carrying "most of the sonic information" of an entire track, both the main synth part AND the bass (a doubled/octave version of the same live Juno part, not a separate bass patch).** Jack Antonoff, on Sabrina Carpenter's "Manchild": "most of the sonic information is really just coming from the Juno — so even the bass, the gallop... that's not my bass, my bass is this." The Juno's part is deliberately swung against a straight LinnDrum (see [[LinnDrum]]) for rhythmic tension — "when we were writing the song it was the live Juno and the Linn." → [[LinnDrum]], [[Rhythm and Groove]], [[Sound Selection Guide]] — Mix with the Masters (Jack Antonoff), [[Behind the Track｜'Manchild' by Sabrina Carpenter]]
- **Another unidentified-model "the Juno" sighting (no product/plugin name given on-screen — could equally be a Juno-6/60, see [[Roland Juno-6 - Juno-60|Juno-6/60]]): used as a simple, deliberately non-virtuosic texture layer over a sample, with the same MIDI copied to a second patch purely to audition an alternate flavor.** Young Guru, building a Chairman of the Board sample flip: added a Juno part that "goes along with the sample," played live and kept simple rather than programmed, then copied the identical MIDI onto a second Juno patch/instance ("a different flavor... different Juno patch") specifically to generate an alternate texture option — that second patch ended up muted and unused, which was fine, since the point was optionality during idea generation, not a guaranteed keeper. Balance rule stated for the layer: mixed so it feels like it was present the day the original sample record was made — not loud enough to sit on top of the sample, not so quiet it does nothing. → [[Layering and Stacking]], [[Sound Palette]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]] — Young Guru (Mix with the Masters), [[Taking your ideas to the next level with Young Guru]]
- **No built-in arpeggiator — but a Pro Tools MIDI clock sync produced an unintended polyphonic arp, a full chord fired on every step instead of one note at a time.** The 106 has no onboard arpeggiator; an engineer clock-synced the hardware to Pro Tools to drive it as an external arpeggiator while a player held chords. Because the synth is genuinely polyphonic, each arp step played the WHOLE held chord (arpeggiated up octaves) rather than a single note — "making a chord within each note of the arp," most audible as the filter cutoff opened. Kept purely as a happy-accident texture, not the intended arpeggiator behavior — worth trying deliberately on [[Model 84 Polyphonic Synthesizer]] by clock-syncing an arpeggiator device to a held-chord MIDI clip rather than single notes. → Mix with the Masters (Lola Young, Jared Solomon), [Behind the Track｜'Messy' by Lola Young](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=02QKFlkbmYY)
- **The sole melodic/harmonic voice across an entire 80s synthwave/pop beat — five separate roles from one "Juno emulation" instrument (main pad, bass, second chorus-lift pad, pluck texture, bell counter-melody).** No product name given on-screen, another unidentified-Juno sighting on this channel (see [[Plugin Wishlist]]); owned lane [[Model 84 Polyphonic Synthesizer]]. Recipe repeated across roles: attack/envelope pulled down for a dry start, then chorus + reverb (often heavy) + EQ (lows, sometimes mids) + delay, tailored per role — see [[Synthwave]] and [[Model 84 Polyphonic Synthesizer]] for the full per-layer breakdown. → [[Model 84 Polyphonic Synthesizer]], [[Synthwave]] — loner, [how to make an 80's synthwave/pop beat for the weeknd.](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dWhh27UFWhY)
- **Used purely as a small textural layer, not a lead or bass** — in a Rhodes-led soulful-sample build, "the Juno" is opened specifically to add "just a little textural part," loaded with a preset called "Girl in Africa," filter cutoff brought down a little for less high end, then a simple original melody played over it. A restrained, understated role next to the same build's heavier-processed Jupiter-8 accent (see [[Roland Jupiter-8]]). → [[TEXTURE Slot]], [[Soulful Sample Stacking Chain]], [[Sound Selection Guide]] — loner, [use this easy technique to take your samples to the next level!? (making a soulful sample)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vN0fgGDxp6E)
- **Daft Punk's "melodic percussive plucky synth" credit** — a beat-recreation of The Weeknd's Starboy-era bassline (itself Daft Punk-inspired, from the "Starboy" title track and "I Feel It Coming") attributes the plucky bass-synth element on both Daft Punk songs to the Juno-106, recreated with Arturia's Jun-6 V; owned equivalent [[Model 84 Polyphonic Synthesizer]] (Juno bass patch recipe) covers the same pluck. → [[Model 84 Polyphonic Synthesizer]] — loner, [the production secrets behind the weeknd's evolving sound!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GMeZtNImaT0)
- **Real hardware has a punchier note-onset transient ("click" at the start of each note) than at least one software emulation (TAL)** — called out as a specific, if small, difference; framed as a general pattern with vintage synths having a better attack "entry point" than some software recreations get right. Worth an ear-check on the owned [[Model 84 Polyphonic Synthesizer]] emulation if a patch feels like it's missing bite on fast, percussive parts. → Disclosure, [Faking "That Analog Sound" (15 Tricks)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HEcJ8vHWk88)

- **A repair tech's case for the Juno-106 as an ideal first hardware synth, plus why some vintage synths stay serviceable forever and others become "doorstops."** Recommended over a DX7 as a beginner's first synth specifically because it's directly programmable — every parameter is a dedicated knob/slider with no menu-diving, so cause and effect is immediate, versus FM synthesis's steep, abstract learning curve (a DX7 mostly gets flipped through presets rather than actually programmed by most owners). **The repairability case, generalizable to any gear-buying decision**: the 106 uses through-hole components (large, hand-solderable parts on the board) rather than surface-mount devices, so a failed part can be desoldered and swapped even decades later, and its two most notorious failure points (the 80017A voice chip, and the analog BBD chorus chips) both have modern reproduction replacements on the market. Its firmware lives on a separate ROM chip, so even if the CPU itself dies, the ROM's contents can be read off and burned onto a fresh controller chip — meaning the whole unit is very unlikely to ever become truly unrepairable. **The general buying signal to look for**: synths built up through the mid-1980s tend to share this repairable through-hole design; later gear increasingly uses surface-mount packages that shrink toward physically unsolderable sizes, which is the actual mechanism behind "some vintage synths remain serviceable forever, others eventually can't be fixed by anyone." → [[Model 84 Polyphonic Synthesizer]] (the owned digital emulation, unaffected by any of this hardware-longevity discussion) — Rosen Sound (Rob Rosen), Anthony Marinelli Music, [[Juno 106 Under the Hood w Rob Rosen-Clip]]

## Used on
- Tame Impala — Kevin Parker's Juno-106 is all over *Currents*-era pads and basses (documented in studio interviews) → [[Psych-Pop (Tame Impala)]].
- House music wholesale — the "Juno chord stab" is a genre ingredient from late-80s Chicago/NY house to today → [[House - EDM]].
- Genre/era: 80s synthpop and Italo, 90s house/techno, the entire [[Synthwave]] lane ("Juno chorus on everything" is literally that playbook's first identity ingredient), modern bedroom pop/dream pop ([[Bedroom Pop - Indie]]).
