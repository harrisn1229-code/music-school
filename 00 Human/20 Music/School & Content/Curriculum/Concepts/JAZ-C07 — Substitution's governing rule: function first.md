# JAZ-C07 — Substitution's governing rule: function first

**Q.** State the rule that decides whether one chord can replace another, say which half of it settles ties, and name the failure mode.

> [!question]- Answer
> **Two chords can substitute for each other when they share enough tones and the same harmonic function** — and **function comes first, tones second.** A substitute sharing three notes that resolves the wrong way is a worse choice than one sharing two that resolves right.
>
> The test on top of that is the melody: a substitution must be completely compatible with what it replaces, so one that clashes with the tune is wrong however good the voice leading. **The failure mode is substituting everything** — against plain harmony a substitution reads as sophisticated, wall to wall it reads as directionless.

## Why it matters

This is what makes substitution a set of rules rather than a matter of taste, and it is the rule the rest of the lesson is applications of. Diatonic substitution falls straight out of it: two diatonic chords a third apart share two of their three triad tones, which is why `Cmaj7 Cmaj7 Fmaj7 G7` becomes `Cmaj7 Am7 Fmaj7 G7` — the I–vi–IV–V that half of popular music runs on.

The caution attached is worth as much as the rule: vi is the strongest I-substitute and iii the weakest, because iii lacks the root and contains B, which drags it toward dominant function.

## Drill

Take a loop of yours and replace one I with vi, then with iii. Sing the melody over both and decide which one the tune survives.
