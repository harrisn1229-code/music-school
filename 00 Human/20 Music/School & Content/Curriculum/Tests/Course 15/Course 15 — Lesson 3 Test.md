> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **Rap is a rhythmic performance — you're producing pocket, not pitch**
>
> **Q2.** **At the syllable level — because you comp *performance* by phrase and fix *timing* by syllable**
> They're two different jobs on two different axes. Lesson 1's rule is about which take to use; this one is about where a syllable lands once the take is chosen.
>
> **Q3.** **Punch-ins line by line — and here that's the normal workflow rather than a fallback for a bad take**
> It isn't a repair. It's how the vocal gets built.
>
> **Q4.** **On bar-ends and key phrases, not across the whole verse — the double is an accent**
> Doubling a whole verse flattens it, because a thing that's always on stops marking anything.
>
> **Q5.** **Structural — it fills the space the lead leaves**
> A dedicated pass rather than a few things thrown on at the end. In a genre where the lead leaves gaps by design, what goes in those gaps is part of the arrangement.
>
> **Q6.** **Harder and faster than on a sung vocal — presence over beauty**
> The lyric has to stay parseable over a loud beat, and intelligibility is worth more here than a natural-sounding dynamic curve.
>
> **Q7.** **Keep them — in a dense verse they're the punctuation**
> Strip the breaths out of a fast verse and the words run together with nothing marking the phrase boundaries.
>
> **Q8.** **Judge against the beat, not against a scale — nudge the verse early and late, then commit**
> *Rubric (5):* says the thing being produced is rhythmic placement rather than pitch accuracy (2) · describes duplicating the track and nudging copies early and late by a small amount, on the order of 15 ms (2) · says you play all versions against the beat and commit to one, since the choice is about feel rather than correctness (1)
> In practice it means the decisions that would be pitch decisions on a sung vocal are timing decisions here. You aren't asking whether a note is in tune, you're asking whether the syllable lands where the beat wants it, and the whole apparatus follows: syllable-level edits, punch-ins to get individual lines sitting right, compression chosen for intelligibility rather than tone. Finding the pocket is a concrete experiment rather than a matter of taste applied in the abstract. Duplicate the recorded verse twice, nudge one copy about 15 ms late and the other about 15 ms early, and play all three against the beat. Late reads as relaxed and heavy, early reads as urgent and pushing, and dead-on often reads as neither. None of them is correct — the point is that they're audibly different and the difference is the performance, so you listen, pick the one the track wants, and commit rather than leaving it wherever the take happened to land.
