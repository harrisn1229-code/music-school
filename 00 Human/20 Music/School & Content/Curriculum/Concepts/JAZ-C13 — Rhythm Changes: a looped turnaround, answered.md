# JAZ-C13 — Rhythm Changes: a looped turnaround, answered

**Q.** What happens in bars 1–4 and bars 5–8 of a Rhythm Changes A section, and what makes the pair worth stealing for a verse?

> [!question]- Answer
> Bars 1–4 are **I–vi–ii–V twice** — a turnaround, looped, and nothing else. Bars 5–8 answer it by going to the subdominant and back: **I → I7 → IV → iv → I → V/ii → ii → V**, with the borrowed **iv** (in B♭, `E♭ → E♭m6`) as the moment the section actually goes somewhere.
>
> Four bars of a static loop do not get boring because four bars of real harmonic movement answer them. That question-and-answer structure inside one eight-bar unit is the transferable part.

## Why it matters

It is a masterclass in loop writing, and the answer to why a four-bar loop that is fine on its own gets tiring over sixteen bars: it was never given anything to answer it.

The form is also a substitution playground — tritone subs on the bridge dominants, an interpolated ii in front of every dominant, diminished passing chords in the A section — so it is where Lesson 3's rules get exercised on real repertoire rather than on an abstract turnaround.

## Drill

Write eight bars where the first four are one loop repeated and the second four move to IV, borrow the minor iv, and come home. Then try the same eight bars with the loop repeated four times instead and hear the difference.
