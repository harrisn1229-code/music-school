## Synopsis

Third and final part of Max Konyi's community-question Q&A on ear training, this installment tackles chord- and progression-level hearing specifically: how to teach chord-hearing to students who already have tonic/scale-degree fluency, why combining scale-degree feelings doesn't just add up to the chord's feeling (a genuinely new "meta" experience emerges), why inner voicings are hard to pick apart, how to tell an inverted chord from root position by ear, and how to approach extended chords (9/11/13) once they start to blur together. ## What's taught

- **Teaching chord-hearing to students who already "get" the tonic feeling**: the bridge is explaining that ALL musical feeling states are the same type of experience (tonic, scale degrees, chord qualities, chord positions, inversions) — once a student can feel the tonic, they already understand what "a feeling state" is; the remaining work is just hearing and consciously labeling each new combination (major on the 4, the flat-7 chord, etc.) enough times that it becomes as automatic as the tonic feeling already is.
- **Chords are NOT the sum of their component scale-degree feelings — combining feelings produces a genuinely new, higher-level ("meta") experience**, the same way syllables fuse into a heard word rather than staying separately perceived sounds. Practical implication: don't try to consciously decompose every chord into its individual scale degrees in real time — instead train each chord type/quality/position combination (major-on-4, minor-on-6, dominant-7-on-♭7...) as its own directly-memorized feeling state, the same way single scale degrees get trained.
- **This "meta" stacking is recursive**: individual scale degrees combine into a chord's feeling; chords combine into a progression's feeling; progressions combine into a whole song's feeling — each level is its own holistic gestalt, not reconstructed consciously from its parts (parallel: even a single note is technically a "chord" of harmonic overtones, and nobody consciously parses that either).
- **Hearing inner voices (the notes buried inside a dense chord) fights a natural fusion instinct** — the ear/brain actively integrates simultaneous notes into one gestalt sound (the same mechanism that fuses word syllables or a single note's own harmonics into "one sound"). Pulling a chord apart into individual inner voices works AGAINST that fusion instinct, not with it — it can be trained with enough repetition, but stays effortful compared to top/bottom-note recognition. Practical fallback for a genuinely hard voicing: brute-force it — play it back repeatedly, match individual pitches on the instrument/voice one at a time, then name what was found.
- **Telling root position from an inversion by ear = recognizing the bass note's own scale-degree feeling**, not calculating from a formula. A root-position bass note carries the "1" feeling — solid, landed, "bang, this is it." An inverted bass note carries whatever ITS scale degree's feeling is (a 3rd in the bass reads as "3," a 5th as "5") — since neither matches the landed "1" feeling, you can hear that a chord is inverted before identifying the full chord or even which inversion it is.
- **Extended chords (9/11/13) blur major/minor identity and become their own "pastel," softened meta-experience** rather than reading as [base chord] + [extension] consciously stacked. Minor-11 ≠ minor-7-add-11 ≠ minor-9-no-11 as separately memorized sounds, not calculated from parts. Practical training order: isolate ONE extension type at a time on ONE chord type at a time (major-9, major-add-9, major-6/9, major-9-♯11...) before mixing them — explicitly a years-long vocabulary-building project, not a quick drill.
- **General framing repeated throughout**: this is explicitly a years-long, not weeks-long, process — Konyi is candid that even after extensive training he still regularly hits sounds he can't immediately identify and has to brute-force. Progress is measured by how often that happens dropping over time, not by ever reaching zero.
