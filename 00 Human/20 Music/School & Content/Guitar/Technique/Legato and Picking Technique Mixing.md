# Legato and Picking Technique Mixing

Part of [[Music Theory]]. Covers **why and how to combine** legato (hammer-on/pull-off), slides, alternate picking, and small economy-picking sweeps within a single line, as distinct from [[Sweep Picking Arpeggio Shapes]] (full-shape sweep-picking mechanics) and [[Blues Lead Phrasing and Touch]] (blues-specific touch vocabulary). The core idea: real playing mixes several techniques inside a two-bar line, so technique choice is really **two decisions at once** — a mechanical one (what's easiest/fastest to execute here) and a musical one (which notes should pop and which should recede). Complements [[Solo Dynamics and Arc]]'s "hammer-on/pull-off drilling → controlled dismount" point — that page covers arc-level dynamics; this page is the same legato-is-softer mechanism applied note-by-note.

## Why mix techniques

- **Isolated technique drilling under-prepares you for real playing.** A transcription of a good player's line typically shows several different techniques (legato, slides, picking, small sweeps) inside a single two-bar phrase, not one technical idea run straight through — so an exercise built around only one technique at a time doesn't reflect how it actually gets used.
- **The end state is not thinking about technique while playing** — technique is drilled and internalized separately in the practice room; while actually soloing, attention should go to the music (what's being played, what the band is doing), not to which technique comes next. Combined-technique drilling (below) is what makes that automatic integration possible.

## Two reasons to reach for legato or a slide instead of picking

1. **Right-hand time management.** A hammer-on or pull-off (or a slide) lets you play a note without a pick attack, freeing the picking hand to get positioned for an upcoming string change or shift — not primarily a speed trick, a *coordination* trick. Worked example: a pull-off from A down to G buys the right hand time before the next note (F, on a different string) has to be picked.
2. **Accent/dynamics control.** A legato note (hammered-on or pulled-off) reads softer than a picked note of the same pitch — so technique choice is also a volume/emphasis choice, independent of which notes you play. **Stated rule of thumb: jazz phrase accents mostly land on high notes, off the beat** (not absolute, but a real starting heuristic). Practical application: pick the note you want to pop as the phrase's accent; use legato on the note right before/after it that you want to recede. The same logic run in reverse softens a landing — **sliding into a resolution note (instead of picking it cleanly) de-accents the arrival**, useful when the note just before the resolution sits higher in pitch and you don't want the ear pulled toward it.

## Slides as a dual-purpose device

- **A slide can execute a note and physically relocate the hand at the same time.** Beyond the right-hand-rest/ease-of-execution benefit above, a slide can move you from the hand position needed for one chord's idea straight into the position needed for the next chord's scale — one motion covering both "play this note" and "get in position for what's next," rather than treating position shifts as a separate mechanical problem. Worked example: sliding out of a G minor chord idea directly into position for a C7 altered scale.

## Small two-string sweeps / economy picking

- **Small (two-string) sweeps work as connective tissue between picked accent notes** — a lighter-weight relative of the full sweep-picking shapes on [[Sweep Picking Arpeggio Shapes]]. Same downstroke-based mechanic, applied two strings at a time inside a longer alternate-picked/legato line rather than as a dedicated arpeggio run.
- **Downstrokes function as the accent; swept notes are deliberately softer/slightly muffled** — the picking-hand version of the legato-vs-picked dynamics point above.
- **How muffled is a taste choice, not a correctness rule** — earlier jazz players' small sweeps tended toward near-ghost-notes/muted; keeping the swept notes cleaner and more audible is a legitimate stylistic alternative, not a "more correct" version of the technique.

## Practice progression

1. **Drill each technique in isolation first** (hammer-on/pull-off, slides, economy picking, alternate picking) — the normal starting point, assumed rather than covered here.
2. **Combine legato and alternate picking inside a 3-note-per-string major scale.** Per string: pick, hammer on, pick — then reverse it (hammer on first, pick the rest); also try two picked notes before a hammer-on. *(general starting point — no metronome number given in the source)*
3. **Notice the scale's tonal character changes with the pattern, not just its speed** — worth treating as a usable option in its own right (a way to make a familiar scale *sound* different), not just a side effect of the coordination drill.
4. **Repeat the same 3-note-per-string drill using slides instead of hammer-ons** — different coordination demand, since the fretting hand actually shifts rather than staying in place.
5. **Economy-pick the same 3-note-per-string scale**, then mix that economy-picking with alternate picking the same way steps 2–4 mixed legato and picking — this is the on-ramp into the small two-string sweep technique above.
6. **Stop building more elaborate combined-technique scale drills once the individual pieces feel comfortable — switch to slow improvisation instead.** Deliberately favor a target technique (e.g. slides) while improvising slowly over a simple turnaround, rather than continuing to engineer more complex mixed-technique scale exercises. Framed explicitly as a periodic check-in ("not the kind of thing you need to work on for years"), not a permanent practice-block topic.
7. **Alternative/parallel method: compose lines deliberately using the target technique and notice where it actually earns its place** — sit down, write lines, identify the specific resolutions/string-changes/position-shifts where the technique fits well, rather than only drilling it inside scales.
8. **Alternate picking is the base everything else is built on** — work on straight alternate picking first with any new material, then look for places to substitute in other techniques rather than picking every note by default.

## Worked example — three ii-V-I lines in F, technique breakdown

All three examples: ii–V–I in F, over an altered dominant (see [[The ii-V-I]], [[Altered Chords]]).

- **Line 1 — Bb major 7 arpeggio idea into a passing tone, resolving.** Upstroke hammer-on opens the arpeggio shape; picks up to a passing A (not in the arpeggio — a G sits between); pulls off A→G (buys right-hand time before the string change to F, which is picked); continues with two slides (right-hand rest); resolves by sliding into the landing rather than picking it, since the note just before sits higher and an accented picked resolution would pull the ear the wrong way.
- **Line 2 — cascading descending shapes, downstroke-pull-off-into-two-string-sweep, repeated.** Overall shape: several small downward movements, each followed by a move back up, an overall cascading descent. First gesture: downstroke, pull-off, two-string sweep. Second: downstroke, two-string sweep, downstroke. On the C7: downstroke pull-off, upstroke, then a shape derived from a Db minor-major arpeggio executed downstroke-upstroke-upstroke (the small-sweep/economy-picking idea above), running down the scale, resolving with another slide.
- **Line 3 — slides doing double duty (phrasing + position shift), the most technique-dense of the three.** Two picked notes open into a 16th-note-triplet "trill" feel via a slide up; goes legato (hammer-on, pull-off); runs up the scale with alternate picking; slides from C up to Db to shift into position for the next phrase (the G minor → C7 altered position shift described above) — nearly the same melodic shape as line 1, now over G minor. On the top note (rhythmically the "and" of beat 3), the pull-off both de-accents the note and buys execution time; resolves back down to A.

- **The fretting thumb should track the fingers across strings during a legato run, not stay locked in one spot — and doing so structurally enforces a light grip.** Thumb positioned behind the middle finger, sliding along as the hand moves from the low E string toward the high E string on a full-fretboard legato scale. Two benefits: consistency (same wrist angle/finger feel on every string, where a locked thumb forces a different angle per string) and grip discipline (a tight grip physically can't let the thumb travel, so the motion itself prevents over-gripping). The same light-grip payoff transfers to sweep-picking shapes even though the thumb usually stays more fixed there. Practice progression: a straight 3-note-per-string scale (worked example: G harmonic minor) ascending/descending focused purely on thumb tracking, then short triplet bursts added in, then practical scale-based phrases run through the full scale rather than straight up/down. Named failure mode: the temptation to re-tighten the grip under a fast/demanding passage, which is exactly where the benefit matters most. — BERNTH, [[TECHNIQUE MISTAKES 89% of Guitarists make & how to fix them!]]

- **Drill shape-to-shape legato transitions deliberately, not just one fixed position.** Most fretting-hand legato exercises sit in a single scale/arpeggio shape; the harder and more realistic drill is connecting two shapes so the transition between them disappears rather than sounding like two separate ideas stitched together. Also: keep the wrist angle **constant** across a multi-string legato run — rotating the wrist per string produces inconsistent results take to take (works "by accident" some passes, fails others), where moving the whole hand up/down with a fixed wrist angle is repeatable. — BERNTH, [[6 Steps To Amazing LEGATO Technique! - Guitar Exercises + Tabs]]
- **Hybrid picking (pick + fingers combined) as a fix for legato's per-string accent problem, credited to Tom Quayle.** Pure legato commonly places an unintentional heavy accent on the first (picked/plucked) note of each new string, with the rest of that string's notes hammered/pulled quietly by comparison — audibly uneven dynamics and timing, since only the first note per string reads clearly. Hybrid picking (combining a pick with fingerstyle plucking on other strings) smooths the string-to-string transition so the attack doesn't spike on string changes, putting the focus back on timing/phrasing/dynamics of each individual note rather than on one loud attack per string.
