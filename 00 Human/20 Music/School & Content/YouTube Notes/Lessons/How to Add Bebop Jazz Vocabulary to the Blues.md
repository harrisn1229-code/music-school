# How to Add Bebop Jazz Vocabulary to the Blues

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7_XBd3rOlnI)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7_XBd3rOlnI)

## Synopsis

Jack Ruch teaches three specific bebop saxophone lines — one each from Cannonball Adderley ("Spontaneous Combustion," off *Live in San Francisco*, 1959), Charlie Parker ("Billy's Bounce"), and Jimmy Forrest (the title track of *Black Forest*) — transposed from their original horn recordings onto guitar, each mapped to a different structural chunk of a jazz-blues 12-bar form (transposed to the key of C for the lesson): the I→IV transition at the top of the form (Adderley), the IV chord sliding back to I and on into the VI7 (Parker), and the VI7–ii–V–I turnaround (Forrest). He then stitches all three together into one continuous 12-bar chorus, closing the loop between isolated vocabulary and a real solo. This is the same channel and the same underlying jazz-blues form already documented from [[The Key To Understanding A Jazz Blues]] (a single Ruch-composed line over the 1-6-2-5) and [[How I Approach Practicing]] (the "transcribe → chunk → name the concept → build your own solo" method) — this video sits between the two: unlike the concept-extraction method, these three licks are taught essentially **verbatim** (transcribed from real horn recordings, not reduced to a reusable "concept"), and the pedagogical point is explicitly the practice of "transcribe different players, then pull the solos apart and combine them with other things you know" — using borrowed vocabulary as raw material for a personal solo rather than analyzing it down to an abstraction first. The most valuable transferable material: horn players lean much harder on the major 7th as a chromatic color inside a blues than guitarists typically do, on downward chromatic runs through altered tensions to resolve a dominant chord, and on superimposing a simple triad (relative to the underlying dominant chord) to generate extensions without thinking in scale-degree numbers.

## What's taught

- **Lick 1 (Cannonball Adderley, "Spontaneous Combustion," *Live in San Francisco*, 1959) — over the I→IV transition (C7/C9 → F7/F9 in the lesson's key of C; B♭ up-tempo blues in the original).** Opens with a classic **diatonic-above/chromatic-below enclosure** targeting the root of C: 9th (D) → root (C) → major 7th (B) — encircling the root with a whole step above and a half step below, matching the exact enclosure shape already catalogued on [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §2. Notably **not afraid to use the major 7th over a dominant/blues chord**, treated purely as a chromatic passing color rather than a "wrong" note — a device the video calls out explicitly as common bebop phrasing, distinct from how guitarists usually approach the same chord. Continues by sliding up through the **3rd and 5th** (plain chord-tone highlighting) before returning to the 9th, with a deliberate pause for phrasing space. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]], [[Guide Tones]]
- **Lick 1, second half — the altered-tension chromatic descent into the IV chord.** Over the same C7, before resolving to F: **♭13 → half-step hammer-on/pull-off trill → 3rd (of the target F chord) → ♭9 (D♭)** — a stepwise descending line through two altered tensions that lands the arrival chord tone (the 3rd of F) *before* the ♭9, then uses the ♭9 as a closing color rather than the final landing note. Explicit reasoning given: a ♭9 a half step above the root "can seem like a wrong note" in isolation but reads as intentional tension-into-resolution once it's placed inside a line moving toward the IV. Mid-descent the line also dips to the plain **5th**, described as "bouncing around between intervals and hitting low notes" — a named, reusable bebop phrasing habit (interval-jumping rather than pure stepwise motion), not tied to this one lick. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]], [[Altered Chords]], [[Turnarounds]]
- **Lick 2 (Charlie Parker, "Billy's Bounce") — over the IV chord, resolving back toward I and on into the VI7 (the top of a jazz blues's 1-6-2-5).** Opens on the root (high E string) then drops to the **♭5** — called out as "a great bluesy note" — establishing the bebop-over-blues habit of hitting a genuine blue note before anything more harmonically dense. Continues down a **bebop-dominant-flavored run**: 9th → root → ♭7 → natural 6 (major 6), i.e. outlining the dominant scale with the same passing-tone logic as [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §1's "Bebop dominant" row (passing tone between ♭7 and root), followed by a 4→9→2 approach shape. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]], [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]]
- **Lick 2, chromatic run into the VI7 chord.** A run **♭3 → 4 → ♭5 → 5** (hammer-on/pull-off into the natural 5th) transitioning into landing the **9th and root of the VI7 chord** on arrival — a chromatic connector shape (not a fixed passing tone, just a run through adjacent half-steps) used specifically to bridge into a chord change, with the explicit teaching point that this only works because the player is **anticipating the coming chord change and playing into it rather than reacting to it once it arrives** — i.e. hearing the target chord tones before the bar line, not after. → [[Guide Tones]], [[Turnarounds]]
- **Lick 3 (Jimmy Forrest, title track of *Black Forest*) — over the VI7–ii–V–I turnaround (the jazz-blues "1-6-2-5"), the most harmonically dense of the three.** Over the VI7 (A7, target chord D): hammer-on/pull-off/slide targeting the **3rd**, then straight down the chord tones **3rd → 5th → ♭7th**, then the **♭9 (A♭, a half step above A7's root)**. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]], [[Altered Chords]]
- **A7 (the VI7) named out loud as a secondary dominant — V/ii — with the explicit generalization that the same altered-tension vocabulary (♭13, ♯9, ♭9) usable over any V7→I resolution works identically over it, because the interval relationship is identical: "C7 moving to F is the same relationship as A7 moving to D."** This is a stated, worked confirmation of [[Secondary Dominants]]'s core claim, applied specifically to the jazz-blues 1-6-2-5's VI7 rather than a generic example — the same altered vocabulary from Lick 1's I→IV transition (♭13/♭9) is explicitly reused here on VI7→ii for the same structural reason. → [[Secondary Dominants]], [[Turnarounds]], [[Bebop Vocabulary]]
- **Over the ii chord (D, played here as a "two-minor or two-dominant" — the video notes both are common in a blues): fall to the major 3rd on the downbeat of the chord change** — a direct application of [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §4's "target the guide tones" rule, stated as a landing point rather than decoration.
- **Superimposing a minor triad a 5th above the underlying dominant root to generate extensions without arpeggiating the dominant chord directly.** Still over the D chord (read here functionally as D7), the line plays an **A minor triad** (A–C–E) — which, against D7, spells the **5th, ♭7th, and 9th** — i.e. a simple, easy-to-grab triad shape used as a shortcut to a dominant-9 sound instead of thinking "5, ♭7, 9" as separate scale degrees. New device for the vault — closest existing relative is [[Upper Structure Chords]]'s bass-note-relationship framework, but this is a **melodic** (single-line, not voiced) application of the same "borrow a simple triad shape to imply a denser chord" logic. → [[Altered Chords]], [[Upper Structure Chords]], [[Guide Tones]]
- **Interval-jumping/bouncing phrasing (same device as Lick 1) reused into the V chord, landing the ♭7th and 5th**, followed by a **C major 7 arpeggio** back over what functions as the I chord — again, deliberately using the major 7th inside a blues resolution rather than a dominant 7th, reinforcing Lick 1's point that bebop phrasing re-harmonizes the "home" chord of a blues as a major 7 sound rather than treating the dominant 7th as mandatory. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]], [[Blues Form and Harmony]]
- **The overarching practice method, stated directly as the reason to learn licks this way: "transcribe different players, then pull the solos apart and combine them with other things you know, to create new ways of navigating through the chord changes."** Unlike [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]]'s method (reduce a transcription to a named, tune-agnostic "concept" before reusing it), this video's approach keeps the licks closer to verbatim — each lick is taught as an actual transcribed phrase, mapped onto a specific structural slot of the form (I→IV / IV→VI7 / VI7-ii-V-I), and the payoff demonstrated live is **stitching multiple transcribed phrases from different sources into one continuous chorus**, seamlessly transitioning between them — a complementary, more literal alternative to concept-naming, useful especially early on before enough vocabulary exists to abstract into concepts. → [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]]
- **Full 12-bar demonstration.** All three licks played back to back over their matching sections of a 12-bar jazz-blues chorus in C, transitioning seamlessly between them — the direct proof of the stitching method above.
