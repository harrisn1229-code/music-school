# Word Painting

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Musical Form]]. **Word painting** (also called *madrigalism*, from its roots in Renaissance madrigals) is when the meaning of a lyric is directly reflected in the music itself — melody, harmony, rhythm, arrangement, or production. Ranges from the obvious (a high note on the word "high") to genuinely subtle, structural choices.

## By device

- **Melody follows the lyric's literal meaning** — a rising melody on "high"/"up," a falling melody on "low"/"down." Some songs use both directions in one line ("Ain't No Mountain High Enough").
- **Chords/harmony follow the lyric** — Cole Porter's "Every Time We Say Goodbye": the progression moves from major to the parallel minor exactly as the lyric sings "how strange the change from major to minor." "Hallelujah" (Leonard Cohen) — the first verse's lyric literally names the chords being played ("the fourth, the fifth, the minor fall, the major lift").
- **Lyrics name the actual notes/chords being played** — "ABC" (Jackson 5): sung note names match the notes/chords underneath (lyric "E" sung on E♭, lyric "C" sung on C; the "A-B-C" chorus lyric matches A♭–B♭m7–A♭/C). "Do-Re-Mi" (*The Sound of Music*) — sings solfège syllables on their corresponding major-scale degrees, in-universe framed as Maria teaching the children to sing. "These Words" (Natasha Bedingfield) — opening lyric describes the chords being played (Dm, C/E, F) underneath a song about a songwriter's writer's block, adding self-aware meta-commentary. "505" (Arctic Monkeys) — as Alex Turner sings "semitone," the chord roots underneath actually ascend by semitones. "Minor Thing" (RHCP) — as the lyric sings "C to D," the chords shift from C♯m to D (the lyric says "key" but it's technically a chord change, likely for the rhyme).
- **Tempo/rhythm follows the lyric** — "Despacito" ("slowly," in Spanish) — the tempo audibly slows during that word.
- **Percussion illustrates the lyric** — "Hallelujah, I Love Her So" (Ray Charles) — snare mimics a knock instead of/alongside the word "knock." "Knock on Wood" (Eddie Floyd) — four snare hits follow the word "knock." "Rocky Raccoon" (Beatles) — a snare hit represents a gunshot.
- **Meter/time signature shift illustrates the lyric** — "Being for the Benefit of Mr. Kite!" (Beatles) — switches from 4/4 to 3/4 waltz time exactly as the lyric describes "Henry the Horse dances the waltz."
- **Instrumental arrangement mirrors the narrative, between lyric lines** — "She's Leaving Home" (Beatles, string arrangement by Mike Leander): a descending string line follows "she goes downstairs to the kitchen"; an alarmed triplet rhythm follows "she breaks down and cries."
- **Production/effects illustrate the lyric** — "Everything I Wanted" (Billie Eilish) — an EQ makes the vocal sound muffled/underwater on the matching lyric. "Killer Queen" (Queen) — vocals through a flanger on "laser beam," mimicking the sound. "Bohemian Rhapsody" (Queen) — a shivering chime effect on "send shivers down my spine." "Don't Stop Me Now" (Queen) — the vocal climbs toward the root note building tension into the word "explode," releasing on it.
- **Recurring sound-effect motifs**: ticking-clock rhythm devices for urgency/time — "Clockwork Creep" (10cc, a song about a time bomb — ticking clock as a literal percussion part, plus piano octaves mimicking ticking); "Hung Up" (Madonna) — a ticking clock keeps the pulse alongside "time goes by so slowly." Heartbeat rhythm — "The Heart of Rock & Roll" (Huey Lewis and the News) — drums mimic a heartbeat on "the heart of rock and roll is still beating."
- **Structural/large-scale word painting, describing the SONG'S larger theme rather than one lyric moment** — "Riders on the Storm" (The Doors): keyboardist Ray Manzarek imagined his classic-rock-organ line as "sounding like raindrops," and the band added thunder/rain sound effects to match, per Manzarek's own account of the songwriting session. "Somewhere Over the Rainbow" (*The Wizard of Oz*) — the melody opens with a big octave leap away from "home," then spends the rest of the tune winding back down to the starting note — mirroring Dorothy's journey away from Kansas and the "there's no place like home" theme; the video argues this isn't overreading given the song was written explicitly for the film, the same way a film composer writes with the characters/story in mind.
- **Comic/novelty word painting** — "F10 da" (Ben Folds) hides a crude lyric by exploiting the letter-names of notes (A, D, F, C...), explicitly for comic/educational effect per the songwriter's own account (quoted: wanting an "educational angle" so kids learn scale degrees and "bad language" at once). "I Bought a Flat-Guitar Tutor" (10cc) matches CHORDS (not just notes) to a similarly playful lyric about note names.
