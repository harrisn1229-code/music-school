# Species Counterpoint

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Part-Writing]]. **Counterpoint** is the combination of two or more melodies, each as important and interesting as the others. **Species counterpoint** is Johann Joseph Fux's 1725 pedagogical system (*Gradus ad Parnassum*) for learning it step by step, in 5 increasingly complex "species" — explicitly modeled on Fux's own (secondhand) understanding of **Palestrina's** Renaissance style, not the Baroque language Fux actually wrote in himself. Praised/used by Mozart, Beethoven, and Haydn as a teaching method regardless.

All species are written against a given fixed melody, the **cantus firmus**, which always starts and ends on its own tonic.

## General melodic rules (apply to every species)
- Primarily **stepwise** motion, with a single clear high or low point, approached and left gradually.
- **Never repeat a note.**
- Occasional leaps are fine but restricted: capped at a perfect 5th, except an octave leap or an upward minor 6th.
- **Never leap a tritone**, and never outline one melodically either (the *diabolus in musica* — "devil in music").
- A leap of a 4th or larger must be **counterbalanced by stepwise motion in the opposite direction** — on both sides of the leap if it's bigger than a perfect 4th.
- Never write 3+ leaps in a row; 2 leaps in a row must outline a major or minor triad (never diminished — it contains a tritone).

## The five species

| Species | Ratio (counterpoint : cantus firmus) | New rule this species adds |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | 1:1 | Only consonant intervals (no 2nds/7ths/4ths); voices can't cross/overlap; ≤3 consecutive 3rds/6ths in a row; perfect intervals (P1/P5/P8) only on first/last note, never repeated, approached by contrary motion + ≥1 voice by step; start and end on a perfect interval with the tonic in the lower voice. |
| **II** | 2:1 | Leaps fine, but never to a new high point on a downbeat; dissonance allowed only on weak beats, only as a passing tone; unisons still only on first/last note; perfect-interval approach rule still applies on downbeats, PLUS never repeat the same perfect interval on two consecutive downbeats (**parallel perfect intervals**). |
| **III** | 4:1 | Never leap more than once in the same direction; leaps >3rd need steps counterbalancing both sides; beat 1 must be consonant, beat 3 can be the one dissonant note in the measure, beats 2/4 dissonant only via passing/neighbor tones. Two named exceptions: **double neighbor tone** (upper+lower neighbor in sequence back to the origin note) and **nota cambiata** (step down, 3rd down, 2 steps up — a 5-note figure whose middle note must stay consonant). |
| **IV** | offset/syncopated | Dissonances now allowed ON downbeats, but only as **suspensions** ([[Non-Harmonic Tones]]) — held over from the previous beat, resolving DOWN by step, never any other direction. Labeled 7-6 or 4-3 above the cantus firmus, 2-3 below it (the only suspension allowed below). Never repeat the same suspension type 3+ times in a row. When nothing legal works, "**break species**" — drop the tie and slip into Species II rhythm briefly rather than force an illegal suspension (should be rare). |
| **V** | florid — freely mixed | Combine all previous species' note values within one exercise; don't sit on one note value too long; whichever species' rules apply to whatever note value is currently sounding; save whole notes for the very end (all-Species-I in the middle is "boring"); Species III+IV combine via dotted half notes; paired eighth notes only on weak beats, approached/resolved by step, max one pair per measure. |

## Three-voice counterpoint (Species I)
Adding a 3rd voice bridges toward Bach's full 4-voice chorale style ([[Part-Writing]]):
- Outer-to-bass intervals must stay consonant; the **upper two voices against each other CAN be dissonant**, even a tritone.
- Resulting sonorities should be triads: root position + 1st inversion for major/minor, **diminished triads in 1st inversion only** (matches the doubling logic in [[Part-Writing]]).
- Incomplete triads (doubled root + 3rd, no 5th) are fine occasionally; avoid open 5ths except on the first/last chord.
- Parallel perfect intervals still forbidden between ANY pair of voices.
- Perfect intervals can now *also* be approached by **similar motion** (not just contrary) if the top voice moves by step and the third voice moves in contrary motion to the others.

## From videos

- **A direct classical-to-pop counterpoint lift, then a worked expansion from 2 voices to 4.** McCartney and Harrison's teenage arrangement of Bach's Bourrée in E minor (pure 2-voice counterpoint — melody on top, independent bass line underneath, harmony emerging only from how the two lines relate, no chordal support) became the basis for "Blackbird." McCartney's extension technique: keep the borrowed top line and bass line, add a third guitar voice, and add a fourth (his own sung melody) — critically, one of the added voices is a **pedal tone on the root note**, whose specific job is gluing the outer voices together harmonically now that there are more of them to reconcile. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs Inspired By Bach]]
- **Pop/rock counterpoint usage, contrasted against homophony (the texture most pop/rock actually uses) — a survey of how the technique shows up outside a classical setting, plus a fugue explainer.** Real pop counterpoint is usually "diluted" by an underlying chordal accompaniment rather than pure (demonstrated on "God Only Knows," Beach Boys — the vocal counterpoint stripped of its chordal support becomes far more exposed). **Most common pop-song device: introduce two melodies separately earlier in the song, then layer them together later** — "For No One" (Beatles, verse melody + French horn line), "Supermassive Black Hole" (Muse, one of the two layered melodies is itself a 4-part homophonic vocal harmony that still reads as one voice against the other). **Escalating layered counterpoint**: "Paranoid Android" (Radiohead) middle section builds 2→4 simultaneous vocal lines; "Country House" (Blur) adds one new counter-melody per chorus. **Textual/dramatic use**: Simon & Garfunkel's "Scarborough Fair/Canticle" weaves an anti-war lyric into the traditional tune for deliberate irony; "One Day More" (*Les Misérables*) builds dense multi-character counterpoint that suddenly resolves to unison on one shared line, dramatizing what the characters have in common. **Fugue**: one voice states a subject, a second voice enters with the same subject while the first continues independently, more voices progressively join (worked on Bach's Fugue in G minor, 4 voices) — described as "a much more complicated round." - **The parallel-perfect-intervals prohibition, reframed from a guitarist's perspective, plus its modern rehabilitation and a practical guitar use case.** Parallel 5ths/4ths (the same interval shape sliding across different roots) trace to medieval music, were formally prohibited starting in 14th-century counterpoint pedagogy (matching this page's "parallel perfect intervals still forbidden" rule above), fell out of favor for centuries, and were rehabilitated with full compositional consent starting in the early 20th century — now common throughout Western music in every style. **Modern guitar-specific application:** doubling a riff in parallel 4ths or 5ths produces a thicker, darker sound — a direct, practical use of the historically-taboo device. — QJamTracks, [[INTERVALS for Guitar –crystal clear and ultimate guide]]
