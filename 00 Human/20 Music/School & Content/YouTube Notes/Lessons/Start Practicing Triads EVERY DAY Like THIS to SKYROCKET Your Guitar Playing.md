# Start Practicing Triads EVERY DAY Like THIS to SKYROCKET Your Guitar Playing

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MvUgoNBTNjA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MvUgoNBTNjA)

## Synopsis
Daniel Seriff's follow-up triad lesson — explicitly pitched as "slightly more intermediate" than his earlier, more basic triad-fundamentals video ([[Copy This Triad Practice Routine, It'll 10X Your Guitar Playing]]), which he recommends watching first for the plain shapes. Where that earlier video was about *finding* all seven diatonic triads of a key inside one hand position, this one assumes you already have the shapes and teaches how to **decorate** them — take an average-sounding triad arpeggio and dress it up so it reads as a real musical phrase instead of a shape drill. Three parts, all worked in the key of G, starting from the first-inversion triad shapes rooted around the 3rd fret of the high E string: part one reviews the plain diatonic run and adds two simple ornaments (a repeating picking pattern, and chromatic slide-ins from a half or whole step below); part two — his favorite and the video's core idea — decorates a triad using notes borrowed from the *next* diatonic triad above it (equivalently, notes from the parent major scale); part three applies all of it to a real chord progression (G–D–Am–D–C, in the style of "Knocking on Heaven's Door"), voice-led by always grabbing the closest available triad shape to the previous one rather than jumping around the neck. The throughline is that none of this needs new theory — it's the same plain triads from the earlier lesson, made musical through a small, repeatable set of embellishment moves.

## What's taught
- **Quick theory recap (same formula as the earlier lesson):** any major key's seven diatonic triads reduce to three qualities — major, minor, diminished — built by stacking every other scale tone (stacking thirds). The fixed pattern by scale degree: I major, ii minor, iii minor, IV major, V major, vi minor, vii° diminished (rare in popular music). Worked in G: G, Am, Bm, C, D, Em, F♯dim, back to G. Assumes the player already knows the major scale's whole-whole-half-whole-whole-whole-half construction and its 7 notes.
- **Part 1 — the plain diatonic run, first-inversion shapes, one hand position:** starting with the triad rooted on the **3rd fret of the high E string (G)**, these are **1st-inversion** triads (the 3rd of the chord is the lowest-pitched note of each shape) — the same 5-fret single-position voice-leading logic documented on [[Chord Inversions]] from the earlier video, here specifically the 1st-inversion zone. Full sequence played through: G major → A minor → B minor → C major → D major → E minor → F♯ diminished → back to G major, all without shifting to a different inversion shape.
- **Decoration 1 — a repeating picking pattern.** The simplest ornament: instead of strumming or block-arpeggiating each triad the same way, apply one consistent picking pattern and repeat it identically through every chord of the diatonic run. Flagged as something easy to overlook but genuinely musical on its own.
- **Decoration 2 — chromatic slide-in from below.** Slide the *entire* triad arpeggio up into place starting from a half step below the target chord; a whole step (two half steps) below also works and is described as sounding "really nice" too. Variant: instead of sliding the whole shape, take just **one note of the triad** — highest, lowest, or middle, "doesn't have to be the highest or lowest note" — a half step below and slide only that note in while the rest of the triad stays put, then arpeggiate normally. Framed explicitly as an experimentation exercise, not a "get it right" drill: "you're not aiming for perfect... it's okay if you hit some clams."
- **Decoration 2, extended by string/note choice on the guitar:** on the G-major shape specifically, three different single-note slide targets were demonstrated — the note on the high E string, the note on the B string, and the note on the G string — each producing a noticeably different color, reinforcing that any one of the three chord tones is a valid slide-in target, not just the top or bottom.
- **Decoration 3, his favorite (Part 2) — embellish a triad using notes from the *next* diatonic triad up.** Sitting on the G major triad (I), the next chord in the diatonic sequence is A minor (ii); every note of the A minor triad becomes a legal decoration target for the G triad currently being played. Three worked hammer-on moves off G major: (1) hammer from **G** (root, high E string) up to **A** (in the Am triad) and back; (2) hammer from **D** (B string) up to **E** (also in Am); (3) hammer from **B** (G string) up to **C** (also in Am). Any of the three can be used alone or mixed/combined together within the same pass through the chord.
- **The generalization behind decoration 3:** because the "next triad up" is itself built from the parent major scale, decorating with the next-chord's tones is the *same move* as decorating with plain major-scale tones around the current triad — two framings of one technique. Explicitly flagged that the "obvious" choice (the next-chord tone right above the current note) isn't always the best musical option — "this is where taste and your ear come into play," i.e. treat the next-triad's notes as a *menu* to audition, not a formula to apply mechanically. Also notes embellishing tones can come from *below* the current shape too, not just above, which is what part three explores.
- **Part 3 — applying the decorations to a real progression, voice-led by proximity.** Progression: **G – D – A minor – D – C** (a common vamp, cited as "in the style of" Knocking on Heaven's Door) — chosen as a stand-in for any similar progression the technique generalizes to. Voice-leading rule stated directly: rather than picking one fixed inversion/shape and sliding the *whole hand* up and down the neck for every chord (which he calls "a cool effect" but not the goal here), find the **closest available triad shape to the chord you're already on** for each change — minimizing hand movement chord to chord, the same "least movement wins" voice-leading principle already documented on [[Chord Voicings]] and [[Chord Inversions]] from Joe Pass and other sources, here applied specifically across triad shapes rather than fuller chord voicings.
- **Worked voicing choices for the G–D–Am–D–C progression:** G at the established 3rd-fret-high-E first-inversion shape; D as "our classic D shape" nearby (exact fret not stated in captions); A minor up at the **5th fret**; then back through the same D and G shapes; C voiced at what the transcript captures as **"5-5-3"** (fret numbers, exact string mapping not stated) — i.e. every chord change is answered with whichever nearby triad shape requires the least jump, not a re-derivation from scratch.
- **Embellishment pass over the progression:** once the bare closest-triad voicings are set, the same decoration moves from parts 1–2 (picking pattern, chromatic slide-in, next-chord-tone hammer-ons, and — new here — grabbing surrounding scale tones from *below* each chord as well as above) get layered on top, played against a backing track. Framed as the payoff of the whole lesson: bare functional voice leading first, musical decoration second.
- **Recommended prerequisite/next step:** review all three triad inversion shapes (major, minor, diminished) on the top three strings first — pointing at his earlier, more basic lesson ([[Copy This Triad Practice Routine, It'll 10X Your Guitar Playing]]) for that foundational shape drill — then apply this video's decoration techniques to the same G–D–Am–D–C progression using the *other* two inversion positions up the neck as further practice, beyond just the first-inversion position worked here.
