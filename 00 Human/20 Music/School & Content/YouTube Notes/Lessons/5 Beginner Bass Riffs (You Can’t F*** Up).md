# 5 Beginner Bass Riffs (You Can’t F*** Up)

## Synopsis

A BassBuzz beginner-repertoire video that solves a specific first-song problem — pick something too hard and you get discouraged, pick something too easy and it's boring — by curating five real, recognizable riffs, each one deliberately chosen to drill a *different* skill rather than just being five isolated tabs to memorize. The five, in order of skill targeted: **I Love Rock and Roll** (Joan Jett, bass by Gary Ryan) for alternating plucking and simple root-note restraint; **Seven Nation Army** (The White Stripes) for hand-shifting via a "look ahead with your eyes" trick; **Something in the Way** (Nirvana, bass by Krist Novoselic) for micro-shifting through an awkward stretch plus note-length/sustain control, and — uniquely among the five — a riff that covers the *entire* song, not just a chorus excerpt; **Zombie** (The Cranberries, bass by Mike Hogan) for stamina/endurance via non-stop chugging that also mixes open strings with fretted notes; and **Under Pressure** (Queen & David Bowie, bass by John Deacon) for a "rhythm mnemonic" hack — inventing a nonsense phrase whose natural speech rhythm matches a syncopated riff — as a way to *feel* a hard rhythm correctly before you can count it formally. Each riff gets real artist/song context (who actually played it, why the choice works as a bassline, any tuning quirks) rather than being presented as a bare tab. ## What's taught

- **[~0:00–0:49] Framing: the first-song problem, and how these five were chosen.** Picking a first song too hard → discouragement; too easy/lame → also discouragement. The five riffs are explicitly picked to sit in that sweet spot *and* to each build a different underlying skill (fretting, plucking, rhythm, shifting, endurance) rather than being five unrelated party tricks.

- **[~0:49–2:17] Riff 1 — I Love Rock and Roll (Joan Jett; bass: Gary Ryan) — alternating plucking + restraint.**
  - **The bassist's-philosophy tip (stated as general, not song-specific):** most good, working basslines are *not* flashy — the ones that "make the most money" often just take the guitar part and simplify it down to big, beefy root notes with lots of space around them, leaving room for the guitar/vocal to shine. Framed bluntly: play all the fancy guitar stuff on bass and you'd be distracting from the song (and "probably get fired").
  - **Fretting:** pinky or ring finger on the 7th-fret notes, index finger on the 5th fret — simple one-position playing.
  - **Plucking:** alternating index-middle plucking with a rest built in — pluck index, pluck middle, take a rest, repeat — called out explicitly as great alternating-plucking-technique practice.
  - **Rhythm quirk:** the final bar of the riff has only **three beats**, not four — sounds odd in isolation but is there because the riff follows the vocal phrasing, not a fixed bar-length habit.

- **[~2:17–5:04] Riff 2 — Seven Nation Army (The White Stripes) — hand-shifting via look-ahead.**
  - Framing: once you can plant your hand in one position and play a few notes (riff 1's skill), the next beginner failure mode is *moving/shifting* the hand — it's easy to stumble and get lost mid-shift. This riff is chosen specifically to drill that.
  - Context: the riff is famously guitar (Jack White, on a weirdly-tuned guitar), not bass — no bassist "originally" played it. BassBuzz picks a specific adapted fingering (of several possible ways people play it on bass) because it's playable, gets a good tone, *and* gives real shifting practice.
  - **Fingering:** pinky, pinky, index, pinky, index, **shift**, middle, index.
  - **The core shifting trick — look ahead with your eyes:** while playing the 5th-fret/7th-fret/5th-fret part of the phrase, look at the **3rd fret** (the shift target) *before* your hand gets there, then simply "plop" the middle finger down where you were already looking. Immediately after playing the index note that follows, start looking *back* at the 7th fret so your eyes are already prepped for the next repeat of the loop. The general principle: let your eyes lead the shift instead of relying on the hand to find the target blind. → new note: [[Look-Ahead Position Shifting]].
  - **Rhythm — "vibe it out" rather than count it:** the riff's rhythm sits ambiguously between a triplet feel and a 16th-note feel ("it's actually kind of both and in betweeny"). Explicit advice: don't try to formally count it — listen and feel your way to matching the recording instead. Claimed kid-tested: taught successfully to a 9-year-old.

- **[~5:04–6:51] Riff 3 — Something in the Way (Nirvana; bass: Krist Novoselic) — micro-shifting a stretch, note length, and a whole-song riff.**
  - **Uniquely among the five riffs, this one covers the entire song**, not just a chorus/intro excerpt: Novoselic drops out entirely during the verses and plays this same 4-bar looping riff only in the choruses, so learning the one riff is enough to play all the way through the song.
  - Context: recorded in a low drop-D♭ tuning to match Kurt Cobain's guitar, but the same pitches are playable on a standard-tuned bass with an adjusted fingering (the fingering below is the standard-tuning adaptation).
  - **Fretting:** simple quarter notes — index, pinky, pinky, pinky, index, pinky, pinky, index.
  - **The hard part — the 1st-to-4th-fret stretch:** flagged as the one thing that can be "a bit of a killer" even though the notes themselves are simple. **Fix: micro-shift as much as needed** — keep using index and pinky, but *scoot the whole hand* along the neck between notes instead of holding a fixed, wide stretch shape. → reinforces [[Fretting-Hand Strength, Stretch, Stamina, and Finger Control]] Exercise 2.
  - **The "gold star" refinement — note length:** get the notes **as long and connected as possible** (deliberate sustain/legato) rather than short and choppy — explicitly called out as the difference between sounding right and sounding thin/disconnected on this riff. → cross-links [[Bass Note Muting Technique]] as the deliberate opposite skill (controlling *silence* vs. controlling *sustain*).

- **[~6:51–8:30] Riff 4 — Zombie (The Cranberries; bass: Mike Hogan) — stamina/endurance and open-string/fretted-note coordination.**
  - Framing: riff 3's forgiveness (long rests during the verses) doesn't generalize — most songs don't give you that luxury, and a beginner's hands slow down and burn out over a full 3-4 minute song even if a short riff is technically playable. This riff is chosen specifically to build stamina.
  - **What builds the endurance:** the riff is non-stop — no rests — chugging **index-middle-index-middle** continuously all the way through with no breaks.
  - **Fretting (per repeating phrase):** open string → index on 3rd fret → index on 3rd fret → pinky on 5th fret → (repeat) → shift down a fret → index on 2nd fret → middle on 3rd fret.
  - **Second skill baked in — coordination:** mixing open strings with fretted notes (not just fretted-to-fretted transitions) is called out as its own coordination challenge, distinct from the stamina piece.
  - **Beginner difficulty ramp — a simplification fallback:** if the full fretting pattern is too hard at first, just repeat the first note of each bar all the way through instead — explicitly endorsed as sounding "okay" as a stepping-stone version rather than something to avoid.
  - → reinforces [[Fretting-Hand Strength, Stretch, Stamina, and Finger Control]] Exercise 3 (stamina), which already documents the general "reuse a stretch's pattern, add continuous plucking-hand reps" method — this riff is a real-song application of it.

- **[~8:30–10:18] Riff 5 — Under Pressure (Queen & David Bowie; bass: John Deacon) — rhythm mnemonics for a syncopated feel.**
  - Framing: many iconic riffs have genuinely complex, hard-to-count rhythms that "trip you up." John Deacon's playing (including this riff, and his other classic riffs like "Another One Bites the Dust") is called out as rhythmically complex beyond the average beginner riff.
  - **Formal counting exists** ("a-1-and-a-2-and-up") and is worth learning eventually, but the video's actual hack is a workaround for *before* you know the theory.
  - **The rhythm-mnemonic method:** invent a nonsense verbal phrase whose natural speech rhythm and syllable stresses match the riff's rhythm, then say the phrase (out loud or in your head) while playing to lock the feel in without formally counting. BassBuzz's own example for this riff: **"apple pineapple mango."** Explicitly framed as personal/replaceable — "feel free to create your own." → this is the same device as the "nice cup of tea" (3-against-2) and "pass the golden butter" (4-against-3) polyrhythm mnemonics already documented in [[Syncopation and Metric Displacement]], generalized here from *counting a polyrhythm* to *feeling any syncopated riff*.
  - **Fretting options, both explicitly left open as equally valid:** an index-finger "finger roll" (named only — the mechanics aren't demonstrated in this free video; BassBuzz says it's taught in depth in the paid "Beginner to Badass" course), or simply alternating index and middle across the shape — "whatever feels easy."
  - **Plucking:** no specific pattern prescribed — "anything that gets you the notes in time is fine," i.e. the rhythm-mnemonic trick is doing the real work here, not a specific plucking discipline.

- **[~10:18–end] Closing.** Five riffs = real progress on fretting, plucking, and rhythm, but continued improvement needs a clear next-thing-to-practice, not video-hopping — points to BassBuzz's "what to learn on bass in order" resource. Closes on a callback to the cold-open "Mary had a little lamb" bit.
