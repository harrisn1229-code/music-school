# Kenny Burrell - How to Make the Blues Sound Outside

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g9If9TAmK44)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g9If9TAmK44)

## Synopsis

Jens Larsen analyzes Kenny Burrell's guitar solo on "Blackbird" from the Jimmy Smith album *Standards*. The through-line: Burrell uses blues language — not altered dominants, reharmonization, or diminished substitutions — as his primary "outside" tension device over straight-ahead jazz changes, deliberately clashing blue notes against clean chord tones and resolving back into the changes. A second, independent tension axis is rhythmic: contrasting straight eighth-note swing against a much harder/sharper swing feel within the same phrase, without changing note choice at all.

## What's taught

- **Blues-as-tension device, the core mechanic.** Rather than reharmonizing to create tension, Burrell repeatedly plays blues language (the ♭5, ♭3, ♯11) directly against otherwise-clean jazz harmony, then resolves back to the straight changes — treated functionally like an altered-dominant substitute even though it isn't one theoretically. Demonstrated in the solo's opening pickup: straight into blue notes (♭5, ♭3) before resolving into a Bb-line associated with both F and C7, landing on the 3rd (A) at the top of the form.
- **The ♯11 as a recurring tension-and-release anchor.** Used repeatedly across multiple phrases as the primary "wrong note against the melody" device: play an ascending F major 7 triad, land on the ♯11 (B), and deliberately sit on/repeat it before resolving down — described as simultaneously a blues note and (in a minor-chord context) readable as a 9th, intentionally ambiguous. Paired later in the solo with the ♭3/♯9 as a second, parallel tension tone doing the same job.
- **Melody-into-blues transformation.** Burrell plays the tune's actual melody near-verbatim for the first several notes of a phrase, then substitutes one melody note for a blues-inflected note (e.g. a ♭3 where the listener expects the diatonic melody note) before resolving back into the written line — using the listener's own memory of the melody as the setup for the "wrong" note to land as tension rather than an error.
- **Rhythmic contrast as an independent tension axis.** Most of the solo sits in straight/medium swing eighths; specific phrases switch to a noticeably harder, more shuffled swing feel — same notes, same harmonic content, but the rhythmic character alone creates the surprise/tension, "not about altered dominants or extra chords... it's really about other aspects of music."
- **Long-arc phrasing over multiple bars.** Ideas are stretched and developed across several bars rather than resolved quickly — flagged as a general mark of a strong improviser: "it's not only about hitting the right notes... it's also about creating a longer story with the melodies."
- **One-note-one-rhythm motif development.** In the bridge, a single pitch (the 9th) repeated with a simple 16th-note rhythmic motif creates contrast against the medium-swing backdrop purely through rhythmic repetition, without any harmonic movement at all.
- **A minor-2-5 (A half-diminished → D7 → G minor) resolved with a straightforward scale run**, contrasted against the blues/tension phrases elsewhere in the solo — shown as the "release" half of the tension-and-release pattern, not every phrase is tension.
