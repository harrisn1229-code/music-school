# Oberheim DMX

## What it is
Oberheim's answer to the Linn LM-1 at half the price — a sampled-drum machine whose hard, slamming kit became **early hip hop's drum sound** (the rapper DMX took his name from it). Where the Linn machines read as polished pop, the DMX reads as *concrete*.

## What makes the sound
- **8-bit companded samples on EPROMs**, like the Linns, but Oberheim's voice cards run each sample through their own **analog VCA/filter stages with tuning** — a harder, boxier, more aggressive print of a real kit.
- **The kick and snare hit like slabs** — short, dense, mid-heavy; they cut through boomy PA systems and boombox speakers, which is exactly why early hip hop chose it.
- Slower/cruder repitching options than the Linn gave it a chunkier, less "produced" character — less gloss, more punch.
- Programmed via Oberheim's step interface with swing; patterns feel blocky and confident rather than slick.

## Digital versions
- **Owned:** [[Drum Rack]] / [[Impulse]] with DMX sample packs; [[kHs Bitcrush]] + [[Saturator]] to push the 8-bit slam; [[RC-20 Retro Color]] for era texture.
- **Paid:** UVI Beat Box Anthology 2, Samples From Mars "DMX From Mars".
- **Free:** DMX kits are standard fare in free vintage-drum-machine archives.

## Used on
- Run-DMC — "Sucker M.C.'s" (1983): the genre-defining *nothing but DMX and voices* record.
- New Order — "Blue Monday" (1983): the iconic machine-gun kick intro is an Oberheim DMX pattern.
- Genre/era: early/old-school hip hop and electro 1981–86 ([[Rap Subgenres]]), post-punk/new-wave dance crossovers.
