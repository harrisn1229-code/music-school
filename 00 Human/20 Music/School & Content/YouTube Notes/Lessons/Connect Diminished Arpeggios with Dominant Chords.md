# Connect Diminished Arpeggios with Dominant Chords

## Synopsis
A tightly-scoped companion piece to [[How To Use Diminished Chords For Rhythm and Lead Guitar!]] — where that video derives the dim7-over-dominant substitution from the "dominant 7 = two overlapping triads" angle and demos it inside a real song, this one derives it from the simpler "strip the root off a dominant 7 chord and you're left with a diminished triad" angle, then spends almost the entire runtime on a single, systematic **resolution drill**: play a four-note diminished-seventh arpeggio (the upper structure of a dominant chord) through all four of its symmetric inversions, and at each inversion find and land on the nearest chord tone of the target chord a half or whole step away. Jack Ruch works this out fully over **A7 → Dm7** (root position and all three inversions, matched to a fifth/♭7/root/♭3 resolution sequence), then repeats the same drill on two more string sets so the same four shapes are mapped across the neck rather than confined to one string trio. He closes by showing the identical A7 diminished-substitution idea has to resolve to *different* chord tones depending on what the target chord actually is — worked back-to-back over a **ii–V–I in D major** (target Dmaj7, whose major 3rd/major 7th change the landing notes) and an **A7→D7 blues turnaround** (I→IV, target a plain dominant 7). The throughline, stated explicitly, is that the shape is the easy part — the actual skill is finding the nearest chord tone of wherever you're going.

## What's taught
- **Where the diminished substitution comes from, upper-structure-triad framing (a second derivation of the relationship already documented on [[Diminished Chords]]).** A dominant 7 chord = root + 3rd + 5th + ♭7. Strip the root and the remaining 3rd-5th-♭7 triad IS a diminished triad — e.g. A7 (A-C♯-E-G) minus the root leaves **C♯-E-G**, a C♯diminished triad. That's why C♯dim works as a substitute for A7 resolving to Dm: it's not a coincidence, it's literally A7's own upper structure. Framed as "a functioning dominant V chord, or a secondary dominant" — see [[Secondary Dominants]] for the general mechanism this is a case of.
- **Triad → dim7: stack one more minor third.** C♯-E-G (minor 3rd, minor 3rd) plus one more minor third above G lands on **B♭**, giving the full symmetric C♯dim7 (C♯-E-G-B♭) — same four notes readable as C♯dim7 = Edim7 = Gdim7 = B♭dim7, the standard "only three unique dim7 chords" symmetry already on [[Diminished Chords]].
- **Why the added B♭ isn't just a random extra note: it's the ♭9 of the dominant chord it substitutes for.** Over A7, B♭ is a half step above the root — the ♭9. Framed usefully: the A7's whole job is to build tension that resolves to the I chord (Dm), so the ♭9 is "the perfect tension note" rather than a wrong note — general framing for why altered/dissonant tones work over a dominant that's about to resolve.
- **The core resolution drill, worked in full over A7 → Dm7 (target chord tones: D-F-A-C).** Play the C♯dim7 arpeggio (C♯-E-G-B♭) through its four inversions — each one reached by moving the *same fingering* up a minor third (3 frets), because the chord is symmetric — and at each inversion, resolve the shape's top note down a half step (if the top note is a half step above a chord tone) or a whole step (if a whole step above) to land on the nearest Dm7 chord tone:
  | Arpeggio shape (low→high) | Resolve from | Direction | Lands on | Dm7 chord-tone role |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | C♯–E–G–B♭ | B♭ | ↓ ½ step | A | 5th |
  | E–G–B♭–C♯ | C♯ | ↓ ½ step | C | ♭7 |
  | G–B♭–C♯–E | E | ↓ whole step | D | root |
  | B♭–C♯–E–G | G | ↓ whole step | F | ♭3 (minor 3rd) |

  - The four positions sit **root position** on C♯ (5th fret area, A-D-G strings as demoed), and the same four shapes replay identically on other string sets (see below) because the underlying interval pattern doesn't change.
- **Same four positions remapped onto the D-G-B string set, different starting order** — starts on G (→ whole step down to root D), then B♭ (→ whole step down to ♭3/F), then C♯ (→ half step down to 5th/A), then E (→ half step down to ♭7/C). - **Same four positions again on the top three strings** — starting points B♭, C♯, E, G, each resolving to a nearby Dm7 chord tone, completing a third string-set pass through the identical four shapes. *(This third pass is the most garbled stretch of the transcript — the individual resolution targets aren't cleanly recoverable, but the pattern is the same four-inversion drill as the two string sets above, just relocated — captured qualitatively per the depth-standard rule for low-confidence transcript detail.)*
- **The reusable practice takeaway, stated explicitly as the point of the whole exercise:** the diminished shape itself is simple and mechanical (same fingering, slides in minor thirds); the actual skill being drilled is **finding the nearest chord tone of the target chord from wherever the arpeggio currently sits** — not memorizing which exact note resolves where, but training the ear/hand to always locate the closest landing spot.
- **The same A7 diminished substitution resolves to different chord tones depending on what it's resolving to — worked as three back-to-back vamps, same dim7 shapes, three different targets:**
  1. **A7 → Dm7** (V7 → i, minor) — the drill above; target chord tones D-F-A-C.
  2. **Em7 – A7 – Dmaj7** (ii–V–I in D major) — same A7 diminished substitution, but the target is now **Dmaj7** (D-F♯-A-C♯), which has a **major 3rd and major 7th** instead of Dm7's minor 3rd/♭7 — explicitly called out as the one thing that changes: "we're going to have to keep track of that when we're resolving to chord tones."
  3. **A7 → D7** (blues I→IV) — same substitution again, now resolving to a **plain dominant 7 D7** rather than a minor or major triad-quality chord; framed specifically as the blues use-case, since A7→D7 is the I-to-IV move of a blues in A.
  - Net lesson: **one arpeggio shape/fingering, three different resolution maps** — the dim7 substitution over a dominant chord is reusable everywhere that chord functions as a V (minor i, major I, or dominant IV target), but the actual notes you resolve TO have to be re-derived from the target chord's own quality each time, not reused from the last example.
