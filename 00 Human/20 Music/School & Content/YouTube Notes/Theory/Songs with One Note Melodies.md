# Songs with One Note Melodies

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x5-xZygYKL0)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x5-xZygYKL0)

## Synopsis

A case-study argument that a good melody isn't measured by how many pitches it uses — surveying songs that stay on one note, then songs limited to just the first 3 scale degrees, to show that rhythm, harmonic context, and scale-degree function can carry almost all of a melody's appeal on their own.

## Techniques

- **Rhythm-over-pitch as a melody-writing device**: "Mr. Brightside" (The Killers) verse stays on essentially one note for 16 bars, working more like a chant/percussion part than a conventional melody — the catchiness comes from its rhythm, not pitch variety. Held-note tension pays off structurally: the pre-chorus's small handful of new pitches lands as a big release purely because so little melodic movement preceded it.
- **A static melody note can still sound like it's moving via harmonic recontextualization underneath it**: Jobim's "One Note Samba" holds one pitch while the chords change color around it (the same C reading as a minor 3rd over one chord, then a major 3rd over the next).
- **Root vs. 5th as the only two scale degrees stable enough to sustain a genuinely static melody**: the root works indefinitely ("Baby You're a Rich Man," Beatles); the 5th works but starts audibly wanting to resolve after enough bars ("Julia," Beatles, 8 bars on the 5th before stepping down). A non-resolved degree (4th, 7th) can't sustain the device at all.
- **The 1-2-3 scale-degree-only chorus, common in recent pop, and why it still works with almost no pitch material**: root = stability, 2nd = tension/motion, 3rd = a second stable-but-less-resolved landing spot — those three roles are enough to build a full melodic arc. Rhythm changes the character of the identical pitch set: "Free Fallin'" (Tom Petty) uses the same 1-2-3 notes as the nursery-rhyme-flavored pop examples, but long drawn-out note values erase that nursery-rhyme quality entirely.
- **Deliberate pitch-count limitation as a usable compositional exercise**: a 5-composer collaboration each wrote a 1-minute piano piece with an imposed 3-note / 2-note / 1-note sectional structure, specifically to force creative solutions unavailable under full pitch freedom.
