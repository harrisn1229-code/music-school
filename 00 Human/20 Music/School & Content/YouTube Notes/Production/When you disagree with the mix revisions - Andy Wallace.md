# When you disagree with the mix revisions - Andy Wallace

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rFIbmUGvAjw)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rFIbmUGvAjw)

## Synopsis

A short (under 7 minutes), talking-head interview segment with Andy Wallace (Nirvana's *Nevermind*, countless other landmark mixes) on the non-technical side of mixing: how he handles a client pushing back on or requesting changes to a mix. There's no processing, no plugin, no gear name-dropped anywhere in the transcript — the entire video is Wallace's philosophy and practical playbook for the revision conversation, told in his own words rather than demonstrated on a session. It splits cleanly into two halves. The first is a mixing-craft point that sets up everything else: he deliberately mixes with built-in headroom for revision, keeping instruments defined and "raw" rather than polished/prettified specifically so that a fader move or a request can shift the balance significantly without the mix falling apart. The second half is entirely about the client relationship — setting expectations before mixing even starts, actively drawing out shy artists who are intimidated by his reputation, and offering pushy or over-involved clients a specific reciprocal deal: they can say anything they want without fear, because he's already committed to pulling back any suggestion that would actually hurt the mix, and to telling them plainly when a change isn't working. He closes with the one genuinely rare failure case — a client so fixated on their own pre-existing reference mixes that no amount of dialogue could bridge the gap, and he ultimately (respectfully, without hard feelings) told them he wasn't the right mixer for that job. The whole thing reads as a coherent, reusable playbook rather than a collection of anecdotes, which is why it's routed to a new standalone technique note rather than left to sit only on this page.

## What's taught

- **Mix with built-in revision headroom, not a polished/finished-sounding balance, so client pushback can move things a lot without breaking the mix.** "If I've done my job properly and gotten things right, hopefully there's a lot of flexibility in that mix where I can bring something up or down a significant amount and not destroy the integrity of what I've established." The mechanism he names is keeping **definition between instruments without prettifying them** — "just keep them raw and powerful" — so that pushing one element up or down doesn't suddenly make another "disappear" or "seem too loud." This is a mixing-craft decision made *in anticipation of* revisions, not a reaction to them. → [[Masking and Carving]], [[Mix Handoff]], [[Client Communication and Revisions]]
- **Set the ground rules for feedback in the very first meeting, before mixing even starts** — not after a request has already caused friction. Wallace addresses "the way in which that would occur very early on, in our first meeting, even before we even start mixing." → [[Client Communication and Revisions]]
- **Actively draw out artists who are too intimidated to give feedback.** Some artists are "too shy... almost afraid to suggest that the great Andy Wallace should do this" — his fix is direct reassurance: "don't be shy, I want your input, that's my job." → [[Client Communication and Revisions]]
- **Offer pushy/over-involved clients a reciprocal-trust deal instead of either capitulating or shutting them down.** He tells them upfront: "you can always make suggestions to me, don't be shy... I'll pull the reins in if I really feel they need to be pulled in. I'll let you know if I think a suggestion you're making is not good for the mix." That standing commitment is what lets the client speak freely — "if they can have that confidence not just in my ability to do the mix but in my agreeing to reel them in if they've gone too far, that gives them a freedom where they can speak their mind" — and it's explicitly not the same as just doing whatever's asked ("okay you want that, ladders-ville, okay, you like that, great, it's done") — "obviously I want anything that goes out with my name on it to be something I'm proud of." → [[Client Communication and Revisions]]
- **When a revision genuinely breaks the mix, say so directly and reopen the conversation rather than silently reverting or silently complying.** "If I feel it doesn't [work], then I'll say that... I'll let you know that this mix isn't working anymore, or whatever change we made there is not good for the mix, it's not helping the mix, we're going away from where we should be." He notes this almost always leads to a dialogue that resolves it. → [[Client Communication and Revisions]]
- **Know when a client relationship is fundamentally unworkable, and be willing to decline the job.** The one rare exception he describes at length: a client so completely fixated on their own pre-existing reference mixes that "every single [element] all the way down the line" had to be reverted to match them — leaving no room for an actual mix. His resolution, stated respectfully and without a bad attitude on either side: "you shouldn't hire me to mix this record because I simply cannot give you what you're looking for, and I don't know that any other mixer is going to do it either... if what you absolutely want are those reference mixes, release those reference mixes, that's the record you want." Framed explicitly as a rare occurrence, not a common outcome. → [[Client Communication and Revisions]]
