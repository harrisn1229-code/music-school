# Songs that use the Lydian mode

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ll8UwRWVkP4)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ll8UwRWVkP4)

## Synopsis

David Bennett defines Lydian as major with a raised 4th degree, explains why it's harmonically the least stable mode (its tritone sits between the root and the raised 4th rather than resolving cleanly like the major scale's does), and surveys where it shows up — mostly film scoring, rarely full pop songs — along with two common misidentifications (Fleetwood Mac's "Dreams," The Simpsons theme) that turn out to be something else entirely.

## Techniques

- **The raised 4th is the whole story.** Lydian = major with scale degree 4 raised a half-step, giving a bright/magical/ethereal quality. Natural home in film scoring (Yoda's Theme and the *E.T.* flying theme, both John Williams; *Back to the Future*, Alan Silvestri). Rare full-song pop examples: "Waltz No. 2" (Elliott Smith), "When We Dance" (Sting), "Possibly Maybe" (Björk), "Dancing Days" (Led Zeppelin, Lydian + blues ♭3), "Oceans" (Pearl Jam, shifts between two Lydian keys).
- **The i–II vamp** — tonic to a major II chord (only possible in Lydian) is the efficient way to state it in two chords.
- **Why Lydian is harmonically unstable**: its tritone sits between the root and the raised 4th itself (vs. Ionian's tritone between degrees 4 and 7, which resolves cleanly onto the two most consonant scale tones). A I→V move in Lydian tends to get re-heard as IV→I in the plain major scale a 4th below — which is why almost no song stays in Lydian for its full duration.
- **Tonic ambiguity, demonstrated mid-song**: "Here Comes My Girl" (Tom Petty) — verse vamps A–B, A reads as tonic (A Lydian); chorus uses the identical notes but E now reads as tonic (A Lydian and E major share every note) — an audible modal shift with no pitch changes. Same device in "Man on the Moon" (R.E.M.), C Lydian verse to G major chorus.
- **Pedaling the tonic stabilizes Lydian** — "Sara" (Fleetwood Mac) uses the same chord progression as "Dreams" but pedals F throughout, which is what actually makes it F Lydian.
- **"Dreams" (Fleetwood Mac) is commonly misidentified as F Lydian** from its F–G vamp, but the melody resolves to A — it's actually A Aeolian (chords vi–♭VII of C), not F Lydian's I–II.
- **Lydian dominant** (Lydian's raised 4th + a lowered 7th, distinct from plain Lydian's natural 7th) is a frequent mixup — The Simpsons theme (Danny Elfman) is often cited as Lydian but is actually Lydian dominant; "Pretty Ballerina" (The Left Banke) is a second example.
- **"Blue Jay Way" (Beatles)** — a borderline case: uses C Lydian's notes but routinely swaps in a minor 3rd, so it only reads as Lydian in the moments it uses the major 3rd.
