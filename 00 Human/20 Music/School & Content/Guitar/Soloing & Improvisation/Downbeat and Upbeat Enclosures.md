# Downbeat and Upbeat Enclosures

Part of [[Bebop Vocabulary]] (§2 Enclosures) and [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] — a third enclosure-sizing rule, distinct from both. [[Enclosures Through the Scale]] fixes the enclosure's *length* mechanically off the scale's own half-step gaps (Jared's descending drill); [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]'s Shape 3 fixes it off the chord's *function* (Mixolydian/Dorian/Aeolian). This one fixes the enclosure's **note-count (2 vs 3)** purely off **which beat the target lands on** — a downbeat target gets a two-note enclosure, an upbeat target gets a three-note enclosure — and then uses that single rule to (a) drill every diatonic arpeggio in a key and (b) build connective lines between chords of a ii–V.

## The core rule
Target a note. Check which beat it lands on:
- **Downbeat → two-note enclosure** — approach from below or above by one note.
- **Upbeat → three-note enclosure** — adds rhythmic tension; either two notes below or above, or one note below and one above (the reverse also works).

This is the rhythmic complement to [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §2's four enclosure *shapes* (chromatic above/below, diatonic above + chromatic below, etc.) — those tables describe what notes to use; this rule decides how many, driven entirely by where the target sits in the bar. *(general starting point: the underlying logic is the same reason a bebop scale adds a passing tone — [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §1 — extra notes exist to keep strong tones landing on strong beats; here the enclosure's own length is what absorbs the offset instead of a scale passing-tone.)*

## Drill 1 — Enclose the first note of every diatonic arpeggio
Take a diatonic 7th-chord arpeggio (worked example: **C major 7** — C E G B) and enclose just its first note (the root) with either a two- or three-note enclosure. Repeat through **every chord of the C major scale** in turn (Cmaj7, Dm7, Em7, Fmaj7, G7, Am7, Bm7♭5) until the enclosure shape is automatic under the fingers and the ear recognizes it on sight. Builds the same "small fragments of information" vocabulary [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §5's digital patterns describe, but sourced from enclosures rather than scale-degree numbers.

## Drill 2 — Walk a scale down, enclose one note en route
Walk down a scale (worked in **C major**, target **G**; then repeated in **D Dorian** off Dm7, target **A**) and pause at a chosen target note to enclose it, sizing the enclosure by the rule above. In the worked example G lands on an upbeat, so it gets the three-note enclosure. A chord-function-aware sibling of [[Enclosures Through the Scale]]'s mechanical descending drill — same "walk the scale, pause to enclose" shape, but the enclosure length here comes from the beat, not from counting scale-tone half-steps.

## Drill 3 — Connect a ii–V line by walking up to the V's target beat
Take a pre-learned dominant-chord phrase (worked example: a "classic G7 dominant phrase" starting on G) and connect it back to the **preceding ii chord** (Dm7) instead of playing it in isolation:
1. Walk **up** the Dorian scale of the ii chord (D Dorian) starting from the dominant phrase's own starting note (G).
2. When you reach that starting note (G) again, check which beat it lands on this time around.
3. **Downbeat → two-note enclosure; upbeat → three-note enclosure** — same rule as above, now used to stitch the walk-up into the memorized phrase rather than to decorate an isolated target.
4. Play the dominant phrase straight out of the enclosure.

Repeating the walk-up from **different starting notes of the Dorian scale** produces different connective-line variations feeding into the same memorized phrase — each variation becomes its own reusable building block. Worked twice in the video: starting on C, the walk-up lands the phrase's starting note (G) on a **downbeat** → two-note enclosure into the phrase; walking down the Dorian scale instead to hit the same target an octave lower lands it on an **upbeat** → three-note enclosure into the phrase, an octave down. Same target, same phrase, different beat placement, different enclosure size — a direct worked demonstration that the rule tracks the beat, not the note.

This is the same "attach a written line to the harmony ahead of it" goal as [[The ii-V-I]]'s secondary-dominant "landing spot" bullets, but the connective device here is rhythmic (beat-triggered enclosure sizing) rather than harmonic (a whole ii–V inserted in front of a target chord).

## How to practice it
- Transcribe real solos specifically listening for how players frame target notes with enclosures — not just what notes they use, but how the enclosure's length tracks the beat it resolves on.
- Don't just play the enclosures mechanically — listen for how they "pull and push and persuade the beat to move forward"; the payoff is the rhythmic momentum, not the note choice alone.
