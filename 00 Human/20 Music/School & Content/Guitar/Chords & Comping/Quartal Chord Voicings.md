# Quartal Chord Voicings

Chords built by stacking **4ths instead of 3rds** — the guitar-fretboard companion to the theory-side [[Chords]] tertial/quartal/quintal breakdown and [[20th-Century Harmony]]'s Stravinsky entry, and the concrete fingerings behind [[Modal Jazz]]'s "quartal voicings" section (the *So What* chord). Jared Borkowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared) devotes a full lesson to defining the sound, mapping shapes to the fretboard, and — the most reusable part — using quartal shapes as a reharmonization tool **over ordinary tertian chords**, not just in modal contexts.

## What it is

Where a normal ("tertiary"/tertial) chord takes 1-3-5(-7) of a scale, a quartal chord takes 1-4-7(-…) — stack 4ths instead of 3rds, off any scale degree, and keep stacking as far as you want. Building through the scale of C major on the 5th-4th-3rd strings gives **3-note quartal chords**; extend the stack across the middle 4 strings and you get **4-note quartal chords**. Sound: open, ambiguous, "less traditional" than tertiary harmony — because a stack of 3rds names a chord quality and therefore implies a function, while a stack of 4ths states a set of notes without committing to major/minor/dominant. That's also why it reads as **more dissonant/modern** to the ear trained on common-practice harmony.

## Why it pairs with modal harmony

In [[Modal Jazz]]'s terms: modal harmony has a tonal center (the root) but treats every other scale degree as equal — nothing resolves anywhere, there's no harmonic motion to track, which is what makes a mode "jammable" indefinitely. Tertiary chords built from that scale start to smuggle back in a sense of function/resolution; quartal chords stay neutral and nebulous, so they're the natural harmonic vocabulary for staying inside a mode without accidentally re-implying a key center. **Historical note:** tertiary/functional harmony dominated Western classical music through the 19th century; in the 20th century, Stravinsky, Ravel, Debussy, Schoenberg and others deliberately stacked voices in 4ths to escape the sense of a traditional key center — see [[20th-Century Harmony]] for the fuller Stravinsky/Hindemith split.

## The *So What* voicing (Miles Davis, *Kind of Blue*, 1959)

The best-known real-world example, and the birth of the term "modal jazz." "So What" sits in **D Dorian** — D is the tonal center, every other note of D Dorian is equally fair game to improvise with, no note besides the root carries extra weight. Bill Evans's signature comping chord between melody phrases: **a stack of four perfect 4ths with a major 3rd on top** — from the bottom, **E-A-D-G-B** — so it's not purely quartal (that top major-3rd breaks the pure-4ths stack), but close enough that it's the standard reference "quartal chord" in jazz vocabulary. Move the top note (B, on the high E string) down an octave to the low E string at the same fret and the five notes reorder into **pure perfect-4th stacks all the way up** — a genuine 5-note quartal chord, same pitches, different voicing.

**On a lead sheet, "So What" is usually written as a static Dm11 for 16 bars** — that's the closest a chord symbol (built from tertiary-harmony vocabulary) can get to naming a Dorian modal sound. Derivation: take Dm7, replace the 5th with the 11 (= the 4, a whole step above the 5) → the resulting shape is one of the stacked-perfect-4ths quartal voicings above. **The more accurate framing is "playing in D Dorian," not "playing over Dm11"** — the chord symbol is a functional-harmony approximation of a modal sound, not the actual thing being played.

## Fretboard shapes

- **3-note quartal chords through D Dorian on strings 2-3-4** — map one shape per scale degree of D Dorian along the top three strings; these are the practical comping/improvising vocabulary for playing over "So What"-style modal vamps. Use the top note of each shape as a melody note (arpeggiate the shapes for melodic lines), or comp with them under a soloist.
- **3-note quartal chords through C major on strings 5-4-3** and **4-note quartal chords through C major on the middle 4 strings** — the general fretboard-mapping drill: build the shape at every scale degree, same "climb the whole scale in one voicing type" logic as the [[Chords by Key]] tonic-to-tonic triad/7th-chord mapping drill, just with quartal stacks instead of tertian ones.

## Reharmonizing tertian chords with quartal shapes (the reusable trick)

Quartal shapes aren't only for modal tunes — they're a genuine **reharmonization/color tool over ordinary minor chords**, tertian or not:

1. **Harmonize the minor pentatonic scale with 4ths.** Take the minor pentatonic scale along one string (e.g. E minor pentatonic on the 2nd string) and stack **two perfect 4ths below** each scale tone — same physical shape repeats at every scale degree. This introduces a **natural 6** into the sound, which is the same thing as treating the minor chord as Dorian even when the underlying chord isn't officially Dorian — described as "totally okay" to do regardless. Slide the same shape to any root to reharmonize any minor or minor-7 chord (worked over both E minor and A minor in the video). → pairs directly with the natural-6 "characteristic note" logic already on [[Modal Jazz]].
2. **Connect shapes chromatically through the blues note.** The same quartal shape, built off the ♭5/blues note of the pentatonic scale, works as a passing/connecting voicing between the diatonic shapes — and the whole set arpeggiates into melodic lines as well as chordal stabs.
3. **Drone/pedal the root underneath** while playing the moving quartal shapes above — a low root note (played or implied by a bassist) keeps the ear anchored while the quartal voicings float ambiguously on top. → [[Modal Jazz]] §Pedal points and drones.
4. **Any chord type, not just minor** — map quartal shapes off the scale a chord actually comes from (e.g. quartal shapes over a G7 pull from the C major scale G7 belongs to), then apply the same reharmonization logic.

## One shape, many chord identities (rootless-voicing crossover)

The same 3-note quartal shape reharmonizes to a **different named tertian chord depending purely on which note you're mentally treating as the root** — a direct crossover with the rootless-voicing logic on [[Chord Voicings]] ("one physical shape, several harmonic identities depending on context"). Worked off one fixed 3-note quartal shape:

| Treated as root | Resulting chord |
|---|---|
| bottom note | minor 11 |
| next note up (4th fret in the worked example) | ♭ major 7♯11 |
| next | sus4 |
| next | 6/9 |
| next | major 13 |
| next | 7sus4 (or minor 11) |
| next | sus2 |
| next | 6/9 (an octave/register up from the earlier one) |

The physical grip never changes — only the ear's assumption about which note is the root does. Stringing several of these root-reinterpretations together in sequence reads as a legitimate ambiguous-but-coherent chord progression, and is exactly the quality that makes quartal shapes so at-home in a modal context: **the chord never insists on one specific identity.**

## Inverting quartal shapes for new colors

Take a quartal shape (e.g. the E-minor-pentatonic-harmonized-in-4ths shape above) and invert it two ways:

- **Bottom note up an octave** → produces a genuinely new shape (no longer a pure quartal stack) built from the same pitch classes — still usable in exactly the same context (same scale, same chord) since the note content is identical. Following the E minor pentatonic scale along the 2nd string with this inversion gives **a whole-step interval on top** — a distinct, "beautiful" color from the plain stacked-4ths version.
- **Top note down an octave** → another new shape from the same notes; following the scale along the 5th string instead gives yet another usable voicing, called out as a favorite.

Both inversions are worth cycling through as a deliberate variation set once the plain quartal shape is comfortable — same "same notes, new physical shape, new usable color" principle that [[Chord Voicings]] documents for altered-dominant and dim7 shapes elsewhere on the fretboard.

## Practice method (pedal points, melody-first, avoid-note handling)

Jens Larsen's practice-focused companion lesson to the definitional material above — less about what quartal chords are, more about how to actually get them into your hands and ears in a usable order.

1. **Voicings first, on a couple of string sets.** Get the 3-note quartal shapes under the fingers before doing anything else with them — Larsen says he personally reaches for the **top two string sets** the most, though drilling every string set is worth it for thoroughness.
2. **Then drill over a pedal point, starting with the ii chord.** Hold a static root (drone, backing track, or looper — see below) and practice the quartal voicings over it, beginning with **the ii chord** (D minor in a C-major context) because it's the most forgiving entry point. Of the seven diatonic 3-note quartal voicings, only **one** actually spells a "normal"-sounding Dm7 on its own — the rest are intentionally incomplete and work by **accumulation**: stack several of them in sequence and let the sum of the notes convey the chord, rather than expecting any single grip to sound complete. Move to the **V chord** next (G — the plain major-scale quartal voicing there already reads as a "G7 with a 13"), then the **I chord** last (see the avoid-note problem below). Scope the whole drill to a plain **2-5-1**, not all seven scale degrees at once.
3. **Melody-first practice technique: improvise a one-string single-note melody, then drop a quartal chord underneath it.** The natural next step once the harmonized-scale voicings are comfortable — you've already practiced playing the scale harmonized on one string, so picking a melody note there and adding a quartal voicing under it is a small step. This is the bridge from "I know the shapes" to actually using them expressively, and it's also how several of the worked ii–V–I examples below are built (a melody line doing more of the harmonic-identity work than any single chord grip).
4. **Practice against a drone/loop, not a self-played root.** On camera the root gets played by hand to demonstrate the sound, but the recommended practice setup is a backing track or a **loop pedal holding the pedal tone**, freeing both hands for the quartal voicings and melody.

**The tonic (I) chord is the hardest of the three — the "avoid note" problem.** Over C major 7, any quartal voicing containing **F** (the 4th/11th) clashes with the major 3rd (E) and stops reading as a resting tonic sound — three of the seven diatonic 3-note quartal voicings contain F and work mainly as **passing chords**, not landing points. **Explicit advice: don't reach for a Lydian sound to dodge this at the start** — raising the 4th to a ♯11 (Lydian) does remove the clash and is common advice for quartal-tonic voicings, but it's framed here as unnecessary complexity for a beginner; stay inside the plain major scale first, add Lydian later once the basic vocabulary is comfortable. → [[Modal Jazz]] (Lydian as the natural "easier tonic" mode for quartal/modal playing generally)

## Quartal voicings for altered dominants (melodic-minor harmonization)

Same harmonize-every-scale-degree-in-4ths method used for the major scale, applied to the **melodic minor scale** instead, to get quartal voicings usable over an **altered dominant**. For a G7alt sound, harmonize **Ab melodic minor** — a half step above the dominant root ("a flat melodic minor, which is the same as G7 altered") — in stacked 3-note 4th-voicings. Stated as a repeatable template: cover other dominant colors the same way later (Lydian dominant, tonic-minor chords, etc.) by harmonizing the matching parent scale in 4ths. → [[Altered Chords]], [[Jens Larsen - Melodic Minor - Altered Scale]]

**Worked ii–V–I application (four examples, Dm7 → G7alt → C69 in C major).** The core device: make the altered dominant *audible* by voicing G7alt with notes **absent** from the preceding Dm7 voicing, not by nailing one textbook-correct chord shape. Open on a quartal Dm7-area voicing chosen to sound unambiguously like D minor 7 (or a one-string melody line doing the same job — examples 3 and 4 lean on this instead of a fixed grip), then move to a quartal G7alt voicing built from the Ab-melodic-minor harmonization — the ♭9/♯9-family notes (Ab, Eb, Bb) weren't present in the Dm7 voicing, so the ear registers "something changed, this is altered" even without any single voicing spelling G7alt cleanly. Example 4 goes furthest: it **avoids the major 3rd of G7 entirely** and still reads as altered purely from the ♭9/♯9-family color notes. All four resolve to a **C69** tonic voicing (not a plain Cmaj7) — consistent with the plain-major-scale (not Lydian) tonic voicings from the practice method above. → [[The ii-V-I]] — Jens Larsen, [[Jazz Chord Essentials 3 part Quartal Harmony]]

**Two extensions named but not developed in the source lesson** (flag for a future enrichment pass if covered elsewhere): using quartal voicings **melodically inside improvised lines**, not just as chords; and building quartal harmony by harmonizing **pentatonic scales** instead of the full major/melodic-minor scale — "another way to approach this and get some other sounds." **The pentatonic-harmonization extension is now covered** — [[Pentatonic Scale Harmonized in Thirds]] does the tertian (3rds) sibling of this technique: stacking the pentatonic scale's own notes in scale-step thirds rather than 4ths, cycled through every scale degree for a second, differently-colored family of voicings from the same five notes — Nathan Borton, [[Joe Henderson's Pentatonic Secrets]].
