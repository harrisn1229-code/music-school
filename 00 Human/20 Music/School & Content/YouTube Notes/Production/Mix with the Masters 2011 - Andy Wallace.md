# Mix with the Masters 2011 - Andy Wallace

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=578iblW3SMI)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=578iblW3SMI)

## Synopsis

Andy Wallace's closing remarks at his 2011 Mix with the Masters seminar (Nirvana's *Nevermind*, Slayer, Rage Against the Machine — see also his later session on [[Mixing Avenged Sevenfold - Andy Wallace]]). Shorter and more reflective than a full session walkthrough, but it packs in several concrete, reusable habits: he corrects a persistent rumor that he replaces all the drums on *Nevermind* with samples (he doesn't — he occasionally augments with production samples, and separately uses "ambient samples" purely to drive reverbs, a different use case entirely); he names a always-ready PCM 42 "throw" setup (a single delay tap with little or no regeneration) as a go-to quick effect; he gives a specific, simple kick mic setup (Sennheiser 421, sometimes doubled); and he lays out his mix workflow in two clean stages — a fast, reference-guided rough pass (3–4 hours) followed by a much longer fine-tuning pass where the real subtlety happens. He also states plainly that he rides faders heavily rather than leaning on compression, which several people are apparently surprised by.

## Techniques

- **A PCM 42 "throw": a single delay tap, often with no regeneration at all, kept permanently set up as a quick reach-for-anything effect.** "One of my PCM 42 things — one of the things I'll usually have set up as just a throw that I can quickly put a little bit of on anything — is just a one-time delay, and a lot of times it doesn't even have any regeneration." The behavior (one tap, feedback near/at zero) maps directly onto a stock delay with Feedback pulled to 0 — no specialized hardware character needed to reproduce the *move*, though the specific unit is a named personal favorite. → [[Delay]] (effect), [[Echo]], [[TEXTURE Slot]]
- **Sennheiser 421 on kick, occasionally doubled, positioned close (a couple of inches from the head), inside near the beater — a deliberately simple, unfancy setup.** "When I would do it I was using nothing really too fancy — I would use a Sennheiser, a lot of times a Sennheiser 421, on the kick, sometimes I use two mics, usually just inside near the head, a couple of inches from the head." → [[Drum Mic Technique]], [[Kick Design]], [[1 Drums Kick Track|Drums Kick Track]]
- **"Ambient samples" set up specifically to drive reverb sends — a distinct use case from augmenting drums with production samples, and a direct correction of the "Wallace replaces all the drums" rumor.** "I do occasionally use samples — either I'll augment with samples that may have been put on there during the production, or I'll set up what I call ambient samples that are primarily used just to drive reverbs." The reverb's source signal is a chosen ambient sample rather than (or alongside) the actual drum mic feed — a way to shape a reverb's character independent of what's driving it in the mix. → [[Reverb]] (effect)
- **Heavy automation ("riding faders") is the primary dynamics tool, not compression — stated as a direct surprise to people who assumed otherwise.** "Several people were surprised that I didn't use as much compression as they thought — I do a lot of riding of faders, a lot." Guitar rides specifically are written down (automated) and sometimes done loud/exaggerated on purpose "because I want to feel that impact" while riding them in. → [[Automation]], [[Compression Staging]]
- **Two-stage mix workflow: a fast reference-guided rough pass (3–4 hours, ~80% there), then a much longer fine-fine-tuning pass where the real work happens.** Before touching faders: listen to a reference mix when one's provided (not always given), and make a simple chart of the song's sections (Verse/Chorus/etc.) to orient quickly, especially useful since he's often hearing the song for the first time. The initial pass moves fast to a "sounds pretty good" state; the much longer second stage is deliberately granular — changes so subtle that "most people would sit and say, what is he doing, this sounded like this five hours ago" — and is where he says the genuinely hard-to-name details actually get resolved. → [[Referencing and Translation]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
