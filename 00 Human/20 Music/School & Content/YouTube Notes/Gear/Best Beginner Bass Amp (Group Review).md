# Best Beginner Bass Amp (Group Review)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H_B4ur4VnaQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H_B4ur4VnaQ)

## Synopsis

BassBuzz's Josh shoots out five beginner-price bass amps (all under $300, one deliberately pushed a little over to test whether spending more actually buys more) from the five biggest names in the category — Acoustic, Eden, Ampeg, Hartke, and Fender — to answer one practical question for someone about to buy their first rig: **which one is actually worth the money.** Method is simpler than the channel's blind-test teardowns ([[Bass Tone Chain Priority]]): play each amp, describe the tone and feel of being in the room with it, note construction/weight/features, and give a verdict. Four of the five amps disappoint for overlapping reasons — thin low end even with a 10" speaker, cheap-feeling enclosures, an EQ interface too confusing for a beginner, or hum/weight that isn't justified by the sound — while the fifth, the **Fender Rumble 40**, is a clear, enthusiastic, no-caveats winner on tone, features, weight, and price. The most transferable finding for a beginner isn't really "which brand" — it's the negative pattern across the four losers (8" speakers, thin low end, confusing EQ, hum, weight without payoff) that tells you what to *listen and look for* even outside this specific five-amp field.

## Techniques

**What's taught — a beginner amp-buying checklist, verdict by verdict:**

- **Speaker size is a hard floor, not a preference.** 8" speakers are "bad news" — wimpy, they do not reproduce bass. **Look for a 10" speaker minimum** on any beginner combo amp; this was Josh's explicit reason for including the Acoustic B15 at all, since it was the only amp he could find that cheap with a 10".
- **Wattage sets the "feeling of being in the room," separate from tone quality.** The Acoustic B15 (15W, 10" speaker) had a fine tone but did not drive a real low-end *feeling* in the room — described as "thin," missing "the feeling of a bigger amp," even though the recorded tone itself was fine. Takeaway: tone-through-a-mic and felt bass-in-the-room are two different things a beginner should weigh separately, and low wattage caps the second one regardless of speaker size.
- **A cranked bass knob that makes the speaker distort ("fart out") is a real failure mode, not just a taste issue.** On the Eden EC10, pushing the bass EQ up past flat caused audible speaker breakup — meaning the amp's *usable* bass output is whatever it gives you flat, not whatever the knob's range implies. Worth listening for specifically when amp-shopping: turn the bass knob up and listen for breakup, not just "does it get louder."
- **A confusing multi-band EQ is a genuine beginner deal-breaker, not just an inconvenience.** The Hartke HD75's graphic EQ (many bands) got called out as too confusing even for Josh after half a lifetime playing bass — a concrete signal that a beginner-oriented amp should have a simple EQ (2–4 named bands: bass/mid/treble, or bass/low-mid/high-mid/treble) rather than a graphic multi-band interface.
- **Idle hum with nothing plugged in is a real spec to check, especially for a practice amp.** The Hartke HD75 had the most intense ambient hum Josh had heard from an amp that size, audible with nothing plugged in — flagged as genuinely annoying for practice use, independent of how it sounds once you're playing.
- **Weight has to be justified by output — a heavy amp that doesn't get loud enough is a worse deal than a light one that does.** The Hartke HD75 weighs 52 lb — about the same as Josh's full touring rig (210 cabinet + head) — while not putting out enough sound to justify that weight, a direct contrast against the 18 lb Fender Rumble 40 that "kicked butt on the competition."
- **Overdrive/distortion built into a beginner amp is a legitimate bonus feature, not a gimmick, framed correctly.** Josh is generally not a fan of onboard-amp distortion for actual bass playing (rarely needed) — but calls out the Acoustic B15's built-in overdrive (more fuzz-like, reminiscent of Larry Graham) and the Fender Rumble 40's built-in drive (more distortion-like, cuts more with the bright button engaged) as genuinely fun for a beginner who hasn't bought pedals yet and wants to explore sounds cheaply.
- **A "shape"/"contour" button that scoops the mids for slap is worth having even on a cheap amp.** Both the Acoustic B15 (shape knob) and the winning Fender Rumble 40 (contour button) offer a one-push modern, scooped slap tone — named as a nice-to-have on both.

**Per-amp verdicts (five amps, all reamped through the same recorded riffs, price-ordered as tested):**

1. **Acoustic B15** — 15W, 10" speaker, the cheapest of the five. Tone is fine but the amp lacks room-filling low-end feel at only 15 watts. Roll low up + mid/highs down = nice vintagey tone; opposite (mids/highs up) = clicky, aggressive tone. Built-in overdrive sounds cool (more fuzz-like, Larry Graham-ish). Shape knob gives a nice slap contour. **Verdict: well-designed and made, but 15 watts is a real ceiling** — fine as a cheap starter, not as good as the Rumble 40.
2. **Eden EC10** — 10" speaker combo. Despite Josh's positive experience with pricier Eden amps historically, this one disappointed — thin, not meaningfully more bass output than the cheaper Acoustic. Cranking the bass EQ makes the speaker "fart out" rather than deliver more usable low end. **Verdict: bummer, not recommended.**
3. **Ampeg BA110** — from a brand with a legendary bass-rig pedigree, but "this is not one of them." Nice natural tone flat, works okay for slap, but wanting more presence/body for its size and weight; enclosure felt cheaper than the others. Built-in "Scrambler" distortion disliked. **Verdict: not a great score.**
4. **Hartke HD75** — deliberately tested slightly over the $300 ceiling to check whether spending more buys more; it didn't. High-quality enclosure and a metal speaker grille (liked, vs. the fabric grilles elsewhere), but the mid-range EQ tone is disliked and the graphic multi-band EQ interface is too confusing even for an experienced player, let alone a beginner. Worst ambient hum of the group, audible with nothing plugged in. 52 lb — as heavy as a full touring rig — without the output to justify it. **Verdict: clear don't-buy.**
5. **Fender Rumble 40 — the winner, no caveats.** Not to Josh's taste lookwise, and he "hasn't always liked Fender amps," but it "blew him away" for the money. Good enough to practice with *and* take to a rehearsal or a low-volume gig, not just a bedroom toy. Detail: flat EQ + gain down already sounds like "a good solid tone"; three built-in EQ-shift buttons (**bright**, **contour**, **vintage**) give one-push tonal shifts without dialing in EQ from scratch — vintage button (enhanced further by rolling treble/high-mids down) turns the modern active-bass voicing into a convincing vintage feel for a cheap amp; contour gives a modern scooped slap tone at a push; bright adds trebly aggression. **All EQ knobs have a center detent**, making it easy to find flat by feel without looking. **4-band EQ** (bass/low-mid/high-mid/treble) — low-mid dials up punchiness, high-mid dials up (or, per Josh's own taste, cuts) crispy finger-clack noise — called out as more useful than a basic 3-band (bass/mid/treble) amp where "the mid doesn't do what you want it to do." Built-in **drive** (distortion) sounds good, more distortion-like than the Acoustic's fuzzier overdrive, stacks well with the bright button; same "fun for a beginner exploring sounds before buying pedals" framing as the Acoustic's overdrive. Construction: solid enclosure, fabric grille (disliked — expected to wear out faster than a metal grille, less "professional") but **only 18 lb** — lighter than the tiny 15W Acoustic amp — genuinely portable (carry in one hand to a rehearsal, bass on back, stand in the other hand). **Overall verdict: "head and shoulders above all the other amps" on price, weight, sound, features, and construction — "the king."**

**Bottom line for a beginner buying their first rig:** 10" speaker minimum, listen for bass-knob breakup, prefer a simple named-band EQ over a graphic multi-band interface, check idle hum, weigh weight against actual output, and treat built-in overdrive as a fun beginner bonus rather than a real feature need — the Fender Rumble 40 hits all of these and is the direct recommendation.
