# Course 06 — Loop Harmony & Modal Colour

Harmony as it actually behaves in loop-based music: four chords that repeat, and everything you can do to them without adding a fifth. Where [[Course 02 — Chord Function]] teaches what chords do, this teaches what they do when they come around again.

**What it assumes**

- [[Course 02 — Chord Function]] — this course is that grammar applied. Functions, cadences, secondary dominants and borrowed chords all appear here as tools, not as new material.
- [[Course 03 — Scale Construction]] — Lesson 4 is modal colour, which means spelling a mode from its own root.

**What this unlocks**

- [[Course 07 — Blues Form & Vocabulary]] and the jazz material — both are applied harmony with a different grammar, and both are easier once loop harmony is fluent.
- Practically: the ability to fix a stale loop on purpose, with a named move, instead of replacing the chords and hoping.

## The lessons

### Lesson 1 — How Loops Behave

**Read**
- [ ] [[Loop-Based Harmony]] → *Vamps and harmonic rhythm*, *Why four repeating chords is satisfying*, *What creates motion when harmony doesn't*

**The material**
- The lesson exists to establish one thing: in loop-based music the harmony is the floor, not the event.
- **[[POP-C01 — Harmonic rhythm|Harmonic rhythm]] is how fast the chords change, independent of tempo.** A track at 140 BPM with one chord per four bars has slow harmonic rhythm; the same tempo with two chords per bar has fast harmonic rhythm.
- Loop production wants it **slow**. The loop repeats dozens of times, so the interest has to come from drums, sound design and the vocal — and fast changes keep pulling attention back to the chords. Over a two-bar loop that gets exhausting quickly.
- **[[POP-C02 — Why four chords is satisfying|Predictability is the feature]], not a compromise.** Once the ear stops tracking the harmony it attends to the topline and the groove instead, and the progression becomes a bed rather than a series of events.
- The corollary is the useful half: **if a loop is boring, the chords usually aren't the problem.** Motion in a loop comes from rhythm, melody, timbre and arrangement. Rewriting the progression is the fix people reach for first and it's rarely the one that works.

**Concepts**
- [[POP-C01 — Harmonic rhythm]]
- [[POP-C02 — Why four chords is satisfying]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — take a loop that feels stale and list everything that changes across 8 bars *other than* the chords. If the list is empty, the diagnosis is done.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — take that same 4-chord loop and add 8 bars of non-harmonic change: filter movement, an arrival hat, a doubled bass octave. Bounce before and after, and play them back to back without looking at which is which.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 06 — Lesson 1 Test]]

### Lesson 2 — Motion Without Chord Changes

**Read**
- [ ] [[Loop-Based Harmony]] → *Pedal point*, *Four chords with a moving bass*, *Reharmonizing a repeated section*, *Voicing over a loop*
- [ ] [[Chromatic Bass Lines]]

**The material**
- Four ways to make a loop move while the progression stays where it is.
- **[[POP-C03 — Pedal point|A pedal point is a sustained bass note held while the chords above it change]]** — usually the tonic or the dominant. An EDM build *is* a **dominant pedal**: sitting on V, refusing to resolve, so the drop supplies the release. The technique is older than the genre by about four centuries.
- **[[POP-C04 — Four chords with a moving bass|A moving bass does the inverse]]** — hold the chords, walk the bass through inversions. `C – G/B – Am – F` reads as far more movement than plain I–V–vi–IV contains, because **the ear takes the lowest note as the root**.
- **[[POP-C05 — Reharmonizing a loop|Three harmonic fixes for a stale loop]]**: reharmonize the last bar so each pass resolves differently, insert a secondary dominant to pull harder into a chord (`Am` becomes `E7 Am`), or move the bass under static harmony.
- **[[POP-C06 — Sus and add9 under a vocal|Sus2, sus4 and add9 voicings remove or blur the 3rd]]** — the note that decides major or minor — so a pad stops colliding with the topline and lets the vocal define the harmony. A topline landing on the **9th** adds colour without changing function: it isn't in the triad, so it floats.

**Concepts**
- [[POP-C03 — Pedal point]]
- [[POP-C04 — Four chords with a moving bass]]
- [[POP-C05 — Reharmonizing a loop]]
- [[POP-C06 — Sus and add9 under a vocal]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — take one 4-chord loop and produce three versions: a tonic pedal, a walking bass under unchanged chords, and a reharmonized last bar. Name what each one did to the feel.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — keep all three versions in one Session row and audition them in order. Then re-voice the pad as sus2 or add9 and check whether the topline sits better against it.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 06 — Lesson 2 Test]]

### Lesson 3 — The Axis and Its Rotations

**Read**
- [ ] [[The Axis Progression]] in full

**The material**
- **[[POP-C07 — The Axis family and its rotations|Four chords, four rotations, and whichever one the loop starts and returns to is heard as home.]]** I–V–vi–IV is bright and lifting — "Let It Be", "Don't Stop Believin'". vi–IV–I–V is the identical pitch content reading as darker and more yearning.
- That's a real tool, not a curiosity: a section's mood can be flipped without changing a single chord, only the point at which the bar line falls.
- Underneath it sits [[POP-C08 — Relative-key switching|the relative major/minor relationship]] — same key signature, same seven notes, different tonal centre.
- Which is why a chorus that moves from C to Am darkens **without any modulation machinery**. Nothing is borrowed and no note is new; only the centre of gravity moved.

**Concepts**
- [[POP-C07 — The Axis family and its rotations]]
- [[POP-C08 — Relative-key switching]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — write one 4-chord loop and play all four rotations. Name which you'd use for a verse and which for a chorus, and say what decided it.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — record each rotation as its own clip on piano. Label one "verse" and one "chorus", then build a two-section sketch that uses both and changes nothing else.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 06 — Lesson 3 Test]]

### Lesson 4 — Colour From Outside the Key

**Read**
- [ ] [[Modal Pop]], then [[Chromatic Mediants and Pop Modulation]]

**The material**
- Three modes carry most of the modal colour in pop, and each one is defined by a single note that produces a single chord.
- **[[POP-C09 — Mixolydian ♭VII|Mixolydian gives you ♭VII]].** In C that's B♭, as in `C–B♭–F`. There's no leading tone, so it lifts and drifts rather than pulling home — it reads equally as borrowing from the parallel minor. It's a Beatles signature, alongside descending chromatic bass under a held chord.
- **[[POP-C10 — Dorian IV vs Aeolian iv|Dorian gives you a major IV]].** `Dm–G` is Dorian, because G major needs a B natural — Dorian's raised 6th. `Dm–Gm` is natural minor, with a B♭. **One note is the entire colour difference:** Dorian lifts, Aeolian stays dark.
- **Aeolian gives you ♭VI–♭VII–i** — F–G–Am in A minor, all diatonic to natural minor. It's the cadence under a great deal of trap and film scoring.
- Then two ways pop leaves the key. **[[POP-C11 — Chromatic mediants and pop modulation|A chromatic mediant is a chord a 3rd away with a quality the key doesn't provide]]** — in C those are **E, A, E♭ and A♭ major**. It shares a common tone and has no dominant pull, so it reads as a scene change rather than a modulation.
- The **truck driver's modulation** shoves everything up a step for the last chorus, unprepared. It's a cliché because it announces itself. Subtler alternatives: shift mode instead of key, approach the new tonic through its own V, or pivot on a shared chord.

**Concepts**
- [[POP-C09 — Mixolydian ♭VII]]
- [[POP-C10 — Dorian IV vs Aeolian iv]]
- [[POP-C11 — Chromatic mediants and pop modulation]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — take a minor loop and play it Dorian, then Aeolian, changing only the 6th. Say which chord each version produced and name the mode from that chord alone.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — drop a chromatic mediant into bar 4 of a loop in C — try E major, then A♭ major — and keep whichever one surprised you. Then try the same lift as a mode shift instead and compare how loudly each announces itself.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 06 — Lesson 4 Test]]
