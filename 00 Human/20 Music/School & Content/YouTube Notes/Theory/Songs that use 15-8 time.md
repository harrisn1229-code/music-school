# Songs that use 15/8 time

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ERnuYncziSc)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ERnuYncziSc)

## Synopsis

Continues the odd-meter series with 15/8, and doubles as the series' clearest illustration that a fixed beat-count can be grouped in genuinely different ways to produce very different feels — plus the simple/compound time distinction, since 15/8 grouped in fives of three is "quintuple compound time."

## Techniques

- **The 7+8 grouping and its uncanny, beat-skipping effect**: Mike Oldfield's "Tubular Bells" divides its 15/8 bar into an alternating 7-beat group then an 8-beat group — Oldfield's own count: "one two three four five six seven / one two three four five six seven eight." Producer Tom Newman credited this exact meter for the 26-minute instrumental's unlikely mainstream success: a bar of plain 8+8 would let the brain switch off, but the alternating 7/8 makes every other rotation feel like it "trips you up" (skips a beat), forcing continuous attention. Same 7+8 grouping: the opening guitar riff of Led Zeppelin's "The Ocean" (verses are plain 4/4; only the riff is 15/8), and The Pretenders' "Tattooed Love Boys."
- **A different grouping of the same 15 beats, for a different feel**: Biffy Clyro's "Mountains" (UK #5, 2008 — likely the highest-charting 15/8 song) groups its verse as a bar of 6/4 followed by a bar of 3/8, rather than 7+8 — same total beat count, distinctly different internal shape and accent pattern.
- **Simple vs. compound time, and 15/8 as "quintuple compound time"**: simple time (4/4) has the quarter note as the prevailing beat, each beat splitting into 2 eighth notes; compound time has a dotted quarter note as the prevailing beat, each beat splitting into 3 eighth notes, which is what gives compound meters their swinging feel. 6/8 = compound duple (2 dotted-quarter beats), 9/8 = compound triple (3), 12/8 = compound quadruple (4), and 15/8 grouped as five even groups of three eighth notes = compound quintuple (5 beats in the bar). Compound 15/8 examples: the outro of Queens of the Stone Age's "I Think I Lost My Headache"; the first section of Soundgarden's "Limo Wreck."
- **The same 15/8 total regrouped differently verse-to-verse, by design, for a deliberately different feel each time** — Dream Theater's "A Change of Seasons," in drummer Mike Portnoy's own words: verse 1 is phrased as a bar of 3/4 (= 6/8) plus a bar of 9/8 (6+9=15); verse 2 is instead phrased as two bars of 6/8 (giving a triplet feel, 6+6=12) plus a bar of 3/8 (12+3=15) — same arithmetic total both times, deliberately different internal grouping each verse specifically so the meter doesn't read as static even though the numbers repeat. A clean worked case for treating a fixed beat-count as a grouping choice, not a fixed shape.
- **Odd meter used deliberately to raise gameplay difficulty**: the boss-battle theme in *Mother 3*'s soundtrack (the "Barrier Trio" encounter) is in 15/8 specifically because the game requires the player to tap along with the music to damage the boss — the unusual meter is a difficulty mechanic, not just a compositional choice.
