# 6 EASY METAL SOLOS That Sound Advanced!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jUd4j9iMaiY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jUd4j9iMaiY)

## Synopsis

BERNTH plays six escalating solo takes over the same simple backing track (root notes A–C–B, a 7-string metal track built with Toontrack plugins), then explains the "twist" behind each — the trick that makes it sound harder/more advanced than it actually is. Genuinely six distinct, transferable devices rather than a difficulty-ladder repeat of the same idea: rhythmic displacement, alternate-picking scale sequences with an off-beat entry, per-note chord-tone targeting (including deliberately borrowed chord qualities for color), alternate-picking/legato balance, 5-string sweep-picking arpeggio shapes, and two phrasing devices (a harmonic-minor-flavored bend, wide interval spacing).

## What's taught

- **Level 1 — rhythmic displacement + pentatonic extension.** Play A minor pentatonic (2-notes-per-string box) in eighth-note triplets against a backing track that has a straight 16th-note feel — the mismatch creates a polyrhythmic feel from otherwise basic note material. Stated explicitly as the trick: "try to consciously experiment playing triplets against straight beats." Second device in the same take: extend the standard 2-notes-per-string pentatonic box to a 3-notes-per-string phrasing for more melodic possibility, plus a repeated note (A on 5th and 10th fret), a blues blue note, and a closing pinch/natural harmonic.
- **Level 2 — alternate-picking scale sequences, off-beat entry.** Play a "first four notes, back to the second note, next four notes" ascending sequence pattern in the 2nd 3-notes-per-string position of A minor — but start the phrase on the *second* 16th note of the beat (a 16th rest first) while keeping the picking pattern unchanged (down-up-down-up straight through, not reset to match the new starting position). Not starting phrases on beat 1 is called out as what makes scale sequences sound more interesting. Second half: a simple alternate-picked melody deliberately accenting the off-beats.
- **Level 3 — per-note chord-tone targeting, including borrowed chord quality for color.** Instead of running one scale over the whole backing riff (as in levels 1–2), track each individual note change in the backing (A, then C, then B) and play an arpeggio that matches *that* note: A minor arpeggio (root-♭3-5) with a chromatic slide up to the ♭6 and back to the 5th; C major/maj7 arpeggio (the iii chord of A minor) with a tapped major 3rd an octave up. On the B note, the "correct" diatonic chord would be B half-diminished (m7♭5) — instead he deliberately substitutes a **B major arpeggio** (a chord quality not in the key) then slides up to the ♯5/augmented 5th and back, producing an "advanced" augmented color from a single-note backing that doesn't actually dictate a chord quality. Framed as legal specifically because the backing is a single melodic line, not a full chord — you can reharmonize a bare note freely since nothing contradicts it.
- **Level 4 — balancing alternate picking and legato within one line.** Two nearly-identical symmetrical A minor scale positions (from level 2) played in 16th-note triplets, switching between straight triplets and straight 16ths mid-lick for rhythmic variety, then finishing the phrase in legato (hammer-ons/pull-offs) rather than alternate picking. Stated rule: balance alternate picking (aggressive sound) against legato (fluid, fusion-style sound) rather than defaulting to one for an entire lick.
- **Level 5 — 5-string sweep-picking arpeggio shapes.** Per-note chord-tone targeting again (same idea as level 3) but voiced as full 5-string sweep-picked arpeggio shapes instead of 2-3-note fragments: a common A minor 5-string shape, an "easy" C major shape (technically fingered as an A-minor-shape variant), a B major shape one fret down from the C shape (last note changed on the A string), then C major and B major shapes that require middle-finger rolling/barring to separate otherwise-simultaneous notes — flagged as the hardest of the five shapes shown.
- **Level 6 — two phrasing devices.** (1) A half-step bend into the root from a half-step below (G♯ bent up to A) at the start of the phrase, with vibrato — this targets the raised 7th degree that's characteristic of A harmonic minor, giving an "exotic harmonic minor sound" via a single bent note without ever playing the full scale. (2) Wide interval spacing: deliberately space notes farther apart (stretching + tapping) instead of playing adjacent scalar motion, producing a "piano player" quality — applied both to a pure-spacing phrase and to a mixed scale/arpeggio phrase in the same solo.
