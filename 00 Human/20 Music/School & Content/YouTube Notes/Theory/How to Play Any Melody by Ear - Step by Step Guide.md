# How to Play Any Melody by Ear - Step by Step Guide

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-gH4sfZ7ALg)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-gH4sfZ7ALg)

## Synopsis
A compact, interactive walkthrough (Max Konyi explicitly designed it to be paused and worked along with) of the 5-step process for figuring out and playing back a melody purely by ear, assuming scale-degree feeling-state recognition ([[Scale Degree Ear Training]]) is already in place. This is the melody-specific companion to [[Chords by Ear: The Real Process]] — same "map what you can, deduce the rest, verify on an instrument" shape, applied to a single melodic line instead of a chord. Konyi plugs his Sonop app for the underlying scale-degree skill and leaves a practice song + answer key in the description for viewers to try the method live.

## Techniques
- **Step 1 — Memorize the melody first.** Sing or hum it back until it's internalized before doing any analysis — the whole method works from your own memory of the melody, not from repeatedly re-listening. Doesn't need to be pitch-perfect, just close enough to recognize. If singing pitches back is hard, Konyi flags it as a skill worth training in its own right — he calls it something he uses "in every facet of my musical life" despite not identifying as a singer.
- **Step 2 — Map the melodic contour, shape only, no pitches yet.** For each note, note only whether it moves up or down from the previous note and roughly by how much (step / leap / large leap) — explicitly NOT scale-degree numbers yet, just the up/down shape. **Draw it on paper** if visualizing melody-as-contour is new — physically sketching the shape helps internalize it.
- **Step 3 — Sing it slowly, one note at a time, feeling for the tonic and any other recognizable degrees.** Linger on each tone rather than singing through at tempo. In particular hunt for the **tonic** (the home/resolving note) — though it's likely present in the melody, there's no guarantee. Pencil in any degrees you can confidently *feel* (not guess) as candidates directly onto the contour map from Step 2.
- **Step 4 — Deduce the rest.** Combine the contour's shape with the degrees already pinned down in Step 3, plus music theory, to work out what the remaining degrees must logically be. Then sing the whole thing back and check whether the felt states actually match the guesses — flagged as easy to fool yourself here, since certain scale degrees share enough harmonic character to be mistaken for each other (see [[Scale Degree Ear Training]]'s confusion table).
- **Step 5 — Verify on a real instrument, the "no-perfect-pitch" tonic-finding trick.** Sing the note believed to be the tonic, then play a genuinely **random** note on the instrument, and try to identify what scale degree that random note is *relative to the sung tonic*. Once that degree is identified, back-calculate the tonic's actual pitch from it (worked example in the video: "if A is scale degree 2, then G must be 1" — reasoning through the G major scale). **Repeat rather than shortcut this** — sing tonic, play note, identify degree, repeat, and if it's still not resolving, change the random note and go again; Konyi is explicit not to "mash a bunch of keys" instead. Once the tonic pitch is confirmed, apply the full contour map onto it and play the melody back against the source to check.
- **Analyze mistakes, don't just move past them.** If the played-back melody doesn't match, the valuable part is diagnosing exactly *which* degree got mistaken for which and *why* — "there's a lot of gold to be found in this and it's a very humbling process."
- **Final consolidation step — fuse sound, label, and instrument together.** Once the melody is correctly worked out, alternate repeatedly between three modes: (1) just listening to the real recording while holding the known scale degrees in mind, (2) singing the melody solo while holding the degrees in mind, and (3) singing along *with* the recording while holding the degrees in mind. Repetition across all three, in isolation and in context, is what eventually lets the melody be heard and identified in real time with zero pausing or singing required — the explicit end state the whole method is training toward.
