# 32-Bar Song Form

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Jazz Harmony]]. The dominant shape of the pre-bebop/swing-era American songbook repertoire — most standards that aren't a blues or [[Rhythm Changes]]'s AABA-with-bridge use some version of this instead. Where Rhythm Changes is "four 8-bar sections, A A B A," this is the **other** common 32-bar layout: **two 16-bar halves**, the second largely repeating the first with a different ending.

## The 16+16 shape

- **Two 16-bar halves.** The second half repeats most of the first half's material, changing mainly the ending so the tune can actually close instead of looping forever.
- **The first chord of each half is usually — not always — the tonic.** "Just Friends" is a documented counter-example: it opens on IV.
- **The first half's ending doesn't resolve to I.** It needs to reach V (or a V-substitute) so the second half has somewhere to launch from; the *second* half's ending is the tune's real cadence.

### Recurring devices, by structural slot

The practical value of this form isn't memorizing any one chart — it's recognizing that unrelated standards by different composers keep reusing the same move in the same *position*. Once the slot is familiar, a new chart in this form reduces to "which variant of the usual devices is this one using," not 32 bars of arbitrary chords.

| Slot | Device | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Early first half | Secondary ii–V to **vi** | Grouped and thought of as one unit, "a 2-5 to six" — never as two separate chords → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Secondary Dominants]] |
| Early-to-mid first half | Secondary ii–V to **IV** | Same chunking logic, "a 2-5 to four" → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Secondary Dominants]] |
| On/around the IV chord | **IV → IV-minor → I** | The same functional shape as [[Borrowed Chords]]'s "heartbreak cadence" (IV→iv→I), confirmed as a real, positionally-predictable device in the jazz standard repertoire, not just a pop/Beatles move. Realized differently tune to tune — sometimes a literal minor IV chord with the minor 3rd audible in the melody, sometimes a backdoor-flavored dominant a half step below the eventual target, sometimes a ♭VII7-ish move. Statistically the most likely single bar to carry this device across the form. |
| Last line of the first half | **V7/V ("the dominant of the dominant")** | Very common in major-key standards specifically in this slot. Can appear plain, as a full ii–V into it, or **tritone-substituted** and still functionally read as the same device → [[Secondary Dominants]] |
| End of first half → top of second half | No resolution to I | The whole reason the form needs two halves — the first half's job is to arrive back at V, not I. |
| Near the very end | A diminished/half-diminished reharmonization | Common in the closing bars of the second half, often feeding a chain back through a secondary dominant into the final turnaround → [[Diminished Chords]] |
| Final 1–2 bars | A turnaround | Length isn't fixed — most tunes in this form use a 2-bar turnaround, but at least one documented tune ("It Could Happen to You") compresses to a **1-bar** turnaround instead → [[Turnarounds]] |

## The 8+8 (asymmetric) variant

Not every tune in this family splits its 16-bar half evenly. Some ("Out of Nowhere," "Just Friends") read more like an 8-bar phrase followed by a contrasting 8-bar phrase, rather than one continuous 16-bar idea — and the slot-devices above show up **inconsistently** in these looser tunes: the IV-minor cadence or the dominant-of-the-dominant may be missing from a slot where the strict 16+16 tunes would place it.

**The reason traces to the melody, not an independent harmonic rule.** In the documented examples, the harmony moves less in the phrase where the melody itself is more static, and moves more where the melody is more active — the harmonic slot devices above are really a description of what tends to happen *under a certain kind of melodic phrase*, and a tune whose melody is built differently legitimately skips them. **Practical implication: learn the melody alongside the harmony** — the "why" behind a form deviation is usually sitting in the tune, not a harmony rule being broken.

## Learning method: form first, chords second

The efficient way to internalize a tune in this form (or transcribe one by ear) is **top-down, not chord-by-chord**:

1. Establish the **form** first — how many bars, how many sections, where the halves/phrases split. No chords yet.
2. Note the **key** if it's already clear.
3. Fill in chords while listening, leaving anything unclear open rather than guessing.
4. **Exploit repetition** — once a section has been worked out, a later repeat of the same section (or a whole standard sharing this form's shape) usually confirms rather than re-derives.

Directly analogous to the 12-bar blues shortcut: knowing just one binary fact ("does it go to IV in bar 2 or not") is often enough to play a new blues immediately, because the rest is shared vocabulary. The same logic scales up to the 32-bar standard form via the slot table above. → [[Transcribing and Playing by Ear]]
