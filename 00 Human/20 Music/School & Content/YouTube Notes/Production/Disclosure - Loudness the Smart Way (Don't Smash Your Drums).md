# Disclosure: Loudness The SMART Way (Don't Smash Your Drums)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xfqCnOYjRNY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xfqCnOYjRNY)

## Synopsis

A tight, practical drum-bus gain-staging lesson: get loudness from compression + saturation working together subtly, not from hard limiting. Disclosure's target is a drum bus that sounds almost the same bypassed vs. engaged — just "a soft squelch in the hi-hats" — while quietly gaining several dB of perceived loudness. The exception that proves the rule: for this specific raw, minimal track (drums/bass/beeps only) he pushes it much harder than he says he ever would on a pop song with a soft lead vocal up front.

## Techniques

- **Plan bus levels by number so the master bus lands with headroom: drum bus ≈ -8 dB, vocals ≈ -8 dB, bass ≈ -10 dB, pads/synths ≈ -10 dB, summing to roughly -6 to -4 dB into the master compressor/limiter.** A deliberate, numbers-first gain-staging habit rather than "turn it up until it feels loud enough" — knowing each bus's rough target lets him predict the master input level before he even gets there. → [[Gain Staging]]
- **Avoid hard limiting on drums — prefer compressor-driven distortion over limiter-driven distortion.** Limiters make their own kind of distortion, and while it "can be nice," he prefers the character a compressor produces instead; the limiter on this bus is barely working, engaging only in the instant the kick and clap hit simultaneously. → [[Limiting]], [[Compression]]
- **Stack two saturation types (tape + tube) as the main loudness-and-character engine, not the limiter.** Framed conceptually: saturation is literally clipping the tops off transients, and "whatever shape it chooses to make that square is the character of your distortion" — different saturation types clip differently, which is why stacking two flavors (not just cranking one) shapes the character more precisely. → [[Saturation and Distortion]]
- **A/B calibration method: crank saturation way past the target to hear exactly what it's doing, then pull back until bypassed-vs-engaged is nearly inaudible.** Pushed hard, the drum bus turns into an obviously "techno rave" sound — useful specifically as a way to *hear* the saturation's character in isolation — then dialed back to where the target effect (a soft squelch in the hats, levels roughly unchanged) is barely perceptible as a difference at all. → [[Referencing and Translation]]
- **Watch for the classic over-compression tell on a drum bus: hats/shakers/tambourines get relatively louder while kicks/claps get relatively quieter.** Squashing a drum bus too hard pulls up the tails/room noise of the quieter transient-heavy elements (hats, shakers) while crushing the loud transient elements (kick, clap) down — an explicit diagnostic to listen for, not just a GR-meter number. → [[Compression]], [[Masking and Carving]]
- **A light-touch compressor (~-4 dB GR, ~30% wet/dry blend) still meaningfully "adds up" in combination with the saturation stages** — no single stage is doing much on its own; the cumulative effect across compression + two saturation types + a barely-active limiter is what does the real work, not any one aggressive stage. → [[Parallel Processing]]
- **Push a drum bus into audibly raw/distorted "hitting the red" territory only when the arrangement is minimal enough to support it** — explicitly more distortion than he'd ever use on a normal pop song with a soft lead vocal up front; justified here because the track *is* just drums, bass, and beeps, so the grit becomes the character rather than fighting other elements for space.
- **Two-layer kick, non-identical samples, frequency-separated instead of phase-aligned.** Rather than actively phase-aligning a two-kick layer (a synth "low kick" plus a separate acoustic-sample "top kick"), just listen and accept it's usually close enough to in-phase — the more load-bearing move is rolling the lows OFF the acoustic top kick and keeping the lows ONLY on the low kick, so the two layers barely occupy the same frequency space to begin with, which minimizes how much any phase issue could matter even if one exists. → [[Kick Design]], [[Phase Alignment]]
