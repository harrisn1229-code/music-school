# Using Space and Pauses in Phrasing

Part of [[Music Theory]] — a companion to [[Singing Along With Your Playing (Phrasing)]] (a different training method aimed at the same four-element phrasing target), [[Phrasing Blueprints]] (a close cousin: also a fixed-rhythm/free-pitch phrase drill from the same channel, but built around letter-form repetition — A A' B, A A A B, etc. — rather than rests), [[Blues Lead Phrasing and Touch]] (note-choice/touch vocabulary within a phrase), and [[Solo Dynamics and Arc]] (the macro shape of a whole solo). This note is Jared's **"phrasing exercise #1"** from the same "Nail the Changes" course — a specific, leveled drill for building rests/space into an improvised line by controlling **only rhythm**, with note choice left completely free.

## The drill

Builds directly on the **"constant atonal improv"** base (play continuously, any pitch, zero rests — the same base [[Singing Along With Your Playing (Phrasing)]]'s step 1 uses) by **subtracting notes to create rests**, rather than adding anything new. Every level below is a **2-bar phrase**: bar 1 is 8 free-pitch eighth notes (any note, reach anywhere — pitch is never graded), bar 2 starts with a fixed rhythmic figure and then rests for the remainder of the bar. **Only the rhythm is controlled; pitch is always free** — this isolates rhythmic phrasing from note choice, the same split [[Solo Dynamics and Arc]]'s §Generating phrase material vocalize-a-rhythm drill uses (vocalize/fix a rhythm, vary only the pitches run through it).

- **Level 1 — land and rest.** Bar 1: 8 eighth notes (free pitch). Bar 2: land on beat 1, then rest for the remaining 3 beats. **Pass condition:** play the 2-bar phrase correctly 4 times in a row, no mistakes, at any tempo you're comfortable with. Demonstrated at 80 BPM.
- **Level 2 — add a second rhythmic variation, chain them.** Play Level 1's phrase 4 times in a row, then immediately chain into a second phrase 4 times in a row: identical shape, but bar 2's landing is a single quarter note instead of two eighth notes. **Pass condition:** both 4-rep blocks correct, no mistakes, at any comfortable tempo. Demonstrated at 100 BPM.
- **Level 3 — add a third variation, chain all three.** Adds a third phrase shape: bar 1 opens with a quarter note (instead of two eighth notes) followed by the remaining eighth notes, then bar 2 lands on **two quarter notes** instead of one. **Pass condition:** all three phrase-shapes (Level 1's, Level 2's, and this one), 4 reps each, correct in a row, at any comfortable tempo — the drill is cumulative, not a replacement of the earlier levels. Demonstrated at 120 BPM.
- **The 80/100/120 BPM tempos are the video's own worked example, not a mandated ladder** — any comfortable tempo passes a level; the point of raising tempo across levels is just to show the drill keeps working faster, not that faster is required.

## How to practice and test it

- **No metronome needed during practice** — freely work the phrase shapes without a click.
- **Use a metronome only when testing whether you've "passed" a level** — its job is to let you state a number (what tempo did I actually nail this at), not to be present for every rep.
- **Track the tempo you pass at, for future reference.** Write it down so you can return later and try to push it higher — the point isn't "I did it once," it's building a record you can beat over time.
- **Any tempo counts as a pass.** You don't need a fast tempo to check off a level — doing it cleanly one time at a tempo you chose is a real, complete win; speed is a separate, optional goal to chase afterward, not a requirement to check the box.
- **Optional layer-in:** combine with the tone/slur/accent "feel" elements from the prior module (see [[Singing Along With Your Playing (Phrasing)]] for the sibling method built on the same course base) once the rhythm is solid — not required, but stated to sound "really good and really musical" layered on top.

## What to notice while doing it

- **A rhythmically-correct phrase can still feel musically unsatisfying — that's useful information, not a failure.** The video's own last demonstrated phrase lands, in Jared's words, in "a weird spot" that "feels left hanging," despite being rhythmically exact. **Separate "did I execute the rhythm correctly" (what the drill grades) from "did that land musically" (a separate, worth-noticing judgment)** — carrying that noticing into real playing (which pitch choices make a given rhythmic landing feel resolved vs. hanging) is named as part of the actual payoff of the exercise, beyond just passing the level.
