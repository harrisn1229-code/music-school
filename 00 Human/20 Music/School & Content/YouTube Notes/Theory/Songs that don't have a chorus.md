# Songs that don't have a chorus

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OeApbSCslEc)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OeApbSCslEc)

## Synopsis

David Bennett defines a chorus precisely (a section periodically repeated with near-identical lyrics/music, usually the dynamic/lyrical focal point) and then catalogs five distinct ways a pop/rock song can get by without one — through-composed form, a chorus-substitute AABA structure, an instrumental "chorus," strophic form, and songs with contrasting sections where none is prominent enough to qualify. Each device is explained via the specific structural role it takes over from a conventional chorus, not just labeled.

## Techniques

- **Through-composed form** — never returns to a previous section once complete. "Bohemian Rhapsody" (Queen): five distinct sections, none repeated; the rock section supplies the dynamic peak/release a chorus would give, the operatic section supplies memorable hooks — neither is a chorus since neither repeats.
- **AABA as a chorus-substitute** — the dynamic arc comes from A/B contrast rather than a verse→chorus energy build ("a swinging ship" vs. "a rollercoaster"). Fell out of fashion after the 1970s but survives: "Make You Feel My Love" (Dylan/Adele), "Don't Know Why" (Norah Jones). Beatles examples: "Yesterday" (7-bar A, 12-bar contrasting B/middle-eight), "The Long and Winding Road" (12-bar A, 4-bar B).
- **Instrumental chorus** — a wordless riff/line fills the chorus's structural slot without lyrics. "Seven Nation Army" (White Stripes) — repeating verse riff, then a 2-bar buildup into a full-energy instrumental version of the riff where a chorus would go. "Baker Street" (Gerry Rafferty) — the iconic sax line plays the same role. In modern electronic pop the same device becomes **the drop**: muted verse → pre-chorus-like buildup → an all-tension-released instrumental peak instead of a sung chorus.
- **Strophic form** — one repeating verse section (AAA…), each verse new lyrics over identical music. Used wherever a group needs to sing along without rehearsal: national anthems, hymns/spirituals, nursery rhymes, folk songs, 12-bar blues (which often repeats the *same* lyric line twice within each verse for extra structure). Contrast still gets built in without a second section: subdividing the verse into sub-phrases ("God Save the Queen" — 6 bars + 8 bars), or fixing the final lyric line/title across every verse. Rock examples are rare but real: Beatles' "Tomorrow Never Knows" (near-strophic, broken by an instrumental section), Tame Impala's "Elephant" (same device).
- **No clean label** — multiple contrasting sections where none repeats/dominates enough to call a chorus. "Golden Brown" (The Stranglers) functions like an instrumental-chorus song — the harpsichord riff fills the role, with an extended solo section and brief vocal outro but no true chorus. "Up the Junction" (Squeeze) uses a long repeating 10-bar verse plus a 10-bar bridge, bookended by an instrumental — deliberately chorus-free because it's a storytelling song and a repeating chorus would eat time needed for the narrative; variety across the 12 verse repetitions comes from a key change and dynamic shifts rather than a hook.
