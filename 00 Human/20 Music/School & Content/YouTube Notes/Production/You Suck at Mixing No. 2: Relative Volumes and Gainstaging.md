# You Suck at Mixing No. 2: Relative Volumes and Gainstaging

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Pmn_ZJ1pdfg)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Pmn_ZJ1pdfg)

## Synopsis
Picks up right where #1 (Preparing Your Track for a Mixdown) leaves off: with every stem bounced, organized, and color-coded, this video walks through rebuilding the mix's relative volumes from complete silence. The core method is to pull every fader to −∞ and bring elements back in one at a time in priority order — most primary element first, then secondary and tertiary elements within the same instrument group, before moving on to the next group — while deliberately not stopping to fix tonal problems that surface along the way.

## Techniques
- **Rebuild relative volumes from silence after a normalized bounce** — because each stem was normalized independently on export, playing them all back together is abrasive and clips constantly (everything is as loud as it can individually be). Select every track and pull all faders down to −∞ before starting the relative-volumes pass. → [[Gain Staging]]
- **Establish the most primary element first, then work outward** — for most tracks that's the kick; bring it up to a safe, non-clipping starting point (roughly −12 dB) — the exact number doesn't matter much since every other element gets set relative to it, not to an absolute target.
- **Work within an instrument group before moving to the next group** — after the kick, bring up the snare, then a secondary/layered snare, then hi-hats (still within the drum group) before moving on to the next group's primary element (e.g. the bass). Finish establishing a group's internal balance before starting a new one.
- **Trust your ears over your eyes while setting levels** — bring a fader up slowly while looking away from the screen, judging purely by what's coming through the speakers rather than watching the meter or the fader position.
- **Loop a specific section instead of playing from the top** — elements dropping in and out as the track plays through different sections makes it hard to judge a level in isolation; loop the section you're actually balancing.
- **Keep a running "fix later" list instead of stopping to solve problems mid-pass** — when an element clearly needs EQ or compression (a muddy bass, in the example), don't detour to fix it now — write a mental or physical note and keep moving. This pass is about relative levels only; tone-shaping comes later in the mixdown process.
- **Session View's faders extend taller than the small Arrangement-view mixer strip** — for finer, more precise fader moves during this pass, pull the Session View mixer wider/taller rather than working in the cramped Arrangement view.
