# String Bending Technique

The mechanics of a clean, in-tune, professional-sounding bend — finger support, pitch accuracy at both ends, and stopping the note cleanly. A companion to [[Blues Lead Phrasing and Touch]] (which covers bend *placement/role* inside a phrase — delayed vibrato, chord-tracking bend distance, bend-as-color-tone) and [[Solo Dynamics and Arc]] (which covers the vibrato habit-break on landed notes); this note is the physical execution layer underneath both.

## The five fundamentals

- **Support the bend with every finger you can fit behind it, not just the bending finger.** Bending with the pinky → back it up with fingers 1, 2 and 3 all fretting the same string behind it. Bending with the 3rd finger → support with fingers 1 and 2. Bending with the 2nd finger → support with finger 1. A single unsupported finger *can* bend, but reliably only up to a half step — a full step (or more) needs the added strength of the supporting fingers stacked behind it. Rule of thumb: default to maximum support for the position you're in; drop to fewer fingers only when the lick's fingering forces it.
- **Let the starting pitch actually sound before you start bending.** The common mistake: starting the bend *as* you pluck, or a hair before — the attack lands already slightly sharp, which reads as an out-of-tune note rather than an intentional bend. You need a genuine (if tiny) moment of the true, unbent pitch before the bend begins. **Practice drill:** initiate the bend in slow motion first, so the ear can clearly separate "clean starting note" from "the bend," then gradually speed the same motion up until it's a normal fast bend — the goal is keeping that brief in-tune window even at full speed.
- **Stop the note at the peak of the bend — don't let the pitch slide back down.** Default rule: any audible sag back down after the peak reads as weak, sloppy, unintentional. Mute the string with the picking (right) hand right at the peak — you can still hold/sustain the bend at the peak as long as needed, the point is just that it never audibly releases. Verified against real recordings: Stevie Ray Vaughan's bends on "Mary Had a Little Lamb" hold clean at the peak with zero release, however small the bend; a bend that appears to "come down" in the "Let It Be" solo (The Beatles) is not actually the string releasing — it's re-plucked at the peak and then bent *down* on purpose (a deliberate down-bend, not a release). **Intentional down-bends are a distinct, separate technique** (bend up, pluck at the peak, then bend back down) — fine when it's controlled and chosen, but never the default.
- **Intonate the bend — land on the exact target pitch, not an approximation.** Bending is one of the only places on a fretted instrument where you have to actively listen for intonation the way a fretless or vocal performer does, since frets normally do that work for you — which is exactly why it's valuable ear-training as well as a technique. **Practice drill:** slide up to the target note first (to hear/learn the exact destination pitch), then bend up to that same note, and alternate sliding vs. bending back and forth until the bend reliably lands on the identical pitch as the slide.
- **Hook the neighboring string under your bending fingertip so it can't slip between your finger and the fretboard.** A named, common failure mode: the adjacent (usually lower-pitched) string slips in under the fretting finger mid-bend, which steals pressure and makes the target note sag/go flat — exactly the "coming down" flaw the peak-stopping fundamental above warns against. Fix: catch that neighboring string on the fingertip itself and let it get pulled *up and away* from the fretboard as you bend, rather than letting it wedge underneath. Same mechanism doubles as **bending vibrato control** — once the neighboring string is hooked and being pushed against, you can oscillate the bend's width using that leverage without losing grip.

## Practice sequencing

Learn the five in order roughly as taught — support → starting pitch → stop-at-peak → intonation → string-hooking — since #3 (stop at peak) and #5 (hook the string) reinforce the same failure mode (pitch sag) from two different mechanical causes (releasing on purpose vs. losing grip by accident), and #2/#4 are both ear-training drills (slide-to-pitch, slow-motion-to-full-speed) worth running against a tuner or drone before trusting them by feel.

## From videos

- **Semitone (half-step) bends are commonly OVER-bent, landing closer to a whole-tone bend than intended, because the small physical distance is easy to misjudge by feel.** Fix: deliberately check the pitch (by ear or reference) and land exactly on the intended half-step rather than bending "a little further" for extra expression — overshooting changes the actual note/harmonic content of the bend, not just its intensity. → BERNTH, [[Biggest Secret To Playing Better Solos!]]

- **Combine multiple bend variations in one phrase for expressive variety, instead of defaulting to a single bend type (usually the whole-tone bend) throughout a solo.** Named variations worth mixing: **bend-and-release** (bend up, then release back down on purpose — see this page's intentional-down-bend distinction above), **pre-bend** (bend the string up silently before plucking, then release to pitch — the opposite order from a normal bend), **whole-tone and half-tone bends**, **unison bends** (bend one string up to match the pitch of an adjacent unfretted string), and **a bend on top of an already-bent note** (bend to the first target, then bend further to a second target without releasing). Worked example lick strings several of these together with vibrato added at the end. Explicitly flagged: relying on only one bend variation (and ignoring the rest) is itself a common soloing weakness. → BERNTH, [[6 Mistakes That RUIN Your Guitar SOLOS!]]
- **Slide-into-bend across multiple positions, to fake one giant continuous pitch rise.** Slide from a low note up several frets/positions, then bend the arrived note a further whole step — timed so the bend begins slightly *before* the slide physically ends, blending the two motions into what reads as a single, larger-than-possible bend rather than an audible slide-then-bend. Worked example spans three pentatonic box positions (slide from the lowest note of position 1 up to bending into the highest note of position 3). — BERNTH, [5 EASY METAL GUITAR SOLO TRICKS (that sound advanced!)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lQ4naP--w04)
