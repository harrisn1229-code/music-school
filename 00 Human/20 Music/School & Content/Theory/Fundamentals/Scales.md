# Scales

Part of [[Music Theory]]. A scale = a sequence of pitches in order, defined by its pattern of half/whole steps (see [[Notes & Pitches]]). The scale's starting pitch is the **tonic**; its numbered pitches are **degrees**.

## Major scale
Step pattern: **W W H W W W H**

| Key | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C | C | D | E | F | G | A | B |
| G | G | A | B | C | D | E | F♯ |
| D | D | E | F♯ | G | A | B | C♯ |
| A | A | B | C♯ | D | E | F♯ | G♯ |
| E | E | F♯ | G♯ | A | B | C♯ | D♯ |
| B | B | C♯ | D♯ | E | F♯ | G♯ | A♯ |
| F♯ | F♯ | G♯ | A♯ | B | C♯ | D♯ | E♯ |
| G♭ | G♭ | A♭ | B♭ | C♭ | D♭ | E♭ | F |
| D♭ | D♭ | E♭ | F | G♭ | A♭ | B♭ | C |
| A♭ | A♭ | B♭ | C | D♭ | E♭ | F | G |
| E♭ | E♭ | F | G | A♭ | B♭ | C | D |
| B♭ | B♭ | C | D | E♭ | F | G | A |
| F | F | G | A | B♭ | C | D | E |
| C♯ | C♯ | D♯ | E♯ | F♯ | G♯ | A♯ | B♯ |
| C♭ | C♭ | D♭ | E♭ | F♭ | G♭ | A♭ | B♭ |

- Each scale uses each letter name exactly once — that's why C♯ vs D♭ spelling matters ([[Notes & Pitches]])
- Ordered by sharps/flats added = the [[Circle of Fifths]]; the accidentals per key = [[Key Signatures]]
- **C♯ major and C♭ major are the two "all-accidental" major keys** — 7 sharps and 7 flats respectively, so every degree of C major (C D E F G A B) just gets sharped or flatted straight across: C♯ major = C♯ D♯ E♯ F♯ G♯ A♯ B♯ (needs E♯ and B♯, not F/C); C♭ major = C♭ D♭ E♭ F♭ G♭ A♭ B♭ (needs F♭, not E). Both are real, usable keys even though they're less common — a capo'd guitarist ends up in C♯ major from C major shapes at the 1st fret, and a horn player who thinks in flats may prefer C♭ major over its enharmonic twin B major. → [[Notes & Pitches]] (why E♯/B♯/C♭/F♭ exist), [[Key Signatures]] (7-sharp/7-flat rows) — Daniel Seriff, [BIGGEST Music Theory MYTH That Will RUIN Your Understanding](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ukUxGfmYJlQ)

## Spelling rules — building a valid major scale
Four rules that guarantee a major scale/key is spelled correctly (and explain why keys like F♯, C♯, G♭, and C♭ need "funny" accidentals like E♯, B♯, C♭, F♭ — see [[Notes & Pitches]]):
1. **No mixing sharps and flats in one key** — a key is all-sharp or all-flat, never both.
2. **All seven letters (A–G) must appear, one of each** — never skip a letter or double one up.
3. **Never use two different accidental versions of the same letter in one key** — no G *and* G♭ together, no A *and* A♯ together.
4. **The next scale degree is always the next letter of the musical alphabet** (wrapping G → A) — never restate the same letter for the next note even if it's enharmonically simpler to think that way.
Applying these forces the "funny" spellings: e.g. F♯ major's raised 6th-to-7th step lands on the pitch also known as F, but rule 2 already used F♯ for the tonic and rule 4 demands the letter after D♯ is E — so it has to be spelled **E♯**, not F. Same logic gives G♭ major its **C♭** (the pitch also known as B) and C♯/C♭ major their full sharp/flat sets above. — Daniel Seriff, [BIGGEST Music Theory MYTH That Will RUIN Your Understanding](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ukUxGfmYJlQ)

## Natural minor scale
Formula vs. major: **1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7** (lower degrees 3, 6, 7 a half-step)

- Every minor scale shares its pitches with one major scale = its **relative major** ([[Keys]])
- Find relative minor: go DOWN two scale degrees from the major tonic (C → A minor)
- Find relative major: go UP two degrees from the minor tonic (B minor → D major)
- For harmonic & melodic minor (and their modes/chords): [[Scale Modes Reference]]
- For the 7 modes generated by starting the major scale on each of its own degrees (Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian…) and the mood each one carries: [[Diatonic Modes]]

## Extended scale degrees (above 8)
Degrees keep counting past the octave and repeat pitch names:
**9 = 2 · 10 = 3 · 11 = 4 · 12 = 5 · 13 = 6**
This is where 9th/11th/13th chord names come from — see [[Extended Chords]] and [[Chords by Key]] (chords are stacked from every-other degree: 1-3-5, 2-4-6, 5-7-9…).

## Pentatonic scales
- **Major pentatonic**: 1 2 3 5 6 (major scale minus 4 and 7). Feel: simple/folk/rock. Contains all the notes of the key's I chord; all pitches adjacent on the [[Circle of Fifths]]
- **Minor pentatonic**: 1 ♭3 4 5 ♭7. Used over minor keys/chords — and over major-key **blues**, where the key-based minor pentatonic is played against major chords
- Two ways to use either one:
	- **Key-based** — one pentatonic scale for the whole progression
	- **Chord-based** — switch to each chord's own pentatonic as the chords change
- Bonus: **major hexatonic** (1 2 3 4 5 6) — rarer 6-note pitch palette (Bruce Hornsby-style)

## From videos

- **Why the pentatonic scale is "foolproof," plus a cross-cultural universality argument.** Pentatonic = the parent major/minor scale with its two semitone-adjacent (tension-generating) degrees removed — major pentatonic drops the 4th and 7th, minor pentatonic drops its own two semitone-adjacent degrees — so no scale tone strongly pulls toward resolution, meaning a pentatonic melody can play over any diatonic chord in the key and reliably sound fine. Direct consequence: pentatonic melodies reharmonize unusually easily ("Uptown Funk," Bruno Mars, cited). **Piano's black keys ARE a pentatonic scale** (G♭ major pentatonic or E♭ minor pentatonic depending on chosen root) — any combination of black keys is guaranteed consonant for the same semitone-removal reason ("I Don't Like Mondays," Boomtown Rats, sweeps the black keys directly). **Near-universal across cultures, not a Western-guitar convention**: Chinese gong/yu scales map onto Western major/minor pentatonic (the guqin's open strings are typically tuned to major pentatonic); Hindustani classical raga scales have direct pentatonic equivalents. **One honest limitation**: pentatonic is excellent for melody but poor for harmony (too few notes for a rich chord vocabulary) — virtually every real pentatonic-melody song is harmonized with chords that reach outside the pentatonic set. → [[Circle of Fifths]] (pentatonic notes are all adjacent on it). — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use the Pentatonic scales]]
- **A minimum-viable-repertoire claim: three named scales, each tied to a concrete listening example, cover 90%+ of common riffs.** Given as the "minimum theory way" to skip a fuller scale/triad/major-minor education and still cover most gig-band material: **major pentatonic** (cited example: **Queen**), **minor pentatonic** (cited example: **Audioslave**), and the **blues scale** (cited example: **"Sunshine of Your Love,"** the same internalization riff already used for the blues scale elsewhere in the vault, see [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]] § *From videos*).
