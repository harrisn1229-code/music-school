# One-Position Fretboard Overview Drill

A four-step practice method for building real fretboard command by *restricting* yourself, not by memorizing more shapes: take one song you already know cold and force the entire solo — every chorus, every chord — to stay inside one general area of the neck. The restriction is what does the work: instead of reaching for a habitual lick, you're forced to inventory what's actually playable right there and build music out of it. Complements [[Guitar Positions]]'s note-by-note position maps (this drill is what makes those maps useful for playing, not just lookup) and sits alongside [[Pentatonic Mastery Practice Routine]] and [[CAGE System Scale Shapes]] as another "confine the hand, force new vocabulary" system — this one is organized around a whole tune's changes rather than one scale shape. Worked example throughout: [[🎵 TADD DAMERON - Lady Bird]], around the C-position.

## Why restrict at all
Ironically, one of the most reliable ways to become free across the *whole* neck is to first limit yourself to *one* area and find genuinely practical, musical solutions inside it — not to keep drilling more scale/arpeggio boxes in isolation. The reframe that makes this work: when something doesn't come out right, don't default to "I need to practice my arpeggios more" — ask **"what's actually available to me right here that I can already play,"** and build the line from that instead. *(general framing, stated as the core idea of the whole method)*

## Step 1 — Choose the song and the position
- **Pick a song or progression you already know by heart** — chords, scales, and their fingerings already learned in some form. This is not a sight-reading or new-material drill; if you're still learning the tune itself, this exercise will just teach you to fumble efficiently. A simple standard, or even a single recurring turnaround (e.g. a dominant-heavy I-vi-ii-V), works.
- **Pick a general *area* of the neck, not a strict single scale-position box.** Choose it because it suits the song's key, not because it matches a textbook position boundary. Don't be rigid about staying glued to one exact box — if the tune's changes move through several scales, small hand shifts within the area are expected and fine; over-strictness defeats the point.
- Worked example: [[🎵 TADD DAMERON - Lady Bird]] (16-bar form, short enough to loop full choruses quickly) around the neck area centered near **C**.

## Step 2 — Prep: check the overview before you improvise
- Before soloing at all, **play through the song's chord voicings across the whole progression, staying inside the chosen area**, purely to see what's physically sitting there under your hand.
- Layer in a short, generic prep drill through the same progression — Larsen namechecks something he calls **"the Barry Harris exercise"** as an example of this category *(likely Barry Harris's sixth-diminished-scale/arpeggio-through-the-changes drill documented on [[Diminished Chords]] — not confirmed by name in this video, flagged as inferred)*. Any simple, fast, easy-to-apply drill that gets your ear and hand oriented in the position works here — the point is orientation, not a specific named exercise.
- **This prep pass is meant to be a standing habit, repeated with a new song every so often — not a one-time warm-up on this one tune.** Doing it once with one song gives you an overview of that one song in that one position; doing it repeatedly across many songs, over a longer stretch of practice time, is what actually opens the whole fretboard up.

## Step 3 — Play the song in the position; find and fix the rough spots
- **Solo the whole form, confined to the chosen area.** Notice as you go: which individual chords are awkward to play, and — more usefully — which specific **chord-to-chord transitions** are the sticking points (not every chord is equally hard; it's usually one or two moves per tune).
- Some rough spots resolve themselves just by repetition — play a few more choruses and your hands find a way. For the ones that don't, **stop and inventory every option available on that one chord, in that position**, before moving on:
  - the chord's own arpeggio (root-3-5-7)
  - a related minor arpeggio (the relative minor, or the chord built on the 3rd of the target)
  - the chord's major triad and the related minor triad
  - the matching pentatonic scale
- **Worked example (Lady Bird's turnaround, A♭maj7 → Am7):** on A♭maj7, the raw material available is the A♭maj7 arpeggio itself, an A♭ major triad or C minor triad, and C minor pentatonic. If the literal **A♭maj7 arpeggio is awkward to finger in this position, sub in the F minor arpeggio or the C minor arpeggio instead** — both are consonant relative-minor-family substitutes for the same major-7 sound (F minor is A♭'s relative minor; C minor is its iii chord) and one of them usually lies better under the hand locally. → this exact A♭maj7→Am7 transition is analyzed further on [[🎵 TADD DAMERON - Lady Bird]]'s soloing-vocabulary section.
- **Reusable substitution rule:** when a Maj7 chord's own arpeggio is awkward in the current hand position, the minor-7 arpeggio built on its **relative minor (6th degree)** or its **iii chord (3rd degree)** covers the same key-center tones minus the root and is worth trying as a drop-in replacement — pick whichever actually sits better under the hand, not whichever is "correct" on paper.
- **The goal of this step is not "practice the A♭maj7 arpeggio harder"** — it's finding which of the *already-available* options makes a natural, playable melodic line in this specific spot, then getting good at making music with that option specifically.

## Step 4 — Build new material inside the same constraint
Once a position's harmony is mapped this way, reuse the *same* confined area to force new vocabulary with a set of restricted solo passes over the full form. Run these as separate practice passes, one restriction at a time:
1. **Chord-tone-only pass** — solo the entire form using *only* each chord's arpeggio notes (the "basic 1-3-5-7 patients" of the song), no scale runs. Forces you to build lines from material you don't normally reach for, since your usual scale-based escape hatch is gone.
2. **Scale-only pass** — same form, same position, but restrict to *only* scalewise (stepwise) motion — no arpeggio leaps at all.
3. **Third-of-the-chord pass** — same idea, but start every chord's arpeggio from its **3rd** instead of its root (Larsen names this explicitly as a direct variant of the chord-tone pass).
4. **Pentatonic-only pass** — solo using only the matching pentatonic scale(s) for each chord, still confined to the position.
- Each restricted pass forces different melodic solutions precisely *because* it removes the easy default — that's the actual mechanism that generates new vocabulary, not additional shape memorization. Treat these as a rotation to revisit, not a single pass to check off.

## Practice routine framing
- **This is a repeat-with-new-material habit, not a single-song exercise.** Map one song in one position, then do it again with a different song (or the same song in a different position) on a regular cadence. Over enough repetitions, the individually-mapped areas start to connect into full fretboard fluency and free movement between positions while improvising — stated explicitly as "for another video," i.e. the connecting-positions-together step is a distinct, later stage this drill sets up but doesn't itself cover.
- Good candidate practice loop: pick from [[Song Practice]]'s current rotation, choose a position from [[Guitar Positions]] that suits the tune's key, and run Steps 1–4 as a single practice-session unit.
