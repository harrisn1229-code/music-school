# How To Improve Your BEBOP LINES and Phrases with DONNA LEE | The Ultimate Bebop Guide...

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j1ynq1ppsWo)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j1ynq1ppsWo)

## Synopsis

Nathan Borton teaches the head melody of Charlie Parker's bebop contrafact "Donna Lee," then takes it apart to extract the four devices Parker leans on throughout: arpeggios (sometimes decorated with extensions), chromaticism, the major bebop scale, and **line closers** — a device with no prior home in the vault. This is a companion piece to an earlier-enriched Borton video on the same tune ([[THIS Song Turns Students Into BEBOP Pros...]]), which broke down Donna Lee's own harmony (the non-diatonic B♭7, the backdoor G♭7→D♭maj7 move); this one stays on the melodic-line-construction side instead — how Parker actually builds phrases out of the harmony, not what the harmony itself is doing. The most valuable new material is the line-closer concept itself: a short, largely memorized cadential tail that either ends a phrase (paired with an ascending arpeggio, the textbook 5–1 bebop shape) or — flipped — opens one, turning the same fixed phrase into a way to generate tonic-dominant (non-resolving) language instead. Both the melody-teaching and the line-building material are consolidated onto a new song-ref page, [[🎵 CHARLIE PARKER - Donna Lee]], alongside the harmonic analysis already logged from the companion video.

## What's taught

- **Learn the melody by ear before anything else.** Sing it, then memorize it away from the page — Borton frames reading it as actively working against the goal, since the point is eighth-note feel and phrasing landing "under your fingers and in your brain." He calls out a couple of corrected notes he plays that match Parker's actual recording rather than the standard real-book lead sheet (uncorrected on the free lead sheet, not itemized in the transcript). → [[🎵 CHARLIE PARKER - Donna Lee]]
- **Arpeggio-up, line-closer-down — the default 5–1 shape.** Start on a chord tone of the dominant (worked example: the 3rd of F7, which is A), walk up the arpeggio, then drop into a memorized **line closer** that resolves the phrase down into the target chord — this example's closer starts on F7's ♭9 (G♭) and walks down to B♭'s 3rd (D). Explicitly named as "a must-know 5-1 bebop line." → [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §7 (new section, full device writeup), [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]
- **The arpeggio doesn't have to voice-lead smoothly into the closer** — you can just jump straight to wherever the closer's own line starts, demonstrated with a second example from the same melody.
- **The same shape with extensions added to the arpeggio.** A further line decorates the arpeggio with the 9th and 11th before its own closer — named directly as "a sound that Wes Montgomery and Grant Green like to use a lot," the identical extension-arpeggio sound already logged from both of those players elsewhere in the vault. → [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]
- **The formula flipped: closer opens the phrase, arpeggio extends it.** The same closer Parker usually uses to end phrases is used to *start* one instead, with the arpeggio then extending/decorating the line rather than resolving it. Named directly as **tonic dominant** language — "a dominant chord that doesn't resolve to the one chord... it just kind of stays in the sound of the dominant" — useful for extending a dominant line before eventually resolving it, or for soloing over a static dominant that isn't moving, like the B section of a rhythm-changes tune. A fourth independent confirmation of the tonic-dominant device already logged three times on [[Bebop Vocabulary]] from other sources. → [[Modal Jazz]], [[Rhythm Changes]]
- **Chromatic enclosures around a dominant chord's root and its ♭5, worked as one line in two parts.** Part one encloses the **root** of the arpeggio/dominant; part two encloses the **♭5**. Both parts generalize into repeatable patterns: the root-enclosure shape can be continued up or down the whole arpeggio (it starts on the 3rd, encloses the root, then jumps down to the ♭7 — the same pattern reusable from every chord tone), and the ♭5-enclosure shape extends to every other altered tension in turn — ♭5, ♯5, ♭9, ♯9. Stated payoff: combine different combinations of these enclosure patterns freely, finish with a line closer, and the result is reliably strong-sounding bebop vocabulary. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §2
- **The major bebop scale, used right out of the gate in Parker's own melody.** Definition matches the vault's existing table exactly: the major scale plus one added chromatic passing tone, the ♯5 (sitting between the 5th and 6th). The practical payoff stated directly: start on **any** chord tone of the major arpeggio, walk the scale up or down, and you land back on the **same note** a measure later — worked concretely (start on the 3rd, and the 3rd is where you land the next measure too). **The caveat, stated as essential:** don't count the 7th as part of the chord tone set when using this scale — move it to the 6th instead, so the underlying "arpeggio" is root-3rd-5th-6th, not the usual 7th-chord shape. Matches [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §1's bebop-major row exactly (major + ♯5, passing tone between 5 and 6). Framed as useful for playing over static major chords or for leading into other lines. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §1
- **A worked 3-6-2-5-1 line built entirely from the bebop-major rule above, over A♭maj7 for one measure into Cm7.** Starts on C over A♭maj7 (I) — deliberately chosen because the rule guarantees landing back on C a measure later, over the following Cm7 (the 3, functioning as a lead into the 6, then the 2-5-1). Explicit shortcut given: if you already know short 2–5 vocabulary starting from the root, "plug and chug" it in directly at that point rather than composing fresh. The demonstrated line then finishes over the final 2-5-1 by pulling material straight from Donna Lee's own melody. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §1, [[The ii-V-I]]
- **Closing etude — a short original solo over the first half of "Donna Lee,"** deliberately mixing licks lifted straight from the melody with the new vocabulary built during the lesson, as a demonstration of blending memorized material with generated lines in real time.
- **Practice recommendation, stated directly and not tied to this tune specifically:** take a well-known transcription (this melody, or any other everybody already knows) further than a first listen — go line by line, extract the licks you personally like, and move each one through all 12 keys. Framed as a resource-mining approach to a familiar melody rather than a one-time study.

No plugin, effect, mixing, or sound/tone-selection content — pure instrument/theory pedagogy (a Charlie Parker melody transcription used to teach bebop line-construction devices).
