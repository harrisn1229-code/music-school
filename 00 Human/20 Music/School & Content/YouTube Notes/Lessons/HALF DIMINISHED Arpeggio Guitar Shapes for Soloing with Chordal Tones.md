# HALF DIMINISHED Arpeggio Guitar Shapes for Soloing with Chordal Tones

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uDv0kQsMa0s)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uDv0kQsMa0s)

## Synopsis
Sixth entry in Jared Borkowski's ongoing series mapping melodic (root-3rd-5th-7th, not scale-run) arpeggio shapes across all five CAGED-derived neck positions for each of twelve chord types — this installment covers the **half-diminished chord** (m7♭5), all worked from a C root. The video is really two things stacked together: (1) five concrete fingered arpeggio shapes for Cm7♭5, one per CAGED-family position, each demonstrated root-to-root with a recommended fingering; and (2) a reusable **five-step practice ladder** — map the chord tones, drill a melodic pattern off each chord tone, improvise with constant chord-tone-only notes, improvise with musical phrasing, then finally add non-chord tones around the shape — applied identically to all five positions/shapes in turn. The ladder is the more valuable, transferable piece: it's presented explicitly as the method Jared runs on *every* chord type in the series, not something specific to half-diminished. Strongest material for Nate: the position-by-position fingerings (useful directly for any half-diminished vamp or ii-V-i in a minor key), the "the 4 always sounds good as a passing extension over m7♭5" ear-training callout, and the ladder itself as a transferable single-chord-type/single-position practice structure — distinct from [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]'s ladder, which targets a whole moving progression rather than drilling one static chord shape across positions.

## What's taught
- **Series context and resource** — 6th video in a 12-chord-type series (major7, minor7, dominant7, diminished7, half-diminished, minor6 next week, etc., one video per type), each covering the same 5 CAGED-derived positions/shapes for chord-tone-only arpeggios. Jared offers a free "chord tone vocabulary pack" PDF (12 chord types × 5 positions/shapes each) via soundguitarlessons.com or the video description as a companion reference. [~0:00] → [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]
- **The five-step practice ladder, run identically on every shape/position (the reusable method):**
  1. **Map the chord tones root-to-root.** Play straight up and down the arpeggio shape from root to root, repeating and pausing on any root you pass through — pure fingering/positional mapping, no musicality yet. [~2:00]
  2. **Drill a melodic pattern off each chord tone.** Starting from the lowest chord tone in the shape, play "up to the next chord tone, then back down" seamlessly off each chord tone in turn, in time — a structured pattern rather than free noodling, meant to internalize the shape's internal intervals, not just its outer boundary. [~4:30]
  3. **Improvise with constant (unbroken) chord-tone-only notes.** Keep a continuous stream of notes going — straight eighth notes recommended over quarters — using ONLY the shape's chord tones, varying rhythm and direction freely; the point is testing whether the chord tones are known well enough to never have to stop and think. Slow down deliberately when it breaks down. [~6:20]
  4. **Improvise with musical phrasing, still chord-tones-only.** Same chord-tone restriction, but now play actual musical statements: an idea, a rest/space, a second idea that answers or concludes the first (recommended default: play a measure, rest a measure, repeat). Landing phrases on the root and reusing exact rhythmic/starting-point patterns is what makes it read as a cohesive musical statement rather than a run. Singing the phrasing along with playing is offered as a personal trick for locking in musicality. [~7:20]
  5. **Add non-chord tones around the shape, freely.** Once the chord tones are fully internalized, experiment with every other note available in the position and judge each purely by ear ("do you like it, right now, in this spot" — explicitly framed as no-right-answer, taste-based, and context-dependent: a note that sounds "wrong" is usually about *where it was placed rhythmically*, not that the note itself is unusable). This step is deliberately left scale-agnostic — no scale/mode names taught in this video — on the stated philosophy that knowing chord tones cold and exploring freely around them is a more advanced, more ear-driven skill than memorizing scale names, and produces genuinely modal-sounding lines without the player needing to know any mode names. [~9:00]
- **Position 1 — 3rd position, C half-diminished (Cm7♭5).** Fingering: 1-4-2-1, then a small shift (not a reach — "hop" the hand, avoid tension) up to 2nd finger into 4th position, then 2-1-4-3, shift back to 3rd position. One position shift total, otherwise ergonomic. [~1:30] → [[Diminished Chords]]
- **Position 2.** Fingering: 3-1-4-3, shift over to 1st finger as the "out-of-position" note, then back to 3rd finger — preferred over a straightforward 4-3-2 fingering for smoother motion; noted as one valid choice among several reasonable fingerings. [~11:30]
- **Position 3.** Fingering: reach with 3rd finger, then 3-pinky-tips-1-pinky-pinky-3-1, landing in 8th position. [~15:00] Ear-training callout on this shape: **the 4th degree consistently sounds good as a passing extension over a half-diminished chord** — described as almost feeling like an honorary chord tone even though it isn't one, distinct from the arpeggio itself but reliably pleasant as a surrounding/non-chord tone. [~16:40] → [[Scale Modes Reference]]
- **Position 4 — the awkward one.** Fingering: 1-4, shift+roll to 1, 11th position, 3-1-pinky, pinky-1-3-1-roll, then pinky, reach with 3rd finger for the root→♭7 stretch, then ♭5/♭3. Explicitly called out as the most physically awkward of the five shapes purely because it's less familiar/less practiced, not because it's inherently harder — stated goal is to get all five shapes equally comfortable over time. Refers to this reach/contract-then-expand hand motion as "the inchworm," a technique named and used elsewhere in the channel's catalog. [~18:20]
- **Position 5 — 13th position, no shifting.** All four fingers land cleanly on frets 13-14-15-16 in the same hand position — no position shift needed, called out as Jared's favorite of the five for how "logical" it feels under the hand. [~24:00]
- **Backing-track/loop-pedal practice setup** — Jared demonstrates step 5 (adding non-chord tones) live against a looped Cm7♭5 vamp, explicitly recommending practicing against a static backing loop of the target chord so every added note can be judged by ear against a sustained harmonic context rather than a cappella. [~9:30, ~19:30, ~25:30]
- **General teaching philosophy stated explicitly** — chord tones ("the good notes") matter more than scale knowledge for improvising convincingly; a player who knows the chord tones cold but no scale names can still explore all the "in-between" notes by ear and land musically, which is framed as *more* advanced than scale-based playing, not less, because it forces genuine aural judgment rather than pattern recall.
