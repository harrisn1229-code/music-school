# Chord-Tone Soloing

Part of [[Jazz Harmony]] — the **improvisation-practice** complement to the strand's melodic-language notes: [[Bebop Vocabulary]] supplies the decoration (enclosures, approach notes, passing tones), [[Guide Tones]] supplies the theory of which notes matter most, and this note is the **practice ladder** that turns both into something a hand can actually do over a real, fast-moving progression. Built around a worked example — a **1–6–2–5** turnaround at two beats per chord (`Dmaj7 Bm7 Em7 A7`, the [[Turnarounds|I–vi–ii–V]] loop that also forms the [[Rhythm Changes]] A section) — but the ladder generalizes to any chord-tone-dense progression, jazz or not.

## The core question: do you have to target every chord?
**No — but practicing as if you do is what earns you the freedom to not.** Outlining every chord tone on every chord isn't the end goal; it's a conditioning exercise that gets the internal ear and the hand used to what the harmony is actually doing, so that later you can target things *only if and when you want to* and still land convincingly. Treat the whole ladder below as "learn it, then forget it" — the mechanical drilling is temporary scaffolding, not the finished sound.

## Step 1 — Treat the whole progression as one chord (the tonic)
Before touching the changes at all, play the **tonic's** scale/arpeggio (major pentatonic, then full chord tones) over the *entire* loop, ignoring the other three chords completely — over `Dmaj7 Bm7 Em7 A7`, that means playing D major pentatonic / D major-7 chord tones the whole way through. This sounds "wrong" against the underlying harmony in theory but works in practice because a fast turnaround like this genuinely functions as a prolonged tonic — it's the seed of the [[#Prolongation — collapsing a fast progression into fewer functions|prolongation]] idea below, introduced early so the ear has already heard it once by the time it's named. *(general starting point: any I–vi–ii–V or similar turnaround at ≤2 chords/bar will tolerate this — try it before assuming you need to chase every chord.)*

## Step 2 — Target beat 1 on purpose
Once scale-noodling feels arbitrary against the harmony ("play play play, where does that sit?"), make hitting the **downbeat of the progression's top** a deliberate goal: play anything at all for a phrase or two, but land on the root or a clear chord tone of chord 1 exactly when it comes back around, every time. The point isn't restraint — you can build real tension by playing continuously through the loop — it's that **you have to know, in your body, when beat 1 lands**, whether or not you choose to mark it. This is downbeat-of-the-progression targeting, a coarser-grained version of the per-chord targeting in [[Bebop Vocabulary]]'s guide-tone material.

## Step 3 — Add tension before the landing
Same target (beat 1 of the loop), but now approach it with **chromatic notes** dropped into an otherwise-diatonic line (major pentatonic / major blues scale / full diatonic scale) right before the resolution. Expect to occasionally miss the landing while learning this — over-adding chromatics and losing track of *where* the tension should resolve is normal and is itself useful feedback (it tells you the ear hasn't fully locked the downbeat yet, which sends you back to Step 2). This is the practice-room version of [[Bebop Vocabulary]]'s enclosures/approach-note vocabulary, aimed at a large target (the whole progression's downbeat) rather than a single chord tone.

🎬 *From video:* three named, drillable shapes for exactly this idea, scaled down to a single chord tone instead of the whole progression's downbeat — approach-tone-below, chord-tone/lower-neighbor turn, and up-scale-step/down-half-step enclosure — see [[#Three chromatic shapes for decorating a chord-tone arpeggio|below]].

## Three chromatic shapes for decorating a chord-tone arpeggio
Three named ways to dress up a plain chord-tone arpeggio (root-3rd-5th[-7th]) with chromaticism — narrower and more mechanical than Step 3's "chromatic tension before the downbeat," these are shapes you can drill on a single chord tone at a time before trying to deploy them freely. All three share one building block: **the note a half step below a chord tone.**
- **Shape 1 — approach tone below.** Precede each chord tone with the chromatic note a half step below it: over B♭7 (root-3rd-5th = B♭-D-F) that's A→B♭, D♭→D, E→F (and G→A♭ if extending to the 7th, A♭). Works on the triad alone or with the 7th added. Play it as a slur — pick the approach tone, then hammer on to the chord tone — for a more fluid feel than picking both notes.
- **Shape 2 — chord tone / lower-neighbor turn.** Same half-step-below relationship, but as a turn rather than a lead-in: land ON the chord tone first, dip to the neighbor a half step below, then return to the chord tone before moving on. The neighbor is always chromatic regardless of the underlying scale.
- **Shape 3 — enclosure (up then down).** Land on a chord tone, move up to the note directly above it *in the chord's own scale/mode* (not always a whole step), back down to the chord tone, then further down to the chromatic half step below, and back to the chord tone. Which note sits "above" depends on the chord's function, not just its quality:
  - **Dominant 7 (Mixolydian)** — whole step above every chord tone except the 3rd, where the scale's own 4th sits only a half step above the 3rd. Over B♭7: root→C (whole)→B♭→A (half)→B♭; 3rd D→E♭ (half, the Mixolydian 4th)→D→D♭ (half)→D; 5th F→G (whole)→F→E (half)→F; 7th A♭→B♭ (whole)→A♭→G (half)→A♭.
  - **ii-chord minor 7 (Dorian)** — whole step above every chord tone, *including the 5th*, because Dorian's natural 6th sits a whole step above the 5th. Worked on Cm7 as ii of B♭: note above the 5th (G) is A natural — whole step.
  - **Tonic-minor chord, same shape (natural minor/Aeolian)** — the note above the 5th shrinks to a HALF step, because Aeolian's ♭6 sits only a half step above the 5th. Worked on the A-minor tonic chord in "Manhã de Carnaval" (Black Orpheus): five, half step up, five, half step down. **The chord symbol alone (Cm7) doesn't tell you which — the function (ii vs. i) does, because that's what fixes the mode.** → [[Modal Jazz]]'s characteristic-note table (Dorian's natural 6 vs. Aeolian's ♭6) is exactly this distinction, generalized.

Directly extends [[Bebop Vocabulary]]: Shapes 1–2 are its "approach notes" section (chromatic from below, as a lead-in vs. as a turn); Shape 3 is its "enclosures" table's "diatonic above, chromatic below" type, worked here as guitar chord-tone-arpeggio shapes over real changes (a 12-bar blues, "Tenor Madness") rather than staff-notation examples. **Same "overuse it to learn it" method as the rest of this ladder:** map a new shape onto every chord tone of the progression, exclusively, before folding it back in tastefully.

**Contrast with a chord-tone-independent variant:** Shape 3 above depends on chord function to pick the note above the target (Mixolydian vs. Dorian vs. Aeolian). [[Enclosures Through the Scale]] strips that decision out entirely — a fixed enclosure rule applied to every scale tone in a descending run, regardless of chord function — a simpler drill worth doing before or alongside this page's function-aware version.

## Step 4 — Outline the chord tones of every chord ("be-all end-all")
Now go the other direction: play **only the chord tones** of each chord as it passes — Dmaj7 → chord tones, Bm7 → chord tones, Em7 → chord tones, A7 → chord tones — with musical phrasing, not a mechanical arpeggio run. Framed as the single highest-leverage drill in the whole method: it's what "finally lets you know where you are" and is worth **weeks or months** of dedicated time, slow and out of tempo if needed. Directly extends [[Guide Tones]]'s "aim at 3rds and 7ths, not roots" principle — chord-tone outlining at tempo is guide-tone theory made physical. The stated goal is to over-learn it and then forget it: once it's automatic, you stop consciously targeting chord tones and just land on them by feel.

## Step 5 — The awareness drill: know your chord at all times
Play *anything* — sound quality doesn't matter here — while continuously tracking, out loud or silently, which chord of the progression is currently sounding ("that's the one... there's the two... there's the five... there's the six"). This is deliberately decoupled from targeting: the goal is pure real-time harmonic awareness, and it only works because Step 4's chord-tone drilling already built the ear that can feel the changes without looking. Awareness, not accuracy, is being trained.

## Prolongation — collapsing a fast progression into fewer functions
**The most powerful concept in the method.** Prolongation treats a fast-moving progression as an extended stay on *fewer* harmonic functions than the chord symbols show — the whole point being to make a two-beats-per-chord progression playable as if it were much simpler, without losing the underlying harmony. Three levels, in order of aggressiveness:
- **All one function.** Play the tonic (chord 1's scale/arpeggio) across the *entire* loop, all four chords — this is Step 1, now understood as prolongation rather than just a warm-up.
- **Halves.** Split the loop in two and assign each half a single function: first two chords = I, second two = V (`Dmaj7 Bm7` → treat as D-major-ish; `Em7 A7` → treat as A7-ish) — or, just as validly, first two = vi, second two = ii (`Dmaj7 Bm7` → B-minor-ish; `Em7 A7` → E-minor-ish). Either grouping is a legitimate read of the same four chords; which one you hear depends on which pair of roots you're leaning toward.
- **Extreme — multi-bar blocks.** Stretch a single function across several full cycles of the progression: four whole loops treated as I, then four more treated as V (in the video, eight bars of "I" then eight of "V" against a progression that's actually cycling every two beats underneath). The instrumentalist can be this loose *because* the rhythm section is still playing the real 1-6-2-5 — prolongation is a soloist's simplification layered on top of an unchanged harmonic bed, not a rewrite of the chart.

Prolongation only reads as prolongation, and not as playing wrong notes, because Steps 2–5 already installed the ear/body sense of where the real chord changes land — that's why it's sequenced last rather than taught first as a shortcut.

## Deliberately "getting ugly" to build confidence
As a final drill: intentionally add tension that sounds bad *to you in the moment*, on purpose, then land it cleanly back on a chord tone or the tonic — the goal is to embolden yourself by proving you can recover from anything, and to stop treating any given phrase preciously. Landing after a genuinely "off" passage is only possible because all the prior steps built a reliable sense of where home is; the recovery, not the ugliness, is the actual skill being trained. Tension itself doesn't have to be dissonant notes — repetition of a consonant idea builds tension too, through anticipation ("what are they going to do next") rather than clash.

## Worked example from the video
`Dmaj7 Bm7 Em7 A7`, two beats per chord, played over a **walking-bass-line comping loop** (the same channel's rhythmic-motif/walking-bass method, written up separately — see [[Jazz Comping — Rhythmic Motif and Walking Bassline]]) — the 1–6–2–5 in D, i.e. the [[Turnarounds|I–vi–ii–V]] turnaround / the [[Rhythm Changes]] A-section skeleton. The whole method is deliberately framed as genre-agnostic: it's really about how to make sense of playing over any run of seventh chords, jazz context or not.
