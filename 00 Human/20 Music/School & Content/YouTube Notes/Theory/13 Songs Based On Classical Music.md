## Synopsis

Another entry in the classical-borrowing catalog series, with one genuinely reusable production technique (Cliff Burton's multi-bass "organ" texture on Metallica's "Damage, Inc.") alongside the usual quote/sample/interpolation catalog spanning metal, hip-hop, and pop.

## Techniques

- **Simulating an organ with layered, effected bass guitars — Cliff Burton, Metallica's "Damage, Inc."** The intro is a four-part passage (inspired by Bach's "Komm, süsser Tod") performed on four separate bass guitars, with distortion and volume swells applied to transform the timbre away from "bass guitar" toward something closer to a pipe organ. A concrete recipe for simulating an unavailable/expensive instrument texture from a more accessible one: stack multiple independent voiced parts of the same instrument, add distortion for harmonic complexity, and use volume swells (fade the attack) to remove the picked/plucked transient that would otherwise read as "guitar" rather than "sustained organ tone." → [[Layering and Stacking]].
- **Public domain as the practical draw, stated directly**: classical music (400 years of it) is attractive to borrow from both because most of it carries no copyright risk and because of the sheer stylistic range available to kick off a new song from.
- **A shared source used by two different artists for two different sections**: Julian Casablancas' "Human Sadness" samples the chord progression from the *first* movement of Mozart's Requiem in D minor (looped throughout), while a different artist (Evanescence's "Lacrimosa") drew instead from the same Requiem's famous *third*-movement Lacrimosa — same source work, different movements, different uses. "Human Sadness" separately closes with a lo-fi excerpt of Mozart's Piano Sonata No. 16.
- **A lullaby melody reused specifically for its cultural sleep-association, not just its notes**: Brahms' "Wiegenlied" (Lullaby) is described as the archetypal lullaby melody — quoted by Beyoncé in "Sweet Dreams" and by The Crystals in their version of "Santa Claus Is Coming to Town," both leaning on the melody's built-in cultural association with sleep/comfort.
- **The same "classical opening, then flipped with a hip-hop beat" structural device used twice by the same artist**: Alicia Keys' debut album *Songs in A Minor* opens with an unabridged rendition of Beethoven's "Moonlight Sonata" (performed in its original key, C minor, despite the album's A minor title) before a hip-hop drum beat flips the piece into something new; her later album *As I Am* repeats the exact same structural idea, opening with Chopin's Nocturne No. 20 before flipping it the same way.
- **Other confirmed borrows**: Zayn's "Blue" performs Bach's Prelude No. 1 in C major (originally written for pre-piano keyboards) on a modern keyboard sound. Deep Purple's cover of "We Can Work It Out" quotes both Beethoven's 7th Symphony and Tchaikovsky's *Romeo and Juliet* in its opening. Deep Purple's "Highway Star" middle arpeggio section is directly credited by Ritchie Blackmore as Mozart-inspired. Neil Diamond's "Song Sung Blue" is based on a simplified version of the string melody from Mozart's Piano Concerto No. 21. Take That's "Forever Love" opens with a segment lifted from Verdi's Dies Irae. Alicia Keys' "Karma" quotes Brahms' Violin Concerto in D major.
