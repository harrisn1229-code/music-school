# The Abbey Road medley EXPLAINED

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3xEMqapKINw)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3xEMqapKINw)

## Synopsis

A song-by-song walkthrough of "The Long One" — the nine-song medley on side two of *Abbey Road*, the Beatles' last studio effort — covering how each track modulates into the next, which motifs recur across the medley, and the recording history behind each transition.

## What's taught

- **You Never Give Me Your Money** opens the medley as a "pocket symphony" in itself: pensive A minor intro → relative-major C major section → a borrowed (non-diatonic) B♭ chord casts a brief Mixolydian color → a guitar-solo motif sequenced up in minor 3rds (C → E♭ → G♭ → A) modulates to A major → a final arpeggio section blending A major/minor. Sets up melodic/harmonic ideas that recur at the medley's end.
- **→ Sun King**: the planned transition was a sustained organ E note (bridging You Never Give Me Your Money's A major and Sun King's E major, both containing E) — audible on alternate mixes but cut from the final release. McCartney replaced it with a tape-loop sound collage (bells → birds → crickets), inspired by Fleetwood Mac's *Albatross* (1968). Sun King itself modulates E major → C major (via a G11 chord) → back to E major (via an A major chord).
- **→ Mean Mr Mustard**: smooth same-key transition (both E major), bridged by a Ringo fill to cover the tempo jump. Contains a meter change from 4/4 to 3/4 near the end. Titular character based on a real newspaper story about a mean, dark-shaving husband named "John Mustard."
- **→ Polythene Pam**: same key as Mean Mr Mustard, lyrically related (siblings in the lyric). **Her Majesty was originally slotted here** (audible on the *Super Deluxe Edition* alternate mix) but was cut for being too jarring a mood shift between the two songs — McCartney ordered the tape destroyed, but studio policy against destroying Beatles tape meant engineer John Kurlander instead spliced it onto the very end of the reel, which is how it became pop music's first widely-cited "hidden track."
- **→ She Came in Through the Bathroom Window**: called out as the medley's best segue, helped by the fact both songs' rhythm tracks were recorded together as one continuous take. Polythene Pam (E major) → Bathroom Window (A major) modulates via **closely related keys** (E major and A major share 6 of 7 notes, differing only on D♯ vs. D♮) — the guitars walk E→D♮→C→B down to the new tonic A. Bathroom Window itself borrows chords from the parallel minor (A minor), including a minor iv (Em) in the key of A major. Lyric based on real events: fans breaking into McCartney's house via a ladder and stealing clothes/photos.
- **→ Golden Slumbers**: no crossfade — a beat of silence separates the songs, unlike every other transition in the medley. Golden Slumbers (per this video, **A minor**) reintroduces You Never Give Me Your Money's opening chord progression (**Am7–Dm7–G7–C**) and instrumentation, deliberately tying the medley's closing third back to its opening. Lyric adapted by McCartney from the Thomas Dekker poem "Cradle Song," reworked from memory because he couldn't read the sheet music his stepsister had left at the piano.
- **→ Carry That Weight**: recorded as one continuous track with Golden Slumbers (hence the seamless join); Carry That Weight is in C major, the relative major of Golden Slumbers' A minor, at the same tempo. Reprises the You Never Give Me Your Money melody with new lyrics ("I never give you my pillow"), orchestrated by George Martin, then returns to the Carry That Weight verse before flowing into a final guitar-arpeggio section (also recalled from You Never Give Me Your Money) leading into The End.
- **→ The End**: same-key (A major) transition. The only Beatles song to feature a drum solo from Ringo Starr, followed by a three-way rotating two-bar guitar battle between Lennon, McCartney, and Harrison — the last song all four members recorded together. Closes on a solo piano A chord with a final A→C modulation under McCartney's closing couplet ("And in the end, the love you take is equal to the love you make").
- **→ Her Majesty**: 20 seconds of silence, then the relocated 23-second fragment plays out as the true closing track — kept in that spot once the band heard it land unannounced on playback and found it funny.
- **George Martin's role**: classically trained, credited in the video as the person who pushed McCartney toward long-form, near-symphonic song structures — directly enabling the medley concept.
