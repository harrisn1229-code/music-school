# Canon and Fugue

Two contrapuntal forms built from a single melodic kernel developed across independent, imitating voices. Companion to [[Species Counterpoint]] (the note-against-note rules that make any of this consonant) and [[Motivic Development]] (the general toolkit for transforming a fragment — canon and fugue are that toolkit applied structurally across whole voices, not just phrase-to-phrase).

## Canon

A canon states a melody in one voice, then joins it with one or more further voices imitating that same melody, offset in time (and sometimes pitch/direction).

- **Strict canon**: the second voice repeats the first voice's melody essentially unchanged, just delayed.
- **Retrograde canon ("crab canon")**: the second voice plays the SAME melody backwards — literally the identical notes, read from the opposite end, the two lines meeting in the middle. Bach's *Canon Cancrizans* (from the Musical Offering) is written as a single line of notation with a reversed clef/key signature at the far end as the only instruction that a performer must also read it back-to-front simultaneously — the notated score is deliberately compact/cryptic rather than laid out as two conventional staves.
- **Puzzle/riddle canon**: the score withholds how to perform it — entry timing, direction, or clef-reading — and the performer has to solve it before playing. Bach marked one *Quaerendo invenietis* ("seek and you will find"): differing clefs signal how many voices and how each reads the pitches (an inverted bass clef means that voice reads the same written notes upside-down — literally different pitches from the same page); an unstated entry point has to be found by testing each bar for the first one where the two voices don't clash.

## Fugue

A fugue opens with an exposition, then develops that material through the rest of the piece. Named parts, in order:

1. **Subject** — the short melody the whole piece derives from, stated alone by the first voice.
2. **Answer** — the subject restated by the second voice, transposed up a fifth into the dominant key (subject in the tonic, answer in the dominant).
3. **Countersubject** — new melodic material the first voice plays underneath the answer, distinct from the subject but recurring later in the piece (including transformed — e.g. in contrary motion/inversion).
4. **Episode / sequence-based development** — further entries build from fragments of the subject (e.g. its first few notes) repeated a step higher each time (sequence), keeping later material audibly derived from the opening subject even as the texture thickens.

**The underlying principle**: independent melodic lines (horizontal) that also combine into coherent harmony when stacked (vertical) — strong counterpoint is both at once, not one traded off against the other.

## From videos

- **Counterpoint as a voice-economy solution under a severe hardware polyphony limit — a modern, concrete application of the form outside classical repertoire.** The original Pokémon Game Boy soundtrack had exactly 3 usable pitched voices (of 4 total — 2 pulse-only, 1 flexible, 1 noise-only/no pitch); composer Junichi Masuda directly studied Bach's 2- and 3-part inventions as his model for writing music that never exceeds 2-3 independent lines yet still implies rich harmony — his own words: "that limitation can lead to some of the best music." A companion voice-economy trick: **arpeggiating a chord within a SINGLE voice** rather than stacking it across multiple voices frees the remaining voices for independent melodic material instead of being consumed by static harmony (worked in the "Pallet Town" theme). → David Bennett Music Theory, [How Pokemon 8-bit music was inspired by classical music](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WyvfKFVRqVs)
- **A worked exposition + two canon forms in one video**: crab canon (*Canon Cancrizans*, retrograde), a solved puzzle canon (*Canon a 2*, clef-inversion + unstated entry point), and the C minor fugue from WTC Book 1 (subject → answer → countersubject → sequence-built continuation, including the countersubject reappearing in contrary motion). Both canons are built on the same "royal theme" given to Bach by the King of Prussia as a compositional challenge — an added constraint (working the device around a pre-existing melody, not one of Bach's own). → [[Species Counterpoint]], [[Motivic Development]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [3 pieces that prove Bach was a genius](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5NrUiceLz_s)
