# Triad Pairs

A **fourth** distinct triad-soloing device alongside the vault's other three: [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]] takes one triad and maps its three close-position zones; [[Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords]] extracts one triad from inside a single seventh chord's 3rd-5th-7th; [[Harmonized Scale Triads for Soloing]] harmonizes a whole scale into seven separate triads used one at a time. This page instead
combines **two triads at once** — a "triad pair" — played together as a compound source of line
material over a single chord, associated with modern/modal jazz phrasing. Worked entirely over a **C
minor blues**.

## Finding a triad pair (the mechanics)

1. **Harmonize the parent scale into seven triads**, one per scale degree (skip-a-note/tertian
   stacking, same derivation as [[Harmonized Scale Triads for Soloing]]). Worked here on **C melodic
   minor**: C minor, D minor, E♭ augmented, F major, G major, A half-diminished, B diminished.
2. **A triad pair = any two of those triads that share no common notes.** In a 7-note diatonic scale
   this constraint always resolves to one specific relationship: **the two triads must sit next to each
   other in the scale** (adjacent scale degrees) — confirmed as true for every pair used in the source
   video without exception.
3. **Fast derivation shortcut, stated directly:** decide which single scale note you *don't* want to
   play over the target chord, remove it from the 7-note scale, and the **remaining six notes split
   cleanly into two adjacent triads** — that's the pair. Faster than deriving and scanning the full
   harmonized-triad table each time.
4. **The pair as a compound sound, not two separate ideas.** Because the two triads are scale-adjacent,
   together they spell out an extended chord sound in one breath — see the worked chord-by-chord
   mapping below for what each specific pair adds harmonically (7ths, 9ths, 11ths, 13ths) on top of the
   underlying chord.

## Matching a pair to each chord — worked on a C minor blues

| Bar / chord | Scale used | Triad pair | Harmonic color added |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Cm (tonic minor) | C melodic minor | E♭ augmented + F major | E♭aug = the upper part of a C minor-major chord (3rd-5th-7th); F major adds the root, 6th (13th) and 11th on top — combined reads as **Cm(maj7) with a 13 and 11**. |
| 4 — C7 altered (V7/iv, resolving to F minor) | D♭ melodic minor (a half step above C melodic minor — same relationship as [[Scale Modes Reference]]'s altered-scale derivation) | E augmented + G♭ major | Same shapes as the tonic pair above, shifted up a half step wholesale — the altered-dominant color over C7. |
| 5–6 — Fm (iv, tonic minor of the IV) | F Dorian (preferred over F melodic minor — the melodic-minor major-7th reads as an odd major-3rd-of-C clash on a minor blues) | F minor + G minor | F minor is the chord itself; G minor (F Dorian's ii, adjacent in the scale) is layered on top for a Dorian rather than melodic-minor flavor. |
| — Cm (tonic, alt. voicing) | C Dorian / C melodic minor (both contain the pair) | C minor + D minor | The chord itself plus D minor stacked on top (9-11-13 color); thought of as a Dorian sound even though the same two triads also exist inside C melodic minor. |
| Turnaround — A♭7 → G7 altered (final cadence, ♭VI7–V7alt) | E♭ melodic minor over A♭7 (A♭7 as a tritone-sub/Lydian-dominant chord — scale = melodic minor from the chord's **5th**, matching [[Scale Modes Reference]]'s Lydian-dominant = melodic minor IV mode); A♭ melodic minor over G7 altered | E♭ minor + F minor (over A♭7); B augmented + D♭ major, 2nd inversion (over G7 altered) | The two-minor-triad pair over A♭7 spells the Lydian-dominant color; over G7 altered the aug+major pair gives the altered-scale tensions. |
| — Cm7 (13 sound, later chorus) | C Dorian | E♭ major + F major | Upper structure of Cm7 (3rd-5th-♭7) plus the 13/11/root added by F major — a **Cm13** color, distinct from the opening's E♭*augmented*+F pair (that one's a melodic-minor/maj7 sound; this one's a plain-♭7 Dorian sound). |
| — Cm (2nd chorus, "complete melodic minor minus the root") | C melodic minor | E♭ augmented + D minor | E♭aug spells the 3rd-5th-7th of Cm(maj7); D minor adds the 9 (D), 11 (F) and 13 (A) — together this is the **entire C melodic-minor sound except the root**. |
| Turnaround, 2nd chorus — A♭7 → G7 altered | A♭7's upper structure is a **C diminished** triad (a *diminished* triad pair, not the augmented/major/minor pairs used elsewhere) | C diminished + B♭ major (over A♭7); A♭ minor + B♭ minor (over G7 altered) | C diminished spells the 3rd-5th-♭7 of A♭7; B♭ major adds 9♯11-13 on top. Over G7 altered, the two-minor-triad pair (same device as the E♭m/Fm pair above) gives the altered color again. |

*(general note, implied throughout but not stated as a single formal rule: every pair in this table is
drawn from a **melodic-minor-family scale rooted somewhere useful relative to the chord** — the tonic
itself for a minor i chord, a half-step up for the V7 altered scale, the 5th of the chord for a
Lydian-dominant V7 — the same three melodic-minor relationships already catalogued on [[Scale Modes Reference]] § Melodic Minor; triad pairs are simply melodic minor's harmonized-triad table read two
notes at a time instead of scale-tone at a time.)*

## Building lines from a pair: four construction patterns

- **Basic combined line (the tonic-chord opening lick).** Arpeggiate triad A, step down one scale
  degree into triad B's 3rd, arpeggiate triad B in first inversion, step down again back into triad A.
  A short back-and-forth that stitches the two triads into a single continuous phrase rather than
  playing them as two separate runs.
- **Uneven note-groupings, not straight 3s.** Explicitly called out as what makes a triad pair line
  sound less mechanical: group the notes in a **4-then-3** pattern (or similar) rather than playing
  clean groups of three (which reads as "just running through the inversions" and sounds systematic).
  A 4+3 split across the pair yields a 7-note pattern that **floats across the bar line** in 4/4 —
  it keeps shifting relative to the beat each repetition instead of landing predictably, which is the
  whole point: it disguises the mechanical, formulaic way the line was actually constructed.
- **Repeated top note as a pedal.** Repeat the top note of triad A while descending through the rest of
  the triad, then move into a descending run of triad B — the repetition plus the 4+3 grouping above are
  the two devices used together throughout the video to keep pairs from sounding like inversion drills.
- **String-set pairing (guitar-specific).** Play one triad of the pair on one string set (e.g. the
  middle strings) and the adjacent triad on the next string set up (e.g. the high strings), alternating
  between them — either one note per string (arpeggio-style) or **two notes per string** for a denser
  version. This is a deliberately practical way to work triad pairs on guitar: it's predictable for the
  picking hand, and because many of the pairs share the *same triad qualities* transposed (e.g. two
  major triads, or two minor triads), the same physical string-set pattern can be **shifted around the
  neck** to the next pair without relearning a new shape. Playing pairs this way — shooting from a low
  triad up to a high one across string sets — produces a dramatic, effective low-to-high line in a short
  span, distinct from staying inside one scale position.

## Worked over a jazz standard: Out of Nowhere's altered dominants (E7alt, D7alt)
A second worked application, over the secondary-dominant E7 altered and D7 altered chords in [[🎵 JOHNNY GREEN - Out of Nowhere]] rather than a minor blues — see that note for the tune's full harmonic analysis. Same melodic-minor-derived pair logic as above, with a new device (**deliberate omission for effect**) added:
- **Favorite pair on E7alt (from F melodic minor): A♭ augmented + B♭ major** — praised specifically for contrast: the augmented sound is strongly present on the first triad, then genuinely absent on the second, giving the pair a real before/after quality rather than just two adjacent shapes.
- **F minor + G minor**, also strong on E7alt — F minor works especially well here because it's already the top part of a lot of common E7alt voicings, so the pair feels voicing-connected rather than arbitrary.
- **B♭ major + C major** — "the one everybody always uses," but it **omits the A♭** (the chord's ♭9), leaving the pair's harmonic identity a little ambiguous on its own. **New device: the omission itself is a usable effect, not just a compromise** — you get some of the altered color but miss the ♭9 specifically, and that partial-information sound is worth choosing on purpose sometimes rather than always reaching for the "complete" pair.
- **Same devices transposed onto D7alt:** **F♯ augmented + A♭(G♯) major** and **A♭ major + B♭ major**; also **E♭ minor + F minor** (the same "chord-tone triad + adjacent minor" logic as the F minor/G minor pair above). Deliberately **leaving out the F♯** while playing A♭-major-triad-heavy material over D7 is called out as its own intentionally-incomplete dominant color — omission used as a compositional choice, the same idea as the B♭/C pair above.

## Practice sequence

1. Harmonize the target scale (melodic minor rooted appropriately for the chord — tonic, half-step-up
   for an altered dominant, or the 5th for a Lydian dominant) into its seven triads.
2. Pick a chord, decide the one scale note to leave out, and confirm the remaining six notes split into
   two adjacent triads — that's the pair.
3. Drill the basic combined line (arpeggiate A, step down into B, arpeggiate B, step back) slowly.
4. Re-group the same pair into a 4+3 (or other non-3) pattern and notice how it shifts across the bar
   line on repeats.
5. Layer in a repeated top-note pedal on the higher triad.
6. Work the same pair as a string-set shape (one triad per string set, one or two notes per string) and
   practice shifting that shape to the next chord's pair.
7. Chain pairs together chord-to-chord through a full progression (a minor blues is a good testbed) —
   tonic pair, V7-altered pair (same shapes, up a half step), iv-chord pair, turnaround pairs.
