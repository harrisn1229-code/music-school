# Drum Sounds - Tchad Blake

## Synopsis

Tchad Blake auditions his tracking-day drum mic palette live — flipping between combinations of amp'd/reamped bass drum, compressed vs. flat overheads, and a SansAmp-driven mic blend — framing the whole exercise as building a ready-to-go set of drum "colors" before a band even shows up, so he can hit record on day one without knowing the material. He then talks through the vintage rack compressors (Spectra Sonics units specifically) that give his drum sounds their idiosyncratic character, including the origin story of how he acquired his two mismatched units.

## Techniques

- **Build a versatile drum-sound "palette" at setup time, before the band arrives or the material is known — mix-and-match multiple mic/processing combinations to cover a wide range of possible needs with minimal repatching.** Explicit goal: "if the band came in and said we don't have time for anything, record without even listening to it... I'm reasonably sure that in 99% of the cases I can make this work." Practice: rapidly audition combinations (a "Sansamp"-driven signal panned one way, compressed overheads panned the other, blended with a flat/unprocessed bass-drum mic) to hear what colors are available before tracking starts, so the setup is ready regardless of what the song turns out to need.
- **A go-to blended drum sound built from three simultaneous elements panned/balanced against each other: a SansAmp-driven signal, compressed overheads, and an unprocessed (flat) bass drum mic** — called out as one of his current favorite combinations, valued for how much color it offers without heavy processing.
- **Vintage-character compressors as a deliberate, load-bearing part of the drum sound — prized specifically for being "radical" and idiosyncratic rather than clean or spec'd.** Names Spectra Sonics compressors as his main drum-compression tool, contrasted against more common choices in that role: a 1176-style compressor, a dbx-style compressor (his own phrasing: "de-stressers" — likely referencing a similarly-classed unit), or a plugin emulation of the Eventide Omnipressor ("Eventide have a compressor that sounds great called the Omnipresser... if you found a real one... they're great"). Explicit reasoning for reaching for character units over clean ones: "how radical they are to begin with" is the appeal, not a liability.
- **Owns two Spectra Sonics compressor units that are deliberately mismatched from each other — bought secondhand, one rescued from a studio's discard pile for $75 and a second found the next day at a guitar shop for $100 — and states plainly that the pair does not match: "frequency response, attack — for these times it's radical, and I love them like that."** Origin story specifically: the first unit was found unplugged in a pile of cables destined for the trash at a studio, tested on a drum-machine track for a Sam Phillips cover of "Give Me Some Truth" (session engineer for a T Bone Burnett-produced record), used completely untouched/unadjusted straight out of the box, and the resulting drum-machine sound on that finished record is that same untouched compressor setting. Declined offers to have them serviced/matched — the mismatch between the two units is treated as a feature of his rig, not a flaw to fix.
- **General mixing/tracking philosophy embedded in the demo: don't chase a single "correct" drum sound — build a small stable of ready mic/processing combinations and let the material pick which one(s) get used, changing the blend (not necessarily the mic positions) to fit different songs quickly.**
