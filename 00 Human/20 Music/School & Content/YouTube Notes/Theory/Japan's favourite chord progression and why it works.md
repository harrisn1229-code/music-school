# Japan's favourite chord progression and why it works

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6aezSL_GvZA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6aezSL_GvZA)

## Synopsis

The "Royal Road" progression — Japan's cultural equivalent of the West's Axis Progression, ubiquitous in anime, J-pop, and video-game soundtracks but rare in Western pop, with a distinctive never-resolves-to-I shape that gives it its melodramatic, ongoing feel.

## Techniques

- **The progression: IV–V–iii–vi**. Name ("Royal Road") translates as "the easy way" — the effortless default route to a melodramatic but not-serious mood in Japanese music.
- **Functional walk**: IV (subdominant, mild tension) → V (dominant, maximum tension) → iii (weak tonic-function — "at rest but not satisfied") → vi (tonic-function, but "wistful" rather than fully resolved). **It never touches I at all** — the loop keeps circling without ever fully landing, which is the source of its ongoing, dramatic momentum.
- **Cultural asymmetry**: about as common in Japan as the Axis Progression (I–V–vi–IV) is in the West, and about as rare in the West as the Axis Progression is in Japan — Bennett attributes this purely to trend/habit rather than any inherent musical reason.
- **Near-miss variant**: "Never Gonna Give You Up" (Rick Astley) is often cited as an example but actually uses a ii7 chord in place of IV — the same notes minus the root, just a different bass note (a G♭ bass gives IV; an E♭ bass gives ii7 instead). The genuine Royal Road progression does appear in Astley's other Billboard #1 hit, "Together Forever."
