# Duke Dumont's "3x30 Method" (Deceptively Simple)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fGCtR7oZWdE)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fGCtR7oZWdE)

## Synopsis

Duke Dumont on getting past a multi-year creative dry spell caused by perfectionism ("paralysis from analysis") — he put so much pressure on each release being groundbreaking that he stopped finishing anything for two or three years. His fix is a volume-over-precision workflow: generate several short idea snippets a day, every day, for weeks, without judging them in the moment; only later, with distance, do the good ones get picked out and finished. The philosophy underneath it — aim for 80% satisfaction, not 100%, because 100% essentially never happens.

## Techniques

- **The 3x30 method: make 3-5 short idea snippets a day, five days a week, for weeks at a time, without listening back.** Snippets range from 16 to 64 bars depending on what the idea needs. Don't evaluate them the day they're made — leave them alone for a month or two, then go back and pull out only the ones that still sound good into a dedicated "keep" folder to finish. The point is generating raw material continuously ("constantly throwing paint at the canvas") instead of getting stuck perfecting one idea in real time ("procrastinating about that hi-hat"). → [[Committing and Printing]]
- **Aim for 80% satisfaction with a finished track, not 100% — because 100% essentially never happens.** His own number: only about 1 in 10 releases even hits 80% for him. Waiting for a release to feel fully realized before putting it out is what causes multi-year dry spells; releasing at "good enough by your own bar" is what keeps output moving. → [[Committing and Printing]]
- **Recognize a musical "flow state" the same way athletes do — and build habits that reliably trigger it, rather than waiting for it.** He frames unconscious, fluent working (not remembering the technical process afterward, only the room/setting) as a distinct mental state you can deliberately induce with the right workflow (here, the low-pressure daily-snippet habit), not something that only happens by chance.
- **Batch similar-sounding new ideas against each other before finishing any of them** — when several new ideas end up sharing a similar bassline/feel (his example: "10 things that sound like [Track A]," "30 things kind of like [Track B]"), group them together and compare rather than finishing the first one that came out; the point isn't which one is objectively best, it's noticing the pattern in what you're drawn to right now.
- **The actual goal of a track is an emotional response — check that against yourself first.** His stated bar for whether an idea is worth finishing: does it create a feeling in him, specifically, before worrying about how anyone else will react to it — "start with yourself... and just hope that other people have that emotional response."
