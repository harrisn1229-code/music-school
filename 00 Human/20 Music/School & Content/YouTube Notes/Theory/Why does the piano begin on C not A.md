# Why does the piano begin on C not A?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5B_5N2M7SXQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5B_5N2M7SXQ)

## Synopsis

A historical answer to why the piano's white keys spell C major rather than A major: the keyboard wasn't built around the major scale at all — it was built around the full set of diatonic scales (what we now call the modes), tracing back to the ancient Greek tetrachord system.

## Techniques

- **Reframe: the white keys aren't "the major scale," they're the whole diatonic-scale family, for free.** C major, A natural minor (Aeolian), D Dorian, E Phrygian, F Lydian, G Mixolydian, B Locrian all live on the same seven white keys — the major scale isn't privileged in the physical layout, it's just one of seven equally-valid starting points on the same note collection. The keyboard is arranged around the diatonic scale in general, not around C major specifically.
- **Historical root: the Greek tetrachord.** A tetrachord is 4 notes spanning a fixed perfect 4th, with the two outer notes locked and the two inner notes movable — the Greeks had several tetrachord types, and the **diatonic tetrachord** (tone–tone–semitone) is the one that produced what we now call the diatonic scale. Stacking four overlapping diatonic tetrachords together built the Greeks' full system (the "Greater Perfect System"), which — mapped onto modern letter names — lands on our modern note B as its lowest pitch (plus one additional lowest note, the *proslambanomenos*, added to complete the two-octave range).
- **Practical takeaway for a player**: since all 7 modes already exist on the white keys with zero accidentals, any mode can be practiced/explored on an unaltered piano just by starting and centering on a different white key — no new fingering or key signature needed, only a different tonal center.
