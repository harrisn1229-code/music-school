# Stuck in Scales? Try Enclosures for Jazz Guitar!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uz04sL6c5ik)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uz04sL6c5ik)

## Synopsis

Nathan Borton's short (under 9 minutes) lesson breaks the enclosure — "two or three notes that frame a target tone" — down to a single rhythmic decision rule rather than a fixed shape: **a target landing on a downbeat gets a two-note enclosure, a target landing on an upbeat gets a three-note enclosure.** That one rule is then reused across three escalating drills: enclosing the first note of every diatonic 7th-chord arpeggio in a major scale, pausing mid-descent through a scale to enclose a chosen note, and — the video's most useful idea — using a Dorian-scale walk-up from a memorized dominant lick's starting note back to the preceding ii chord, timing the walk-up so the lick's own starting note lands back on a specific beat and gets enclosed accordingly, turning a static ii chord into a connective line feeding straight into a pre-learned V-chord phrase. The most valuable material is the ii–V connective-line device (Drill 3) and the closing worked example, which plays the exact same target/phrase pair two different ways (landing on a downbeat vs. an upbeat an octave lower) purely to show the enclosure size changing with the beat, not the notes. Distinct from — and directly complementary to — the same folder's [[Enclosures Through the Scale]] (a different, scale-function-independent mechanical rule for enclosure length) and [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]'s Shape 3 (enclosure shape fixed by chord function/mode rather than beat).

## What's taught

- **Enclosures defined:** two or three notes that frame ("enclose") a target tone, creating tension and resolution plus rhythmic motion — described as "the DNA of jazz," part of why players like Charlie Parker sound the way they do.
- **The two enclosure types, by beat, not by shape:**
  - **Downbeat enclosure (two notes)** — approach the target from below or above by one note. Worked example: target D on the A string, approached from below or above.
  - **Upbeat enclosure (three notes)** — adds rhythmic tension by starting on an upbeat; either two notes below or above the target, or one note below and one above.
  - Full rule + worked-out drills → [[Downbeat and Upbeat Enclosures]].
- **Drill 1 — arpeggio enclosures through the key.** Take a C major 7 arpeggio, enclose just its first note (the root) with a two-note enclosure (a three-note version is also demonstrated), then repeat the same enclosure treatment through **every diatonic chord of the C major scale** (Cmaj7, Dm7, Em7, Fmaj7, G7, Am7, Bm7♭5) until the shape is comfortable under the fingers and recognizable by ear. Framed explicitly as more than a finger exercise — each enclosed arpeggio becomes a usable "fragment of information" that links directly into jazz vocabulary.
- **Drill 2 — walk a scale down, enclose one note on the way.** Play the enclosed C major 7 exercise first, then walk down the C major scale, choosing one note along the way to target and enclose (worked target: G). Size the enclosure by checking which beat G actually lands on — in the demonstrated example G lands on an upbeat, so it gets the three-note enclosure. Repeated with **D minor 7 and the D Dorian scale**, targeting A on the way down, to show the same rule applies over a ii-chord/Dorian context, not just a major-scale/tonic context.
- **Drill 3 — connect a ii–V with an enclosure-timed walk-up (the video's centerpiece).** Over **Dm7–G7** (a plain 2-5): start with a memorized dominant-chord phrase over G7. To connect it back to the preceding Dm7 rather than playing it cold, walk **up** the D Dorian scale starting from the phrase's own starting note (G) — when the walk-up reaches G again, check which beat it lands on and enclose it accordingly (upbeat → three notes, downbeat → two), then play straight into the memorized dominant phrase. **Repeating the walk-up from different starting points on the Dorian scale generates different connective-line variations** feeding into the same memorized phrase, each one becoming its own reusable building block toward other ideas.
- **Worked closing example — same target/phrase, two different beats.** First pass: start the walk-up on C, arriving at G (the dominant phrase's start) on a **downbeat** → two-note enclosure, then the dominant phrase. Second pass: walk **down** the Dorian scale instead, landing the same target an octave lower, arriving on an **upbeat** this time → three-note enclosure, then the dominant phrase an octave down. Framed as producing "a nice, beautiful line" with real forward momentum purely from the beat-driven enclosure choice — the notes targeted don't change, only the enclosure size, driven by which beat the target happens to land on that pass.
- **Practice framing:** transcribe real solos and notice how players frame target notes with enclosures — and when practicing, don't just play the enclosures as isolated shapes; listen for how they "pull and push and persuade the beat to move forward." The payoff is rhythmic momentum, not just note choice.
