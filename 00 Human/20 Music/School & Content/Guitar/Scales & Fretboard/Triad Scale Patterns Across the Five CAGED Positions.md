# Triad Scale Patterns Across the Five CAGED Positions

Part of [[Guitar Positions]]. A **scale-pattern drill**, distinct from every other triad page in the vault: [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]] relocates one triad across three neck zones, [[Harmonized Scale Triads for Soloing]] and [[Triad Melodic Devices for Jazz Soloing]] use harmonized/inverted triads as **lick-starting motifs**, and [[Close Position Triad Voice-Leading Etude]]/[[Spread Triad Voice-Leading Etude]] chain the diatonic triads into a **composed etude**. This page instead runs a plain **1-3-5 triad off every note of the scale, in order, through the full scale form** — a *scale pattern* (like melodic thirds) built from triads rather than a chord-connecting or lick-building device. The goal is purely to break up linear scale-running (the "sounds like a scale going up and down" problem named directly in the source) into something more melodically fluid, the way jazz players' lines rarely just run the scale straight.

**Origin story worth keeping on the page:** the author was told by a big-band director years ago that "everybody practices triad scale patterns through the scale" as a basic jazz-improvisation exercise; he later bought the popular reference book **"Patterns for Jazz"** hoping to fill the gap and found it **"completely useless for a guitarist"** — its exercises don't map onto guitar fingerings in a usable way. His fix was to build the same idea on his own CAGED scale-form system instead of the book's generic exercises — worth remembering as a caution before buying that book expecting guitar-idiomatic patterns.

## The drill: 4 patterns × 5 positions = 20 reps

**The four triad-scale-pattern types** (each one is a 1-3-5 major/minor/diminished triad — diatonically correct quality per scale degree — built off each successive scale note, run through the whole scale form):

1. **Ascending** — play an ascending 1-3-5 triad off each scale note in turn, moving *up* the scale form. Also used on the way back *down* the form (still ascending triads each time, not reversed) — the author flags this explicitly as an easy point to get wrong: don't switch to descending triads just because you're descending the scale form.
2. **Descending** — same idea, but each triad itself runs 5-3-1 off each scale note, again moving both up and down through the scale form using descending triads throughout.
3. **Alternating ascending→descending** — first triad ascending, next triad descending, next ascending, etc. (up-down-up-down...) as you climb the scale form. Physically a little easier than pure ascending/descending once it clicks, and described as the "really cool sound."
4. **Alternating descending→ascending** — the mirror image: first triad descending, next ascending, etc. **Explicitly not the same drill as #3 reversed** — because the pattern is anchored to a *scale form* (a fixed fret layout), starting the alternation on a different foot lands the up/down triads on different physical spots within the shape, so both directions are worth drilling separately, not just one direction "backwards."

**The five scale-form positions** (CAGED order, all worked in the key of C major): **A form → G form → E form → D form → C form.** Per-position notes from the video:
- **A form** (first, unnamed by letter in the transcript but demonstrated first, before "G form" is introduced by contrast) — where all four patterns are first drilled.
- **G form** — outlines a G chord shape up the fretboard.
- **E form** — described as physically awkward in the ascending pattern.
- **D form** — flagged as the hardest of the five to see "bird's-eye view," i.e. hardest to visualize as a shape rather than note-by-note; the author notes some people call this shape "Dorian" (because scale degree 2/D is the form's lowest note) but explicitly rejects that label here — it's still being played as C major, not treated as a modal sound, since what matters is how the shape is *used*, not which note happens to sit lowest in the box.
- **C form** — the final, highest position; the ascending pattern is called out as the trickiest to execute cleanly of the whole set.

That's **4 patterns × 5 positions = 20 individual drills** for one scale/key — the same 20-drill grid transposes to any of the seven scale types the author's free "Parent Scales" PDF covers (linked in his video description), and to any key.

## Practice method: milestone-gated, not time-boxed

**The milestone:** for each of the 20 drills, the bar to clear before moving to the next one is simply **"can I play it at least once, in time, at any tempo"** — not clean, not fast, just once through correctly at a steady pulse. Sequence per drill:
1. Map out the fingering slowly, out of time — just get the note order right first (rhythmic subdivision doesn't matter yet; treat it as triplets, quarter notes, or eighth notes, whichever is comfortable — "just as long as it's in the right order").
2. Once the order is solid, work it up to playing it **in time**, up and down the form, at any tempo.
3. Hit the milestone (one clean pass, in time) → move to the next drill. Don't wait for it to feel polished — the habit and the physical foundation are what the milestone certifies, not mastery.

**Named cost, stated directly:** this is genuinely slow work — "hours and hours and hours of practice" per the full 20-drill grid for one scale, because you don't advance to the next drill until the milestone is hit on the current one. Framed as a puzzle to enjoy rather than rush.

## Applying it to real music — three-step process

Demonstrated over the A section of **"I'm in the Mood for Love"** (the Julie London recording with Barney Kessel on guitar cited as the inspiration for working on the tune). Progression as given *(transcript numbers a little compressed/fast — treat scale-degree framing as solid, exact chord spelling as a reasonable reconstruction rather than a verified lead sheet)*: **Cmaj7 – Am7 – Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7 – Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7 – F7 – Em7 – D♯dim7 – Dm7 – G7 – Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7** (the "1-6-2-5...1...2-5...1" section, then the bridge-ish move through F7/Em7/D♯dim7 back to a closing 2-5-1).

**Step 1 — patterns only, but musicalize the rhythm.** Play literally the triad-scale-pattern shapes over the changes, but deliberately break up the straight, even rhythm they were drilled in — vary note durations, repeat notes, leave space — trying to make a mechanical pattern read as a phrase rather than an exercise. Named explicitly as hard and often still sounding like "an exercise" at first — that's expected, not a failure state.

**Step 2 — constant-note stream with pattern fragments dropped in.** Play a steady stream of notes (constant eighth notes, quarter notes, or half notes — whatever tempo is manageable, slow it down if needed) using *any* notes (staying in the scale is fine as a default), and periodically substitute in a fragment of one of the 20 drilled triad patterns wherever it fits, rather than playing a full pattern start to finish. This is the bridge step between "pure drill" and "real playing" — small, opportunistic insertions rather than a scheduled full pattern.

**Step 3 — real music, patterns optional-and-incidental.** Play an actual expressive phrase over the tune, ignore the chord changes' exact color the way scale patterns "override the need to perfectly hit the chord changes" (their own melodic momentum carries through passing chords outside the key), and let bits of the drilled triad vocabulary surface **only where they naturally fit** — explicitly framed as sounding forced a lot of the time at first, and that's fine; the payoff is gradual, not immediate. Stated directly: "it's already helping a lot... I'm liking the amount of times that I'm incorporating it... there's a texture that breaks up stuff I'm used to."

**Why patterns survive passing/out-of-key chords:** the source's stated reasoning — the triad-scale-pattern's own melodic momentum is strong enough that it doesn't need to track every passing chord exactly; playing the pattern through a brief out-of-key chord even adds its own useful tension, as long as the surrounding harmony is broadly in the key.

## The freshness-plateau cycle (practice philosophy, worth keeping)

Named directly as a recurring pattern in improvisation practice: you find something new, lean on it heavily and it sounds great for a while, then it goes stale and your playing feels stuck — the fix each time is the same shape as this whole page: drill something you genuinely can't do yet from the ground up (milestone-gated, slow), work it into the playing, and the freshness returns — then the cycle repeats. This is the same "prioritize what's undertrained, not what's already comfortable" instinct behind [[Vision-Driven Practice and the Overwhelm Reflex]] and [[The Practice Method That Makes Failure Impossible]], applied specifically to melodic vocabulary rather than repertoire or fretboard visualization choice.
