# Neapolitan Sixth (N6)

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Chords]] / [[Altered Chords]]. The **Neapolitan chord** is a major triad built on the **lowered 2nd scale degree**. Despite the naming pattern of other altered-root chords ([[Borrowed Chords|♭III, ♭VI]]), it isn't actually borrowed from the parallel minor — it's usable in both major and minor keys.

## Why "sixth"
The chord is almost exclusively used in **first inversion** — root position is rare, second inversion rarer still. That's why it's called the Neapolitan **6th** (N6), not "the Neapolitan sixth chord" (as if six modified "chord"). In the rare root-position case it's simply "the Neapolitan chord"; in the rare 2nd-inversion case it's the "Neapolitan six-four."

## Function
- Functions as a **subdominant/pre-dominant** chord despite being built on the supertonic (♭II) — it commonly resolves toward dominant-function harmony.
- Common resolutions: to a **dominant seventh in third inversion**, or to a **cadential 6/4** ([[Part-Writing]]).
- Grouped with the **predominant** family alongside the other subdominant-function chords (ii, IV) rather than treated as its own category.

## Origin
Named for "from Naples" — associated with the operas of Neapolitan composers like **Alessandro Scarlatti**, though the chord isn't actually native to Naples and was already common well before Scarlatti's time, in music far from Italy.

## From videos

- **A second confirmed non-Neapolitan ♭II, alongside "Things We Said Today" above**: "If I Fell" (Beatles) opens ♭II→I (D major resolving straight to the tonic D♭ major) — a tritone substitution of a V→I cadence, not a Neapolitan, since it resolves TO the tonic rather than TO the dominant. Full worked context (the same D chord flips function entirely 4 bars later, becoming a pivot into a genuine new key) → [[Tritone Substitution]], [[Modulation (Key Change)]]. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[this Beatles song changes key in the first 8 bars]]
- **The precise two-part test (major triad on ♭2 AND leads to V — not just any ♭2 chord), a pop/rock/film survey, and three worked counter-examples distinguishing a true Neapolitan from a similar-looking tritone sub or Phrygian borrow.** Pop/rock passing the full test: "Hello" (Lionel Richie, A minor), "The Globalist" (Muse, identical key/chords), "Going to a Town" (Rufus Wainwright), "Do You Want to Know a Secret" (Beatles). Major-key use (more chromatically foreign, since minor keys only need one note altered to build ♭II): "Indiana Jones" theme (Williams). Film scoring: "Sally's Song" (Danny Elfman), *Who Wants to Be a Millionaire?* theme. **Most pop examples use ROOT POSITION ♭II, not true first-inversion Neapolitan SIXTHS** — the classical N6 convention specifically requires first inversion (the minor-6th interval between outer voices is where "sixth" comes from); a genuine modern N6: "Wolf at the Door" (Radiohead, E♭maj7/G). **Historical origin**: began as a chromatic variant of the common i-iv-V-i minor progression. Later Romantic usage shifted to root position (Chopin's Prelude in C minor, carried into pop via "Could It Be Magic," Barry Manilow, which opens with a direct Chopin excerpt). **Three counter-examples, each explained by a DIFFERENT concept**: "Because" (Beatles) has a ♭II chord but resolves to a diminished chord, not V — fails the pre-dominant test, so NOT Neapolitan despite Lennon writing it right after hearing "Moonlight Sonata." "Things We Said Today" (Beatles) — its ♭II chord IS the dominant function itself (a tritone sub), not pre-dominant → [[Chord Substitution]]. "Nights in White Satin" (Moody Blues) — a ♭II chord with no dominant-adjacent function at all, best read as Phrygian/Phrygian-dominant borrowing instead → [[Diatonic Modes]]. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use the Neapolitan Chord]]
- **D♭maj7 (♭II maj7) named explicitly "the Neapolitan subdominant" by Jens Larsen, with two concrete jazz uses, from a systematic root-by-root survey of all 12 chromatic roots × 5 chord qualities against the key of C major.** Most common use: as a **suspension inside a ii–V ending**, sitting under the melody's root right before the final cadence resolves. Second use: as a **pivot chord for modulating to another key**, cited in **"All the Things You Are"** and in a standard whose title the transcript garbles (likely a jazz standard with "dream" in the title). Both uses are jazz-practical applications layered on top of this page's existing classical first-inversion/N6 convention and its two standard resolution targets (dominant 7th in 3rd inversion, cadential 6/4). → [[Borrowed Chords]], [[Secondary Dominants]] — Jens Larsen, [[30+ Chords in C major? - Boost your chord progressions and jazz harmony]]
- **A jazz-vocabulary reading of the Neapolitan as a V-chord substitute in a major ii–V–I in C, named directly on-camera as "the Neapolitan subdominant."** Derivation given: take the borrowed minor subdominant (F minor, the key's "four minor" chord — see [[Borrowed Chords]]) and voice it with its **♭6 in the bass** (D♭) — jazz treats that bass-position chord directly as **D♭maj7** in the key of C, substituting for G7 ahead of Cmaj7. Framed as "a great minor subdominant" alongside the rest of the four-minor family, i.e. functionally grouped with borrowed-iv chords rather than treated as a classical-only device. **Variant given:** the same D♭maj7 voiced as **D♭maj7♭5** instead of a plain major 7th, for a spicier color. **A further move worth cross-linking (see [[Borrowed Chords]] for the full derivation):** the same D♭maj7 flipped to **major quality with the ♭6 still in the bass** — D♭maj7♯5, reframed as "a four MAJOR chord with a ♭6 in the bass" rather than the minor-flavored original. → [[Borrowed Chords]], [[Chord Substitution]], [[The ii-V-I]] — Jens Larsen, [[How many ways can you Reharmonize a II-V-I in C major? - Modernize your chord progressions]]
- **N6 is almost always first inversion, and that's WHY it's called "sixth"** — the naming logic (N6 = Neapolitan chord + its near-universal first-inversion usage) plus its two standard resolution targets (dominant 7th in 3rd inversion, or a cadential 6/4). — Toby W.
