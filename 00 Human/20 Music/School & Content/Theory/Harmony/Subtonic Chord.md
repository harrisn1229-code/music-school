# Subtonic Chord

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Modal Interchange]] / [[Diatonic Modes]]. The **subtonic chord (♭VII)** — a major chord built on the LOWERED 7th degree of the major scale — functions as the de facto "7th chord" of a major key in real songs, despite not being diatonic to major at all.

## Why ♭VII, not vii°

Harmonizing every degree of the major scale gives the fixed pattern major-minor-minor-major-major-minor-**diminished**. The actual diatonic 7th-degree chord (vii°, "the leading tone chord") is almost never used in practice — see [[Diminished Chords]] for why (it's a weaker, incomplete substitute for the dominant 7th). ♭VII fills that gap instead.

## Two framings, same chord

1. **Modal mixture / borrowed chord**: ♭VII is borrowed from the parallel minor key (in C major, B♭ major is "on loan" from C minor) — the classic blues/rock major-minor blend. → [[Modal Interchange]].
2. **Mixolydian**: the Mixolydian scale IS the major scale with a lowered 7th, so ♭VII is Mixolydian's own natural diatonic chord, not a borrowing at all — this is the correct framing whenever a song's actual mode is Mixolydian rather than plain major. → [[Diatonic Modes]].

The lowered 7th is Mixolydian's **character note** — the single note that distinguishes it from plain major, and the source of Mixolydian's wider, more "open" quality.

## From videos

- **A song survey covering both framings, plus the parallel explanation for vii°'s absence.** Modal-mixture uses: "Don't Stop" (Fleetwood Mac), "Mr. Blue Sky" (ELO), "I'll Be There for You" (The Rembrandts), "It's Only Love" (Beatles, alternates ♭VII and the natural leading tone chord to chord), "Sweet Child o' Mine" (GN'R, V then ♭VII — spelled with the correct lowered-7th enharmonic, e.g. C♭ not B, for notational precision). Mixolydian uses: "Heroes" (Bowie, D Mixolydian, chorus opens on the natural ♭VII) and "Hey Jude" (Beatles, shifts from plain F major into F Mixolydian right at the famous outro's ♭VII entrance — the "opening up" feeling is directly tied to that modal shift). **Why vii° (the actual diatonic 7th chord) is essentially unused**: it's a weaker, incomplete version of the dominant 7th (its notes ARE the top three notes of V7) — "Tiny Dancer" (Elton John) nearly uses it plain but adds the missing note, turning bare B° into G7/B for a stronger resolution. → [[Diminished Chords]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use the Subtonic chord]]
