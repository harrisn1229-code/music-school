# Vocal Tuning

**Tuning as a workflow, not as an effect.** Auto-Tune-as-a-sound is [[Pitch and Formant]]. This is the decision layer: what to correct, what to leave, and how hard.

## The two modes

| | Automatic | Graphical |
|---|---|---|
| **What it does** | Snaps everything to a scale in real time | Note-by-note editing of the pitch curve |
| **Speed** | Instant | Slow — minutes per phrase |
| **Sound** | Audible when fast; the modern pop/rap sound | Transparent when done well |
| **Use for** | Stylized effect, rough comps, live | Preserving a performance |

**Automatic** needs a key and scale, and it will confidently retune a passing note or a blue note into something you didn't want. **Graphical** lets you fix the two notes that are actually wrong and leave everything else — which is usually what you want on a sung lead.

The practical answer is often both: automatic at a gentle setting to catch drift, graphical for the handful of notes that still stand out.

## Retune speed

**The single most consequential control.** It sets how fast correction pulls a note to pitch.

- **Slow (long)** — the note's natural scoop, slide and vibrato survive. Correction is inaudible. This is transparent tuning.
- **Fast (short/zero)** — the pitch snaps instantly, killing scoops and flattening vibrato into a stepped line. **This is the T-Pain / hard Auto-Tune sound**, and it's a creative choice, not a mistake.

There is no "correct" setting — it's a stylistic dial with a genre on each end. What *is* a mistake is reaching for fast retune to fix a pitch problem and getting the effect by accident.

**Fast retune destroys vibrato.** If a singer's vibrato is part of why the take is good, a fast setting will remove exactly that.

## When not to tune

The question the placement asked (VOX-10), and the one worth having a real answer to.

**When the deviation is the expression.** Blues, soul, gospel and a lot of rock live *between* the notes. A bend, a scoop into a note, a phrase leaned into slightly flat — these are doing the emotional work. Snap them to the grid and you remove precisely what made the take good.

**When the genre's identity is a human voice.** Folk, soul, live-feel material, singer-songwriter. Audible correction in these contexts reads as a mistake even when it's subtle.

**When the singer is good.** A strong performance with two off notes needs two notes fixed, not a tuning pass.

**When it's already the sound.** If the vocal is meant to be obviously processed, correct pitch is beside the point.

**The general rule:** correct the genuine mistakes, leave the rest. Over-tuning is far more common and far more damaging than under-tuning, because it's invisible while you're doing it and obvious a week later.

## Practical order

1. **Comp first.** Tuning a take you'll later replace is wasted work. See [[Vocal Comping and Takes]].
2. **Fix timing before pitch** — a note that's late often reads as out of tune when it isn't.
3. **Tune the lead**, then decide about the stacks. Doubles and harmonies usually want *more* correction than the lead, because tuning discrepancies between layers are much more audible than a single voice being slightly off. See [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]].
4. **Listen in the mix, not soloed.** Tuning decisions made in solo are almost always too aggressive.

- **A working producer's blunt statement of the "when not to tune" case: heavy tuning/gridding is what sucks the soul out of a track.** Atticus Ross, describing a 90s over-produced radio-rock workflow he actively rejects — drums snapped perfectly in time, guitar glitches cleaned up, vocal "perfectly aligned to their grid, tuned" — "to my mind every essence of any soul would be sucked out of the music, and it would just become an unbearable listening experience to me... the imperfections are as important as the things that are correct." A direct, real-world reinforcement of this page's existing "when the deviation is the expression" and "when the genre's identity is a human voice" rules above — not a new rule, but a working example of a producer choosing to leave a vocal untuned/unquantized as policy, not by accident. → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]] — Mix with the Masters (Atticus Ross), [[Atticus Ross' Production Process]]

For deliberately robotic tuning: apply the macro correction, then flatten modulation and drift toward zero.
