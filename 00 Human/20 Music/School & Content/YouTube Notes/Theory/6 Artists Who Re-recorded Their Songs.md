# 6 Artists Who Re-recorded Their Songs

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5OQoFhjI3lU)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5OQoFhjI3lU)

## Synopsis

David Bennett surveys six real cases of artists re-recording their own songs — some for artistic reasons (a changed voice, a missing arrangement piece, a genre reinterpretation), some for legal/rights reasons — with concrete detail on what actually changed between versions in each case.

## Techniques

- **Transposition + arrangement swap to match a changed voice**: Joni Mitchell re-recorded "Both Sides Now" at 56 (original at 21) — replaced solo acoustic guitar with a lush orchestral arrangement (Vince Mendoza, Grammy-winning), slowed the tempo, and transposed down a major third specifically to sit her older, deeper voice comfortably.
- **A jam session cut in two, then re-recorded a third time for tempo**: The Beatles' 10-minute "Revolution" take 18 was split into "Revolution 1" (first 4 minutes) and "Revolution 9" (remaining 6 minutes, reworked into a sound collage); McCartney/Harrison argued Revolution 1 was too slow for a single, so Lennon re-recorded the whole song faster/heavier from scratch — that remake became the canonical single despite being recorded last.
- **One new bassline transforming an abandoned track**: Radiohead shelved "Nude" through three album cycles (OK Computer, Kid A, Hail to the Thief) until Colin Greenwood wrote a new, more rhythmically active bassline — the missing piece that finally made the arrangement work, eventually released on In Rainbows.
- **Genre-reimagining while keeping the melody/changes intact**: Herbie Hancock's "Watermelon Man" (1962 jazz quintet) was reworked for 1973's *Head Hunters* — same melody and chord progression, full swap to synths/Rhodes/electric bass/funk drums, a new B section, and a distinctive intro/outro built from a hindewhu (a Central African Pygmy one-note bamboo flute technique, played here on a beer bottle by percussionist Bill Summers).
- **Legal/rights-driven re-recording**: Taylor Swift's re-records exist to route future revenue/control away from a masters dispute — deliberately straight re-creations rather than reinterpretations, since the goal is a sound-alike replacement.
