# How to Get Better at Guitar - The science of getting past a plateau

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ifvexnRqsgQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ifvexnRqsgQ)

## Synopsis

Jared Borkowski (Sound Guitar Lessons) steps back from a specific exercise or tune to make the research case for *why* plateaus happen at all and what to do about them — built almost entirely around direct quotes from Anders Ericsson's book *Peak: Secrets from the New Science of Expertise*. The structural arc: why plateaus happen (challenge stops → progress stops, because the brain is wired to conserve effort once something reaches "good enough"), a doctor-performance study and a memory-athlete case study as evidence, then Jared's own distilled practice method — "repetition with manipulation" plus parameter-locked exercise design — as the guitar-specific application of the research. This is the theoretical/citation-backed companion to two notes already in the vault: [[Technique Is Part of the Path (The Red-Flag Repair Loop)]] (Jared's own "go to the exact spot" method, which this video supplies the Ericsson research grounding for) and [[Interleaved Practice, Chunking, and Deliberate-Practice Drills]] (Daniel Seriff's toolkit, which independently cites the same *Peak* source and the same "target the trouble spot directly" principle). Most valuable if you already buy the practice methods on those notes and want the actual research citations behind them, plus one genuinely new named technique: **repetition with manipulation**.

## What's taught

- **Why plateaus happen, mechanistically (0:50–4:00ish).** When you first start something new, improvement is rapid because you're being genuinely challenged — you have to think, and thinking is metabolically expensive. As soon as a skill reaches a "functional/acceptable" level, the brain — evolved to conserve calories and shortcut anything it can — stops being challenged by it, and progress stops **at exactly that point**, not later. The plateau isn't a ceiling on ability; it's the point where the *task itself* stopped being new. Doing more reps past that point doesn't help, because volume isn't the variable that drove the earlier gains — novelty/challenge was.
- **Ericsson quote — doctors' skill actually *declines* with years in practice.** "Research on many specialties shows that doctors who have been in practice for 20 or 30 years do worse on certain objective measures of performance than those who are just two or three years out of medical school... little of it challenges them or pushes them out of their comfort zones." Cited as proof this isn't guitar-specific — any skill, done a lot without being challenged, doesn't just plateau, it can regress.
- **Ericsson quote — the plateau-as-limit illusion.** "When you first start learning something new it is normal to see rapid or at least steady improvement and when that improvement stops it is natural to believe you've hit some sort of implacable limit so you stop trying to move forward and you settle down to life on that plateau. This is the major reason that people in every area stop improving." Jared's gloss: this is the mechanism behind "fixed mindset" — not that people naturally have hard skill ceilings, but that *believing* you've hit one becomes self-fulfilling because you stop pushing. **Takeaway stated directly: never assume you've hit a real skill limit at a plateau.**
- **The "Steve" case study — a memory-capacity research subject.** Ericsson studied a subject ("Steve") working to extend how long a string of digits he could hold in memory. When Steve hit what looked like a hard plateau, the researchers discovered it wasn't his overall memory capacity maxing out — **he was consistently messing up one or two specific groups of digits within the string.** Fixing exactly those groups let his overall capacity keep expanding. Quote: "His problem was not that he had reached the limit of his memory but rather that he was messing up one or two groups of digits in the entire string." Direct guitar parallel Jared draws: your apparent ceiling on a piece/technique is very often one or two specific components, not the whole skill.
- **Ericsson quote — cross-training as the general fix.** "The best way to move beyond [a plateau] is to challenge your brain or your body in a new way. Bodybuilders... will change the types of exercises they are doing, increase or decrease the weight... and switch up their weekly routine... most of them will vary their patterns proactively so they don't get stuck on plateaus in the first place. Cross training of any sort is based on the same principle." Jared explicitly wants practicing to feel more like cross-training — constantly varied movements/exercises — instead of the same repetition loop.
- **Ericsson quote — isolate the weak component, don't drill everything.** "Any reasonably complex skill will involve a variety of components, some of which you will be better at than others. Thus when you reach a point at which you are having difficulty getting better, it will be just one or two of the components... not all of them... The question is which ones — to figure that out you need to find a way to push yourself a little... harder than usual. This will often help you figure out where your sticking points are." **The diagnostic method, concretely:** push a little past your current limit (Jared's worked example: speed up tempo past clean) and watch for what breaks down *first* — that's the actual weak component, not the whole passage. Then push one level deeper than "the fast part is hard": is it a specific finger's technique, a pull-off, the picking-hand tone, the time feel? Jared: identifying the real cause is "the biggest thing" he does in private lessons — students often think the problem is one hand when it's actually the other (worked anecdote: a student was convinced the left hand was the issue; the real cause was the right hand missing a string).
- **Ericsson quote — the full method, stated as a recipe.** "First figure out exactly what is holding you back, what mistakes are you making and when. Push yourself well outside your comfort zone and see what breaks down first. Then design a practice technique aimed at improving that particular weakness." Jared's framing: **designing your own exercise on the spot, targeted at the exact identified weakness, is a more valuable skill than any specific guitar technique** — because it's the meta-skill that gets you through every future plateau, not just the current one.
- **Ericsson quote — why this isn't more widely used.** "The power of this technique is that it targets those specific problem areas that are holding you back, rather than trying this and that and hoping that something works. This technique is not widely recognized even among experienced teachers... and is a remarkably effective way to rise above plateaus." Named explicitly as the *default* failure mode most players (and even teachers) fall into: unfocused "try stuff and hope."
- **"Repetition with manipulation" (Jared's own named method, distilled from all the above).** Repetition alone is necessary to practice something, but repeating it identically stops driving improvement once it's no longer challenging — so **repeat the material while deliberately varying it**: a different spot on the fretboard, a different string set, a different position, a different key. Explicit personal motivation stated: Jared is "scared" of anything becoming a fully automatic habit, because he feels that disconnects him from genuinely expressive, intentional playing — automaticity is treated as a risk, not just a plateau-avoidance tactic. Framed as inherently uncomfortable (it's the mechanism that keeps you out of the comfort zone that causes plateaus in the first place) — not something anyone finds naturally pleasant, though it can become "exciting"/"stimulating" — and the stated long-term payoff is a genuine craving for challenge, where a session without something to strive for starts to feel wrong.
- **Design exercises with strict, specific, pass/fail parameters.** A good self-designed exercise needs a rule specific enough that success/failure is unambiguous — Jared's examples: *"play this three times in a row at this tempo without buzzing a single note"* (buzz = instant fail, hit tempo clean = pass, no ambiguity), or *"improvise over this progression using only chord tones 3 and 5"* (won't produce a great solo, but it's a targeted constraint drill for a specific musicianship muscle, same logic as a weightlifting exercise isolating one muscle). **Manipulate the rule once the current version is solid** — e.g. switch the allowed chord tones to 7 and 1, or change the time signature from 4/4 to 3/4 on the same material — to keep generating fresh, specific challenge instead of re-running an exercise that's already stopped teaching you anything.
- **Worked example of the parameter-locked idea: scale patterns instead of "just run the scale."** Naming the common failure — practicing a scale by running it straight up and down produces solos that sound like "playing a scale," not music — the fix is a written-out melodic pattern that forces you to jump around inside the scale shape instead of moving linearly. Jared plugs his free PDF download for this specifically: **top 3 pentatonic scale patterns for more melodic soloing** (linked in his video description, not captured here since it's off-platform).
- **Closing self-diagnosis prompt (a 3-part question Jared poses directly to the viewer, meant as a real practice exercise, not just video engagement bait):** (1) where in your playing have you hit a plateau/felt stale, (2) what do you think the actual weak spot is that's causing it, (3) what's a specific exercise you could design to target exactly that. Directly mirrors the Ericsson recipe above, turned into a self-audit.
