# Pick Grip, Attack Mechanics, and Picking Patterns

The mechanical half of playing bass with a pick — how to physically hold one, where the motion should come from, and which stroke pattern to use — as opposed to the tone/when-to-reach-for-one question, which lives on [[Pick vs. Fingers on Bass — Tone and Technique Tradeoffs]]. Source: a dedicated BassBuzz pick-technique video built around the presenter's own "ex-pick-hater" journey — he spent years dismissing picks as a fingerstyle crutch before digging into how working pick players (McCartney, Bobby Vega, Jason Newsted, Carol Kaye, David Ellefson, Kim Gordon) actually do it, and reverse-engineered the five components below from watching their technique.

## Learn fingerstyle first — sequencing, not a tone judgment

Even as a pro-pick video, the explicit recommendation is to build alternating-finger plucking (index/middle) **before** adding a pick: starting on a pick can undermine commitment to learning to alternate-pluck, and players who've been pick-only for years often struggle badly picking up fingerstyle later. This is the same sequencing point [[Pick vs. Fingers on Bass — Tone and Technique Tradeoffs]] already carries from BassBuzz's curriculum roadmap (pick work as early as Level 2–3 for rock/punk-focused players, but fingerstyle-solid-first as the general default) — two separate BassBuzz sources independently landing on the same order. See [[String Crossing and Plucking Patterns]] for the alternating-finger foundation this is built on top of.

## Pick anatomy — three variables, and what each one trades off

**Size:**
- **Standard** (same size most guitar players use) — the default.
- **Jazz picks** (tiny) — named as more of a guitar-player thing, not covered further for bass.
- **Big triangle picks** — more surface to hold onto; specifically useful for a beginner who keeps dropping the pick.

**Thickness/gauge:**
- **Thin** — more flexible, glides off the string more easily (harder to get stuck on the string), and the extra flex adds a clicky sound from the pick bending — a beginner-friendly starting point for exactly those reasons.
- **Thick** — darker, bassier tone; less flexible, so it responds to the string differently (less give, more direct transfer of the stroke into the string). Stated as the presenter's own preference for most playing.

**Material** — Tortex, nylon, felt, wood, metal, credit card: each hits the string differently and changes tone. No single material is prescribed; the guidance is to buy a variety pack and compare by feel.

**Troubleshooting table (map the symptom to the fix):**

| Symptom | Fix |
|---|---|
| Dropping the pick a lot | Try a big triangle pick |
| Getting stuck on the string | Try a thinner pick |
| Don't like the tone | Try a different material |

## How to hold the pick

Between thumb and index finger (all correct grips are variations on this): thumb out in front, pressed into the side of the index finger at roughly a 45° angle (or tilted further down so the index tip runs parallel to the thumb); slide the pick in with its tip pointing away from the pick hand.

**Five-point checklist:**
1. Thumb sits **below the center** of the pick — only a little bit sticks out past the thumb.
2. The **tip of the thumb** goes past the pick.
3. The **side** of the index finger presses the pick — not the pad.
4. Wrist **straight or slightly bent forward**.
5. Grip firm enough not to drop it, loose enough not to over-grip.

## Five components of picking-attack mechanics (reverse-engineered from famous pickers)

Compiled by comparing how McCartney, Bobby Vega, and Jason Newsted each actually pick, since no single source describes the "right" way — the conclusion drawn from all three is that there **isn't** one right answer; the same player shifts between options depending on the part and to avoid fatigue.

1. **Where the motion comes from.** McCartney: mostly wrist, side-to-side. Bobby Vega: elbow movement + forearm rotation, a distinctly different style. Jason Newsted: a mix of everything — wrist, elbow, and whole-arm movement at the shoulder. Takeaway: no fixed source is correct; shift between shoulder/elbow/forearm/wrist/fingers (or blend them) depending on the line and to stay fresh.
2. **Strap length.** Carol Kaye plays with the bass sitting above the waist, David Ellefson (Megadeth) mid-waist, Kim Gordon (Sonic Youth) just below the waist — three working pros, three different heights, so strap length is not a picking-technique constraint worth optimizing.
3. **Where along the string to pick.** Closer to the neck = rounder, more tubular tone. Closer to the bridge = sharper, scrapier. The middle is the general default unless a specific tone is the goal. Same principle as [[Playing Fast on Bass — Efficient Technique, Setup, and Bridge Plucking]]'s Lever 3 (bridge plucking for speed via less string give) — that note covers the *speed* payoff of a bridge-side attack point; this component covers the *tonal* dimension of the same position choice, pick or fingers either way.
4. **Attack direction.** Straight across-and-back — suits fast chains of same-direction notes. Or strike down into the strings then back up on the upstroke — more natural when the motion is driven by forearm rotation, and better suited to funkier lines.
5. **Pick angle/slice.** Dead-on (flat against the string) is the default most of the time. More angle produces more pick-scraping noise — a deliberate tool for a nastier, grittier sound when that's the goal, not a technique flaw.

## Three picking-pattern approaches

**1 — Down picking.** Down strokes only. The simplest starting point (no per-note decision to make) and gives a consistent attack that suits driving a song hard. **Watch for:** when crossing strings, don't twist the *angle of the hand* to reach the new string — move the *whole arm* up and down instead, keeping the picking motion/angle consistent across the string change.

**2 — Alternate picking.** Down + up strokes, same logic as two-finger alternate plucking giving more speed than one finger alone. Trade-off: a natural **uneven lilt**, since downstrokes land stronger than upstrokes by default. Best for fast single-string runs, or casual lines that don't need down-picking's driving consistency.
- **Practice drill:** run any scale you already know down-up per note. Worked example in the source video: a G minor scale, one downstroke-upstroke pair per scale tone.

**3 — Economy picking.** Identical to alternate picking on a single string, but when crossing strings, chain multiple strokes in the *same* direction whenever the pick is already positioned to continue that way — e.g. after a downstroke ends a phrase on the E string, the pick is already sitting ready to downstroke the A string too, so continue down rather than flipping to up just to alternate. This is the pick-technique equivalent of fingerstyle **raking** — see [[String Crossing and Plucking Patterns]]'s Drill 2 (rake vs. alternate at a direction-change fork) for the same underlying idea worked out on the fingerstyle side; the fix there ("pre-decide, don't leave the fork to chance in the moment") applies here too.
- **Practice drill — 3 notes per string, ascending then descending:** ascending, each string starts on a down: `down-up-down` on the E string sets up a down-start on the A string too, then repeat `down-up-down` per string through A, D, G. Descending (coming back down through the strings), each string instead starts on an up: `up-down-up` per string, repeating the upstroke across each string crossing. Flagged in the source video as genuinely hard even for an experienced player ("I still kind of suck at it") — the value of the simple repeating pattern is building the down/up-continuity coordination in isolation before applying it to a real, less-regular bass line.

## From videos

- **A specific, riff-length down/up stroke pattern that isn't simple down-picking or strict alternation — enabled by a ghost note doing the stroke-direction bookkeeping.** BassBuzz's breakdown of Nirvana's "Smells Like Teen Spirit" (Krist Novoselic) gives the exact stroke pattern for the intro/chorus riff, down/up marked: `down up down down up down up down down down down up down down`. The pattern isn't chosen arbitrarily — it falls out of a **beat-3 ghost note** (a muted pick pluck, `X` in tab): down-strokes feel more natural landing on the beat, so the ghost note on beat 3 supplies a down-stroke that isn't tied to a real pitch, which frees the next (real, off-beat) note to land on an up-stroke, and resolves back to a down-stroke on beat 4. A concrete case of this note's "no fixed source is correct, shift per line" component 1 above, but the more specific lesson is that a well-placed ghost note can be the thing that makes an otherwise-awkward stroke pattern fall naturally under the hand, rather than fighting it. → [[Ghost Notes (Slap Bass)]] (the ghost note's own mechanics), [[Pick vs. Fingers on Bass — Tone and Technique Tradeoffs]] (eighth worked example, same riff) — BassBuzz, [[The 5 Levels of Smells Like Teen Spirit (For Bass)]]
- **Down-picking's specific payoff: a driving, "insistent" feel from equal-strength attacks, as a deliberate choice when a locked-in, correct-notes-and-rhythms line still isn't "rocking hard enough."** Alternate picking naturally accents every other note (down-up-down-up gives a two-note accent pattern), while all-downstrokes hits every note with the same attack — down-down-down-down — which reads as more consistent and forward-driving, especially on chugging eighth/quarter-note lines. Framed as making a bassline feel "consistent and insistent and persistent," a subtle shift that can push a whole song (or just a section of it) toward feeling more relentless. Worked examples: **Best of You** (Foo Fighters, bassist Nate Mendel) — a demoed alternate-picked take has the right notes and rhythms but is missing this driving quality compared to the original's down-picked version. **The Ramones** — cited as one of the classic down-picking bands, with bassist Dee Dee Ramone's lines described as close to entirely down-picked (e.g. **Blitzkrieg Bop**), which is named as a direct driver of the band's punk drive. **Fingerstyle equivalent:** one-finger plucking (repeatedly plucking with a single finger instead of alternating) is named as the fingerstyle analog of all-down-picking, and is called a "super common session player trick" (the source video points to a separate BassBuzz video on it, not covered further here). This note's existing "1 — Down picking" section above already covers the mechanical execution (consistent attack, move the whole arm rather than twisting the wrist across a string change) — this bullet adds the *why reach for it* feel-payoff and the worked band examples. → BassBuzz, [[Why Beginner - Intermediate Bassists Sound “Karaoke-y”]]
