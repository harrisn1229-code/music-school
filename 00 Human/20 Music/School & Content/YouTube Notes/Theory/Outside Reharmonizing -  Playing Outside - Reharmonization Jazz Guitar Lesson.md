# Outside Reharmonizing -  Playing Outside - Reharmonization Jazz Guitar Lesson

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QYhfyDtShgs)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QYhfyDtShgs)

## Synopsis

Jens Larsen frames reharmonization not as a chord-substitution catalog for its own sake but as a specific tool for **playing outside**: the goal of "outside" playing is to introduce notes the listener isn't expecting at that point in the form, but a soloist can't just pick random non-chord tones and still make a coherent melody — so instead of abandoning chord tones, replace the *chords themselves* with a different, self-consistent progression, then play normal strong chord-tone melodies on top of that substitute progression. The substitute sounds far away from what's expected, but because it's still a real, logical progression, the lines built on it still sound intentional rather than random. He demonstrates the whole approach as a single continuous worked pass through **"There'll Never Be Another You"** (E♭, the same tune he used in his memorize-a-standard video), moving through the form four bars at a time and offering 2–4 substitute options at each stage — explicitly noting which options fit the tune's actual melody and which are "further away" and clash with it. A recurring meta-point ties the whole video together: **every substitute needs a logical starting point and a logical way back inside**, and this "out and back" shape, more than any individual chord trick, is what makes the outside sound land as exciting rather than just wrong. Two named devices recur constantly across different spots in the form: **chromatic parallel planing** (sliding a chord shape up by half or whole steps before resolving) and **tritone-substituting an entire two-bar ii–V** (not just the V) to stretch tension further than a single-chord tritone sub would. Several techniques double as both **soloing-only substitutes** (played mentally against a band holding the real changes) and **written reharmonizations of the melody statement itself** — Larsen flags which is which as he goes.

## Techniques

### 1. Core method: substitute the whole chord, not just the notes, so the melody stays coherent (~0:30–2:30)
Stated as the thesis of the whole approach: don't try to pick "outside" notes directly against the original chords — that produces melodies that don't make sense. Instead, **replace the chord itself with a different, self-consistent progression**, then build normal chord-tone lines on *that* — the lines sound intentional because they're diatonic to something real, even though that something is far from what the listener expects at that point in the form. → [[Reharmonization]]

### 2. Bars 1–2: reinterpreting a minor-key cadence as if it resolves major, before circling back to the real minor target (~2:30–5:00)
The tune's opening cadence is a **D half-diminished → C minor** move (ii–i in C minor). First substitute: **pretend the ø7 wants to resolve to C *major*, not C minor** — reharmonize it as **Dm7** (a plain minor 7, not half-diminished) followed by an **altered dominant** that still lands back on **C minor** — so the ear briefly expects a major resolution and gets pulled back to the real minor target instead. A second, independent option for the same two bars: **cascade two ii–V's a half-step apart** (two stacked minor two-fives, transposition a half step) still fit to this melody — offered as a second "fits the melody" option, not layered on top of the first. Both are explicitly usable in the *theme* (the actual melody statement), not just while soloing — Larsen suggests saving them for the second half of the form once the listener has heard the plain version once. → [[Reharmonization]] § *3. Tritone-substitute the dominants* (adjacent device), [[The ii-V-I]] § *The minor version*, [[Chord Substitution]]

### 3. Bars 1–2, further-out option: fix only the start and end chords, fill the middle by ear (~5:00–5:45)
A "further away" alternative to #2 that no longer fits the melody: fix only **E♭maj7 as the start** and **G7alt as the end** two bars later, then fill in whatever chords connect them by taste — stated as genuinely open-ended ("that's pretty much going to be up to your own taste"). Larsen's own stated preference within that freedom: bias the filler chords toward **directional motion back inside**, because a substitute that's clearly heading home reads as exciting rather than merely wrong — this preference is the seed of the worked example in #4. → [[Reharmonization]]

### 4. Bars 1–2, the actual worked "short trip out": chromatic planing from a side-slipped tonic through a reinterpreted ii chord into the altered dominant (~5:45–8:30)
Larsen's own go-to solution for this exact two bars, built by chaining earlier ideas: keep the **Dm7-instead-of-Dø7** substitute from #2, then notice a *second* way to voice "that same D-minor sound" is an **F major 7** (an F major 7 shares four of D minor 9's notes — 3rd, 5th, ♭7, 9th — read from D, so it functions as a color-shifted stand-in for the ii chord). Chaining that with a side-slip of the tonic gives a four-chord, bar-by-bar chromatic ascent: **E♭maj7 (bar 1) → Emaj7 (still bar 1/2) → Fmaj7 (chromatic step up, standing in for the ii) → G7alt → resolve to C minor**. **Why it works, stated directly:** E major where the ear expects E♭ is "pretty far out" on its own; F major 7 substituting for the expected D half-diminished reads as "a D minor with a fifth and a ninth" — different color, same functional slot — so the ear can follow the ii-chord logic even through the substitution; and the whole four-chord run is one continuous **half-step-up chromatic planing** line, which is what makes disparate substitute chords cohere into a single directional gesture rather than a grab-bag. Explicitly named as a general tool he reaches for often ("these type of parallel movement... is something I do quite a lot"), not a one-off for this tune. → [[Chord Substitution]] (chromatic planing as a substitution device — candidate for a numbered technique, none of the existing 1–5 quite covers parallel chord-shape motion; flagged in Routed to), [[The ii-V-I]] § *The minor version*, [[Reharmonization]] § *7. Modal interchange* (functional-color-swap kinship)

### 5. Form-placement principle, stated directly as the throughline (~5:45, restated ~13:00)
"We have a sort of logical place to start and then we can easily move outside and also in a logical way move back inside, and we have a progression that somehow is going to make sense to us." Restated later as a general blues-form analogy: playing an altered dominant for four bars before the IV chord (instead of the usual one bar) is effective specifically *because* the listener would never expect it in bar 1 of a blues — **the form itself, not the chord in isolation, is what makes a substitute effective or not.** Matches [[Reharmonization]]'s existing *When not to* section (reharmonize the repeat, not the first pass) with an additional blues-specific worked case. → [[Reharmonization]] § *When not to*, [[Blues Form and Harmony]]

### 6. Bars 5–8 (Cm7 × 2, cadence to A♭): standard secondary-dominant fill vs. a chromatic passing chord that breaks the melody (~13:00–14:00)
The tune's own common practice: fill the two-bar Cm7 → A♭ cadence with a full secondary ii–V chain — **Cm7 → F7 → B♭m7 → E♭7 → A♭**. A second common variant substitutes a **chromatic passing chord** in the same slot — Larsen names it only as "a B" (unresolved in the transcript exactly which quality/voicing) and flags directly that **it does NOT fit the melody**, unlike the F7 fill, which does. **General principle stated:** always look for a **chromatic passing chord** to connect one chord to the next — a reusable device independent of this specific tune. → [[Chord Substitution]], [[The ii-V-I]]

### 7. Bars 5–8, tritone-substituting an entire two-bar ii–V rather than just the dominant (~14:00–16:00)
Over the long two-bar **B♭m7 → E♭7 → A♭** cadence, instead of tritone-subbing only the E♭7, Larsen substitutes the **whole two-bar ii–V** with its tritone-related minor ii–V: **Em7 → A7 → A♭**. Named three different ways in the same breath — "a minor 25," "a tritone 25," or functionally "an altered dominant for two bars" — because all three framings describe the same stretched-tension effect: instead of resolving quickly, the ear sits in unresolved dominant tension for the **full two bars** before landing. **Explicit mechanism:** this is just applying more of the same tension for longer, not a qualitatively different move — "having an altered-to-altered dominant is not going to be a strong sound by now [if it's brief], but... stretching it out and using it longer" restores the effect, the same logic as the four-bar-altered-dominant blues example in #5. → [[Reharmonization]] § *3. Tritone-substitute the dominants*, [[Chord Substitution]] § *3. Tritone substitution*, [[The ii-V-I]] § *The minor version*

### 8. Bars 9–12 (IV → ♭VII7 → I): three ways to redirect a resolution the listener already hears coming (~17:30–21:30)
The tune moves **A♭ (IV) → D♭7 (a backdoor-flavored ♭VII7) → E♭ (I)** — described as a spot where the listener can already hear the return home coming *before* it arrives (because of the ♭VII7's strong pull), making it a prime spot to subvert. Three redirects of the same D♭7, in increasing distance from home:
  (a) **Resolve D♭7 as a dominant to G♭ instead of up to E♭** — "already pretty far away," described as landing somewhere that reads almost like E♭ *minor* in feel.
  (b) **Resolve the same D♭7 to C major instead** — explicitly named **"almost a Coltrane thing to do"** (a half-step-down deceptive resolution in the Coltrane-changes family) — held for **two bars**, then typically approached into the next line's F7 via a preceding **Cm7 → F7** (a secondary ii–V feeding the F7 that was already coming next in the form).
  (c) A parallel option for the same A♭ → D♭7 → E♭ stretch: **A♭m → B major → E♭** — pick a triad or pentatonic shape that works over the A♭m/D♭7 pair and **move it up in parallel** before resolving to E♭; described as **not very outside** on its own but effective because the B major (as a B♭maj7-adjacent color) "sounds a bit strange" against the A♭ and D♭7 depending on rhythmic placement — the tension comes from the parallel-planing friction, not from a harmonically distant target.
  → [[Reharmonization]] § *3. Tritone-substitute the dominants* (D♭7's ♭VII7 role), [[Chord Substitution]] § *5. Coltrane changes* (option b), [[Diminished Chords]] (candidate ♯4-diminished framing, not explicitly named here but the same slot as the sibling Body and Soul video's function-change device)

### 9. Bars 13–14 (F7, the dominant of the dominant): usually left alone, but the same tools still apply (~21:30–23:00)
F7 in this spot is normally **not** reharmonized much for two compounding reasons: it often doesn't fit the melody, and it's already commonly played with a **Lydian dominant** sound (the standard chord-scale choice for V/V), so it already carries some "outside" color before any substitution — reducing the payoff of messing with it further. Larsen still notes the same tools apply if desired: since this chord commonly holds for a full two bars in this form, subbing in a **diminished** or **altered** sound for the whole stretch is "effectful" precisely because it's not expected there and the listener has to sit in the clash. → [[Reharmonization]], [[Diminished Chords]]

### 10. End of the first half (the A♭-melody turnaround): a chromatic half-step-above approach chord, named as common across multiple standards (~23:00–24:00)
At the end-of-first-half two-five (melody on A♭), a **chromatic passing/approach chord a half step above the target dominant** — described as "almost like a side slip," functioning as a dominant into the next chord before resolving normally. **Named as a recurring device across standards, not tune-specific**: cited as usable in *"There'll Never Be Another You,"* **"It Could Happen to You,"** the end of the first A of **"Have You Met Miss Jones,"** and (transcript garbled, likely "Gone with the Wind") a title captioned as **"G With the Wind."** → [[Chord Substitution]] § *3. Tritone substitution* (adjacent chromatic-approach family), [[Turnarounds]]

### 11. F minor slot: substituting a major triad for the expected minor subdominant, by matching function not quality (~24:00–25:15)
A substitute credited directly to a teacher at the Conservatory: in place of the expected **F minor** (subdominant minor role in E♭), play an **E major** triad instead — a substitution Larsen says he wouldn't have expected but finds genuinely beautiful in context, not just clever. **Functional explanation given:** F minor is the (borrowed) minor subdominant in E♭; E major is *also* analyzed here as a minor-subdominant-family substitute — i.e. the swap **keeps the harmonic function (subdominant) constant while changing the chord's quality**, the same principle as changing a chord's quality/color under a fixed melody note. A parallel, smaller-scope version of the same idea: use **m7♭5 in place of a plain m7** to create a minor-cadence flavor in the same slot. → [[Reharmonization]] § *1. Change the chord's function under a held note* (this is exactly that device, with the "held tone" generalized to "held function"), [[Borrowed Chords]] § *iv (minor four)*

### 12. Ladybird turnaround, displaced to start on the ii-adjacent chord instead of the tonic (~25:15–26:15)
A **"Ladybird" turnaround** (named after Tadd Dameron's tune of that name — a chromatic major-7 turnaround) applied starting from the **F minor** chord rather than from the tonic E♭, since the phrase in this tune begins on F minor, not on the I chord — the same turnaround shape, transposed to fit where the form actually starts. Cited comparison (offered tentatively, self-corrected on air — "correct me if I'm wrong"): a version of **"Embraceable You,"** possibly Charlie Parker's, using the same device at the end of its first half. Flagged as **unresolved/tentative** rather than a confirmed citation. → [[Turnarounds]]

### 13. General rule: minor cadences and major cadences are interchangeable, direction preserved (~26:15–26:45)
Stated as a standalone, reusable rule: **any minor cadence can be swapped for a major cadence** (and vice versa) while keeping the same resolution direction/target — i.e. changing a cadence's quality doesn't require changing where it resolves to. Offered without a specific worked example at this point in the video, as a closing generalization of the m7↔m7♭5 and Fm↔Emaj swaps already demonstrated in #11. → [[The ii-V-I]] § *The minor version*, [[Reharmonization]] § *1. Change the chord's function under a held note*

### 14. Closing turnaround: reduce to the essential two-bar-plus-ii–V skeleton before refilling with new material (~27:00–29:00)
The tune's closing turnaround is nominally a full chain (**A♭ – Gm – C7 – Fm –** ...), but Larsen points out **you don't have to play all of it** — it reduces to "just two bars of E♭ and a two-five." Once reduced, the same tools from bars 1–2 apply again: **E♭ → E → up to F** (the identical chromatic-planing device from #4, reused on the ending). Because the underlying skeleton is really a **cycle-of-fifths/circle-of-fifths motion**, it can also be rebuilt as a **chain of dominants**: **E♭ → A♭7 → G7 → G♭7 → F minor**, or made **fully chromatic** in its approach to F minor, or even have the **F minor itself swapped for B7** (its tritone-related dominant) as a further variant. → [[Reharmonization]] § *1. Change the chord's function under a held note* (the reduce-then-refill move — matches the sibling Body and Soul video's device #1 exactly), [[Chord Substitution]] § *3. Tritone substitution*, [[Turnarounds]]

### 15. Closing turnaround, counterpoint variant: chromatic-ascending bass line into a tritone-sub dominant (~29:00–30:00)
A distinct final variant on the same closing turnaround: the tune needs to get from **E♭ to F minor** via a C7. Substitute the C7 with its **tritone sub, G♭7**, then notice that **E♭ can move chromatically UP to G♭** (rather than the more obvious descending bass motion) — described as weak-sounding played as static chords but effective once treated as a **counterpoint line**, moving "increasingly away and into space" before landing on a dominant that resolves normally and gets the line back inside quickly. Matches the ascending-mirror-of-tritone-substitution principle already on file (if C→B♭ descending works, C→E ascending also works because E is B♭'s tritone sub) — same mechanism, worked here as a full melodic bass line rather than a single chord swap. → [[Chord Substitution]] § *3. Tritone substitution*, [[Reharmonization]] § *3. Tritone-substitute the dominants*

### 16. Meta-point: both a soloing tool and a written-arrangement tool, discovered largely by ear (~30:30–32:00)
Closing framing, applicable to the whole video: these substitutes work equally as **soloing vocabulary** (played mentally over a band holding the real changes) and as **written reharmonizations of the melody statement itself** — some options were flagged fitting-the-melody, some not, throughout. Larsen states many of these ideas were **found by accident while playing**, with the theoretical explanation worked out afterward — process note rather than a technique, but consistent with how loosely-labeled ("minor 25 or tritone 25 or altered dominant for two bars," #7) some of the devices are named on the fly. Recommends listening to **Brad Mehldau's** soloing for more of this kind of chord substitution creativity (transcript also has a garbled, unresolved reference — captioned "a lot of keys J" — possibly a mis-transcription of another artist's name; not confident enough to assert who).
