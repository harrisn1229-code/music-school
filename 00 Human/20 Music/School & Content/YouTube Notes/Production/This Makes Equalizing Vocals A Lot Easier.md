## Techniques
- **Every processor in a vocal chain — not just EQ — alters the frequency response, which is why EQ placement in the chain matters.** Saturators and compressors introduce harmonics (multiples of the fundamental) plus non-harmonic distortion, both of which amplify whatever range they land in. Reverb creates multiple decaying "taps" of the original signal — the earliest/loudest taps boost the same frequency range the dry signal already occupies. Delay does the same thing. **Practical implication: if you EQ only at the start of a chain without accounting for this, a lot of that balance gets quietly undone by everything downstream.** → [[EQ]], [[Compression]], [[Reverb]], [[Delay]], [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[All Processing is Connected - Crucial Knowledge for Pro Mixes & Masters]]
- **Why EQ the start of a vocal chain at all, if downstream processors will partly undo it? To control what gets FED into those processors, not to fully balance the vocal in one pass.** The first EQ's job is damage control and pre-shaping, not final tone: high-pass below the fundamental to remove plosives/rumble/unmusical low content before it can be amplified by later saturation or compression; a bell cut on an overly hot low-mid range before it gets compressed harder than it should be; a small boost on a range you WANT more of before a compressor, since makeup gain after compression will amplify that pre-boosted range further and can pick up a slightly more "processed" character as a side effect. → [[EQ]], [[Compression Staging]]
- **Dynamic EQ vs. a dedicated de-esser for sibilance — same CPU cost, different control tradeoffs, plus a dual-filter dynamic-EQ recipe if you go that route.** Both cost about the same computationally (a dynamic bell filter ≈ a de-esser as a processing load). Dynamic EQ makes it EASIER to pinpoint the exact offending frequency; a dedicated de-esser or multiband compressor typically gives MORE control over the attack/release of the reaction. If using dynamic EQ for de-essing specifically: **run two filters** — one narrow band (~200–300 Hz wide) that attenuates more aggressively, PLUS a second, more gradual bell filter in the general sibilant area for gentler broad control; fine-tune both bands' center frequencies until they land exactly on the sibilance — the more accurately positioned, the less total attenuation is needed to control it. → [[De-essing]], [[EQ]]
- **A seven-part conceptual map of where different kinds of vocal information sit across the frequency range — a mental model for deciding what to EQ and why, not a fixed recipe.** From low to high: **(1) Below the fundamental** — unrelated/unmusical (mic rumble, plosives, ambient noise, room noise); nothing musically useful lives here, safe to high-pass. **(2) The fundamental range** — the note actually being sung; everything else in this list is built on top of it. **(3) 2nd/3rd-order harmonics** — fullness and warmth; too much amplitude here reads as muddiness. **(4)–(5) 4th–5th (and sometimes up to 7th) order harmonics** — vowel articulation, and can carry the start of nasal/congested-sounding resonances if a vocalist is prone to them. **(6) 8th–11th order harmonics, roughly 2–5 kHz** — a vocal formant carrying vowel + consonant information; this is the range the ear is most sensitive to (see [[EQ]]'s critical-band/ear-canal-resonance notes), so amplifying here reads as increased clarity/intelligibility. **(7a) 5–12 kHz** — percussive, language-specific consonant content (T, K, S and other non-tonal sounds) — not tied to the fundamental note, important for articulation but the first place things turn unpleasant if overdone; **(7b) above that — "air"** — very-high-order harmonics, room reflections, and other delicate, easily-masked detail. From the fundamental range up through the high-order harmonics, everything moves together when the note changes (they're all mathematically tied to the fundamental); the percussive and air ranges are the language-and-room-dependent parts layered on top. → [[EQ]], [[LEAD Slot]] (2.5–6 kHz), [[Vocal Mic Technique]], [[Pitch and Formant]]
