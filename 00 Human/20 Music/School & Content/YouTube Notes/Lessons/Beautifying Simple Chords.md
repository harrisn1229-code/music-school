## Synopsis

An early Max Konyi guitar-teacher lesson (2019, before his ear-training/theory-analysis channel focus) walking through "chord beautification": taking the most basic possible progression (C–G–Am–F, i.e. I–V–vi–IV) and dressing it up with extensions and inversions WITHOUT changing any chord's fundamental quality — C stays major, G stays major, Am stays minor, F stays major throughout. Demonstrates the actual decision process chord by chord on guitar, with exact fret numbers, landing on Cmaj7 (1st inversion), Gadd11 (2nd inversion), Am7add11 (no 5th), and Fmaj7sus2. ## What's taught

- **Core method: think in scale-degree numbers, not letter names.** C–G–Am–F in the key of C is 1–5–6–4 (or i–V–vi–IV as chord qualities/positions). Numbering generalizes the vocabulary across keys — once you know "what a 1 chord can take" (7, 9, 11, 13, 6, sus2, sus4...), that knowledge transfers to any key's 1 chord, not just C.
- **Beautification = same chord quality, plus deliberate extensions, plus inversion — not a different chord.** The whole exercise never alters whether a chord is major or minor; it only adds color tones and repositions the bass note. Extension choice should be deliberate, not "grab the fanciest-sounding option" — every extension (7, 9, 11, 13, sus2, sus4, 6) carries its own distinct emotional coloring, and the actual craft is knowing those colorings well enough to choose on purpose (do you want the 9's color? the 7's? do you still want the 5 or 3 present?), not stacking notes just because they're available.
- **Worked voicings, chord by chord (all guitar, fret numbers as given):**
  - **C → Cmaj7, 1st inversion** (3rd in the bass, not the root) — on the A string, frets 7-9-5-8, giving scale degrees 3-7-1-5 from the bass up. Chosen specifically for the "particularly gorgeous" quality of that scale-degree ordering.
  - **C → G bass motion**: rather than jumping, the bass drops from C's 3rd (E) down a whole step to G's 5th (D) — a smooth, deliberately chosen stepwise bass move rather than a root-to-root leap.
  - **G → Gadd11 (add4), 2nd inversion** (5th in the bass) — explicitly rejected G7 (dominant 7th) here as "a little too hokey" for the intended vibe, despite it being the obvious/default extension for a V chord; chose the 4th/11th instead. Voicing: 5th (D, bass) – 3rd (B) – 4th (C) – root (G), with the 3rd and 4th sitting a half step apart deliberately — a specific, intentionally-chosen "bite" color, learned and categorized as its own sound through repeated use rather than derived from a rule.
  - **Am → Am7add11, no 5th** — a root(open A)–♭3–11–♭7 voicing with the 5th dropped entirely. Framed as standard practice, not a compromise: **the 5th of a chord adds little color and is already implied by the root's own overtone series**, so dropping it (especially on guitar, with only four fretting fingers) is the default move, not an exception. Deliberately chose the 11 over the 9 here because the 9 "can make it a bit dark," and the desired vibe was simpler/more open, less jazzy.
  - **F → Fmaj7sus2** — 1-5-7-9 (root, 5th, 7th, 9th/2nd — no 3rd at all), a genuinely sus voicing (3rd fully replaced by the 2nd) rather than an add9. Fret numbers on the A string: 8-10-9-8.
  - **Continuity byproduct**: all four voicings share the same top note across the whole progression — not a deliberate rule applied on purpose, but a natural side effect of the individual voicing choices made chord by chord; noted as equally valid to have a changing top note instead.
- **General principle for how this knowledge is built**: none of these specific voicing choices are "spontaneous" — they come from having played each configuration many times before, listened to the result, and mentally filed/categorized it as a specific sound with a specific feeling. The craft is a vocabulary built through repeated deliberate experimentation (try an extension, listen, keep or discard, name it, remember it for next time), not a formula applied fresh each time.
