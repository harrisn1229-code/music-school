## Synopsis

A reference-song for every common chord TYPE (not progression) — the ear-training companion to identifying quality/color by sound rather than identifying a Roman-numeral function. Each chord type gets one memorable opening-chord example from a well-known song, the exact technique [[Identifying Chord Progressions by Ear]]'s "build harmonic vocabulary" step calls for.

## Techniques

Reference song per chord type (all confirmed as the OPENING chord of the cited song, easy to recall/test against):

- **Major triad**: "Mr. Blue Sky" (ELO) — F major.
- **Minor triad**: "Still D.R.E." (Dr. Dre) — B♭ minor.
- **Major 7th**: "This Guy's in Love with You" (Herb Alpert) — D♭maj7↔G♭maj7 pivot; the same chord is reused in "Half the World Away" (Oasis), which only applies the maj7 to the second chord.
- **Minor 7th**: "Rocket Man" (Elton John) — Gm7.
- **Dominant 7th**: "Let's Dance" (David Bowie) — E♭7, built by stacking the chord tones one at a time.
- **Diminished triad**: "Ghost Town" (The Specials) — opens by climbing a series of diminished triads.
- **Augmented triad**: "Oh! Darling" (The Beatles) — E augmented.
- **Sus4**: "Pinball Wizard" (The Who, iconic riff) and "Crazy Little Thing Called Love" (Queen) — both alternate a major chord with its sus4, using the tension-then-release the sus4 exists for.
- **Sus2** (rarer — Bennett notes it lacks sus4's satisfying resolution utility): "The Sound of Silence" (Simon & Garfunkel) — opening guitar riff arpeggiates an E♭sus2.
- **6th chord** (a dated, 1940s-60s-coded sound — a major triad + 6th degree, creating mild friction against the 5th a whole tone away): "My Baby Just Cares for Me" (Nina Simone). "Take On Me" (a-ha) — opening chord, deliberately in that retro tradition; voices the 6th and 5th an octave apart rather than adjacent, which diffuses some of the characteristic rub.
- **Power chord (5 chord)**: two notes (root + 5th), sometimes tripled with an octave-doubled root; most associated with distorted guitar — "You Really Got Me" (The Kinks), "Smells Like Teen Spirit" (Nirvana). Piano example: "All of Me" (John Legend) — opening riff's first chord is a clean F5.
- **7♯9 ("the Hendrix chord")**: a dominant 7 with both the major 3rd (in the voicing) and a ♯9 (functioning as a clashing minor-3rd color) stacked together, for a crunchy sound. "Purple Haze" (Jimi Hendrix, the namesake use). Also: "Michelle" (The Beatles) — the verse's 2nd chord is a B♭7♯9.
- **Minor-major 9** (a minor triad + major 7th + 9th — the "major" in the name refers to the 7th, not the 9th, which stays the same regardless of chord quality): a near-cliché "mysterious" sound closely associated with the James Bond theme, both the original and echoed across many later Bond songs.
