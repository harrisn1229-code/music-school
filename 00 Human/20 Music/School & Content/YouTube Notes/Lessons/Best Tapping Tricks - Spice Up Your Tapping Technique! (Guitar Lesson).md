# Best Tapping Tricks - Spice Up Your Tapping Technique! (Guitar Lesson)

## Synopsis

Five two-hand tapping tricks, all demonstrated inside one composed lick built on A minor pentatonic (fretting hand) against A Dorian's color tones (tapped) — same notes as C major/A Aeolian reframed, giving a Dorian-vs-Aeolian color choice depending on which chord/tonal center the phrase resolves against.

## Techniques

- **Combine two scales via hand split: fretting hand plays a familiar scale box, tapping hand adds the other scale's color notes.** Worked example: A minor pentatonic box (5th fret, low E string) in the fretting hand, with the tapping hand adding B (major 2nd) and F♯ (major 6th, the characteristic Dorian-defining interval) — notes outside the pentatonic box. A general lick-writing technique: pick a familiar scale shape, then tap in a second scale's distinguishing notes rather than relearning a whole new fingering.
- **Match tapped-note articulation to the surrounding hammer-ons/pull-offs so the tapped notes disappear into one continuous legato stream.** The goal (and the mark of an advanced player) is that a listener can't identify which notes were tapped versus hammered/pulled — achieved by matching note spacing/timing and attack between the tap and the surrounding slurred notes, not by the tap technique itself.
- **Vary rhythmic phrasing between ascending and descending passes of the same lick** (e.g. tap-then-pull-off going one direction, a different grouping going the other) — a way to add interest without changing the pitch content.
- **Deliberately let tapped notes overlap for dissonance**, rather than only using tapping for single clean melodic notes. Worked example: tap a minor 2nd or major 7th interval (a semitone apart) against a sustained note, letting both ring together — a tension effect BERNTH names as one of his own frequent licks.
- **Double tapping**: tap a note, pull off, hammer the same note back on, repeat rapidly — goal is a fast, even stream. Main risk is unwanted ringing from neighboring strings; recommended to start practicing on an **outer string (low or high E)**, where there's only one neighbor to control, before moving to inner strings.
