# Circle-of-Fourths One-Position Scale Drill

Part of [[Guitar Positions]] — Jared Borkowski's own scale/mode mastery-check drill, part of the "Vocabulary" category of his personal practice-tracking system (previewed on [[This might ruin my channel Practice Log No. 1|Practice Log #1]], demonstrated in full on [[The Scale Practice Method That Forces You to Really Know Your Fretboard]]). Unlike [[Continuous Scale Exercise]] (same channel — training the *reactive* skill of finding the nearest correct note when a tune's actual key changes) or [[Never-Ending Scale Exercise]] (Rosenwinkel/Jens Larsen — decoupling scale-swap timing from the harmony on purpose), this drill isn't about a tune at all: it's a **pure vocabulary-mastery gate** for one scale or mode at a time — proof you can execute it, clean, in every key, without a chord progression involved.

## The core idea
Take one scale or one mode. Stay in roughly one fretboard position/area (small shifts as the shape moves are fine — same spirit as [[One-Position Fretboard Overview Drill]]'s "don't be rigid about a strict box"). Play it through **all 12 keys back to back, in the circle of fourths, in one continuous take, with zero mistakes.** That single clean pass is the mastery gate — it's what earns the checkmark in a scale/mode tracking list.

## Two axes of scale practice (pick one per pass)
1. **Same root, all positions** — play all five scale-forms/shapes off one root note, walking the shape up the neck.
2. **Same position, all roots** — stay in one fretboard area and cycle through all 12 keys (this is the axis this drill uses). Both are worth drilling; they build different fluency (shape-recall vs. key-recall).

## Rules of the drill
1. **Subdivision depends on the exercise type:** 16th notes for a **parent-scale** pass (e.g. harmonic minor — just play every note available in the position, any starting point); **8th notes** for a **root-to-root mode** pass (e.g. Lydian — start and end each key's run on the mode's own root, the only way the modal color is actually audible rather than sounding like its parent scale). The mode version needs the faster subdivision because root-to-root phrasing requires more pause/restart overhead between keys.
2. **Metronome click = quarter notes, placed on the offbeat**, not the downbeat.
3. **Start each new key on the next available beat** — the gap before a key's run starts isn't fixed; it depends on exactly where the previous key's run ended.
4. **Range: lowest note to highest note and back**, within the position, fingered with a consistent "inchworm" (rolling, no-open-string) fingering scheme rather than ad hoc.
5. **Sequence: full circle of fourths, all 12 keys**, staying in the same position/area the whole way through.
6. **Pass/fail gate: one clean take through all 12 keys with zero mistakes** — not multiple clean reps. A "clean take" after many failed attempts still counts, because each individual key/scale-form already got repeated 2–3+ times chasing it — that repetition *is* the practice, the final clean pass is just the proof.
7. **Log the tempo you clear it at, and never repeat that tempo once checked off.** Next time you revisit a checked-off scale/mode, it has to be at a **higher tempo**, or you move to a different scale/mode instead — the discipline that keeps this from becoming comfort-zone repetition.

## Worked reference points (from the source video)
- **Harmonic minor (parent scale):** 90 BPM, 16th notes, one position — took **20+ attempts** to land the first clean pass through all 12 keys.
- **Lydian (mode), starting from D♭:** root-to-root phrasing, **8th notes at 180 BPM** (a doubled 90 BPM quarter-note click) — the doubled subdivision compensates for the extra per-key pause/restart the root-to-root method requires.

## Why bother (stated purpose, not just drilling for its own sake)
The point isn't the scale drilling itself — it's what the drilling is *for*: being genuinely ready to use these scales improvisationally, understanding the fretboard well enough that finding any scale/mode in any key/position stops being a lookup, and stress-testing actual technical execution (not just conceptual "I know this scale") under a real time constraint. The same focused, milestone-gated, "clean-take-then-move-on" method is meant to transfer directly to repertoire practice, not stay confined to pure scale drilling.

## Underlying prerequisite
Built on major-scale fingerings playable inside **any 3-fret position** (all other scales are derived/fingered relative to that), per Jared's separate "Simplifying Jazz Guitar" series (not yet in the vault).

## Arpeggio/chord-tone variant
The same milestone-tempo-tracking mechanic, applied to chord-tone vocabulary instead of scales/modes: cycle a given **arpeggio type** through the **circle of fourths** inside a fixed fret-position range (e.g. positions 4–5–6), one chord type per pass — **major triad, minor triad, diminished triad, augmented triad, sus2, sus4, major 7, major 6, dominant 7, minor 7** — each type gets its own tracked tempo milestone, same check-off/never-repeat-the-cleared-tempo rule as the scale version above. Part of Jared's **Single Location Improv Method** (playing all chord-tone formations and improvising within three fret positions), the system underlying his course "Nail the Changes." Demonstrated on [[The ultimate practice system is coming together (how I'm tracking progress)]], including a worked major-triad pass through all 12 keys (C → F → B♭ → A♭ → D♭ → G♭ → C; shapes start repeating at D♭, only five distinct shapes across 12 keys).

## From videos

- **Why the circle of fourths specifically, not fifths, is the drilling sequence for running material through all 12 keys: it's the most closely related adjacent key, changes only one scale note per step, and it's the more common real-world modulation direction.** Moving a key center by a fourth (equivalently, a fifth in the other direction) changes only **one note, by one half step**, out of the entire scale's note collection — the closest possible relationship between two different keys. Practiced in one fixed fretboard position, each new key overlaps six notes with the previous one and differs by exactly one — sharpening the ear on *which specific note* changed rather than presenting a totally unfamiliar collection at every step. **The fourths direction specifically (not fifths) is used because moving up a fourth is more common in actual chord progressions/real music than moving up a fifth** — drilling that direction better matches what actually shows up in tunes.
