# Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs

**Everything around the lead** — doubles, harmonies, ad-libs — and the rule that decides how each one gets treated.

## The governing rule

> **The further a part is from delivering the lyric, the more it can become texture.**

That single sentence answers most stack questions, and it's the one the placement asked for (VOX-11).

| Part | Position | Level | Processing | Job |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Lead** | Centre | Loudest | **Least** | Carry the lyric |
| **Double** | Wide L/R | Well under lead | Moderate | Thicken — not heard as separate |
| **Harmony** | Wide, often narrower than doubles | Lower still | More | Colour |
| **Ad-lib** | Widest, furthest back | Varies | **Heaviest** | Energy, answer, texture |

The lead stays present and relatively dry because every word has to be parseable. An ad-lib doesn't — nobody needs to catch every syllable — so it can take heavy delay, reverb, filtering and pitching.

## Doubles

**A real double is a second performance of the same line, layered with the first.**

**A copied-and-delayed double is not the same thing.** An identical copy summed with itself just gets louder — it can't thicken, because there's no variation to create the effect. Delay or pitch it and you get comb filtering or a slapback, not a double.

What makes a real double work is that the two takes differ slightly in timing, pitch and tone. That variation *is* the thickening.

**Practical:**
- **Track doubles, don't fake them.** Ask for the same delivery, not a different interpretation.
- **Tighten timing** on the consonants — doubles that disagree on where a word ends read as sloppy rather than wide.
- **Tune doubles harder than the lead.** Pitch disagreement between layers is far more audible than one voice being slightly off.
- **Pan hard and drop the level.** A double you can consciously hear as a separate voice is too loud.

**A counter-perspective on the "tune doubles harder" rule above — Kevin Parker (Tame Impala) argues the opposite is what makes his signature double read as a double at all.** On "The Less I Know The Better," the only vocal layer is a single double (no harmony) — Parker states his own pitch isn't razor-precise take-to-take, and that's *why* the double is audibly perceived as two voices rather than one. Counter-example cited: Stevie Wonder's famously exact pitch meant his own doubled vocals reportedly didn't read as doubled at all — just louder, since there was no audible disagreement between the takes for the ear to latch onto. Reconciling with this page's own rule: both are describing the same mechanism (pitch variation between layers is what the ear perceives as "doubled" vs. "loud") — the difference is intent. Tune hard for a clean, controlled double; leave natural pitch drift in for a looser, more audibly-doubled character. → Kevin Parker, [[Behind the Track - 'The Less I Know The Better' by Tame Impala]]

**[[Artificial Double Tracking (ADT)]]** is the deliberate fake — the Abbey Road technique, a delayed and modulated copy. It's a *sound* (very Lennon), not a substitute for a real double. Worth knowing as the exception that proves the rule.

## Harmony stacks

- **Fewer, louder** beats more, quieter — three well-sung harmony parts beat eight mushy ones.
- **Double the harmonies too** if they need to compete with a doubled lead.
- **Keep them below the lead** even in the chorus. A harmony at lead level turns the lyric into a chord.
- **Watch the top voice** — the highest harmony is what the ear tracks, so it needs to be the most accurate.

## Ad-libs

Secondary vocal responses layered around the lead — the strand's other strong answer on the placement (VOX-07).

**What they do:**
- **Fill the gaps** at the ends of phrases, where the lead has stopped
- **Answer the main line** — call and response
- **Add energy** without adding words
- **Widen the stereo picture**, since they usually sit off-centre

**Arranging them:**
- **Space, not density.** Ad-libs go where the lead *isn't*. An ad-lib on top of a lyric fights it.
- **Pan them off-centre**, keeping the lead centred. Kenny Beats' point about aux percussion applies here identically — see [[Width and Stereo]].
- **Vary the treatment** across the track — a filtered one, a heavily delayed one, a dry close one. They don't all need the same chain.
- **Print the good accidents.** Ad-libs are often the best thing on the record and are almost always improvised.
- **A chopped, repeated ad-lib can work as a continuous background texture, not just a phrase-gap filler — dial it down in level rather than removing it once its role becomes clear.** A rhythmic "so-so-so-so" vocal chop, originated as an unplanned improvised ad-lib on an original demo vocal, was kept running throughout the whole track as a textural bed layer; an early mix had it much louder before it became clear its job was texture, not a lead element — pulled down in level, not cut. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]] — Ryan Tedder, [[Behind the Track - 'greedy' by Tate McRae]]
- **A naturally quiet good accident doesn't need a louder retake — automate it up instead.** Billie Eilish did a genuinely quiet, natural laugh on a verse take that Finneas and the engineer liked; rather than asking for a louder take, they rode the gain up **+27 dB** on that single moment so it would pop out in the mix — a concrete, quantified version of "print the good accidents," where automation (not performance) does the rescuing. → [[Automation]], [[Billie Eilish Vocal Chain]], [[Gain Staging]] — Mix with the Masters (Finneas), [[Vocal production for Billie Eilish by Finneas]]

- **The "Prismizer" technique (Bon Iver's *22, A Million*, Frank Ocean's *Blonde*): a MIDI-controlled harmony engine sidechained from a clean pre-recorded vocal, played live like an instrument.** Setup: a software-instrument track running a harmony-generation plugin (Antares Harmony Engine EVO — not owned, see [[Plugin Wishlist]]) as a MIDI-controlled effect, input source MIDI Omni, sidechained from the pre-recorded vocal; playing notes/chords on a MIDI controller (a chord-trigger effect turns one note into a full chord's harmonies) generates live-performable harmonies from the recorded take. **Reverb/delay must come AFTER the harmony engine, never before** — it needs the cleanest possible input to track pitch accurately. Tested on other sources: guitar works with caveats (DI only, no drive/distortion, single notes track cleaner than chords); drums work differently than expected — the harmony's KEY setting (not the hit itself) drives character, low key reads gated/80s, high key reads melodic/synth-like; strings and piano largely fail — the engine's tracking can't cleanly handle timbres/ADSRs that diverge far from a voice, producing distortion, missed notes, or phase cancellation instead of usable harmony. → [[Plugin Wishlist]] — Sage Audio, [What is the Prismizer Effect and Can it Be Used on Instruments](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6uQIHGJ9AhE)

- **Chop a spoken-word vocal into a rhythmic pattern, then reverse the resulting background layer, as a texture rather than a harmony device.** Credited as a James Blake/Frank Ocean-catalog move: take a spoken-word sample (Frank Ocean's spoken word, in the source case), chop it up so it lands as a rhythmic pattern rather than a continuous phrase, then reverse that chopped background-vocal layer — the reversal is applied AFTER the chopping, not before, so each reversed fragment still lands on the rhythm grid. Paired in the source with a duplicated lead vocal pitched up 7 semitones for a second, not-quite-diatonic layer — see [[Pitch and Formant]] for that half. → [[Pitch and Formant]], [[Layering and Stacking]] — loner, [James Blake's production tricks are GAMECHANGING!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9FX7p23hSlc)
- **Deliberately don't tune a vocal stack — the natural pitch variation between takes is what makes it sound rich, not a flaw to fix.** Record many real takes ("a truckload... until we had perfect takes") rather than comping/tuning one take into many; every layer bending slightly differently into a note is exactly what reads as human, wide, and "chorus"-detuned. Tuning every layer individually (or worse, running Auto-Tune on each) collapses that variation and causes phasing instead. Same logic extends to harmonies — rarely tuned either, for the same reason. → [[Vocal Tuning]], [[Melody Writing]] — Disclosure, [How To Get MORE From Your Vocals (12 Tips)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZCpgHFNlXxg)
- **A stacked "hype vocal" chant chorus, grouped and bus-processed as one texture layer** — record several takes of a shouted hype phrase, route them all to a shared group, apply compression + EQ (with a high-pass) across the whole group, then send the group to a room reverb — a distinct role from lead/ad-lib vocals: pure energy texture for an EDM build/drop. → [[Layering and Stacking]] — Max Konyi, [How to Make Dubstep in Studio One with Stock Instruments | Weekly Challenge #10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sD3mE-siI5M)
- **Turn a hype-vocal hit into a riser with an extreme two-octave pitch bend** — pick one hit from the hype-vocal stack and pitch-bend it rising two full octaves over its duration — builds a riser from vocal material instead of a synth sweep or noise source, reusing an asset already in the mix.
- **"Dup and loop" a short vocal phrase — pitched down, looped, then sped back up and chorused — as a pre-drop buildup stutter** — a vocal-based alternative to a snare-roll/synth buildup, placed immediately before the drop for tension. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
