# How to Play Feel Good Inc. on Bass

## Synopsis

BassBuzz's Josh teaches the "Feel Good Inc." (Gorillaz) bassline as a five-challenge progression, each challenge adding one new layer of difficulty on top of the same short riff rather than teaching it all at once: (1) right notes with a fretting/plucking fingering system explicitly *not* synced to each other, (2) the same notes with correct syncopated rhythm, (3) the same notes with correct note length/muting, (4) the riff's signature slide, and (5) the short bass solo that recombines everything. ## What's taught

**Challenge 1 — notes, with independent fretting/plucking fingering:**
- **Named anti-pattern: "finger syncing"** — the common beginner instinct to mirror plucking-hand finger choice to fretting-hand finger choice (fret with index, pluck with index; fret with middle, pluck with middle) — explicitly called out as something to *never* do, because it's inflexible and breaks the moment a line needs to pluck the same note twice or fret with the ring/pinky, which happens in basically every real bassline.
- **The fix: fretting fingering and plucking fingering are two independent systems that don't relate to each other.** Work out each hand's pattern on its own terms.
- **Fretting for this riff:** since it's just open strings plus 2nd and 3rd fret, use index for every 2nd-fret note and middle for every 3rd-fret note — worked sequence: open, open, index, middle, middle, index, open, open, middle, index, middle, open.
- **Plucking for this riff:** straight alternating index-middle regardless of what the fretting hand is doing (open strings included) — index, middle, index, middle, index, middle, repeated for the full 12-note riff. Syncing the plucking pattern to the fretting pattern above would force inefficient same-finger repeats.

**Challenge 2 — rhythm and syncopation:**
- **Syncopation, defined practically:** notes that land squarely on the beat are easy to feel/sing; scoot those same notes to start *off* the beat and they push against the pulse — harder to feel, but is what makes a groove-based riff (like this one) sound cool rather than square.
- **The riff's actual rhythm mixes on-beat and off-beat notes** — flagged as the normal case for a funky riff, not an edge case. *(Exact beat-by-beat count is garbled in the caption transcript — treat the specific subdivision as approximate; the reusable takeaway is the method below, not the precise count.)*
- **The "beat ruler" visual method** — a horizontal ruler graphic marking each beat and its off-beat subdivision, used to see at a glance which notes of a line land on vs. off the beat, useful even for players who already read standard notation.
- **The humming cheat code for internalizing syncopation: fill in the silent beats with a hummed sound, don't just silently count.** When intuitive feel breaks down under syncopation (you lose track of how much space is between two hits), hum something across the rests to keep tracking every beat rather than leaving them blank.
- **Borrow the drum part's actual hits as your humming syllables.** For this riff: a rest lands on beat 2 and beat 4, and there's a snare on beat 2, snare on beat 4, kick on beat 1 in the real drum track — so hum **"bum-snare, bum-bum-bum-snare, bum-kick, bum"** in place of counting numbers, locking the bass rhythm to the drum pattern that's actually driving it.
- **Player testimony on why syncopation feels "wobbly":** Shay Adelican (Gorillaz's current bassist) on this exact riff — it can feel wobbly to play because it's a funky one; the advice is to relax into it rather than fight the wobble.

**Challenge 3 — tight note lengths / muting:**
- **Sounding "right notes, right time, still wrong" is a note-*length* problem, not a note-onset problem.** Starting a note on time isn't enough — it also has to *end* on time, or the whole line reads as sloppy even with correct pitches and correct rhythm.
- **A held note has to be actively ended**, either by touching the string with the fretting hand or the plucking hand — bass notes don't stop on their own.
- **The specific mechanic for this riff's ending rest:** release the fretting pressure on the last note before the rest, plus additional safety mutes as needed, so beat 4's silence is actually silent. → full technique detail lives on [[Bass Note Muting Technique]].
- **Reference point (not a rule to copy):** comparing the studio-track bass to the Letterman live performance, the live note lengths are audibly looser/different from the tightly-controlled studio version — the video's take is that unless you're deliberately reinterpreting the part, matching the version everyone already knows (the studio note lengths) is what makes your playing "land" as recognizable to a listener.

**Challenge 4 — the slide:**
- **Press down the whole slide, but don't over-press.** Full contact throughout the slide, but excess finger/thumb pressure causes the hand to get stuck partway through — a real failure mode, not just a comfort issue.
- **Look at where you're going, not where you came from.** The eyes should already be on the destination fret before/during the slide, not tracking the starting position.
- **Finger choice for this specific slide: pinky**, because it requires the least amount of hand-position shifting for what comes right after — stated as the practical reason, not an absolute rule (any finger technically works).
- **Slides are about the destination, not the starting point.** This slide's notation carries no note leading into it and uses a scoop mark instead — the takeaway generalizes: when a slide has no defined entry note, the exact starting fret barely matters, only the landing fret does.
- **Starting-distance sweet spot: "some frets away," not one fret and not the max.** One fret away reads as anticlimactic (too small a gesture to register as a slide); the maximum possible distance is excessive; a middle distance gives the slide its audible sweep without being either.
- **Execution for this riff: from a few frets below, press down, pluck, then immediately slide up to the 7th fret** — the pluck happens at the start position, not the landing position.
- **Expect discomfort as a normal, temporary cost.** Skin scraping against metal frets/strings during slides stings until the fingertip toughens up with repetition — framed as an expected part of learning slides, not a sign of doing it wrong.

**Challenge 5 — the bass solo (recombines everything):**
- **Fretting for the solo:** pinky, index, pinky, shift, index, open.
- **Plucking for the solo:** alternating index-middle-index-middle-index, then free choice for the final two notes after the phrase's break — i.e. the independent-fingering system from challenge 1 is exactly what makes this solo playable without finger-syncing collisions.
- **Rhythm for the solo, via the same beat-ruler method as challenge 2:** on, off, off, off, off, on, on.
- **The solo is explicitly framed as a synthesis exercise** — fretted-note independence, syncopated rhythm, and rest-muting all show up in one short passage, so nailing it is the real test of challenges 1–3 rather than new material on its own.

**Tuning (song-specific, but the "why" generalizes):**
- **The riff is in E♭**, and Gorillaz's actual touring/session bassist Shay Adelican confirms in a shared voice note: "the riff is E flat, so I detune my E string to E flat... yeah, the song is in E flat."
- **You only need to retune the E string** (down a half step to E♭) — this riff only uses the E and A strings, so the D and G strings can be left standard/ignored entirely for this song.
- **Shay's own approach differs from the beginner teaching method:** rather than crossing from the low string to the A string as taught in challenges 1–5, Shay plays the *entire* main riff on the single detuned low string — likely because the notes sound fatter on the thicker string, and it means less on-stage retuning overall. BassBuzz doesn't teach it that way to beginners because the cross-string version is judged easier to learn first, but names it as the "cooler," more advanced alternative once the basic version is solid.

**Song context:** "Feel Good Inc." is the lead single from Gorillaz's 2005 album *Demon Days* — described in the video as the riff that "sold a million bass guitars," with Murdoc's (the band's in-universe bassist/frontman persona) own music-video commentary cited as calling out the riff's syncopation as key to why it hits so hard.
