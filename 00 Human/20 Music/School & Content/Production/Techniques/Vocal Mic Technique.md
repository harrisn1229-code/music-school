# Vocal Mic Technique

**The part of the vocal sound that's decided before any plugin loads.** Distance, angle and what the singer hears — none of which can be fixed later.

Scoped to what's actually owned: the **[[Neumann TLM 102|TLM 102]]** (large-diaphragm condenser) and the **[[Shure SM57|SM57]]** (dynamic), usually through the [[UAD Neve 1073]].

## Proximity effect

**Bass response rises as a directional microphone gets closer to the source.** This is the placement's blank (VOX-06) and it's the most useful mic fact there is, because it turns distance into a tone control.

- **Close (2–6 in)** — noticeably more low end. Intimate, thick, "radio voice". Also more plosive risk and more mouth noise.
- **Mid (8–12 in)** — the neutral working range for most vocals.
- **Far (12 in+)** — thinner, more room in the sound, more natural dynamics.

**Use it deliberately:**
- Want weight and intimacy on a quiet verse → move in.
- Vocal getting boomy or muddy → **back the singer off** before reaching for an EQ cut. It's the same fix at the source rather than after the fact.
- Want a consistent tone → keep distance consistent, which is what a pop filter doubles as (it sets a minimum distance).

**It only applies to directional mics** — cardioid and its relatives. An omni pattern has essentially none, which is exactly why omni is sometimes the right choice for a singer who moves.

## Distance and angle

**Off-axis** — angling the mic slightly rather than pointing it straight at the mouth:

- **Reduces plosives** (the blast of air from p/b sounds misses the capsule)
- **Reduces sibilance** slightly, since high frequencies are more directional
- **Softens the top end** a little — useful on a bright voice

The standard move is to have the singer address the mic slightly off-centre, or tilt the mic a few degrees. Combined with a pop filter it removes most plosive problems at the source rather than with the 100 Hz surgical cut described in [[Rap Vocal Production]].

**Consistency is the real skill.** A singer who moves 6 inches during a take produces level and tone swings that no compressor fully fixes. For a mover: back them off slightly, or accept it and ride the fader.

## TLM 102 vs SM57

| | [[Neumann TLM 102\|TLM 102]] | [[Shure SM57\|SM57]] |
|---|---|---|
| **Type** | Large-diaphragm condenser | Dynamic |
| **Detail** | High — captures air and nuance | Lower — rounder, more limited top |
| **Room sensitivity** | **High** — picks up the room | **Low** — rejects room, forgiving |
| **Handles loud** | Fine, but can get harsh | Extremely well |
| **Best for** | Sung leads, quiet/intimate takes, anything wanting air | Loud/aggressive delivery, untreated rooms, a deliberately grittier sound |

**The room decides more than the mic does.** In an untreated space the SM57's rejection is often the better result, because a detailed condenser capturing a bad room gives you a detailed bad room. That's a real and underrated reason to reach for the dynamic on rap vocals.

## The headphone mix

**Singers pitch and phrase against what they hear** — so the monitor mix is part of the performance, not a convenience (VOX-08, which you got).

- **Enough vocal to hear pitch**, not so much that they under-sing. Too loud in the cans and singers pull back; too quiet and they push and go sharp.
- **A little reverb helps almost everyone.** It makes singing feel easier and encourages a more confident take. Print dry, monitor wet.
- **Keep the low end controlled** — a bass-heavy mix makes pitch hard to hear.
- **Ask.** "More or less of yourself?" costs ten seconds and improves every subsequent take.

**One headphone off** is common for pitch reference and is fine — expect a little bleed.

## From videos

- **Choose a tracking mic for transient/proximity feel over fidelity, and deliberately record in-room rather than isolated when the take calls for it.** Jack Antonoff tracked "I'm Not Joking" on a Shure 50-series dynamic instead of a condenser or an SM58 specifically for the transient response of lips hitting the mic and to feel live with the band, not booth-isolated. Also states vocals can be deliberately left sonically un-glued from the rest of the track (rather than matched in reverb/frequency to blend), for a "hear the overdubness" aesthetic — citing later Lou Reed and Beatles records. → [[Jack Antonoff Vocal Chain]] — Jack Antonoff / Bleachers (Tape Notes), [[TN 182 Bleachers (Jack Antonoff) - Full Video Episode]]
- **Raw-over-clean is a deliberate mic philosophy applied at the source, not fixed in post — including swapping the "usual" mic mid-album for a performance reason.** Myles Smith / Peter Fenn tried cleaner mics early on and rejected them for polishing away wanted grit/texture; most ad-libs/BVs go through a [[Shure SM7]] "just because it sounds raw" (one full song, "Solo," was tracked on an iPhone for the same reason), and on *Dying Day* the entire lead vocal — usually tracked on a Neumann U87 (not owned) — moved to the SM7 purely because it let the singer sit back and relax into a more natural take, a second independent "performability over fidelity" data point alongside Kevin Parker's SM7 choice below. Acoustic guitars, by contrast, go through a Soyuz pencil mic specifically because "the source sound is so important" to their overall approach — same source-first philosophy, different instrument. → [[Shure SM7]] — Myles Smith / Peter Fenn (Tape Notes), [[TN 184 Myles Smith & Peter Fenn - Full Video Episode]]
- **Choose a tracking mic for performability, not just tone — a stated, specific tradeoff.** Kevin Parker (Tame Impala) tried a U47 clone that "sounded incredible" but couldn't hold it while performing; settled on a [[Shure SM7]] specifically because he could hold and move with it in-hand while singing, which he says mattered more than he'd expected. Tracked through a dbx 165 compressor live, with post-processing kept deliberately plain ("just compression and EQ… quite stock standard") — the character work lives in the effects bus, not the tracking chain. → [[Tame Impala Vocal Chain]] — Kevin Parker (Tape Notes), [[TN 188 Tame Impala - Full Video Episode (Part 1)]]
- **Michael Jackson's vocal chain, era by era (Bruce Swedien) — mic and preamp were re-evaluated each era against the singer's delivery and the recording medium, never a fixed "house sound."** *Thriller*: Shure SM7 (low output, chosen specifically because Michael "could pack a punch" — wrong choice for a whispery singer) → Harrison console's own transformer mic pre → an 1176 at an extreme 12:1 ratio used purely as a peak-catching safety net ("wasn't compressing, was just capturing, preventing overloads"); the real coloring/leveling came from hitting analog tape hot and using tape saturation as the actual compressor, while Swedien publicly disdained hardware compression ("compression is for kids"). *Bad*: same SM7, but a high-fidelity Studio Technologies preamp powered from an external battery (charged separately from the studio's own power) specifically to eliminate power-supply noise — driven by the move to digital recording (a Mitsubishi 32-track format, extremely flat/transient-accurate, "less forgiving" than tape), which made signal-path precision critical in a way tape's own coloration had made less urgent. *Dangerous/HIStory*: a specific Neve preamp (Brent Averill-built module) with the SM7 for most songs, but a tube Neumann U47 for ballads specifically, where Michael sang noticeably softer — mic choice tracked vocal delivery within one artist's catalog, not fixed per artist or album. → [[Compression Staging]] — Anthony Marinelli & Matt Forger, [[The Evolution of Michael Jacksons Vocals - From Thriller and Beyond]]

- **A seven-part conceptual map of where different kinds of vocal information sit across the frequency range — useful as a diagnostic frame when a voice isn't cutting through, independent of any specific EQ move.** Low to high: unrelated/unmusical content below the fundamental (mic rumble, plosives) · the fundamental itself · 2nd/3rd-order harmonics (fullness/warmth) · 4th–7th-order harmonics (articulation, nasal resonance) · 8th–11th-order harmonics around **2–5 kHz** (the formant carrying vowel/consonant clarity — the ear's most sensitive zone) · **5–12 kHz** percussive consonant content (T/K/S) · "air" above that (very-high harmonics, room reflections). Full breakdown with the EQ implications on [[EQ]]. → [[EQ]], [[Pitch and Formant]] — Sage Audio, [This Makes Equalizing Vocals A Lot Easier](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dM8TyAYzpxs)
- **A U47-style large-diaphragm tube condenser (Wunder Audio CM7) as a standing vocal-chain default, chosen over vintage U47 hardware even when a real one is available in the room.** Jack Antonoff: "every time I go to a studio there's always a vintage 47 and they always sound great, but I end up using it more on guitars and usually just go through the CM7... it's just like a wonderful jumping-off point." No dedicated vocal booth — the mic stays permanently set up in the writing/production room rather than being reserved for a separate "vocal session," so an idea can be captured the moment it happens (see [[Vocal Comping and Takes]] for the full capture-from-writing workflow). → [[Vocal Comping and Takes]], [[Plugin Wishlist]] — Mix with the Masters (Jack Antonoff), [[Sonically enhancing the meaning of songs with Jack Antonoff]]
- **A U-47 as the historical default vocal mic — but chosen per-voice, with real fallback options, not run on autopilot.** Al Schmitt: "my go-to microphone is a U-47. If that doesn't work with the artist, I'll try a Brauner microphone, or I have so many options, so I'll go get one that finally works." Names the U-47 as Frank Sinatra's mic (also Nat King Cole's and Dean Martin's) and calls it the one that "usually works for everybody" — but immediately qualifies that some voices (a "girl singer" is the stated example) work better on something else, so the audition stays real even with a strong go-to. Not owned — see [[Neumann U47]] (comparison point to the owned [[Neumann TLM 102]]). → [[Sound Selection Guide]] — Al Schmitt (Mix with the Masters), [[Recording techniques with Al Schmitt]]
- **A singer's own mic technique can do most of a tracking compressor's job before the signal ever reaches it — a direct, named case of the proximity-effect discipline this page already teaches.** U-47 → Neve preamp → compressor, "but we weren't hardly using any compression at all — she's got great mic technique, she leans in and she backs off, so it makes my life so much easier." Where this page's proximity-effect section above frames "back the singer off before reaching for an EQ cut" as an engineer's corrective move, here the same in/out movement is something a skilled performer self-manages live, removing the need for gain reduction almost entirely. → [[UAD Neve 1073]], [[1 Main Vocal Track]] — Al Schmitt (Mix with the Masters), [[Recording techniques with Al Schmitt]]
- **Pop screens now vs. the pencil trick then, and a tape-era-vs-DAW-era mindset shift on sibilance.** "I always use a pop screen on it since we have them now... years ago we used to take a pencil and put it in front of the mic to help diffuse the pops" — same physical principle (break up the plosive blast before the capsule), cruder tool. On sibilance specifically: don't chase it at the mic unless it's genuinely bad (only then try a different mic or windscreen) — a couple of stray sibilant sounds get fixed afterward "in Pro Tools, we just dip those things out," explicitly framed against the tape era when nothing recorded straight to tape could be un-fixed later. → [[De-essing]] — Al Schmitt (Mix with the Masters), [[Recording techniques with Al Schmitt]]
- **Vocal booth/room choice scales with how loud the surrounding ensemble is, and the stated reason is fixability, not just isolation for its own sake.** A screaming-loud big band or full orchestra calls for a real vocal booth with good acoustics (named favorites: the booth at Capitol Studios, and Avatar Studios in New York) specifically so the vocal stays isolated enough that "if there's a little pitch problem we want to fix, fine, we do it" later — isolation as insurance for a downstream fix, not just a cleaner take today. Distance data point: roughly **six to seven inches**, adjusted by ear per singer until it reads right on the meter/monitor. → Al Schmitt (Mix with the Masters), [[Recording techniques with Al Schmitt]]
- **Pick the mic to add warmth on TOP of an already-good singer, not just to correct a problem voice.** Go-to mics named: **Sony C800** or **Elam 251**; on a specific session the Elam 251 was chosen deliberately for its warmth, "because these guys are amazing singers and I really want to catch all the warmth of their vocals and have them sound really rich." Distinct case in the same breakdown: the demo/reference vocal being played back "sounds really thin, doesn't sound like he used a great mic," yet still carries brightness that "adds to the character of the record" — the mic choice is about which flavor of tone to capture, not a fix for a weak voice. Owned equivalent for either named mic (neither owned) — closest is the **[[Neumann TLM 102|TLM 102]]** (large-diaphragm condenser, detail/warmth over the SM57's dynamic rejection). → [[Sound Selection Guide]] — Kuk Harrell, [[Radio-ready vocal effects with Kuk Harrell]]
- **Take one headphone ear off specifically to keep the natural (untuned) voice audible under a live auto-tune plugin while tracking.** When recording a melodic sample vocal with a pitch-correction plugin live in the signal path the whole time, auto-tune in both ears can mask whether the underlying pitch/delivery is actually landing — pulling one ear off the cans keeps the raw voice reference available alongside the tuned monitor sound. A concrete, stated reason behind this vault's existing "one headphone off is common and fine" note. Same source also sings on a wordless "ooh" vowel instead of lyrics — a plainly-stated workaround for not being a strong singer, letting him hit notes he couldn't reliably land on full lyrics. → [[Vocal Comping and Takes]], [[Sampling and Sound Selection]] — loner, [how to add your own vocals to your r&b samples (in depth tutorial)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NP27-3YFlDk)
- **A vintage-character mic-emulation plugin as the ONLY processing on unprocessed vocal samples, for period color.** Splice-sourced soul vocal one-shots (already screened for minimal baked-in processing — see [[Sampling and Sound Selection]]) each got a single pass of a plugin captioned "King's Microphone" (name uncertain, unconfirmed vendor — see [[Plugin Wishlist]]) purely for "a bit more of a vintage sound," rather than a full processing chain. → [[Sound Selection Guide]] — loner, [these 1970's soul secrets will change how you make melodies!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oRLzsshf6yg)
- **Track at a distance from the mic to capture the room's own reverb/spill as texture** — mic at one end of the room, singer at the other, gain pushed up to catch spill and hiss deliberately; used on a lead vocal, cited as the same approach Jimmy Napes uses on Sam Smith (moving around the room while tracking BVs, e.g. "Stay With Me" chorus BVs reading as a "choir"). — Disclosure (Sol State), [How to Produce Vocals Like a PRO (Disclosure's 12 Secrets)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IwKZcTI3428)
