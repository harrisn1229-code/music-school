# The Five Pillars of Jazz Guitar Practice

Part of [[Song Practice]]'s practice-methodology material — Nathan Borton's top-level framework for **what** a jazz-guitar practice system needs to cover and **how** to structure a session around it, as opposed to a single technique or tune. Where [[Transcribing and Playing by Ear]] and [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]] each document one specific *applied* method, this note is the organizing scaffold those methods (and others) slot into.

## The long-term mindset (read this before the pillars)

Real integration of a practiced concept into free playing takes roughly **6 months to a year**, not the "few weeks" most players expect. Practicing something for a month and seeing no change in your soloing is the **normal**, expected result — not a signal the concept isn't working or that it should be abandoned. The fix for the frustration this causes is consistency plus small, achievable milestones along the way, not more hours crammed into a short window (cramming 5 hours one day and 30 minutes the rest of the week is explicitly called out as the wrong shape — steady daily/weekly reps beat binges). Hold this mindset across every pillar below.

**Day-to-day version of the same rule: spaced daily reps beat one long cram session, even within a single week.** A different Borton talk gives the mechanism directly — grinding a lick for two straight hours in one sitting makes you sound proficient *that day*, but if you don't come back to the same material the next day for the same amount of time, it fades; one long session doesn't stick the way daily short reps do. **Concrete fix: if you only have an hour a day for something, split it into 30 minutes a day rather than one 2-hour block** — set a timer for 30 minutes, and when it goes off, don't go back to that lick that day; pick it back up tomorrow. Daily short reps build a *habit* and reinforce the material in a way that outlasts cramming (explicitly likened to cramming for a test, which is the wrong model). Combine with the hard-timer daily-cycling template below: the template's 30-minute blocks split *a single day* across two pillars; this rule says not to let any one topic (a lick, a tune, a technique) eat the same block every day without spacing it out. — Nathan Borton, [Master Class: "How To Get The Most Out Of Your Practice Time"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HHzjs-wU2sw)

## The five pillars

A complete jazz-guitar practice system dedicates real time to all five — none substitutes for another (knowing theory alone doesn't make you a better improviser; vocabulary without technique doesn't come out cleanly; etc.):

### 1. Oral (ear) skills — transcription
- Ear skill is trainable, not innate — years of consistent, focused transcription work is what builds it.
- Two starting disciplines, run in parallel: **transcribing short licks** off records, and **transcribing whole solos or several choruses** start to finish — write both down on paper.
- Concrete, bounded goals work better than open-ended ones: *5 new licks over 2 months*; *commit to one full solo, however long it takes, and finish it*.
- At this stage the goal is purely **listen → replicate → perform-along** — training the ear↔fretboard connection. Don't yet try to fold what you transcribe into your own solos; that's a separate, later stage (see Pillar 2's internalization methods). Trust the process even without visible payoff yet.
- Byproduct: consistent transcription trains interval/scale/arpeggio recognition **and** time feel/rhythm, not pitch alone.
- → Full applied ear-only workflow (finding key/chords/melody, real-time transcription mechanics) lives on [[Transcribing and Playing by Ear]]; what to *do* with a solo once it's transcribed lives on [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]].

### 2. Vocabulary — internalized language
- Every high-level jazz player has spent real time learning the genre's shared melodic/rhythmic language — patterns generated from scales alone don't substitute for a vocabulary other musicians also recognize; that shared vocabulary is what actually lets musicians communicate with each other on the bandstand.
- **Three acquisition methods:**
  1. **Aural transcription of canon solos** over common progressions (ii–V–I named specifically) — e.g. Charlie Parker on "Donna Lee," Miles Davis on "So What," Grant Green on "Cool Blues."
  2. **Written transcription-study resources** — Joe Pass's *Guitar Style* (owned, [[Joe Pass Guitar Style - Enrichment|Joe Pass Guitar Style]]), Barry Galbraith's solo-method book, "Omnibook"-style written-solo collections — study visually how players tackle common progressions.
  3. **Jazz-improv books that explain the *why*, not just notation** — e.g. Jerry Coker's *Elements of Jazz Language*.
- **Internalizing what's gathered, two methods:** (a) **"plug and chug"** — take one new lick/idea and play it over an entire tune/form everywhere it fits; (b) **write your own short solos/etudes using only the newly gathered vocabulary**. Both force the brain to draw on known material under real conditions rather than improvising from nothing.
- **Quality over quantity is explicit and load-bearing:** truly internalizing just **5 licks** with the long-term mindset above can generate hundreds of distinct improvised lines (Grant Green cited as proof); half-learning and dropping 20 different phrases wastes practice time. Depth beats breadth here.
- → [[Bebop Vocabulary]] § From videos carries the full breakdown plus the specific book/solo names.

### 3. Technique — physical execution
- A lack of technique — not a lack of ideas — is often *why* vocabulary fails to come out in real playing; a line that doesn't sit well on the instrument gets abandoned regardless of how good the idea is. The ideas that actually survive into your playing are the ones you can execute "without tons of physical effort."
- On guitar this reduces to two things: **picking** and **fretboard fingering**.
- **Match picking style to the line, deliberately** — is this phrase best played Benson-style, strict alternate picking, or via hammer-ons/pull-offs? Also identify the picking style of whoever you're transcribing from — it makes their lines far more intuitive to absorb.
- **Isolate string-changing mechanics as their own skill** — sweep picking, string skipping, various ways to execute an arpeggio shape — separate from the specific line you're trying to play.
- **Method: isolate the physical skill first, then attach vocabulary to it.** Drill small alternate-picking or sweep-picking fragments in a "black and white," mechanical way, *then* fit language or rearranged existing vocabulary into those already-isolated shapes — this is easier and more reliable than forcing an unpracticed line to work through brute repetition.
- **Actively re-examine fingerings — don't lock in the first one that came out.** Students commonly transcribe a line, land on whatever fingering happened first, and never revisit it even when it's awkward. Deliberately re-fingering a line "can go from awkward to easy and fluid," and a good fingering choice should align with the picking style chosen for that section — fingering and picking are not independent decisions.

### 4. Theory and harmony
- High-level improvisers directly associate specific *sound types* (altered, ii–V–I, etc.) with the language that goes over them — theory alone doesn't make you a better improviser, but it's the framework that lets you correctly identify which sounds are available, so vocabulary and patterns get applied to the right target.
- **Analyze a tune as harmonic devices and progressions** (ii–V–I, backdoor dominant, tritone substitution, ♯IV walkdowns, etc.) rather than a flat sequence of isolated chords — this both tells you which vocabulary is idiomatic over a given passage and makes tunes dramatically easier to memorize than chord-by-chord rote.
- **How to build the framework:** find a teacher who does harmonic analysis (local jam-session pros, online teachers), or use theory books matched to the angle wanted — **language-based**: Jerry Coker's *Elements of Jazz Improvisation*; **tune-based**: *Hearing the Changes*.
- Goal state: know a tune's actual sounds and the vocabulary idiomatic to them, then apply transcribed language or chord-scale/pattern theory to generate fitting ideas.
- → [[Jazz Harmony]], [[The ii-V-I]], [[Chord Substitution]], [[Reharmonization]] for the theory content itself.

### 5. Repertoire — learning tunes
- Under-invested pillar for online-only learners specifically, because they don't get out to play with others enough — knowing a tune cold is "your license to play with others": it frees attention from getting the chords right onto actually making music with the band.
- **Find your "core 30"** — roughly 30 tunes that get called at jam sessions everywhere; familiarity, not mastery, is the bar to be able to sit in almost anywhere.
- **Five-step, ear-first tune-learning process** (full mechanics on [[Transcribing and Playing by Ear]] § From videos):
  1. Learn the **melody by ear** from the original recording (or an early version likely reading the real score).
  2. Work out **chords from a great pianist's recorded version** (Bill Evans, Oscar Peterson, Wynton Kelly named) — pianists state harmony most clearly.
  3. **Listen to the bass on downbeats**, where the harmony shifts — bass usually carries the root, piano the quality.
  4. **Pitch-shift the recording up an octave** (Steve Kabalch's trick) to bring the bass into the guitar's own register and hear it more directly.
  5. **Check your work last** — cross-reference a chord chart (online, iReal Pro) only *after* doing the ear-work; doing the heavy lifting by ear first makes the tune both easier to play and stickier in memory.
- Related but deliberately separate discipline: chordal/comping practice over the tune once learned (not covered in the source video, soloing-focused).
- → [[Internalizing a Tune Through Repetition]] documents a complementary, repetition-driven method for the same "learn a tune" goal — Borton's process front-loads finding the material by ear; Seriff's process (that note) front-loads raw repetition once you already have melody+chords in some form. Use Borton's process to *find* a tune's content, Seriff's to *internalize* it once found.

## Daily practice-cycling template

For a **1-hour session**, don't try to touch all five pillars every day. Instead:
- **Pair two related pillars per day**, split into two **30-minute** timed blocks, chosen so there's a natural carryover between them.
- **Use a hard timer, not a soft "when it feels done" cutoff.** When it goes off, move to the next block immediately, even mid-idea. Rationale: leaving something incomplete creates a natural pull to return to it, sustaining motivation over the long run better than fully exhausting a topic in one sitting.
- **Example day pairings:**
  - Day 1 — 30 min **ear skills** + 30 min **repertoire** · carryover: linking what you hear to what you need to actually play.
  - Day 2 — 30 min **vocabulary** + 30 min **technique** · carryover: applying the physical skill directly to the language you're learning.
  - Day 3 — 30 min **theory/harmony** + 30 min **repertoire** · carryover: deepening understanding of the tunes you're learning.
- The pairing, time split, and even single-pillar-only days (e.g. an all-technique day) are all adjustable to whichever pillar you feel weak in — the one non-negotiable is the long-term mindset above.
- Borton's own long-run pattern is coarser-grained than the daily template: he cycles primary focus across **months** (language for a couple months, then chords, then transcribing), always holding the long view rather than trying to advance every pillar at once.
