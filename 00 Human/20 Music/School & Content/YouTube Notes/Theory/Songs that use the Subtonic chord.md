# Songs that use the Subtonic chord

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2PiiTl7htzM)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2PiiTl7htzM)

## Synopsis

The chord built on the major scale's lowered 7th degree (♭VII, "subtonic") functions as the de facto 7th chord of a major key in practice — nearly always used instead of the actual diatonic 7th-degree chord (vii°, the "leading tone chord"), which turns out to be almost never used in real songs. Explains both halves: why ♭VII gets borrowed constantly (modal mixture / Mixolydian), and why vii° gets avoided almost entirely (it's a strictly weaker version of the dominant 7th).

## Techniques

- **Every major scale's 7 diatonic triads follow the fixed pattern**: major-minor-minor-major-major-minor-diminished. In C: C-Dm-Em-F-G-Am-B°. The 7th (B°, diminished) is the outlier that almost never appears in real songs.
- **The subtonic chord — ♭VII, a major chord built on the LOWERED 7th degree — is the de facto substitute**, despite not being diatonic to the major key at all: "Don't Stop" (Fleetwood Mac, key of E, ♭VII = D major). "Mr. Blue Sky" (ELO, key of F, ♭VII = E♭ major). "I'll Be There for You" (The Rembrandts, key of A, ♭VII = G major).
- **Modal-mixture framing (borrowed from the parallel minor key)**: in C major, B♭ major is "on loan" from C minor — the same blues-derived major/minor blending mechanism behind a lot of rock and blues harmony. "It's Only Love" (The Beatles, key of C: B♭→G→C) — alternates the flattened 7th (B♭, minor-scale-flavored) and the natural 7th (G-chord's leading tone, B natural) chord to chord within the same progression. "Sweet Child o' Mine" (Guns N' Roses) — pairs V and ♭VII in the opposite order (dominant first, then subtonic); the ♭VII chord is spelled C♭ rather than the enharmonically-identical B natural specifically because it functions as a LOWERED 7th degree of the key (D♭ major), and naming it "B" would misleadingly suggest the 6th degree instead — a real notation-precision point, not pedantry.
- **Mixolydian framing — when the subtonic chord isn't modal mixture but the song's actual mode**: the Mixolydian scale IS the major scale with a lowered 7th, so ♭VII is Mixolydian's own natural, diatonic chord — no borrowing required. "Heroes" (David Bowie, D Mixolydian) — the chorus opens on the subtonic (C major) as a natural Mixolydian chord, not an accidental/borrowed one.
- **The lowered 7th is Mixolydian's "character note"** — the single note that distinguishes it from the major scale, giving Mixolydian its wider, more "open" quality compared to plain major. "Hey Jude" (The Beatles) stays in plain F major for its first three minutes, then the famous outro introduces the subtonic (E♭ major), audibly shifting the song's tonality from F major to F Mixolydian right at that moment — the "opening up" feeling is directly tied to that modal shift.
- **Why the ACTUAL diatonic 7th-degree chord (vii°, the "leading tone chord") is essentially never used**: B° in C major is functionally a weaker, incomplete version of G7 (the dominant) — the top three notes of G7 ARE literally a B diminished triad. Any time a progression would resolve B°→C, adding the missing note (G) into the voicing turns it into G7 (or G7 first inversion) and creates a stronger resolution — so composers almost always reach for the fuller dominant chord instead. "Tiny Dancer" (Elton John) very nearly uses the plain leading-tone chord resolving to the tonic, but Elton adds a G into the voicing, turning it into G7/B rather than leaving it as bare B° — Bennett A/B's the two versions to demonstrate the (subtle but real) difference in resolution strength.
