> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **Only which chord tone is in the bass — two inversions for a triad, three for a seventh chord**
> The notes themselves never change, but the bass note is the most important one for the feel.
>
> **Q2.** **Voice leading · bass-line control · hand economy**
> *Also accepted:* keeping common tones · walking the bass by step · fewer jumps between shapes
>
> **Q3.** **Drop the 5th, then the root — the 3rd and 7th stay, because they carry quality and function**
> A rootless voicing is the default when a bass player is present, not an exotic case.
>
> **Q4.** **Bass note as root · relate every upper note to it as a degree · name the chord from those degrees**
> The name then tells you what it is functioning as, and therefore where it wants to go.
>
> **Q5.** **♭7, 9 and 11 over C — C7sus4(9)**
> *Also accepted:* C9sus4 · C7sus4 add 9
> B♭ is C's ♭7, D is the 9, F is the 11 — and there is no 3rd, hence sus4.
>
> **Q6.** **When the bass note is already a tone of the upper chord — it is just an inversion**
> A♭/C is first-inversion A♭ because C is A♭'s 3rd. F/C is second-inversion F.
>
> **Q7.** **The reading is genuinely ambiguous — only the surrounding harmony decides**
> With no 3rd and no 7th there is nothing in the chord to fix its quality or its function.
>
> **Q8.** **A chord name is chosen to expose a relationship, not to state a fact**
> The pitches are the fact. The name is a claim about what they are doing.
>
> **Q9.** **The one that reflects the sound you hear and the voice leading as it resolves**
> Not the spelling with the fewest accidentals, and not the one a chart would print.
>
> **Q10.** **Em6 · C♯m7♭5 · a rootless A9 — and each name predicts a different next chord**
> *Rubric (5):* names the Em6 reading (1) · names the half-diminished a minor 3rd below, C♯m7♭5 (1) · names the rootless dominant 9th a fourth above, A9 (1) · says each name implies a different resolution (1) · concludes the writer picks whichever name gets them where they are going (1)
> E, G, B and C♯ are one collection of pitches and three claims about it. Called Em6 it is a coloured minor chord and behaves like one. Called C♯m7♭5 it is a half-diminished chord and wants an F♯ dominant after it, on the way to a minor tonic. Called A9 with the root left to the bass it is a dominant, and it wants to fall a fifth to D. Nothing in the notes decides between them, which is exactly why the choice is worth something: the name is where you record your intention about the next bar. That is the same licence a diminished 7th's four names and an augmented triad's three give you, and it is why "what is this chord really" is usually the wrong question.
