> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **How fast the chords change — not a measure of tempo**
> The two are independent parameters. A slow ballad can change chord every beat and a fast track can hold one for eight bars.
>
> **Q2.** **The one changing twice a bar — tempo and harmonic rhythm are independent**
> Both tracks share a tempo, so the only thing separating them is the rate of chord change. That's the whole point of treating harmonic rhythm as its own parameter.
>
> **Q3.** **Slow — one or two chords spread across several bars**
> *Also accepted:* one chord per two bars · one chord held for four or eight bars
>
> **Q4.** **Repetition turns each change from information into an interruption**
> *Rubric (5):* says the loop repeats dozens of times (2) · says fast changes keep pulling attention back to the harmony (2) · notes that a piece which never repeats keeps making the changes informative, so they stay worth attending to (1)
> The first time a progression moves, the movement is content. By the twentieth pass the listener already knows where it goes, so the change stops telling them anything and simply demands attention on a schedule. In a piece that develops, every change is new and the demand is repaid. In a two-bar loop it isn't, and the cost lands on the vocal and the groove, which are the things that actually differ pass to pass.
>
> **Q5.** **No — it's correctly scoped for a loop that repeats**
> The interest is meant to come from the drums, the sound design and the vocal, and holding the harmony still is what leaves room for them.
>
> **Q6.** **Predictability is the feature — the ear stops tracking harmony and attends to what is changing**
> The progression stops being a series of events and becomes a bed. In a genre where the topline and the production are the song, that's the right trade.
>
> **Q7.** **About two passes**
> *Also accepted:* two or three times round · a few bars
>
> **Q8.** **Rhythm · melody and topline · timbre · arrangement**
> Rhythm means the groove and how it varies; melody means the topline supplying the movement the chords aren't; timbre means filter sweeps, wavetable position and automation; arrangement means scheduled absence — dropping elements moves a track as much as adding them.
>
> **Q9.** **You give up harmonic surprise and buy the listener's full attention for everything else**
>
> **Q10.** **Because the chords are rarely the problem — nothing above them is changing**
> *Rubric (5):* says the progression is usually not what's wrong (2) · identifies the real cause as nothing above the harmony varying (2) · gives a concrete check, such as listing everything that changes across eight bars other than the chords (1)
> Staleness reads as a harmony problem because the harmony is the part you can name, but a loop that repeats is supposed to repeat. The diagnosis is one list: play eight bars and write down every element that is different at the end from the start — drum variation, filter movement, an added layer, a dropped one, anything in the topline. If that list is short or empty, rewriting the chords will produce a loop that goes stale in a different key. Fix what isn't moving first, and only reharmonize if the list is already full.
