# Wish I knew this before spending $3k on plugins (might save you $69) [Ft Disclosure, Laxcity, Eskar]

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s7cx6n8HqRQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s7cx6n8HqRQ)

## Synopsis

A gear-acquisition-syndrome corrective, built around a blunt confirmation from Disclosure themselves: their biggest hit ("Latch") and another major song used essentially nothing but stock Logic plugins, maybe an H-Delay. The through-line — plugins make an already-good idea 5-10% better; they cannot make a weak idea good, and no amount of spending changes that math. The back half pivots to practical buying philosophy once you've accepted that framing: buy for quality and personal fit over hype/deals, budget speakers can absolutely work if you learn your room, and Laxcity's specific contribution is treating creative limitation (and even deliberately "breaking" plugins/DAW parameters outside their intended range) as a feature, not a constraint to escape by buying more tools.

## Techniques

- **Plugins are a 5-10% multiplier on an already-good idea, not a substitute for one.** Disclosure's own framing, backed by their biggest hit being made almost entirely in stock Logic: money spent on plugins only pays off once the underlying idea/arrangement/song is already strong; no amount of gear spending fixes a weak idea. → [[Committing and Printing]]
- **Budget studio monitors work if you learn the room, not the speakers.** Won a mix award using cheap secondhand speakers ("Tannoys... bought at my dad's auction house") plus stock Logic plugins — the actual lever was understanding listening position and doing basic room treatment to reduce echo, not the speaker hardware itself. → [[Referencing and Translation]]
- **Deliberately break plugins/DAW parameters outside their intended range for discovery** — Laxcity's stated method: tweak controls that "aren't supposed to be touched" (his example: a master-bus pitch control) and record whatever comes out; treating a DAW's edges/misuse as a sound-design source rather than staying inside intended parameter ranges. Everything in a DAW can be recorded, so an accidental discovery is always capturable. → [[Ear Candy and Hiding Synths]], [[Sampling and Sound Selection]]
- **Buy plugins like a chef buys knives: a few specialized, high-quality tools, not twenty overlapping ones.** The "quality + specialization" framing — a small toolkit of tools that each clearly earn their slot beats a large collection with heavy overlap; deal-driven purchases of things you weren't already excited about are the specific purchases most people end up regretting.
- **Diagnose the actual bottleneck before buying: "where are you struggling most, and what tool or resource would actually fix that?"** A concrete filter for whether a purchase is solving a real problem (mixing, EQ, loudness, sound design, arrangement) versus being acquisition for its own sake.
- **Optimize plugin purchases for fun, not for a "perfectly efficient" toolkit** — the actual risk isn't wasted money, it's losing the enjoyment that keeps you making music at all; a plugin that inspires you is worth buying even if it's redundant with something you already own, because sustained motivation outweighs toolkit efficiency.
- **Don't do surgical EQ/detail work in the middle of a creative flow state — save it for later.** When ideas are flowing, stop reaching for precise EQ moves entirely (his own example: only did one wide, quick change on a clap sound, on purpose, to avoid breaking flow) rather than context-switching into a mixing mindset mid-idea. → [[Automation]]
- **DAW-stock weak points worth third-party upgrades for: EQ, limiters, synths, reverb** — called out specifically as the categories where most DAWs' built-in tools lag noticeably behind dedicated third-party options, as distinct from areas where stock tools are already competitive.
