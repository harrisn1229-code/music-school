# Jazz Chord Essentials   3 part Quartal Harmony

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ITRYMElc3Js)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ITRYMElc3Js)

## Synopsis

A 15-minute lesson from Jens Larsen on **3-note quartal voicings** — chords built by stacking 4ths instead of 3rds — pitched specifically as a *practice method and application path*, not just a definition (the definition itself is covered in more depth by the Jared Borkowski video already on [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]). Larsen's real contribution here is the **practice sequence**: get the 3-note quartal shapes under the fingers on the top two string sets, then drill them over a pedal point starting with the ii chord, improvising single-note melodies on one string and dropping a quartal chord underneath, then repeating for the V and I chords of a ii–V–I. He flags the **tonic (I) chord as the tricky one** — quartal voicings that include the 4th degree (F, over a C major chord) land on the "avoid note" and stop sounding like a plain major 7, so three of the seven diatonic voicings are usable mostly as passing chords rather than resting points; he explicitly recommends **not** reaching for a Lydian sound to dodge this at the start, just working within the plain major scale and adding Lydian later once the basic vocabulary is comfortable. The second half harmonizes the **melodic minor scale in stacked 4ths** the same way the major scale was harmonized, specifically to get quartal voicings for an **altered dominant** (G7alt = the melodic minor a half-step above the root, i.e. Ab melodic minor) — then works four short 251-in-C examples showing how a quartal G7alt voicing reads as "altered" precisely because it introduces notes (♭9/♯9/♭13-family tones) that weren't present in the preceding Dm7 voicing, making the harmonic shift audible even though none of the voicings individually spell out a textbook chord. Two extensions are named but deferred to future lessons: using quartal shapes melodically (in lines, not just as chords) and building quartal harmony from pentatonic scales instead of the full major/melodic-minor scale.

## Techniques

- **Practice order: voicings first, then pedal points, ii before V before I.** Step 1 — get the 3-note quartal voicings under the fingers on a few string sets; Larsen says he personally uses the top two string sets the most, though all string sets are worth checking out for thoroughness. Step 2 — drill each voicing over a static pedal point (a drone or backing track holding the root), starting with the **ii chord** (D minor, in the key of C) because it's the easiest one to land cleanly — of the three-note quartal voicings available, only one voicing actually spells out a "normal"-sounding Dm7, so the practice goal is learning to use the *sum* of several voicings together to convey the chord rather than expecting any single grip to sound complete on its own. → [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]
- **Melody-then-chord practice technique: improvise a one-string single-note melody first, then harmonize it by dropping a quartal chord underneath.** Framed as a natural next step once the harmonized-scale voicings are comfortable — you're already used to playing the scale harmonized on one string, so picking a melody note there and adding the quartal voicing under it is a small step, not a new skill. This is the practical bridge between "I know the shapes" and "I can use them expressively." → [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]
- **Confine the practice scope to a plain major 2-5-1 (ii, V, I) rather than all seven diatonic degrees at once.** Explicit scope-limiting advice: don't try to drill quartal voicings over every scale degree simultaneously — pick the three most useful chords (ii, V, I) and get those solid first. The V chord (G) is called out as already naturally sounding like a "G7 with a 13" using the plain major-scale quartal voicing, so it's a good next pedal point after the ii. → [[The ii-V-I]]
- **The tonic (I) chord is the hardest of the three because of the "avoid note."** Over C major 7, any quartal voicing containing F (the 4th/11th) creates dissonance against the major 3rd (E) and stops reading as a resting tonic sound — three of the seven diatonic three-note quartal voicings contain F and are described as usable mainly as **passing chords**, not landing points. **Explicit recommendation: don't reach for a Lydian sound to solve this at the start** — even though it's common advice that Lydian gives more usable tonic voicings (raising the 4th to a ♯11 removes the clash), Larsen frames it as unnecessary complexity for a beginner at quartal harmony; stay in the plain major scale, add Lydian later once the basic vocabulary is comfortable. → [[Quartal Chord Voicings]], [[Modal Jazz]] (Lydian as the "easier tonic" mode for quartal/modal playing)
- **Practice with a drone/backing track, not a played root note.** When demonstrating on camera Larsen plays the root himself to convey the sound for the video, but says the actual practice setup should use a backing track or a **loop pedal holding the pedal tone** so both hands are free for the quartal voicings and melody. *(general practice-setup note, not a specific product)*
- **Harmonize the melodic minor scale in stacked 4ths, the same method used for the major scale, specifically to get quartal voicings for an altered dominant.** For a G7alt sound, harmonize **Ab melodic minor** (a half step above the dominant root — "a flat melodic minor, which is the same as G7 altered") in stacked 3-note 4th-voicings across a couple of string sets, the same way the major scale was harmonized earlier in the lesson. Stated as a repeatable template: to cover other dominant colors later (Lydian dominant, tonic-minor chords, etc.) do the same harmonize-in-4ths pass over the matching parent scale. → [[Quartal Chord Voicings]], [[Altered Chords]], [[Jens Larsen - Melodic Minor - Altered Scale]] (the deeper altered-scale-degree breakdown from the same channel)
- **Four worked 251-in-C examples (Dm7 → G7alt → C69), all using the same core device: make the altered dominant audible by voicing it with notes absent from the preceding ii-chord voicing, not by nailing a "correct" chord shape.** In each example Larsen opens on a quartal Dm7-area voicing chosen to sound unambiguously like D minor 7, then moves to a quartal G7alt voicing built from the Ab-melodic-minor harmonization — the ♭9/♯9-family notes (Ab, Eb, Bb, spoken as examples in example 1) weren't present in the Dm7 voicing, so the ear registers "something changed, this is now altered" even without any single voicing spelling out G7alt cleanly. **Example 3 and 4 lean on a played single-note melody line, rather than a fixed chord grip, to spell out the D minor sound**, then bring in G7alt-derived notes underneath/around it — example 4 specifically **avoids the major 3rd of G7 entirely** and still reads as "altered" purely from the ♭9/♯9-family color notes used. All four resolve to a **C69** tonic voicing (not a plain Cmaj7) — consistent with the earlier point that plain quartal major-scale voicings, not Lydian ones, are the intended landing sound. → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Altered Chords]], [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]
- **Quartal voicings work as sound-first accompaniment, not strict harmonic spelling — a lot of the individual voicings are "incomplete" chords.** Repeated framing throughout: the goal of using several quartal voicings together is to convey the *sound* of the underlying chord/scale, not to voice every chord tone correctly each time — e.g. only one of the seven diatonic 3-note quartal voicings actually spells a textbook Dm7 on its own; the rest work by accumulation and context (what came before/after) rather than individually. → [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]
- **Two extensions named but explicitly deferred to future lessons:** (1) using quartal voicings **melodically, inside improvised lines**, not just as chords; (2) building quartal harmony by harmonizing **pentatonic scales** rather than the full major/melodic-minor scale, described as "another way to approach this and get some other sounds." Neither is developed in this video — flag for a future enrichment pass if Larsen publishes the follow-ups. → [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]
