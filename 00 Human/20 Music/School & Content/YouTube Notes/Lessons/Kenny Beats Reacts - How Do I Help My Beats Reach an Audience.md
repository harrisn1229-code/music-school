# Kenny Beats Reacts - "How Do I Help My Beats Reach An Audience?" (Real Talk) [Music Marketing 2021]

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4evR4UjSe7w)

## Synopsis

Kenny Beats responds to a viewer question about getting beats heard. Core position: "if your music's good enough, someone will find it" stopped being true — there's too much content competing for attention now, so making the music and making the music *discoverable* are two separate, equally hard problems. His practical answer for producers without a marketing instinct: don't force yourself to become a content creator, collaborate with someone whose actual skill is presentation (design, visuals, "the language" around the music) instead.

## Techniques

- **"If it's good enough, someone will find it" is no longer true — treat discoverability as a separate skill from making good music, not a byproduct of it.** Making something good and making people aware it exists are described as equally hard, distinct problems — solving one doesn't solve the other.
- **A cited example of a low-effort but effective format: just film yourself making beats, with no persona/gimmick added** — Count's approach (his own example): no costume, no attempt at comedy, literally recording the beat-making process itself as the content — led to placements with high-profile artists within the year. The lesson isn't "you must vlog," it's that the content doesn't need a forced hook — the process itself can be the interesting part.
- **When you can't make your own presentation/marketing interesting, collaborate with someone who can, rather than trying to force a skill you don't have.** If design/visual sense/"finding the right language" for how a song gets presented isn't your strength, actively seek a creative partner whose strength it is — described as a slow relationship to build (took years to find the right collaborator) but a genuinely load-bearing part of an artist's output actually reaching people, distinct from the music itself.
- **The listener's first exposure to music is often the surrounding experience, not the audio itself** — cited example: a stadium built specifically to stage an album listening event, so the music is inseparable in memory from the imagery/venue around its first listen. The broader point: how people *find* a piece of music shapes how they experience it, before they've heard a single note.
