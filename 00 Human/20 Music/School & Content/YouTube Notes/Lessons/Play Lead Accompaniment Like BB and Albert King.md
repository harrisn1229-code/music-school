# Play Lead Accompaniment Like BB and Albert King

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7fw3uWdDxQU)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7fw3uWdDxQU)

## Synopsis

A short, focused lesson from Jack Ruch on **lead accompaniment** — the B.B. King / Albert King skill of singing a verse and threading short guitar lines into the gaps, rather than soloing continuously. Where his companion videos ([[Blues Phrasing in the Style of B.B. and Albert King]], [[From BB king to Mike Bloomfield - A Classic Slow Blues]]) work out full note-for-note vocabulary in specific hand positions, this one isolates the **rhythmic side of the skill**: a slow 12/8 blues in G with a recorded vocal verse, and a repeatable method for figuring out *where in the bar* a lead-fill phrase should land relative to the singing. He plays two full examples over the same backing track/vocal — first a near-verbatim Albert King transcription, then his own phrasing using the same rhythmic template but a different (more major-leaning) note palette — and closes with specific reference recordings to transcribe from. The most portable idea isn't either example's licks, it's the **counting method**: treat the 12/8 groove as a 4-count where each count is a triplet, and notice that in a slow blues verse, lead-fill phrases consistently land on **beat 4** or **beat 2** of that count — giving a concrete template to plan a fill's landing point before worrying about which notes to play.

## Techniques

### The rhythmic phrasing template (the core lesson)
- **Count a slow 12/8 blues groove as "1-2-3-4," with each count covering three eighth notes (a triplet), not as 12 discrete eighth notes.** This collapses the underlying 12/8 feel into the same 4-beat frame a vocalist phrases against, which is the actual planning unit for where a fill goes — thinking in raw eighth notes obscures where the phrase boundaries actually are.
- **Lead-fill phrases in this style land on one of two spots: the "4" count or the "2" count** — almost never anywhere else. Both worked examples in the video are built entirely from phrases landing on beat 4 or beat 2 of the 1-2-3-4 count, giving a binary decision (land on 4, or land on 2) rather than an open-ended rhythmic choice.
- **Use a full verse as a phrasing template, then reuse its landing-point map with different notes.** The video's second example deliberately keeps the *exact same* landing-point sequence as the first (four-count, four-count, four-count, two-count, two-count, four-count, two-count) while swapping in a different note vocabulary — proving the rhythmic skeleton and the melodic content are separable, and that the skeleton is the more reusable half.
- **The template shifts with the song and the groove.** Stated directly: a different tune's vocal phrasing/lyrics will land fills in different spots even within a slow 12/8 blues, and a shuffle or funk blues changes both the phrasing *and* the lines played even more. The 4-count/2-count landing pattern is a starting template to internalize the *method* (counting through the form and noticing where gaps open), not a fixed rule to paste onto every tune.
- **Practice method, stated directly: transcribe verses from real recordings and count through them as you learn them, tracking where the phrases land relative to the beat** — rather than only copying note choice. Pick a slow blues if working on 12/8 phrasing, a shuffle if working on shuffle phrasing, so the rhythmic vocabulary matches the groove being studied.

### Two worked examples over the same backing track (G, slow 12/8, sung verse)
- **Example 1 — a near-verbatim Albert King transcription.** Confined mostly to the **top part of the minor pentatonic box**, then a position shift (slide up) into more minor pentatonic material for later phrases — deliberately narrow note choice so the rhythmic landing points are the whole lesson.
- **Example 2 — Jack's own phrasing, same rhythmic template, wider note choice.** Moves up the fretboard, blends in the **major pentatonic** shapes that stretch up the neck alongside the minor pentatonic in the same hand positions, and specifically adds the **major 6th (E, in the key of G)** as a color note outside the minor pentatonic — a brighter, more major-leaning sound layered onto the same landing-point map as example 1. Includes some larger bends in his own direction, inspired by but not copying the reference players.
- **Both examples share the same overall shape**: three phrases landing on the 4-count, a bar of rest for the vocal, two phrases landing on the 2-count, another 4-count phrase, and a closing phrase landing on the 2-count — a concrete 7-phrase template that can be lifted wholesale and re-voiced with new notes (as example 2 itself demonstrates).

### Listening list (stated as the real practice priority)
- **Albert King — *Live Wire/Blues Power*** and ***Thursday Night in San Francisco*** — named directly as the go-to live records for studying this style; late-'60s Stax-era Albert King recordings generally recommended.
- **B.B. King — *Live at the Regal*** — named directly as full of strong examples of this lead-accompaniment style.
- **Stated priority, directly**: pick a tune matching the groove being studied (slow blues vs. shuffle vs. funk blues), transcribe a few verses of the guitarist's between-vocal-line ideas, and count through the phrasing as it's learned — verbatim licks *and* the rhythmic placement both have value, but the placement is what generalizes.
