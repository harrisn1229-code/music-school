# The EASIEST way to learn any song by ear in under 5 minutes.

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8A9DNYjHym0)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8A9DNYjHym0)

## Synopsis
Another Jesse Glick "5 Minute Monday," and deliberately the densest one of the four in the vault so far — a compressed, four-stage curriculum for learning a song's chords entirely by ear, opening with a jab at the "four chords can play any song" meme ("that's not true at all... with seven chords, you can actually learn basically every song ever by ear"). The stages build on each other: know the major scale well enough to sing it from any root → get comfortable singing/finding any numbered interval up from a root ("do me" = a third, "do re mi fa" = a fourth) → learn diatonic harmony (which scale degree gets which chord quality, worked live in C: major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished) → apply all three together as a **listening heuristic**: since most pop songs skip the diminished vii°, every key really only has three major chords (I, IV, V) and three minor chords (ii, iii, vi), so hearing "major or minor" on a new chord immediately cuts the guesswork to three candidates, and the bass note (matched against scale-degree knowledge) picks the winner. Explicitly paced fast on purpose — Glick tells the viewer up front it's their job to slow down and rewatch — and the content, while introductory, lands almost exactly on techniques 1 and 2 of [[Identifying Chord Progressions by Ear]] (bass-note dictation + major/minor-halves-the-candidates) and the diatonic-quality derivation already tabulated on [[Chords by Key]], just taught end-to-end as one connected beginner on-ramp rather than as separate reference pages.

## What's taught
- **The major scale is the prerequisite, and it has to be internalized well enough to sing, not just played.** Formula from any root: **whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half** — demonstrated twice, once in C and once starting cold on a random fretted note (B♭), to show the pattern is root-independent. *(General major-scale construction — already tabulated on [[Scales]]; no new information there, so not separately routed.)*
- **Finding any numbered interval up from a root by singing, no instrument needed** — once the scale is internalized, sing scale-degree syllables up from "do" and count: "do me" (skip re) = a third; "do re mi fa" = a fourth; "do re mi fa sol la" = a sixth. Demonstrated first on guitar (fretting up the C major scale and counting 1-2-3-4 to land on the fourth, 1-2-3-4-5-6 to land on the sixth) and then purely vocally, framed as the actual milestone: "if you've gotten so good at the scale that you can recall any of those notes from your head just knowing the root note, congratulations — you've just started your basic ear training of intervals." *(Same singing-from-root method already covered in depth by [[Scale Degree Ear Training]]'s and [[Intervals]]'s existing video entries; not separately routed to avoid duplicating identical technique.)*
- **Diatonic harmony, worked live in C, chord quality by scale degree:** I = major/maj7 (C), ii = minor/m7 (Dm), iii = minor (Em, quality only — "major minor minor" pattern stated, not built out to a full 7th chord on camera), IV = major/maj7 (F), V = major/dominant 7 (G7), vi = minor/m7 (Am), vii = diminished (B°, explicitly flagged as "tricky" and shown only as a shape — Glick tells viewers not to worry about what diminished *means* for this lesson, just to recognize the shape). Full quality sequence stated plainly: **major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, [diminished]** — matches [[Chords by Key]]'s "Ma-mi-mi-Ma-Ma-mi-dim" chant exactly, worked by ear rather than derived from theory.
- **Relative-minor fact, stated as practical reassurance rather than derived:** "all of the major keys are the same as their relative minor key" — C major's seven chords are A minor's seven chords, D major's are B minor's; you just start the same seven-chord set on the vi degree instead of the I. Matches [[Chords by Key]]'s existing "each minor key's chords = its relative major's chords, renumbered" line.
- **The core by-ear identification heuristic — the video's actual payoff, and the reason it's titled "the easiest way":** most pop songs skip the diminished vii°, which leaves exactly **three major chords (I, IV, V)** and **three minor chords (ii, iii, vi)** per key, no more. So the process for identifying an unknown chord in a song, once the key is known: (1) **decide major or minor by ear first** — this alone cuts the candidate pool from seven usable chords to three ("you can at least cut that in half to figure out which of the three chords it is"); (2) **listen to the bass note of the chord** and match it against scale-degree knowledge of the key to pick the one correct candidate out of the three. Worked with concrete numbers both ways: in the key of C, a minor chord you hear can only be Dm, Em, or Am; in the key of G, only Am, Bm, or Em; a major chord in C can only be C, F, or G.
- **Stated payoff of drilling this repeatedly:** familiarity with common numbered-interval progressions by ear — namer-checks 1-4-5, 2-5-1, and 1-6-2-5 as the shorthand patterns that start popping out once the diatonic-harmony-by-ear process is practiced enough to translate from fretboard knowledge to real-time listening.
- **Format/practice note:** filmed deliberately fast ("this lesson has so much to cover, I'm going to be moving relatively quickly... it's your job to slow down as we go along") — a stated four-stage curriculum (scale → intervals → diatonic harmony → by-ear application) compressed into under 8 minutes, meant to be rewatched rather than absorbed in one pass.
