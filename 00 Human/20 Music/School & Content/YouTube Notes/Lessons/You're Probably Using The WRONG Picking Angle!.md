# You're Probably Using The WRONG Picking Angle!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oRfkxPgzUbg)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oRfkxPgzUbg)

## Synopsis

A pick-slanting deep dive built around two of the presenter's own solo licks: the two picking angles for even vs. uneven note groupings per string, worked examples transcribed from real solos, and a practice method for internalizing string transitions using programmed, tempo-adjustable backing tracks with accented string-change notes.

## Techniques

- **Angle 1 — for even note-count-per-string ascending lines** (groups of 2, 4, 6, 8 before a string change): pick angled so, after finishing a group, the pick is already in the air clear of the next string rather than trapped between strings — avoids having to "jump over" a string. Worked example: an E Phrygian dominant lick (from the artist's song "The Kindling") in groups of 4.
- **Angle 2 — for uneven/odd note-count-per-string ascending lines** (e.g. groups of 3 or 6 ending on a downstroke): the opposite slant is required, since the pattern ends on a downstroke on the low string and needs the other angle to clear on the way to the next string without unwanted noise. Matches the note-count parity rule already on [[Inside-Outside Picking and Pick Slanting]] (even groupings → consistent slant; odd groupings → the slant flips every crossing).
- **You need both angles, fluently and individually, before combining them.** Real solo lines mix even and uneven groupings freely (not always mathematically even), so the practice path is: master angle 1 alone → master angle 2 alone → then lines that force switching between them mid-phrase, building up from a 5-note gap between switches (easier) to a 3-note gap (harder, less time to reposition the pick).
- **"Anchor points" for note-spacing / timing at speed.** At fast tempos it's not possible to consciously track every individual pick stroke — instead, pay attention specifically to the string-change notes (where the picking angle switches) as anchor points; if those land tight and evenly spaced, the notes between them tend to fall into place on their own.
- **Backing-track practice method (DAW-based).** Program a MIDI drum/bass backing track (EZdrummer/EZbass in Cubase) with the hi-hat accenting the first note of each string-change group — turns note-spacing practice into a concrete, audible target (lock every pick stroke to the grid, match hi-hat accents to string-change notes) rather than an abstract "play evenly" instruction. Because the drum/bass parts are MIDI, tempo can be freely rescaled (half-speed for learning, full speed for testing) without re-recording anything — export a slow, mid, and fast version of the same backing track from one file.
- **Progression through backing-track "difficulty":** starts with hi-hat accents marking every string-change note explicitly (maximum scaffolding); moves to a plainer beat that still hits the important accents but leaves more of the 16th-note spacing to the player's own internal timing (less scaffolding, more musical); can also use a loud, unambiguous double-bass-drum pattern (one kick per note) as an alternative rhythmic anchor for very fast 16th-note or 16th-note-triplet runs, both for rhythm playing and for lead lines.
- **Note-spacing as a distinct problem from "clean playing."** Uneven note values within a supposedly steady run is called out as a common, often-unnoticed cause of a line sounding unprofessional even when the notes themselves are technically correct — worth deliberately drilling as its own skill, not assumed to come free with clean execution.
