# Course 08 — Melody Writing

How a melodic line is built, what it does against the chords underneath it, and what to change when it isn't working. Most strong melodies are **one short idea developed**, not a long line invented — this course is mostly about that sentence.

**What it assumes**

- [[Course 02 — Chord Function]] — the harmony a melody is written against.
- [[Course 05 — Subdivision, Feel & Genre]] — the rhythmic vocabulary behind Lesson 3's "change the rhythm first" rule, which is the single highest-yield fix in the course.
- [[Course 06 — Loop Harmony & Modal Colour]] — Lesson 2 splits on whether the harmony under the melody is moving or static, and that's loop harmony's territory.

- Everything upstream cashes out here: chord function supplies the harmony to write against, loop harmony supplies the context, and rhythm supplies the first thing to change when a line is dull.

## The lessons

### Lesson 1 — The Line Itself

**Read**
- [ ] [[Melody Writing]] → *Contour, motion, and range*
- [ ] [[Motivic Development]] in full — a single table, and the highest-value page in the lesson

**The material**
- **[[MEL-C01 — Melodic contour|Contour is the melody's shape over time]]** — how it rises and falls, independent of the actual pitches.
- A strong contour is **balanced**: what goes up comes back down, producing an arc with **one clear peak** rather than a flat line or a wandering one. Balance the *motion* as well — after a run of stepwise movement, take a leap.
- **[[MEL-C03 — Motifs and how to develop them|A motive is a short fragment used as raw material to generate much more music.]]** Two or three bars is plenty; the value is in what you do with it afterward.
- The nine named development techniques are **repetition · sequence · inversion · interval contraction · interval expansion · diminution · augmentation · rhythmic metamorphosis · imitation.** They are the mechanism behind "one idea developed" — each one produces new material without introducing a new idea.
- Worth noticing: several of them change *only the rhythm* (diminution, augmentation, rhythmic metamorphosis) and still read as new material. That's the same principle Lesson 3 uses to fix a boring melody.

**Concepts**
- [[MEL-C01 — Melodic contour]]
- [[MEL-C03 — Motifs and how to develop them]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — write four bars you like, then generate sixteen more using only techniques from the [[Motivic Development]] table. Name the technique used on each.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — do the same in Ableton, one clip per variation, with the technique in the clip name. Then draw the contour of the original as a line on paper and check it has a single peak.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 08 — Lesson 1 Test]]

### Lesson 2 — Melody Against Harmony

**Read**
- [ ] [[Melody Writing]] → *Melody against harmony*, *Writing to a loop vs writing to changes*
- [ ] [[Non-Harmonic Tones]]

**The material**
- The same note means something different depending on the chord underneath it. **The root sounds settled** — it's where a phrase goes to end. **[[MEL-C02 — Landing on the 3rd vs the root|The 3rd colours the chord]]**, brighter or sadder, without changing the harmony, and it's less conclusive. The **7th** leans and wants to move. The **9th** is open and floating.
- **Landing on the root constantly is the most common reason a melody sounds boring.** It agrees with the harmony on every downbeat, so nothing is ever held in tension long enough to want resolving.
- Then the distinction that decides how you write: **[[MEL-C04 — Writing to a loop vs to changes|over moving changes the harmony supplies the motion]].** Hold one note and let the chords recontextualize it — 5th, then 3rd, then 9th — and it moves without moving.
- **Over a static loop nothing does that for you**, so the melody has to generate its own movement through rhythm, contour and phrasing. Static loops do tolerate more repetition, because the listener is already in a looped frame.
- This is the practical reason a topline written over changes often collapses when it's dropped onto a one-chord loop. Nothing was wrong with the line; the thing that was moving it went away.

**Concepts**
- [[MEL-C02 — Landing on the 3rd vs the root]]
- [[MEL-C04 — Writing to a loop vs to changes]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — for one melody note held over four different chords, name what degree it is against each. Then say which of the four sounded most settled and why.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — write one melody over a moving progression, then play the same melody over a one-chord loop on the same tonic. Mark every note that stopped working and say what it had been doing before.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 08 — Lesson 2 Test]]

### Lesson 3 — Melody as Arrangement

**Read**
- [ ] [[Melody Writing]] → *Register, range and intensity*, *Building tension across a section*, *What makes a hook*, *Fixing a boring melody*
- [ ] [[Arrangement and Song Form]]

**The material**
- **[[MEL-C05 — Register and intensity|Higher reads as more intense]]** — partly convention, partly physiology, since a singer audibly works harder up there. That's why verses sit lower and choruses lift, and why "move the chorus up a third" often beats rewriting it. Widening the range does similar work.
- **[[MEL-C06 — Building tension across a section|Building tension]]** across a verse: climb in register, shorten the note values so the line gets busier, repeat a fragment in ascending sequence so it gathers, and **withhold the tonic** so nothing resolves until the chorus does it.
- The counter-move works too — strip everything back right before the chorus so it arrives against space rather than against a peak.
- **[[MEL-C07 — What makes a hook|A hook needs three things, in order]]: repetition** — it must come back, and be short enough to hold after one pass, which outranks everything else. **Rhythmic distinctiveness** — identifiable when tapped, with the pitches stripped out. **A singable range with clear contour.** Then contrast against what surrounds it. Simplicity usually beats complexity.
- **[[MEL-C08 — Fixing a boring melody|Fixing a boring melody has a ranking, and the ranking is the content.]] Change the rhythm first** — most boring melodies are boring *rhythmically*, circling even note values on the beat. Then **widen it**: add a leap, push the top note higher. Then **move where it sits against the chord**, targeting 9ths and 7ths instead of roots and 5ths.

**Concepts**
- [[MEL-C05 — Register and intensity]]
- [[MEL-C06 — Building tension across a section]]
- [[MEL-C07 — What makes a hook]]
- [[MEL-C08 — Fixing a boring melody]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — tap the rhythm of a hook you know, with no pitches, and see whether it's still identifiable. Do it with three hooks and one melody you find forgettable, then say what separated them.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — take a melody that isn't working, apply the triage list in order, and stop at the first fix that solves it. Bounce before and after side by side, and note which step it was.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 08 — Lesson 3 Test]]
