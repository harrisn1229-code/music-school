# Aleatoric Music

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[20th-Century Harmony]]. **Aleatoric** (from Greek *alea*, "dice") — also called chance music or indeterminacy — is music built with a more *intentional* degree of unpredictability than the baseline variation of any live performance.

## Two categories

**Aleatoric composition** — randomness decides *how the piece was written*, but the resulting score is fixed and sounds the same every performance.
- Example: **Iannis Xenakis's** 1975 work *N'Shima* (brass, cello, vocalists) used patterns of molecular motion to generate the composition — called **stochastic music**.

**Aleatoric performance** — the score itself leaves choices open, so the piece sounds *different every time it's performed*.
- **Senza misura** — sections where performers repeat a passage an unspecified number of times, each at their own tempo, independent of each other.
- Other forms: performers directed to play whatever they want; specific passages played at unspecified times; sections determined by chance elements like coin flips or audience participation.

## The famous example
**John Cage's *4′33″*** (1952) — one or more performers on stage doing nothing for four minutes, thirty-three seconds. Often ridiculed as nonsensical, but Cage's own point was different: the piece isn't a performance of silence, it's a deliberate frame that forces the audience to actively listen to *ambient* sound (traffic, rain, whispered conversation) as the actual musical content. The ultimate aleatoric-performance piece — the performer has no control over the actual sound, only over the framework that makes the audience listen.

## From videos

- **The origin story behind that same "A Day in the Life" crescendo: McCartney's fully-improvised instruction didn't work on a classically trained orchestra, and George Martin's fix is the actual reusable technique.** McCartney's original ask — start on the lowest note your instrument can play, work up to the highest, entirely at your own pace — left the session musicians confused rather than inspired; players used to fully notated parts don't have a framework for "purely random." Martin's solution kept the aleatoric character but added just enough scaffolding to make it executable: tell each player roughly which note to be at, at which point in the passage, while still leaving the pacing between those checkpoints up to them. The generalizable move for directing trained/notation-reliant performers toward an aleatoric result: don't ask for pure randomness — give sparse checkpoints (a note, a rough timing) and let genuine randomness fill the gaps between them. → [[Musical Form]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[How The Beatles wrote A Day In The Life]]
- **A "controlled chaos" aleatoric instruction used in mainstream pop production, not just avant-garde classical**: George Martin's instruction to the 40-piece orchestra on "A Day in the Life" (The Beatles) — each player starts on the LOWEST note their instrument can play and travels to the HIGHEST over 24 bars, with no fixed notated pitches in between, landing together only on a shared target (an E major chord). A concrete, low-tech recipe for reconstructing this kind of source when there's no fixed part to transcribe: multiple independent chromatic lines each traveling low-to-high, assigned to different instrument voices, plus one genuinely unrehearsed/unplanned live part layered on top for extra unpredictability. → David Bennett Music Theory, [I recreated "A Day In The Life" from scratch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Yfdso0HerjE)
- **Aleatoric composition vs. aleatoric performance, with Xenakis's stochastic-music example and Cage's 4′33″** — full breakdown above; the composition/performance split is the key distinction (does the randomness happen once while writing, or every time it's played). — Toby W.
