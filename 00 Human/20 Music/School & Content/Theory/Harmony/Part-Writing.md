# Part-Writing (Four-Voice Chorale Style)

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Chord Progressions]]. **Part-writing** is writing music in the four-voice (SATB) style reverse-engineered from J.S. Bach's ~200 chorale harmonizations — the standard textbook corpus for common-practice "rules," because chorales have fast harmonic rhythm and huge, consistent sample size. Not really "rules" so much as patterns Bach and his contemporaries consistently followed because they sounded good; each style has its own rulebook.

## Vertical rules (one chord, four voices)
- Soprano↔alto and alto↔tenor must each stay within an **octave**; tenor↔bass can be any distance.
- Voices stay in their proper order (tenor shouldn't sit above alto — Bach occasionally broke this for melodic shape, but it's the exception).
- With 4 voices and only 3 notes in a triad, one note must be **doubled**. Root-position triads default to doubling the root unless another rule forces otherwise (see doubling-in-inversion below).
- Each voice has a real singable range (amateur congregational singers, not virtuosos).

## Horizontal rules (voice leading)
- Move each voice the **shortest distance** to the nearest chord tone in the next chord.
- **Keep the common tone** across a chord change whenever one exists — always a safe, good move.
- The **bass typically moves in contrary motion** to the upper three voices — preserves voice independence.
- **Never** write parallel perfect octaves, parallel perfect 5ths, or parallel perfect unisons between any two voices — this is *the* defining rule of counterpoint (no two voices doing the exact same melodic thing = independence).
- An outer-voice (soprano or bass) **leading tone must resolve to tonic**.
- Never move any single voice by an **augmented 2nd or augmented 4th**.
- The 3-move safety net that avoids all of the above automatically: (1) keep the common tone, (2) move to the nearest chord tone, (3) use contrary motion.

## Doubling by inversion
| Position | Double this note |
|---|---|
| Root position | the root |
| 1st inversion, major triad | the soprano |
| 1st inversion, minor triad | the bass *or* soprano |
| 1st inversion, diminished triad | the bass |
| 2nd inversion (any quality) | the fifth (i.e. the bass note) |

- **Diminished triads are always used in first inversion** — root position and 2nd inversion are avoided.
- **Second-inversion triads are heavily restricted** to three specific, named uses:
  - **Cadential 6/4** — tonic in 2nd inversion moving to a root-position dominant at a [[Cadences|cadence]]: I6/4 → V → I.
  - **Passing 6/4** — the bass moves stepwise *through* the 2nd-inversion chord as a passing tone: I → I6/4 → I6.
  - **Pedal 6/4** — the bass note is a held pedal tone before and after: I → IV6/4 → I.
  - A 2nd-inversion triad outside these three contexts reads as stylistically wrong for the common-practice style.

## Melodic minor in part-writing
Common practice used harmonic minor by default, but its raised leading tone creates an awkward **augmented 2nd** (between ♭6 and the raised 7th). Melodic minor exists specifically to cure it:
- **Ascending** augmented 2nds → raise the **6th** degree too (creates a major IV6 chord along the way).
- **Descending** augmented 2nds → **don't raise the 7th** at all (creates a minor v chord) — no upward leading-tone pull is needed moving down anyway.

## Seventh chords in part-writing
The 7th of any seventh chord **must always resolve down by step** — approach it by common tone, step, or downward leap, but never an upward leap. (See [[Seventh Chords]] for the dominant-7th's four non-standard resolution detours.)
