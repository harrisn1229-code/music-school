# What chord is the open strings of a guitar?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4msfezGlF3o)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4msfezGlF3o)

## Synopsis

Names the chord formed by a guitar's six open strings (E A D G B E), using it as a worked example of why quartal-tuned instruments resist tertian chord-naming, then puts the resulting dissonant chord to actual use in a chord progression via a chromatic voice-leading line.

## Techniques

- **Why the open strings are awkward to name: the guitar is tuned in perfect fourths, but the standard chord-naming system assumes tertian harmony (chords built by stacking thirds).** Quartal harmony (chords built by stacking fourths) doesn't map cleanly onto that vocabulary, which is why "the open strings" resists a clean label at all.
- **The one non-fourth interval in standard tuning is the key that unlocks a name: G to B is a major third, not a fourth** — which means the top three open strings (G, B, E) form an E minor triad in first inversion. Building outward from that anchor: low E completes a plain E minor; adding D gives E minor 7; the remaining A is the awkward one — technically the 11th degree, but voiced low in the chord (second-lowest note) it reads better labeled add4 than add11, since "11th" implies a note voiced high. **Net result: E minor7 add4** (or equivalently, choosing A as the root instead: **A9sus4/E**) — the same six notes, two defensible names depending on which note you treat as root.
- **General labeling principle for a non-tertian cluster: any note in the stack can be argued as the root, producing a different valid name each time — pick the interpretation that's least of a stretch, usually the note that reads most stable in context, not just whichever produces the "simplest" chord symbol.** → [[Chord Naming by Context]]
- **Turning a dissonant, unplaceable chord into something musically usable: build a chromatic voice-leading line through ONE voice across the surrounding chords, and let the target chord be wherever that line naturally passes through.** Worked progression: Em → Em(maj7) [top voice E→D♯, sets the chromatic line in motion] → **open-string chord** (Em7add4) [top voice continues D♯→D natural — the chord is used *because* it contains the note the line needs next] → A major [top voice continues D→C♯] → Am → E major → C → Asus2 (turnaround back to the top). The dissonant chord stops sounding arbitrary once it's framed as "the note the chromatic line needed to pass through here."
