# Blues Form and Harmony

**The most influential twelve bars in popular music**, and — until 2026-08-04 — a subject the vault had no note on at all, despite covering Neapolitan sixths and species counterpoint.

## The 12-bar form

Twelve bars, three chords, in three four-bar phrases. In **A**: the chords are **A7, D7, E7** (I7, IV7, V7).

| Bars | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1–4** | I7 | I7 | I7 | I7 |
| **5–8** | IV7 | IV7 | I7 | I7 |
| **9–12** | V7 | IV7 | I7 | V7 |

In A: `A A A A / D D A A / E D A E`

**Bar 12 is the turnaround** — it usually goes to V7 to send you back to bar 1, or stays on I7 if it's the last chorus.

### The variants worth knowing

**Quick change** — bar 2 moves to the **IV** instead of staying on I. `A D A A / D D A A / E D A E`. Extremely common; arguably the default in modern blues.

**8-bar blues** — a real, separate form, not a truncation. "Key to the Highway," "Worried Life Blues." Same harmonic vocabulary, compressed.

**Minor blues** — `i7 iv7 v7` instead of dominants, often with a **♭VI** at bar 9 and — importantly — **the V frequently stays a dominant** rather than becoming minor, because you still want the leading tone pulling home. That's the half you had (BLU-10): the tonic goes minor, but the dominant usually doesn't.

## Why every chord is a dominant 7th

The question that seems wrong until it isn't (BLU-06).

In a major key, only the V chord is diatonically a dominant 7th. In a blues, **I, IV and V are all dominant 7ths** — which shouldn't work, because I7 contains a ♭7 that in functional harmony would demand resolution.

**Blues doesn't obey major-key functional harmony.** The dominant 7th isn't a chord that *needs* to resolve here — it's the **home sound**. The ♭7 is a colour, not a tension.

This is the single most important conceptual shift in the strand, and it explains almost everything else: why the blues scale works over all three chords, why the ♭3 isn't wrong, why the form circles rather than resolves.

## Turnarounds

**The last two bars**, and the job is to *break* the resolution so the ear expects bar 1 again.

If bars 11–12 just sat on I, the form would sound finished every twelve bars. A turnaround creates motion and usually **lands on V**, which is what makes the form circular.

**A common turnaround in A:** `A7 – F♯7 – B7 – E7` — a cycle of dominants walking back to V. Or a chromatic line descending over an A pedal.

The pattern generalizes: any short sequence that ends on V and gets you there with some movement will work.

## Call and response

**The 12-bar form *is* call and response**, built in — which is what BLU-13 was asking.

The lyric structure is **AAB**:

| Bars | Role |
|---|---|
| 1–4 | **A** — the call, a statement |
| 5–8 | **A** — the same line repeated |
| 9–12 | **B** — the answer, the punchline |

The repeat in bars 5–8 is the point: it gives the listener the line twice before resolving it, and it gives the singer time to think of the answer.

**In performance** it shows up between voice and instrument — the vocal states a phrase in the first half of a four-bar section, and the guitar or horn answers in the gap. **The space in the form is where the response lives**, which is why blues arrangements leave so much room.

## Feel

**Shuffle divides the beat unevenly — long-short, triplet-based. Straight divides it evenly.**

A shuffle is a triplet with the middle note removed. That's the same fact as [[RHY-C03 — Swing is a triplet with the middle note gone]], and the swing amount is a continuous dial, not a switch. Most blues is shuffled; a "straight" blues reads as rock.

See [[Rhythm and Groove]] and [[Pocket and Microtiming]].

## Where it went

The question worth being able to answer (BLU-15), because it explains why this matters to music you actually make.

**Rock** took the 12-bar form and the pentatonic vocabulary almost wholesale. Early Beatles and Stones material is blues form with different production — which is why [[60s - Beatles]] and the blues share so much DNA.

**Hip hop** inherited it indirectly, through **sampling**. Soul and funk records built on blues harmony got sampled, so the ♭3/♭7 vocabulary and call-and-response phrasing survive in the loops even where the 12-bar form itself doesn't. The dominant-7th-as-home-sound and the blue-note melodic language are still there in a genre that almost never plays a 12-bar.
