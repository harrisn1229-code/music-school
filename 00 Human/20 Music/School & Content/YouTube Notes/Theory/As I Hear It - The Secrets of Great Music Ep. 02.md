## What's taught

- **Art Tatum, "Yesterdays" (1954) ending** — approaches the final tonic through a chord a half-step above it voiced as a Lydian-dominant/altered-dominant sound, landing on a minor-major-9 (or minor-6/9) as the "one" chord rather than resolving through a conventional V7. Takeaway: **ending a song/section on a minor-major-9 (or minor-6/9) is a satisfying, sophisticated alternative full stop** — it reads as coherent because it's literally the i chord of the melodic-minor scale extended upward, so any diatonic color tone stacked on it (6, 9, 11) stays in-scale rather than clashing.
- **Florida Georgia Line, "Blessings" (country, key of E)** — over a repeating descending-bassline verse progression (E–B/D♯–C♯m7–G♯m/B), the SAME bass note gets reharmonized with a secondary dominant on just one later pass (an E9 in 2nd inversion functioning as V7/vi, since E is the 5th of A) leading into the vi chord, rather than swapping out the whole progression. Takeaway: **reharmonize one bass note per repeat instead of the whole progression** — because the "normal" version has already been heard enough times, the one altered pass reads as a deliberate, tasteful lift rather than a random substitution; bonus points if the substitution is functional/logical (relates meaningfully to what comes before/after) rather than just a cool vertical slice dropped in.
- **Claire Fisher, "Rocking in Rhythm" (Cuban 6/8 feel)** — the rhythm section states a straightforward 6/8 groove while the melody accents dotted-quarter groupings hard enough that it reads as its own felt 4/4 swing layered on top; tilting attention toward the melody surfaces the 4/4 feel, tilting toward the bass/percussion snaps back to 6/8 — two genuinely coexisting feels, not one feel disguised as another. Takeaway: **superimpose a felt pulse on top of an established groove by accenting groupings that don't match the groove's own subdivision** (e.g. accenting triplets in groups of 2 instead of 3 and treating that new accent pattern as an implied new beat) — a melodic form of metric modulation performed without an actual tempo change.
- **"Rituals" (drum & bass)** — a single melodic interval (a plain 5th above the bass) is held sustained and spread wide (not voiced close) while only the bass note underneath changes on a set path: ♭2→5, then ♭3→5 an octave up (repeated), finally resolving ♭7→1. Takeaway: **the power of a single, wide-spread held interval over a moving bass** — spreading the two notes across octaves (rather than close together) is what lets the ear track the interaction between them as two separate lines instead of fusing them into one indistinct chord; the choice of interval matters (a plain 5th here stays cool/aggressive against the tritone-adjacent ♭2 bass motion — a 3rd instead would shift the vibe warmer/darker).
- **Doom Eternal score (Mick Gordon, metal)** — a riff repeatedly implies a lowest note (an open-string-feeling E♭) as the absolute floor, establishing that boundary purely by never going below it; right as a section peaks (drums locking in, riff resolving), the line drops to a genuinely lower note (D) the listener was never given reason to expect. Two details that make it land: the low note is MUTED (not sounded) on earlier passes, so it's felt as a rhythmic gap rather than heard, and the actual pitch is only revealed the first time it's really played; the reveal is timed to coincide with the drums/groove locking in, not dropped in cold. Takeaway: **define a "play space" by consistent repetition, then exceed it exactly once for a genuine shock** — establish clear limits (register, harmonic vocabulary, dynamic range — whatever the piece's boundary is) early and consistently enough that the listener internalizes them without being told, then break exactly one of them once it's been earned.
