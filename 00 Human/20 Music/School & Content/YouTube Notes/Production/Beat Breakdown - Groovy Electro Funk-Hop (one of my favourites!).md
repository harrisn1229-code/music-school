# Beat Breakdown - Groovy Electro Funk-Hop (one of my favourites!)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6FfICkz4tYI)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6FfICkz4tYI)

## Synopsis

Max Konyi breaks down the trailer music he wrote for his own "Musical Warp Drive" course (made in **Studio One**; every technique below is DAW-agnostic and maps directly onto Ableton) — a short, dense "electro funk-hop" cue built to be cool and video-gamey without being distracting under spoken narration. It's a genuinely rich lesson precisely because it's SHORT: with almost no processing budget to hide behind (his own summary — "the amount of inserts on here is nothing"), the entire track's identity comes from composition and sound choice alone. Highlights: harmonizing three monophonic synth voices instead of playing block chords (a classical/orchestral way of thinking about harmony), a Lydian-intro-to-relative-Dorian-verse modulation that's really the same 7 notes reframed, a deliberately non-diatonic "magical" chord dropped into an otherwise-minor progression, a polyphonic bass patch that plays its own harmony line against the root, a bass fill used instead of a drum fill at a transition, and a minimal-processing mixing philosophy (sound design does the work, not inserts). Genuinely useful as a case study in getting maximum musical mileage from minimum production budget.

## Techniques

**Harmony-as-harmonized-monophony (intro, ~0:30–3:00)**
- **Harmonize three monophonic synth voices instead of playing block chords** — three separate monophonic Omnisphere synth instances, each with a slightly different tone, panned hard center/left/right, each playing ONE note at a time; stacked together they read as three-part harmony/chords without ever using a polyphonic chord voicing on one instrument. Described as a "classical"/orchestral way of thinking about harmony — take single-note instrumental lines and harmonize them in parts, rather than defaulting to stacked-chord thinking. → [[Melody Writing]], [[Omnisphere]]
- **A drone bass under harmonized monophonic lines is what actually establishes the key/mode** — a Serum bass patch holding a sustained low E is the only thing that tells the ear "this is E Lydian" — the three harmony lines run the Lydian scale fast enough that the mode wouldn't register from them alone; the drone gives all the moving parts above it meaning. → [[Diatonic Modes]]
- **Modulate to a relative mode by holding the same 7 notes and shifting the tonal center** — the intro (E Lydian) moves into the main beat's C♯ Dorian section using the exact same pitch collection, just re-centered — "relative modes, the same set of notes just different key centers." Practically: write the intro in one mode, then re-anchor the bass/drone a third below to land in its relative Dorian for the verse, funk-cadence feel (i → IV, minor-to-major-and-back). → [[Diatonic Modes]]
- **8-bit/chiptune lead + harmony line in oblique/contrary counterpoint** — a simple square-wave-ish lead melody doubled by a harmony line that does NOT always move in parallel with it: sometimes in close harmony (a 3rd), then skipping to a 6th, sometimes one voice holds while the other moves — genuine two-part counterpoint rather than a fixed-interval harmony doubler. → [[Melody Writing]]

**Main-beat chord writing (~3:00–8:00)**
- **Outline a chord with 3 monophonic voices playing its individual chord tones**, rather than one instrument voicing the whole chord — same three-synth-harmony technique as the intro, now explicitly outlining Cm7(add11) → F6/9 (a i–IV funk cadence, minor to major and back). → [[Melody Writing]]
- **Layer a full chord voicing UNDER a thinned, rootless duplicate of the same chord, filtered down** — a Serum chord patch plays the full voicing; a second instance of the same patch plays the identical chord *without the root* and with a filter pulled down, sitting more subtly underneath — a cheap way to add harmonic thickness without the second layer competing with or muddying the first. → [[Serum 2]]
- **A polyphonic bass patch that plays its own harmony line against the bass note, moving in contrary motion** — most bass patches are monophonic; this one plays two notes at once — a lower bass line (root motion) and an upper harmony line a 3rd/5th above it — with the upper line descending while the lower line ascends (and vice versa), landing back together. A pitch-bend on the last note ties the phrase off. → [[Bassline Writing]]
- **Octave-jump muted short notes as a rhythmic, drum-like bass part** — a Serum bass patch playing very short, muted octave jumps that function almost as a percussion part rather than a melodic bass line — deliberately emulating the tone AND the rhythmic playing technique of a real electric bassist (palm-muted octave pops). → [[Bassline Writing]], [[Serum 2]]
- **Two-part drum programming: a sample-drum layer for the main hits + a separate realistic-kit layer for hats/fills** — Battery 4 (very few samples, just the core hits) layered with Addictive Drums 2 (hi-hats — alternating closed/open plus small fills) for a more "realistic" composite kit than either alone. → [[Rhythm and Groove]]
- **Let instruments breathe: schedule melodic elements so they don't sound simultaneously** — a descending chord-tone figure finishes and clears out of the way right before a "blip" melody enters, so there's never ambiguity about which part carries the melody at any instant — arranging by *when* things sound, not just what they play. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **Intentionally "late"-reading chord hits via filter movement + sidechain, left uncorrected on purpose** — a chord hit's filter envelope plus kick-triggered sidechain compression makes the chord sound like it's being "squeezed out of the kick drum" rather than landing exactly on the beat — a deliberately loose, felt timing rather than a mix problem to fix.
- **A bass fill instead of a drum fill at a section transition** — rather than a busy drum fill, pull the bass out of its normal register for a short bass run/lick; because the sub frequencies briefly disappear, the low end "gets to breathe" for a moment and the bass gains a fresh, higher tone right as everything else descends into the next section. → [[Bassline Writing]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]]

**Breakdown section (~8:00–13:00)**
- **A randomized pattern generator + a swept filter on a sustained "cello" pad reads as ensemble harmony**, not a static chord — an Omnisphere string-type patch run through a randomized note-pattern generator with a filter being manually swept up and down, outlining Amaj7 → Badd9(sus)/Bsus → F♯7 → Cmaj7 → D6/9, closing on the classic C-D-E ascending-bass "big finale" cliché into E major/Lydian. → [[Chord Progressions]]
- **Two panned kalimba-type ("colimba") patches each picking DIFFERENT individual chord tones, hard-panned L/R** — rather than both playing the same notes, one grabs one subset of a chord's tones (e.g. the 7th, root, and 9th) and the other grabs a different subset — together, stereo and textural, "harmonized" purely through complementary note selection rather than unison doubling. → [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **A deliberately non-diatonic "magical" chord dropped into an otherwise-minor progression, voiced without a root** — in a C minor context, a C MAJOR chord (borrowed, non-diatonic — "you're not expecting to hear that") appears voiced as 7‑9‑♯11‑5 (no root, root left to the bass), followed by a D chord voiced 5‑9‑3‑6 (also rootless) — the surprise of an out-of-key major triad mid-minor-progression is the intended effect, not a "fix." → [[Borrowed Chords|Borrowed Chords]]
- **A tempo-automation ramp at a phrase's end "melts" into the final landing chord** — automating tempo to swell/decelerate right at the last few notes of a phrase, timed to dissolve directly into the next held chord, for a smeared, non-mechanical arrival.
- **Build a "fattest possible" final chord as its own separate mini-composition, not an afterthought** — the ending "digital wash" chord (a big E Lydian voicing with 6, 9, ♯11 extensions) was written and voiced in a separate session specifically to be the best/fattest Lydian voicing possible, THEN run through the same downsample/distortion modulation as the intro bass for a cohesive "video-gamey" character across the whole track.
- **Ride-cymbal swells (two instances, panned hard L/R) for a deliberately "epic" breakdown moment**, plus a sub-heavy kick timed to land exactly when the non-diatonic "magic" chords hit, physically reinforcing the harmonic surprise with a rhythmic accent.

**Mixing philosophy — sound design over processing**
- **Minimal-processing mixing: the character comes from the SOUNDS, not the inserts.** Buses get a filter and light EQ (cutting lows/unneeded content); a few compressors where volume is uneven; the whole mix bus is just compressor → saturator → EQ → limiter, nothing more. His stated rule: "I'm not spending so much time trying to polish something that wasn't working to begin with — I'm just trying to choose the best sounds I possibly can." Fix problems by choosing a better sound or subtracting with EQ before reaching for more processing.
- **The whole track ran on a tiny instrument count**: two Omnisphere instances (one all-8-bit patches, one "real"/sampled patches), a handful of Serum synths, Battery 4, and Addictive Drums 2 — a reminder that a dense-sounding arrangement doesn't require a large instrument roster.

## Sound/tone selection

- **8-bit/chiptune lead and harmony patches for a "video gamey" aesthetic** — an explicit genre-fusion choice (funky + video-gamey) rather than a straight retro-synth pastiche. → [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **A Serum bass patch with the Distortion unit's downsample amount AND mix both automated/modulated together** — keeps the tone "clean enough to have a good balance" while still giving the bass movement and character rather than a static crush. → [[Serum 2]]
- **Two panned kalimba ("colimba") patches picking complementary individual chord tones** for a textural, harmonized arpeggio layer. → [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **A polyphonic "pulse bass" patch** (plays a bass line AND a harmony line simultaneously) as a distinct sound-design choice from a standard monophonic bass. → [[Sound Selection Guide]], [[Bassline Writing]]
