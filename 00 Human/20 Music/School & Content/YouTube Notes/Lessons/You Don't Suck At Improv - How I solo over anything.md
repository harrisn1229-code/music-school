# You Don't Suck At Improv: How I solo over anything

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LT8bWsEcZBs)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LT8bWsEcZBs)

## Synopsis

The core method here is the same five-CAGED-position walk with matched mode/pentatonic/chord-tone targeting already captured in [[Positional CAGED Soloing with Matched Modes and Arpeggios]] (from an earlier video in this batch), reapplied to an F Dorian tune instead of Em–G–D–C. Three genuinely new ideas beyond that overlap are extracted below: the "practice improv, not its prerequisites" framing, an ear-based method for confirming a mode against an ambiguous key signature, and a minor-pentatonic substitution shortcut for Dorian/Phrygian contexts.

## Techniques

- **The core meta-point: practicing scales, fretboard knowledge, or chord theory in isolation does not train improvisation — improvisation requires its own dedicated practice.** Named as the actual gap for intermediate players who've "done everything right" (learned modes, pentatonics, fretboard notes, chord construction) but still can't improvise musically: none of that prerequisite work is itself improv practice. The fix demonstrated through the rest of the video is applying all of it together, live, over real changes — repeatedly, across many tunes ("once you do this with 10 different tunes, it gets a lot easier").
- **Confirming a mode by ear, not just by key signature.** A key signature alone can be ambiguous — three flats could be E♭ major, its relative C minor, or (as in this video's tune) F Dorian, all sharing the same key signature. Method: check which chord actually functions as the tonal center by ear, not just by scanning for the "textbook" relative-minor answer — the source tune's key signature technically supports a C minor reading, but the chord progression's actual center doesn't sound like C minor by ear, so F Dorian is used instead. Explicitly framed as an ear judgment call: "if it does sound like C minor to you, that's not wrong either."
- **Chord-tone targeting simplified to a single rule: land on a chord tone specifically on beat 1 of each new chord.** A lighter version of full-bar chord-tone targeting — the requirement is only that the first beat of a chord change resolves to one of that chord's three tones, not that the whole bar tracks the chord. Explicit warning against overdoing conscious targeting: too much deliberate thinking produces shy/hesitant-sounding solos, so the goal is a balance between autopilot and full conscious control, not maximum targeting at all times.
- **The minor-pentatonic substitution "secret" for Dorian/Phrygian contexts.** Because the standard 5-note minor pentatonic scale omits exactly the one scale degree that distinguishes Dorian (natural 6th) or Phrygian (♭2nd) from plain natural minor, a "Dorian pentatonic" or "Phrygian pentatonic" position is actually pitch-identical to the plain minor pentatonic shape in the same position — you can substitute the already-known minor pentatonic shape directly, with no new fingering to learn, and it will sound correct in a Dorian or Phrygian context specifically because the pentatonic subset never touches the note that would expose the difference.
- **Sequencing note (same underlying method as [[Positional CAGED Soloing with Matched Modes and Arpeggios]]):** the five positions are found by walking the E string's own notes at the roots of each mode in the parent key — 3rd, 6th, 8th, 11th, 13th fret in this F Dorian example — with the 15th position looping back to the 3rd position's shapes an octave up.
