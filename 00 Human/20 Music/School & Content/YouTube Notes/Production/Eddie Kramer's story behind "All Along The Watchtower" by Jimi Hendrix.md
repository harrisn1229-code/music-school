# Eddie Kramer's story behind "All Along The Watchtower" by Jimi Hendrix

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N6m019pawms)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N6m019pawms)

## Synopsis

A short Mix with the Masters segment in which Eddie Kramer — the original engineer on Jimi Hendrix's *Electric Ladyland* — solos and talks through one specific, famous element of "All Along The Watchtower": the interlocking 12-string/6-string guitar part that opens the song, played live together by Dave Mason (12-string) and Hendrix (6-string) with Mitch Mitchell's drums bleeding in behind them, printed to **one blended stereo track**. Kramer's story has two halves. First, the historical account: how that one track got its crunchy, jangly, dry character — an overdriven Helios console mic pre pushed hard enough to sound almost amp-distorted (without an amp), tape hit hard on the way to print, bass rolled off the 12-string to keep it out of Hendrix's way, and a bare whisper of a delay he isn't fully sure he added, blamed on how many generations the tape was transferred through over 50-plus years. Second, and more useful for reproducing the sound today: his own recommended modern recreation path — Waves' HLS plugin bundle (built to model exactly this console/tape/limiter chain, developed with his own input), and a manual fallback (drive a normal mic pre's input into gradual distortion, then reach for the plugin's EQ) for anyone without it. He closes with concrete, budget-conscious mic advice: mic the acoustic close to the last fret rather than the soundhole to kill body boominess, and — since the actual vintage mics used are lost to memory and the era's tube mics now cost thousands — reach for the Shure KSM 141 as a modern, affordable stand-in that was deliberately designed to combine the brightness of an AKG 451 with the vintage-preferred voicing of a Neumann KM 84. Real YouTube captions, clean and confident — Kramer speaks in complete, unhurried sentences and the transcript reads cleanly throughout; the only genuine uncertainty is in the content itself (his own hedged memory of 50-year-old mic choices), not the transcription.

## Techniques

**The guitar blend itself:**
- **Two guitarists' interlocking live parts, printed to ONE blended stereo track rather than kept on separate channels.** Dave Mason's "slightly out of tune" 12-string (described by Kramer as "prang prang") plays over Hendrix's heavy rhythmic upstroke/downstroke 6-string ("chugga chugga chugga chugga ga"), captured together as a live take with Mitch Mitchell's drums audibly bleeding into the same signal — "that sound of those two blended together makes that one stereo track." Kramer calls out the moment the two guitar parts finally lock in together as "a wonderful moment in the song." *(~0:00–1:00)* → [[Layering and Stacking]], [[1 Acoustic Guitar Track|Acoustic Guitar Track]]
- **Bass rolled off the 12-string specifically so it doesn't fight the other guitar's heavy low end.** "Certainly, a lot of bass has been rolled off in order to let it sit in the track and not interfere with the heavy bass end of Jimmy's guitar." A textbook pre-emptive carve between two same-register parts — general-starting-point guidance since no exact frequency/dB is given. → [[Masking and Carving]], [[EQ]], [[BASS Slot]]
- **Deliberately dry — little to no reverb, to protect the crispness a blended live guitar-pair take needs to cut through the track.** "There's no reverb on that that I can remember, and I don't think we actually added too much reverb because we needed something fairly dry in the mix to keep that crispness." Processing beyond that: compressed and EQ'd, no further detail given. → [[Reverb]], [[Compression]]

**Saturation — console + tape stacked:**
- **The Helios console's mic pre deliberately overdriven, on the way in, for a "slight crunch" that reads almost like amp distortion despite having no amp in the signal path — then the tape itself hit hard as well, on top of the pre's own drive.** "The Helios mic pre would break up quite quickly, and I'm obviously overdriving it, because it sounds almost like it's amplified through an amp. It's not going through an amp — I think I'm just pushing the envelope and driving it really hard so you get that slight crunch... and then, of course, the tape is being hit really hard too. I can imagine the needles are banging off the end stops." Two independent saturation stages stacked in series (preamp drive, then hot tape level) rather than one device doing all the work. → [[Kramer Tape]], [[UAD Neve 1073]], [[Saturation and Distortion]]
- **Modern recreation, plugin path: Waves HLS — built specifically to model this exact chain (Helios mic pre + Pi/Pye limiter + tape machine), and a plugin Kramer says he still reaches for himself.** "Definitely use the Waves HLS. What we did was we tried to recreate the three elements: the tape machine, the Helios mic pre, and the Pi limiter... it's HLS and PIE, I believe... The plug-in is actually very nice, I use it myself quite often. Certainly in the EQ world — you can't get it as a mic pre, although I've heard some rumors that somebody's trying to make a digital mic pre that sounds somewhat similar." Unowned — see [[Plugin Wishlist]].
- **Modern recreation, no-HLS fallback: drive a normal mic pre's input into gradual, minimal distortion, then lean on a plugin's EQ stage for the rest of the character — his own stated "best way" to get close without the specific plugin.** "The way I would approach it would be use a normal mic pre, start to gradually distort the input, just gradually, just enough, and then use the plug-in for the EQ, and you'll get very, very, very close to that." Best-match owned equivalent for the preamp-drive half: [[UAD Neve 1073]]'s Unison mic gain pushed for harmonic push rather than clean level (the note's own "drive the red gain for thick harmonic push" guidance); the EQ half maps to [[Pro-Q 4]] or a channel-strip EQ stage. → [[UAD Neve 1073]], [[Kramer Tape]], [[Saturation and Distortion]]

**A near-inaudible delay, maybe:**
- **A delay added so faint it's barely detectable even on careful re-listening — possibly to make the part "maybe slightly bigger," and possibly a byproduct of repeated tape transfers rather than a deliberate mixdown choice.** "I think now, on careful listening again, I'm hearing just a touch of some kind of delay, but it's very, very, very faint. Just enough to sort of make it maybe slightly bigger, that we could have added at the very end in the mixdown process — because this was transferred so many times, it is very conceivable that I stuck something like that in there." Notable as a case where even the original engineer isn't certain a processing decision was deliberate — worth remembering that "barely there" is a legitimate delay setting in its own right, not just an EQ or reverb move. → [[Delay]]

**Mic technique and gear:**
- **Mic the acoustic close to the last fret, not the soundhole — roughly 2½–3 inches away, specifically to eliminate body boominess.** "I like to mic it — if this is the hole of the acoustic guitar, and the fretboard is here, it ends here — I like to mic it right by the fretboard, probably about 2½ to 3 inches away. I like to get really close, so I'm trying to eliminate some of the boominess from the body of the guitar... the idea is get up away from the hole of the actual guitar, right by the last fret — you're eliminating the possibility of extra boom." Notably closer and more fretboard-specific than this vault's existing acoustic-mic default (Zach Scholz's ~45°-off-axis, ~10"-off-the-fretboard-end spot already logged on [[1 Acoustic Guitar Track|Acoustic Guitar Track]]) — a second, more aggressive close-mic option worth trying when boominess (not detail/air) is the specific problem. → [[1 Acoustic Guitar Track|Acoustic Guitar Track]]
- **Original session mics: genuinely uncertain, by Kramer's own admission — "a little hard to remember from all those 50-plus years ago."** Best recollection: probably an AKG C60 (tube miniature condenser) on one guitar, possibly a "67" (a Neumann U67-class tube mic) on Dave Mason's 12-string, possibly an AKG C40-class mic on Hendrix's 6-string. Logged as spoken/qualitative color, not a confirmed spec — treat as historical texture rather than a setting to copy. Also unsure of the actual 12-string's make: "I have no idea which type of 12-string, whether it was a Gibson or who the hell knows what it was — it could have been something Dave Mason brought in, or something Jimmy had in a case somewhere."
- **Shure KSM 141 as the modern, affordable stand-in for that vintage tube-mic-on-acoustic sound — a mic explicitly engineered to combine the character of two mics Kramer already likes.** "There's a mic that Shure makes called the KSM 141, and the idea there with this mic was to combine the best of two microphones that I happen to like very much: the AKG 451 — very bright, great for acoustic guitar, great for percussion — and the Neumann KM 84 [KM 184 line]... I like the KM 84, the more vintage one, slightly better I feel. So what they did was combine the sounds of those two." Specs given: 20 dB pad, switchable omni/cardioid, ~$600 vs. ~$8,000 for a new-build 67-class mic. "Does a very lovely job on recording acoustic guitars, percussion, any of that stuff... I particularly like that KSM 141." → [[Shure KSM 141]], [[Gear Index]]
