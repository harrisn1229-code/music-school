# Hybrid Picking Exercises

Jared Borkowski's (Sound Guitar Lessons) **seven-exercise system for hybrid picking** — pick held in the hand while the fingers (usually middle, ring, pinky) pluck alongside it. Framed as useful for three separate goals: seamlessly switching between pick-style and finger-style playing, playing chords without always strumming (a pick player's route into finger-style-feeling comping), and speeding up single-note lead/solo lines by adding finger-plucks to string crossings that alternate picking handles less cleanly. Works identically on acoustic or electric. Built on one foundational concept — **planting** — then five chordal exercises, then two lead/single-note exercises.

## Foundation: planting (do this before any of the seven)

**Planting** = a deliberate, tiny moment of *preparing* a note before playing it — the picking-hand finger settles physically onto the string (checking "am I on the right spot, does it feel right") *before* the pluck, rather than swooping in and hoping. Pulled straight from classical guitar right-hand pedagogy; genuinely obscure outside that world, which is why Jared front-loads it here. This is the same core idea documented for the four-finger case on [[Right-Hand Pinky Fingerstyle Technique]] ("plant → pluck" calibration, exercises 1–2 there) — treat these as the same technique taught by the same channel in two different contexts (hybrid pick+fingers here, pure fingerstyle p-i-m-a-c there).

- **Why it matters:** without planting, tone/volume/attack point vary pluck to pluck because the finger lands on the string slightly differently each time. Planting is what makes a consistent, secure, controlled sound possible — described as giving "security, confidence, consistency, tone, timbre, control."
- **How it feels in real time:** as soon as you pluck the *current* note, you're already placing the *next* finger on its string — the plant is mentally tied to the previous pluck, not a separate step you consciously schedule. When you pause mid-phrase, you should be able to feel "I'm planted, I'm stable, I'm ready" rather than reaching blind.
- **Foundation drill:** on open strings, pluck with the pick, then plant-and-pluck with one finger (start with **middle**, since the pick occupies the thumb-equivalent role in hybrid picking), moving string to string. Listen for identical tone/volume every time — if it varies, keep drilling planting before moving to the numbered exercises below.
- **The right hand is "the expressive hand."** Quoting a classical guitar teacher: players over-focus on the fretting hand (which frets, which notes) and under-focus on the picking hand, but dynamics, tone, timbre, and speed control all live in the picking hand — planting is foundational to that control.
- Applies whether you're using fingerpicks, nails, or bare fingertip flesh.

## The seven exercises

All demonstrated first on **open strings** (no left-hand distraction — pure right-hand coordination), then applied to a scale, then applied to chords. **No metronome on exercises 1–5** — the goal is tone/attack consistency, not time; slow down/speed up freely, add a metronome later only if timing specifically needs work.

1. **Ascending: pick → middle → ring → pinky.** Pick the 4th string, then finger-pluck middle/ring/pinky in order, ascending across the next three strings. "Pinky" here (classical letter *c*/chiquito) isn't a classical-guitar finger — it's used freely in hybrid picking. Once solid on open strings: (a) run it along an **E natural minor scale** on one string (or any string) to drill the finger-order against real fretted notes and improvise with it; (b) apply the identical up-and-down pattern to **chords** — worked example: C, first-inversion G (G/B), and Am, sweeping the four-string pattern across each shape.
2. **Descending: pick → pinky → ring → middle.** Mirror of exercise 1 — same string set, order flipped. Same progression: open strings → same E natural minor scale → same chords (C, G).
3. **Ascending+descending with a "full plant" twist.** Pattern is pick → middle → ring → pinky → ring → middle, but instead of planting one finger at a time, **plant all three fingers (middle/ring/pinky) simultaneously right after the pick stroke**, then release/pluck each one individually in order on the way up (harder — genuine multi-finger control). On the way back down, revert to **sequential planting** (one finger planted-then-plucked at a time, as in exercises 1–2). Explicitly called out as classical-guitar prep for fast arpeggiation techniques that require a full plant to execute at speed — not the point of this exercise specifically, but exposure toward it. Apply to chords the same way as 1–2 once solid on open strings.
4. **Alternating pick and full block chord ("number four out of seven").** Pick, then chord — but "chord" here means **all three fingers plucking simultaneously** (a full plant + simultaneous release, not a roll), alternating: pick–chord–pick–chord. You technically plant with the pick too, but the goal is for that plant to become fast/imperceptible while the finger-side planting stays deliberate. Apply to any chord voicing, e.g. **Dm7 → Ddim → Cmaj7**, alternating pick and block chord on each.
5. **Full block: pick + all three fingers plucked together, as one hit.** The last of the five chordal exercises. Distinguishing focus versus 1–4: **evenness** — every string in the simultaneous pluck must come out at equal velocity/volume, none popping out louder than the others. Described as harder than it sounds despite being conceptually the simplest ("simple in concept but harder than the others"). You'll hear a small gap/silence before the hit from the planting moment — that's expected and intentional at first; the advanced version is closing that gap while staying planted and secure, not skipping the plant.
6. **Lead playing — melodic thirds ("every other note") pattern, hybrid-picked across string crossings.** First of the two single-note/solo exercises. Setup: know a scale form (Jared uses **C major / A natural minor, 5th position** as the reference shape — works with any scale/position). Pattern: up a third, down one note, up a third, down one note (a "skip one, step back" melodic contour usable on any scale). **Pick every note that stays on the same string; use a finger (Jared defaults to middle) for the note that crosses to a new string.** Practice ascending primarily (descending optional/secondary). Left-hand timing note: fret the new note right before you actually pluck it, not early — fretting ahead of the pluck lets the string ring the wrong pitch briefly. Apply the identical crossing-note-gets-a-finger logic to a **pentatonic scale**, but the picked pattern there is a plain **skip-one-note-up** run rather than melodic thirds (the pentatonic's structure doesn't support true melodic thirds) — same hybrid-pick-on-the-crossing-note mechanic either way. Generalizes: any time a lead line skips to a new string, that's a candidate for a finger-pluck instead of a pick stroke — start with adjacent-string crossings, then extend to skipping multiple strings/bigger jumps once comfortable.
7. **Lead playing — harmonic (two-note) hybrid picking.** Two variants, both using a pentatonic scale (not the full 7-note diatonic scale):
   - **Diatonic-interval double-stops:** pick one note, finger-pluck a second note simultaneously, moving that harmonized pair up/down through the pentatonic shape — alternates between a 4th interval and a 3rd interval as you move through the scale. Cited as a Stevie Ray Vaughan-style sound (used often, not exclusively).
   - **Drone-note technique:** hold one note constant (Jared uses the **pinky** to hold it, though any finger works) while picking/hybrid-picking a moving line against it — demonstrated with an **open low string drone** (open D, open E) under a blues-scale lick, the exact device used in this video's own intro lick. One extra sustained note "really fills up the sound" — a cheap, high-payoff harmonic thickening move for lead lines. Also a Stevie Ray Vaughan-associated device.

## Practice notes

- Once the five chordal patterns (1–5) feel secure, **improvise freely with them** rather than only drilling in isolation — Jared repeatedly demos noodling over each pattern as soon as it's playable, treating that as real practice, not a reward after practice.
- You don't have to master all seven before using any of them musically — Jared explicitly frames the end goal as finding your own finger combinations/orders beyond the seven once the vocabulary is comfortable.
