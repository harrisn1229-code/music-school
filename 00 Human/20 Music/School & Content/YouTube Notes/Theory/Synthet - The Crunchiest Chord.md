## Synopsis

A short diminished-chord primer aimed at demystifying them: dim triads and dim7 chords are just stacks of equal intervals (minor 3rds), which is why they sound tense/spooky (the classic "falling arpeggiated diminished" horror-movie cliché) and why there are only three unique dim7 chords total — every dim7 is enharmonically one of three shapes, transposed. The practical payoff is using a diminished chord as a passing chord between two diatonic chords (e.g. C major → D minor) for extra tension, and randomly trying dim7 insertions between chords in a progression until one sounds good.

## What's taught

- **Diminished triads = stacked minor 3rds, dim7 = four stacked minor 3rds** — that equal spacing (as opposed to a normal triad's major-3rd + minor-3rd stack) is what gives diminished chords their tense, "neither major nor minor," unresolved character. → [[Diminished Chords]]
- **Only three unique dim7 chords exist** — because a dim7 is symmetric (equal intervals all the way round), transposing any dim7 up a minor 3rd lands on a chord containing the exact same four notes. Easy to remember and easy to reuse across keys.
- **Use a diminished chord as a passing/transition chord** — inserted for a beat between two diatonic chords (demoed: C major → [C diminished] → D minor) to add suspension/tension before the target chord lands. Workflow tip: don't overthink the theory — try inserting random dim7 chords between the chords of an existing progression until one sounds good; described as landing "a pretty jazzy feel."
- **The horror-movie sting** is a diminished (typically dim7) arpeggio, ascending or descending in minor 3rds — same underlying chord as the passing-chord trick, just arpeggiated and isolated rather than passed through.
