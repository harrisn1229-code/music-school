> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **Finish the drum groove, then write the bass around it — the trap is following the kick map**
> Bass notes do not have to start on the kicks. Assuming they do produces a line that's locked and lifeless.
>
> **Q2.** **The pocket — and without a real loop playing, any rhythmic choice is provisional**
> A finished groove will overrule decisions made against silence, which is why the ordering is a dependency rather than a preference.
>
> **Q3.** **Land the roots under the chords by ear, before any decoration — one root per bar is the rung**
> Quality doesn't matter yet because only the root is sounding. Major or minor is a question about the chord above, not about the note you're playing.
>
> **Q4.** **The same line an octave too low goes from fine to undefined mud — move it up, don't process it**
> Register is a writing decision. Reaching for EQ on a line that's simply sitting too low is treating a symptom.
>
> **Q5.** **It's an ear-training gap, not a writing gap — that's what Course 09 is for**
> *Rubric (5):* identifies it as an ear gap rather than a failure of writing ability (2) · says the step is diagnostic, so failing it tells you something specific rather than something general (1) · sends the answer to the ear-training method running alongside this course (2)
> The distinction matters because the two failures look identical from the inside — you sit there, nothing comes, and the natural conclusion is that you can't write bass. But hearing the root under a chord is a perceptual skill with its own training path, and it's the one place in this course where a production problem is really an ear problem. It doesn't mean the writing is weak, and it doesn't mean you need more theory. It means the drill is tonic recognition and pitch matching, and it belongs in the ear-training routine that runs daily alongside this course rather than in another attempt at the bassline.
>
> **Q6.** **Jump up the octave and back — occasionally through a passing tone, the 5th above being the classic**
> A strong line can be mainly octaves with the occasional tiny fill. That motion is where a disco or funk line's bounce comes from, because it mimics how a player physically moves around the neck.
>
> **Q7.** **Steal the melody's rhythm — the rhythm of what the melody just played, not its notes**
> The busyness then hides inside phrasing the listener has already heard, which is what keeps it cohesive instead of arbitrary.
>
> **Q8.** **Play a too-busy line through the whole loop, then keep only the killer moments**
> Never repeat the same fill in consecutive phrases. Deleting down from too much is easier than deciding what to add to too little.
>
> **Q9.** **Imagine a bassist physically playing it — rewrite anything no player would phrase that way**
> The exemption is when the unplayable line is the point, such as reversed audio. It's the same four-limb constraint that governs realistic drum programming, applied to one player instead of a kit.
>
> **Q10.** **Never two genuinely different basslines at once — doubling the same line is fine**
> Doubling is one line on two instruments, which thickens a single statement. Two distinct low melodic lines are two statements competing for the same space.
>
> **Q11.** **The ear can't separate two melodic lines that low, so they read as mud rather than as richness**
> *Rubric (5):* says two distinct low lines clash and read as mud instead of richness (2) · contrasts with chords and melody, which sit high enough to be separated by the ear (1) · says most muddy low ends are an arrangement problem rather than an EQ problem (2)
> Up in the mid and high range the ear separates simultaneous parts easily — that's how a chord, a topline and a counter-melody can all be present and individually followed. Low down that separation collapses. Two independent lines an octave below middle C don't read as two voices in dialogue, they read as one indistinct rumble whose pitch keeps changing for no apparent reason, and no amount of panning or EQ recovers the distinction because the information the ear needs isn't there to begin with. Which is the practical point: when a low end is muddy the reflex is to reach for EQ, and the actual cause is usually that two things are playing that shouldn't both be playing. Count the distinct melodic lines in your soloed low end before you touch a filter.
