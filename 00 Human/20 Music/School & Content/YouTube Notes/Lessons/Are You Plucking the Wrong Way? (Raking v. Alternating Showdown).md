# Are You Plucking the Wrong Way? (Raking v. Alternating Showdown)

## Synopsis

A "versus battle" framed BassBuzz lesson that puts strict alternating and raking head-to-head across three real basslines (The Cars, Metallica, Red Hot Chili Peppers), then pulls the results into a general decision rule. This is the theory companion to [[Fix Your Plucking in 5 Minutes]] (the live-coaching drill session) — where that video teaches *how to drill* the rake-vs-alternate fork, this one teaches *how to predict* which side of the fork wins for a given bassline before you ever sit down to drill it. The most valuable idea is the closing "math hack": string-crossing note groups that come in **evens** (twos, fours) favor strict alternating because they land cleanly on the two plucking fingers; groups that come in **odds** (ones, threes) favor raking because raking is what makes an odd group line up with an even number of fingers. The video also does useful myth-busting — the "real bass players only strictly alternate" bass-forum wisdom gets named and debunked with counter-examples (Dave LaRue, Adam Nitti, Jeff Berlin, Mohini Dey, Billy Sheehan), landing on "there's no wrong technique, most pros use both depending on the line." It closes with a genuinely useful beginner warning: at slow tempos almost any plucking habit works, including bad ones, so sloppy technique hides until you try to speed up — meaning beginners have to deliberately drill clean alternating even on lines where they could get away with being lazy.

## Techniques

- **Basic mechanics, restated cleanly.** Strict alternating = index/middle back and forth 100% of the time, regardless of string crossings or note groupings (I‑M‑I‑M… or M‑I‑M‑I…, doesn't matter which finger leads). Raking = when a string crossing brings you down to a lower-pitched string, reuse the finger that just plucked instead of alternating — e.g. plucking G string with middle, then crossing to D, you pluck D with middle too (reusing), then cross to A and reuse index there: I‑M‑M‑I‑I‑M across a G‑D‑A‑ style crossing sequence.
- **The math hack — pick technique by note-grouping parity, before you even try both.** If a string-crossing bassline groups notes in **evens** (twos or fours — e.g. octave‑fifth‑root played two-notes-per-string), strict alternating gives the simplest result, because an even note-group matches the even number of plucking fingers (two: index, middle). If it groups notes in **odds** (ones or threes), raking is usually the better call — raking is specifically what lets an odd-numbered group land cleanly on the two-finger rotation (a 1-note-per-string line raked gives you the option of all-index or all-middle; a 3-note group raked lets the same I‑M‑I pattern repeat every time instead of drifting). This is the single most transferable rule in the video — a fast heuristic to apply to a *new* bassline without having to work out both fingerings by hand first.
- **Mixed basslines need a chunk-by-chunk decision, not one global rule.** A line can require alternating in one bar and raking in the next (worked example: "Johnny, Kick a Hole in the Sky" below) — apply the parity hack per string-crossing chunk, not to the whole bassline at once. When more than one pattern works equally well for a chunk, there's no single "correct" answer — pick a favorite and stay consistent with it.
- **Case study — "Just What I Needed" (The Cars): even grouping → alternating wins.** Chugging eighth notes, originally picked but fine fingerstyle. Crossing A string → E string: strict alternating keeps I‑M‑I‑M through the crossing (no pattern change); raking reuses the middle finger on the crossing and flips the whole rest of the bar to M‑I‑M‑I (same flip repeats on the next A→E crossing). Verdict: alternating wins — raking here adds a finger-lead switch (more coordination) with **no efficiency gain**, since the crossing notes are evenly grouped. Named as the case where raking is *pointlessly* complex rather than helpful.
- **Case study — "My Friend of Misery" (Metallica): odd/mixed grouping → raking wins clearly.** Alternating the riff's second bar gives a straight I‑M‑I‑M‑I‑M‑I‑M with no pattern change, but raking gives I‑I‑I‑M‑M‑M‑I‑I — far fewer finger changes, which the video calls a big difference in how playable the line is. Raking also gives the riff its "flowing," ringing-notes feel; it's genuinely hard to play clean with strict alternating (the host had to practice the alternating version specifically to demo it, and was at half speed when he started). Bass line credited to current Metallica bassist Robert Trujillo (caption garbles the exact wording of the aside about him).
- **Case study — "Johnny, Kick a Hole in the Sky" (Red Hot Chili Peppers, *Mother's Milk*): a draw, split by bar.** Bar one, beats 1–2, first string crossing: notes are grouped in twos and the line is fast, so strict alternating (keep I‑M rocking, no change) beats raking (I‑M‑M‑I flips mid-bar, adds complication for no gain) — alternating wins this half. Bar two: notes come in **groups of three** — straight alternating all the way through feels clunky, but raking (I‑M‑I, then repeat I‑M‑I each time) lets the exact same three-note pattern repeat on every group, which simplifies the coordination — raking wins this half. Net verdict: a genuine draw, split within the same riff — the concrete proof that the parity hack should be applied per chunk, not per song.
- **The "big bad plucking trap": slow-tempo tolerance hides bad habits until speed exposes them.** At slow speeds almost any plucking pattern "works" — most simple basslines are even playable with a single finger — so sloppy alternating (a finger repeating on the same string when it shouldn't) or lazy raking (raking when you should be strictly alternating) goes unnoticed. Push the same habit to a faster tempo and it causes stumbling, and the problem is easy to miss in the moment because attention is usually locked on the fretting hand, not the plucking hand. **The fix is deliberately drilling clean alternating even on lines simple enough to fake your way through** — skipping that step at slow tempo is what causes stumbling later at speed. Same root-cause framing as [[Fix Your Plucking in 5 Minutes]]'s "indecision at the fork" diagnosis, from the tempo-tolerance angle instead of the decision-fork angle.
- **When each technique tends to win, as a general summary (beyond the parity hack):** strict alternating is "often simpler" because it removes plucking decisions entirely (just alternate through everything), but it can make a song *harder* when note grouping/string-crossing works against it (the Metallica case). Raking gives a flowing feel and is named as **essential** for funky rake patterns (**"Strut" by The Meters**) and **funky ghost notes** (**"Girls on Film" by Duran Duran**) — two named go-to reference tracks for when raking is the right call, not just a stylistic option.
- **Raking as a crutch — a stated pedagogical stance.** The host doesn't let students in his paid "Beginner to Badass" course rake early on (encourages avoiding it, doesn't police it outside class) because raking can mask/enable bad alternating technique rather than being used as a deliberate choice — echoes the plucking-trap warning above: build the disciplined default (alternating) before leaning on the shortcut (raking).
- **Myth-busting: "real bass players only strictly alternate" doesn't hold up.** Bass-forum wisdom cites Dave LaRue and Adam Nitti as never-rake examples — the video counters that Dave LaRue is shown raking in his own playing, and Adam Nitti's actual quoted position is "alternating is not better than raking, neither is raking better than alternating — there are two different technical disciplines that you should master." Also debunks "raking is inherently slower" by naming fast players who use it: Jeff Berlin, Mohini Dey, Billy Sheehan. **Takeaway for practice, not just trivia:** there is no universally "correct" technique to standardize on — most working bassists use both, chosen per line, which is the whole thesis the parity hack and the three case-study rounds are building toward.
