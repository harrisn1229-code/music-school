# Identifying Chord Progressions by Ear

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Transcribing and Playing by Ear]]. **A method for hearing a progression and naming it**, as opposed to hearing single intervals ([[Intervals]]) or single scale degrees ([[Scale Degree Ear Training]]).

## Why this feels impossible, and why that's not your fault

It is not one skill. It is **five skills stacked**, and attacking it head-on fails because you're trying to do all five simultaneously:

> *"People have a hard time learning chord progressions by ear because this skill is really a combination of other skills. If you're not solid on your music theory, intervals, cadences, scale degrees, and melodic dictation, you're already working at a major disadvantage."* — Joe Luegers

The practical consequence: **if a progression exercise feels hopeless, drop down a level.** Practise melodic dictation, or cadence identification, or scale-degree recognition on its own. Coming back up is much faster than grinding the composite skill.

---

## The five techniques, in the order you should reach for them

### 1. Dictate the bassline first — and this alone is often enough

Ignore the chords entirely. Ignore the melody. **Listen only to the lowest note** and work out its scale degree. Root-position chords put their root in the bass, so bass degree = chord.

This turns a hard harmonic problem into an easy melodic one — you're just doing melodic dictation on one line. It's the highest-yield technique by a distance, and it's worth using exclusively until it's automatic.

**Where it breaks:** inversions, where the bass isn't the root. In practice, popular music uses few inversions and the ones it does use are the same handful over and over — a first-inversion V under a descending bass, most commonly. → [[Chord Inversions]] · [[Chromatic Bass Lines]]

### 2. Hear major vs. minor

Only **three major and three minor** triads exist diatonically in a key:

- **Major:** I, IV, V
- **Minor:** ii, iii, vi

Getting the quality right immediately halves the candidates. Combined with technique 1, quality often confirms a bass degree you were unsure about.

### 3. Find the cadence at the end

Stop listening to individual chords and listen to the **relationship** between the last two. A V–I perfect cadence has an unmistakable tension-and-release; once you've identified it, you know where the progression lands and can reverse-engineer backwards from there.

Progressions are much easier to hear backwards than forwards, because the ending is the part your ear is most confident about. → [[Cadences]]

### 4. Listen for specific scale degrees in the texture

Each scale degree appears in only a few chords, so hearing one eliminates most of the options. In any major key:

| Scale degree | Appears in |
|---|---|
| 1 | I, IV, vi |
| 2 | ii, V |
| 3 | I, iii, vi |
| 4 | ii, IV |
| 5 | I, iii, V |
| 6 | IV, vi |
| 7 | iii, V |

*(vii° excluded — see the caveat below.)*

Worth memorising, and it's mechanical rather than musical: derive it once by writing out the triads and it's yours. Verify against [[Chords by Key]].

### 5. Build harmonic vocabulary

The slow one, and the one that actually decides whether the other four work.

> *"None of this really matters unless you know what chords are in what keys and have a few basic chord progressions memorized in every key."*

There is no shortcut. Knowing the diatonic set of every key cold is the prerequisite, not the reward — and it's the same thing [[Music Theory Workbook - Enrichment|Bowman]]'s 30 key-by-key lessons drill.

The source's own checklist for this step is in the vault as [[Ten Essential Progressions]] — ten progressions to get under the fingers in all twelve keys. **That is the actual homework behind this whole note**, and the other four techniques get much easier once it's done.

## The vii° caveat

The source drops vii° from consideration entirely: *"you're more likely to see fancy things like modal mixture than you are to ever see a diminished triad."*

That's a fair simplification for pop and it makes the tables above cleaner. Know that it **is** a simplification — vii° is real, it's common in classical and in jazz as a passing chord, and it does show up. For ear training on popular music, ignoring it is the right trade. → [[Diminished Chords]]

## How to practise it

A well-formed drill has this shape, and it's worth insisting on all four parts:

1. **Establish the key** — play a scale and a perfect cadence first. Without this your ear has no reference and you're guessing at absolute pitch instead of function.
2. **Repeat the progression several times** rather than once.
3. **Scaffold the bass** — boost it on the last repeats, so technique 1 is available while you're still learning it.
4. **Answer in Roman numerals first, letter names second.** Function is the skill; the letter names are transposition, which is a different one. Getting `I–vi–IV–V` right in the wrong key is a pass.

- **A beginner-level, explicitly-numbered restatement of techniques 1 and 2 above, combined into a single two-step by-ear process:** since most pop songs skip the diminished vii°, every key really has only **three major chords (I, IV, V)** and **three minor chords (ii, iii, vi)** — so on hearing an unknown chord, first decide major or minor by ear ("you can at least cut that in half to figure out which of the three chords it is"), then listen to the bass note and match it against scale-degree knowledge of the key to pick the winning candidate out of the three.
