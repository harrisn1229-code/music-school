# Ed Sheeran didn't copy Marvin Gaye... he copied a different soul song

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FcU0Rc5iXFg)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FcU0Rc5iXFg)

## Synopsis

Analysis of the "Thinking Out Loud" vs. "Let's Get It On" lawsuit, arguing the shared features are generic soul-style elements (not protectable), while offering Van Morrison's earlier "Crazy Love" as the more likely actual influence — its chorus melody is genuinely close, transposed into the same key. Uses this as a case study for the more important conceptual point: the difference between "shares a style" (not infringement) and "shares a melody" (potentially infringement), and how the *Blurred Lines* verdict blurred that line by ruling on vibe/style similarity rather than melody or chords.

## Techniques

- **Style vs. substance, defined precisely: a musical style is just a checklist of generic features, and two songs sharing a style/vibe isn't itself evidence of copying.** "Thinking Out Loud" and "Let's Get It On" share an anticipated rhythm, a similar (not identical) chord progression, melodies both starting on beat 2, and a "laid-back soul" feel — all features common across the soul genre broadly, not unique to either song. The video's proposed actual influence, Van Morrison's "Crazy Love" (released 3 years before "Let's Get It On"), shares the same anticipated-rhythm/chord-progression pattern too — but its chorus melody, transposed into the same key as "Thinking Out Loud"'s, is genuinely close, which the video treats as the real evidence of influence (as opposed to generic style-sharing).
- **Melody is the load-bearing distinguishing feature in practice, not chords/rhythm/vibe** — the video's own test case: a 12-bar blues vamp with identical chords/style/arrangement still distinguishes "Johnny B. Goode" from "Hound Dog" purely by melody. This is offered as the honest line between "same style, different song" and "actually copied."
- **The *Blurred Lines* precedent as a named cautionary case, argued to have shifted what counts as protectable**: Robin Thicke/Pharrell Williams were found to infringe Marvin Gaye's "Got to Give It Up" copyright not for melody or chord similarity, but for sharing a similar vibe/style/aesthetic — a precedent the video argues makes future "sounds vaguely similar" lawsuits easier to win, loosening copyright protection away from its stated purpose (protecting a composer's actual musical ideas) toward a tool for estates/rights-holders to extract money from any stylistically-adjacent hit.
