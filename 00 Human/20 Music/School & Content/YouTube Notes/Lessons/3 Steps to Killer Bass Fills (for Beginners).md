# 3 Steps to Killer Bass Fills (for Beginners)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KD-dNZXT3ug)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KD-dNZXT3ug)

## Synopsis

Josh (BassBuzz) tackles the "epic bass fill" panic-moment beginners hit — the urge to play something impressive at the end of a phrase with no idea what notes to use — by breaking fill-writing into a repeatable **three-step process: pick a rhythm, pick a shape, combine them.** The pitch throughout is deliberately anti-theory: you don't need to know scale degrees or even whether a chord is major or minor to build a solid fill, because the rhythm alone carries most of the "this sounds intentional" weight ("even if the notes you play are garbage, if you sell the rhythm it'll still kind of work"). The video introduces **two note-shapes for fills** — the already-documented root-fifth-octave shape (works blind, major or minor) and a **new-to-this-video three-note "triangle" shape** that requires knowing the chord's quality but rewards it with a distinctly Motown-flavored, more melodic fill. Both shapes get worked live over play-along tracks, first over a straightforward G–Em–D progression and then over a minor-key Gm–E♭–Cm–Gm progression, so the same formula is demonstrated on both a major-ending and a minor-ending phrase. The most valuable structural idea, stated almost in passing at the very end, is that fill-writing is as much about *restraint* as vocabulary: "sometimes there will be a big glorious space in the music for your awesome bass fill, sometimes there might be another fill in the way" — knowing when NOT to fill is named as the next-level skill once the mechanics here are solid.

## What's taught

**The core problem framing (0:00–1:00)**
- A fill is just "some cool transition notes," not a shredding moment — reframing it this way is itself the first anti-panic move.
- **Rhythm carries a fill more than note choice does.** Even wrong notes read as intentional if the rhythm is confident and in the pocket; this is why the 3-step process below puts rhythm first, not last.

**Fill placement — the three rhythm questions to answer before picking any notes**
1. **Where does the fill go?** Fills typically sit at the **end of a musical unit** — the end of a 4-bar phrase, or the end of a section (e.g. verse → chorus). → this is the same "lead-in" placement logic as chromatic walk-ups/walk-downs on [[Bass Walkup and Walkdown Fills]], just at a larger (phrase-level) time scale rather than a single beat-level lead-in.
2. **How much space does the fill get?** Fills typically run **2 beats or 4 beats**. This video sticks to 2-beat fills throughout (the two worked play-alongs both carve out a 2-beat space at the end of a 4-bar phrase).
3. **What subdivision fills that space?** Starter options, easiest first: **two quarter notes**, **eighth-eighth-quarter**, or **four eighth notes**. Demoed live over a looped 2-bar phrase, all three feels back to back on the same notes so the rhythmic difference is audible in isolation from any note-choice variable.

**Leveling up the rhythm — steal from the drums**
- For a more complex, higher-intensity fill rhythm once the basics feel comfortable: **imagine what a drum fill in that spot would sound like, then copy that rhythm onto the bass** and plug in notes from your chosen shape. Demoed with an "80s-style" drum-fill rhythm sung/scatted, then played on bass both with the shape's "correct" notes and with a different note selection on the identical rhythm — proving the rhythm is what's doing the work, not the specific pitches (same "sell the rhythm" point restated with a worked A/B).
- *(general starting point, not stated as a hard rule: the more elaborate the borrowed drum-fill rhythm, the more the fill will "pop" — a direct intensity dial.)*

## Techniques

### Shape 1 — Root-fifth-octave (works on ANY chord, major or minor)
- The video's recommended default when you're not sure whether the chord under the fill is major or minor — the shape is quality-blind, so it's "pretty foolproof." Full theory/derivation of this shape already lives on [[Jamerson's Root-Fifth-Octave Exercise]] (ingredient 1) and [[Root-Fifth Bass Lines and the 3-Step Style Rhythm Formula]] (the plain root-fifth acoustic case); this video's contribution is specifically *how to use it as a fill*, not new theory about the shape itself.
- **Finding the root for a fill:** identify the chord the fill leads OUT of / resolves toward — typically the **last chord of the phrase**, since that's the chord the fill is decorating on its way into the next section.
- **Worked example — G major (1 bar) → E minor (1 bar) → D major (2 bars) progression, 2-beat fill at the phrase end, four-eighth-note rhythm.** The fill leads into/sits on the D chord (last chord in the phrase), so **root = D**; shape = D (root), A (fifth), D (octave). Two orderings tried live over the play-along track: **root–fifth–octave–fifth** (D-A-D-A), and **octave–fifth–root–root** (D-A-D-D, octave down to root-root). Explicit takeaway: **you don't have to use every note of the shape, or use it in a fixed order** — both orderings use only 3 distinct pitches across 4 rhythm slots, repeating notes freely.
- → [[BASS Slot]] · [[Chords]] § *Triads* (root-fifth-octave is a stripped-down triad skeleton, missing the third by design so it stays quality-neutral).

### Shape 2 — The major/minor "triangle" shape (NEW — not previously documented in this vault)
A three-note shape usable on both major and minor chords, but **repositioned differently for each** — unlike root-fifth-octave, this one requires already knowing (or guessing correctly) the chord's quality, and in exchange gives a more melodic, "Motown-y" color suited to rock, blues, pop, and funk as well as soul.

- **On a major chord:** the **root sits at the top** — the highest-pitched note of the three-note shape — with **two "flavor" notes below it**, drawn from the major scale of that root. Find the root, then "stick the rest of the triangle on below."
- **On a minor chord:** same physical three-note triangle shape, but the **root moves to the middle note at the far end of the triangle** rather than the top — with one flavor note below and one above, drawn from the **minor scale** of that root. Named explicitly as giving "nice, safe minor sounds."
- **Theory-optional framing:** you can "skip the theory and just memorize the shape" — knowing the flavor notes are major/minor-scale tones is offered as color commentary, not a prerequisite for playing it.
- **Worked example — major triangle, same G–Em–D progression as above, four-eighth-note rhythm.** Phrase ends on D major → root D at the top of the triangle, flavor notes below drawn from the D major scale. First fill tried: **D–D–A–B** (root, root, then the two major-scale flavor notes) — *(confidence note: the immediate playback readback in the transcript is garbled ("D ba D") where the spoken setup line clearly states "D D a B" — treat the D-D-A-B sequence as the reliable read and the playback line as unreadable audio, not a contradicting data point).*
- **Worked example — minor triangle, new progression: G minor (1 bar) → E♭ major (1 bar) → C minor (1 bar) → back to G minor (1 bar), fill on beat count matching the earlier four-eighth-note rhythm.** Last chord = G minor → **root = G**, minor triangle positioning: root at the far-end-middle, flavor notes from the **G minor scale** — one **below** the root (**F**, the ♭7) and one **above** (**B♭**, the ♭3). First fill tried: **F–G–B♭–F** (flavor-below, root, flavor-above, flavor-below) — a genuine partial-triad/7th-flavor melodic move rather than a straight scale run. → [[Chords]] § *Triads*, [[Seventh Chords]] (the ♭3/♭7 pair here is functionally a rootless minor-7 fragment).
- **After the first pass, more choices open up:** change the note order, or borrow a more complex rhythm (see "leveling up the rhythm" above) to intensify the same shape — the video explicitly frames the worked fills as starting points to vary, not fixed answers.

### Step 3 — Combine rhythm + shape (the actual "fill-writing" move)
1. Pick your rhythm (above).
2. Pick your shape — root-fifth-octave (quality-unknown/safe default) or the triangle (quality-known, more melodic).
3. **Assign shape-notes to rhythm-slots and just try stuff.** You don't need every note of the shape, don't need to use it in order, and real-time experimentation over the track is explicitly recommended over pre-planning it on paper ("if doing it in real time is too hard or overwhelming" is the only reason given to plan ahead first) — but planning ahead is offered as a fallback for anyone who finds live improvising too much pressure.

### Knowing when NOT to fill
Stated as the next-level skill once the mechanics above are solid, and deliberately left as a closing thought rather than expanded on: **"finesse is knowing when to fill and when not to fill — sometimes there will be a big glorious space in the music for your awesome bass fill, sometimes there might be another fill in the way."** No formula given for this judgment call — it's named as a listening skill to develop by ear (leave a gap for another instrument's fill, don't stack two fills in the same space), not a rule to apply mechanically.
