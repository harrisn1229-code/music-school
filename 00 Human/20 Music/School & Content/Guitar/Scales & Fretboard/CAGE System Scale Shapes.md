# CAGE System Scale Shapes

Part of [[Guitar Positions]]. The CAGE system finds one chord in five places on the neck using the five open chord shapes **C – A – G – E – D** as movable/barred grips (e.g. a C chord fingered as a C-shape, then as an A-shape barre, then a G-shape barre, then an E-shape barre, then a D-shape barre, walking up the neck). This page is the **scale layer on top of that chord map**: instead of using the five CAGE grips to outline chord tones for fast-moving changes (that's [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]'s job), it uses them as **fixed anchor points for a pentatonic scale and a major scale**, so a soloist can navigate the whole fretboard over one static key/chord rather than memorizing five disconnected scale boxes with no relationship to each other.

## Finding the pentatonic scale from a CAGE root
**Relative-minor-pentatonic shortcut:** the major pentatonic scale over a chord is identical in pitch content to the **minor pentatonic scale starting 3 frets below the major root on the low E string** — e.g. C major pentatonic = A minor pentatonic (same five notes), so C major pentatonic can be played by finding the A minor pentatonic box shape 3 frets down from the C root. This is the standard relative-minor relationship, stated here as a fast on-the-fly way to locate the shape rather than counting scale degrees.

## Technique 1 — map the pentatonic scale onto all five CAGE positions
For a given chord (worked example: C), find the **pentatonic scale shape that sits at each of the five CAGE root positions** (C-shape, A-shape, G-shape, E-shape, D-shape) — five pentatonic box shapes, each anchored to a different CAGE grip, covering the full neck. **Practice sequence:** drill each of the five shapes individually first, then start soloing within one position at a time, then work on **connecting the five positions into one continuous line** rather than treating them as isolated boxes — a solo built from stringing the five CAGE-anchored pentatonic positions together end to end. *(general starting point: work through the shapes slowly at first — the demonstrated C-shape and A-shape positions are described as "a little bit tricky" — before soloing over them.)*

## Technique 2 — overlay the major scale on the same five positions
The same five CAGE roots also generate a **major scale shape at each position** (not just pentatonic) — the major pentatonic covers roughly **80% of the chord progressions** a player will solo over day to day, so it's the default; the full major scale gets layered in on top **for extra color/flavor** rather than as the primary vocabulary. Each of the five major-scale shapes is shown as a **one-octave shape starting from that position's root** — extend up a second octave by ear, following the same string-set logic, rather than memorizing a separate two-octave fingering. Combined, the pentatonic-plus-major-scale-in-all-five-CAGE-positions vocabulary is framed as covering **~90% of the songs** a player will ever solo over.

## Technique 3 — borrow rhythm from a familiar song melody (the "rut" fix)
**A phrasing drill, not a note-choice drill — for when the pitch vocabulary above is solid but the lines still don't sound musical.** The stated diagnosis: memorizing the pentatonic and major-scale shapes doesn't by itself produce musical phrasing — that comes from **rhythm**, which most players under-practice relative to note choice. The fix: pick a **famous song melody** (his worked example: "…Baby One More Time" by Britney Spears), strip away its actual pitches, and **replay only its rhythm using whichever scale notes you already have under your fingers** (the CAGE-position pentatonic/major-scale shapes above) — same rhythmic contour as the borrowed melody, different pitch content pulled from the position being drilled. This decouples rhythmic vocabulary from pitch vocabulary so a player can practice phrasing independent of relearning notes, and gives an instant supply of practice material (any melody you can hum) rather than needing to compose or transcribe original rhythms. Framed explicitly as the single most valuable tip in the lesson — "stick around to the end."

- **3-note-per-string symmetrical shapes double as all 7 modes for free, and are the shred/sweep-friendly counterpart to CAGED's 2-note-per-string arpeggios.** Extending a chord to its color tones (worked example: Am11 = A-C-E-G-B-D) and voicing it 3 notes per string produces 7 symmetrical shapes across the neck, one per scale degree — each shape IS that degree's mode (2nd shape of C major = D Dorian, 3rd = E Phrygian, 4th = F Lydian, 5th = G Mixolydian, 6th = A Aeolian, 7th = B Locrian). *Pros:* naturally suited to fast, symmetrical shredding; better for sweep picking than CAGED's 2-note-per-string arpeggios (see [[Sweep Picking Arpeggio Shapes]]); gives all 7 mode shapes vs. CAGED's 5, so it's the stronger system specifically for learning modes. *Cons:* players who memorize the shapes by rote (very common among shredders) skip the underlying theory and don't connect chords/scales/arpeggios; some fingerings repeat a note on the same string, which can sound "off" if played carelessly — start with non-repeating shapes. → BERNTH, [[6 Life-Changing Guitar Music Theory HACKS!]]
- **Build each scale-form position incrementally from an arpeggio instead of memorizing it as a standalone shape: root-3-5 triad → add the 7th (7th arpeggio) → add the 2nd/4th/6th (full 7-note scale/mode).** Worked across every degree of C major: count up 3 and 5 notes for each degree's triad (three usable root-first fingerings per triad, one per starting finger — index, middle, pinky — so a position change never requires the same finger every time), count up 7 for the matching 7th arpeggio, then add the remaining 2nd/4th/6th to complete that position's full scale shape. Since each position is built FROM an arpeggio, the same seven positions double as the seven modes (Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, Aeolian, Locrian off degrees 2-7) — but now understood via their underlying arpeggio rather than as abstract mode names. Stated goal: keep "seeing" the arpeggio inside the scale shape while playing it, so switching between scalar and arpeggiated/sweep-picked lines mid-solo becomes fluid. → [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]] — BERNTH, [[You're Probably Visualizing The Fretboard WRONG!]]
- **CAGED vs. 3-notes-per-string (3nps) vs. interval-based visualization — a pros/cons comparison, landing on "learn both CAGED and 3nps, they solve different problems."** CAGED pros: natural bridge from chord shapes to scales/arpeggios, positions stay in the same C-A-G-E-D order in every key so they're easy to remember relative to each other, forces real-note thinking (not just dot-diagram memorization) which pays off as theory understanding, produces more musical improvising. CAGED cons: shapes mix 3-note-per-string and 2-note-per-string fingerings within one position (slower for pure speed), resulting arpeggio shapes can be awkward for sweep picking, and only 5 patterns total vs. the 7 needed to map all 7 modes cleanly — under-serves mode study. **3nps** (symmetrical 3-note-per-string shapes, 7 positions instead of 5, and those 7 positions ARE the 7 modes — e.g. in C major the D-rooted shape is D Dorian, the G-rooted shape is G Mixolydian, etc.): pros are speed/shred-friendliness and being the best available tool for internalizing modes since it's a complete 7-shape set; cons are that shredders using it commonly skip the underlying theory and just run shapes, and some fingerings repeat a note within a string-set (avoid that at first — prefer non-repeating fingerings). **Interval-based** (apply the scale's formula — major = 1-2-3-4-5-6-7, natural minor = 1-2-♭3-4-5-♭6-♭7 — directly to any root instead of recalling a shape): framed as a later-stage upgrade once shape-based systems get too slow for fast, complex chord-change soloing, where there's no time to reference a shape or think through accidentals. → [[Intervals]], [[Diatonic Modes]]. **His CAGED drill order:** at each of the five positions, chord shape → basic arpeggio → major pentatonic → full major scale, in that sequence, before moving to the next position. — BERNTH, [[The BEST System For Amazing Guitar SOLOS! - **There is no objectively "better" fretboard-visualization system — CAGE, pentatonic shapes, or anything else — only whichever one you already know well enough to reference fast.** Answering "should I always visualize the major scale instead of pentatonic shapes," the position taken: a visualization system's only job is helping you navigate, so there's no right/wrong choice — players default to whatever they learned first (pentatonic scales for single-note players, the **CAGE** open-chord shapes for chord players, which is specifically why the CAGE system exists as a reference at all), and everything learned afterward naturally attaches back to that first reference point (e.g. "that's usually where the 13th sits, and this is shifted, so that's the ♭13"). Only switch references if the current one is causing a genuine processing hiccup or extra mental steps — not because a "more correct" system exists.
