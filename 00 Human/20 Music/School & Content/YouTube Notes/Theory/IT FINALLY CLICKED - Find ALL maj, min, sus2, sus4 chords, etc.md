## Synopsis

A from-scratch chord-construction lesson: every chord type as a scale-degree recipe applied to the major scale — major (1-3-5), minor (1-♭3-5), sus2 (1-2-5), sus4 (1-4-5), augmented (1-3-♯5), diminished (1-♭3-♭5), major 7 (1-3-5-7), dominant 7 (1-3-5-♭7), minor 7 (1-♭3-5-♭7), plus guide tones (3rd and 7th determine chord quality) and extensions (9ths built by writing the scale out twice, staying natural unless a chord symbol says otherwise). All beginner-level and thorough, but ground the vault's existing theory notes already cover in comparable or greater depth.

## Techniques

- **Every chord type as a scale-degree formula off the major scale**, worked concretely across F, G, D, and A: major = 1-3-5; minor = flatten the 3rd; sus2 = 1-2-5 (replaces the 3rd with the 2nd); sus4 = 1-4-5 (replaces the 3rd with the 4th); augmented = sharpen the 5th; diminished = flatten both the 3rd and 5th of a minor triad; major 7 = add the scale's 7th degree; dominant 7 = flatten that 7th; minor 7 = flatten both the 3rd and 7th.
- **A chord's specific voicing (which strings, which octave, how many doublings) doesn't matter as long as the correct notes are present** — demonstrated repeatedly (an "F chord" using 4 strings is still a 3-note triad because one note repeats; a 6-string G major chord is still a triad for the same reason).
- **Guide tones explicitly named: the 3rd and 7th determine chord quality; extensions (9ths, etc.) stay natural by default** and only get altered when a chord symbol explicitly specifies (e.g. G7♯5, D7♭9) — building a 9th chord means adding the scale's 2nd degree an octave up, not automatically flattening or sharpening it.
- **Enharmonic edge case flagged**: a G♭ minor chord requires a "B double-flat" rather than being respelled as A natural, since chord-tone spelling follows the formula's letter-name logic strictly rather than defaulting to the simplest enharmonic spelling.
