# Wes Montgomery Blues Lesson | D Natural Blues

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lmG0VnudIN0)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lmG0VnudIN0)

## Synopsis
Jack Ruch transcribes and breaks down a short stretch of Wes Montgomery's guitar solo on **"D Natural Blues"** (from the 1960 album *The Incredible Jazz Guitar of Wes Montgomery*), applying the same chunk-analyze-template method he lays out in full on [[How I Approach Practicing]] — but this time the vehicle is a real jazz guitar icon's playing rather than his own transcription-derived variation. He isolates four short passages, all falling over the tune's I chord (D7) or IV chord (G7), and names the harmonic device behind each: a plain D7 (Mixolydian) scale into chord-tone targeting on the IV, a chromatic walk-down into a D7♭9 arpeggio, a whole-half (tonic) diminished scale used as a connector back from IV to I, a C major 7 arpeggio played over D7 for its upper extensions followed by a D minor pentatonic blues cadence into the IV, and a v-minor (A minor 7) arpeggio over D7 decorated with chromatic notes. After naming each device he immediately plays his own line built from the same "recipe" — the identical concept-not-lick framing from [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]]. Short (7 minutes) and dense — almost every sentence maps to a concrete, reusable arpeggio/scale choice over one of two chords, making it a compact companion piece to the longer Delilah breakdown.

## What's taught
- **Tune and source:** "D Natural Blues," Wes Montgomery, from *The Incredible Jazz Guitar of Wes Montgomery* (1960), key of D. Ruch restricts the whole lesson to material falling over the **I chord (D7)** and **IV chord (G7)** of the jazz-blues progression — points to his separate "jazz blues" lesson ([[The Key To Understanding A Jazz Blues]]) for the full-progression breakdown. (~0:00–0:40)
- **Passage 1 — D7 scale into IV-chord targeting.** Wes opens playing out of a **D7 scale** (D major scale with a ♭7, i.e. D Mixolydian — the note C is the altered tone). As the harmony moves to the IV chord (G7) he **targets chord tones of G7 directly on the change**: 3rd → root → ♭7, landing squarely on the new chord instead of continuing to run the old scale through the change. → [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]], [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] (~0:40–1:10)
- **Passage 1 continued — chromatic walk-down + D7♭9 arpeggio.** Back over D7, Wes plays a short **chromatic walk-down from the 5th to the 3rd** of D7 (A down to F♯, filled in chromatically), then moves into a **D7♭9 arpeggio** (root-3rd-5th-♭7-♭9), then targets G7 chord tones again as the chord changes. Ruch singles this combination out — "what really makes this solo special is this little chromatic walk down to the third, followed by that D7♭9 arpeggio" — as the one worth stealing outright. → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]], [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]] (~1:10–2:10)
- **Ruch's own line, built from Passage 1's recipe:** D7 scale → chromatic walk-down → D7♭9 arpeggio → target a G7 chord tone on the change — same four-step recipe, new notes, played immediately after naming it. Direct demonstration of the [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]] method's step 5 (use the concept set as a template for an original line). (~2:10–2:35)
- **Passage 2 — G7 chord tones + chromatic passing tones, then a diminished-scale connector back to I.** Over the G7 (IV) chord, Wes outlines chord tones decorated with chromatic passing tones, then plays the **D tonic diminished scale — the whole-half (half-whole, in Ruch's naming "whole-half") diminished scale, starting on C♯** — used specifically as connective material to bring the line **from G7 back to D7** across the chord change, not as a static "correct scale for G7." → [[Scale Modes Reference]] § Whole Tone / Diminished, [[Diminished Chords]] (~2:35–3:20)
- **Ruch's own line, built from Passage 2's recipe:** G7 arpeggio + chromatic notes, then the tonic diminished scale to bridge G7 back into D7. (~3:20–3:35)
- **Passage 3 — C major 7 arpeggio over D7 for upper extensions, then D minor pentatonic as a bluesy cadence into the IV.** Wes opens the next 12 bars with a line straight out of a **C major 7 arpeggio played over the D7 chord** — C major 7 gives the notes C-E-G-B, which against a D7/D-major-triad reads as **♭7-9-11-13**, i.e. the chord's full stack of upper extensions from a single familiar arpeggio shape a whole step below the root. Wes follows it with **D minor pentatonic**, played as "a bluesy little end to this phrase that brings you into the four chord" — swapping to the tonic-minor-pentatonic blues sound specifically as a cadential gesture into the IV chord, not as an outlined-changes line. → [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]], [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]], [[Extended Chords]] (~3:35–4:25)
- **Ruch's own line, built from Passage 3's recipe:** C major 7 arpeggio over D7, then D minor pentatonic, landing on the G7 change. (~4:25–4:35)
- **Passage 4 — A minor 7 (v-minor) arpeggio over D7 with chromatic decoration.** The final line uses **A minor 7 — the "five minor," i.e. a minor 7 arpeggio built on the 5th of D7** — played straight over the D7 chord, giving the extended sound of the **5th, ♭7th, 9th, and 11th** from a single minor-7 shape (Ruch's phrasing implies but doesn't fully complete the extension list on-camera before moving into the demonstration). Wes adds chromatic notes into the line to tie it together. → [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]], [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] (~4:35–5:10)
- **Ruch's own line, built from Passage 4's recipe:** A minor 7 arpeggio over D7 plus chromatic connective notes. (~5:10–5:30)
- **Closing recommendation:** listen to the original Wes Montgomery recording directly, and use this transcribe-analyze-build-your-own-line approach generally to build personal vocabulary rather than memorizing licks verbatim — same closing message as [[How I Approach Practicing]]. → [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]] (~5:30–7:14)
