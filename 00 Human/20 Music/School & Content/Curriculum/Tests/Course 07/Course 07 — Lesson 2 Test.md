> **Q2.** **To break the resolution so the ear expects bar 1 again — it usually lands on V**

> **Q3.** **A7 – F♯7 – B7 – E7**
> The alternative is a chromatic line descending over an A pedal.
>
> **Q4.** **It stops instead of circling**
> That's the difference between twelve bars and a loop — the turnaround is what makes the form repeat rather than end.
>
> **Q5.** **AAB — bars 1–4 state a line, 5–8 repeat it, 9–12 answer it**
>
> **Q6.** **The repeat is what makes the answer land — it sets the expectation the B phrase pays off**
> Hearing the same line twice builds the pressure that the third phrase releases. Cut it and the answer has nothing to answer.
>
> **Q7.** **The vocal states a phrase and an instrument answers in the gaps**
> The space the form leaves is where the response lives.
>
> **Q8.** **Through sampling — soul and funk records built on blues harmony got sampled**
> So the ♭3/♭7 vocabulary and the call-and-response phrasing survive in the loops even where the 12-bar form doesn't.
>
> **Q9.** **The 12-bar form and the pentatonic vocabulary, almost wholesale**
> Early Beatles and Stones material is blues form with different production.
>
> **Q10.** **Because the gaps are structural — they're where the response goes**
> *Rubric (5):* says the form is call and response (1) · says the space after each call is where the answering phrase belongs (2) · says filling it removes the dialogue, leaving statements with no reply (2)
> The 12-bar isn't a chord loop that happens to have room in it. It's a conversation with the turns built into the bar count: each four-bar phrase states something and then leaves space, and that space is the other half of the exchange. An arrangement that fills every bar isn't adding density to a neutral structure — it's talking over the reply. What's left is three statements in a row and no dialogue, which is why it sounds busy and static at the same time.
