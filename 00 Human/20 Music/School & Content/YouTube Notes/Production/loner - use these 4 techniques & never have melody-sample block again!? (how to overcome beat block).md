# loner - use these 4 techniques & never have melody/sample block again!? (how to overcome beat block)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V3YjbP0vOkw)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V3YjbP0vOkw)

## Synopsis

A short, focused workflow video (no full sample build shown start-to-finish) where loner lays out four repeatable habits for breaking out of beat/sample block, each demonstrated live rather than just described. **Technique 1** is chord-jacking with intent: pull a two-chord loop from an existing song (PARTY NEXT DOOR & Drake's "Come and See Me," Em7–Dm9), then deliberately transform it — transpose it into a different key, re-invert the voicing, and humanize the timing — so it stops being a copy and becomes new material. **Technique 2** is a personal sample library ("stack/stash") of previously bounced elements (counter-melodies, accents, perc loops, textures), organized by category and labeled by tempo/key, dragged back onto new samples instead of always building from nothing — demonstrated live by pulling a Rhodes texture into the F# minor progression from technique 1 and running a short processing chain on both the new texture and the original piano part. **Technique 3** argues for actually learning piano — specifically, learning ONE scale (his is C minor) really well and using a synth's root-note remap control to play familiar C-minor shapes while the instrument sounds in whatever the sample's real key is, which he says is faster and more musical than programming MIDI by hand. **Technique 4** is a beat-block-specific move: bounce out a counter-melody from the stash and drop it into a brand-new, otherwise-empty project as a seed, then freestyle a fresh chord progression around it — proven live by reusing the exact same counter-melody and sounds from technique 2/3 to build a completely different-feeling C-minor sample in under a minute. Light on hard numbers (auto-captioned narration, fast demo pace) but every technique is a concrete, repeatable habit rather than a vague pep talk — this is a companion/reinforcement of loner's "personal sample library" and "beat-block starter" habits already logged elsewhere on [[Sampling and Sound Selection]] from other videos in his catalog, this time with the clearest, most explicit walkthrough of *how* he builds and organizes the stash folder itself.

## Techniques

**1. Chord-jacking: borrow a progression, then make it unrecognizable**
- Pick a simple, learnable progression from an existing song you like rather than starting from a blank piano roll — here, PARTY NEXT DOOR & Drake's "Come and See Me" (Em7–Dm9, a two-chord loop in A minor/all-white-keys). Play/record the chords in as-is first as raw material, not as the final part.
- **Transpose it into a different key first** — pitched down 3 semitones here (4 also suggested as an option) — moving out of an all-white-keys scale into a scale with a few black-key accidentals reads as noticeably darker/less recognizable than the source, and this single move does most of the work of making a borrowed progression stop sounding like the original. → [[Chord Progressions]], [[Keys]]
- **Re-invert the voicing** — the borrowed chords start in root position (no inversion); bring the two middle notes of each chord up an octave for a wider frequency spread, then bring it back down an octave if the result reads too thin/high-pitched. → [[Chord Inversions]]
- **Humanize the rhythm and density last** — push some of the chord notes back slightly in time so the part reads more like a strummed/played performance than quantized block chords, then add a few extra passing notes once the voicing and timing feel right. *(general starting point — no exact ms/velocity given, auto-caption transcript)* → [[Pocket and Microtiming]]
- Net effect: same two-chord DNA as the reference song, but a different key, a different voicing, and a looser feel — described as "great for getting ideas started," the actual output becomes the seed the rest of the video's techniques build on. → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]], [[Reharmonization]]

**2. The "stack/stash": a personal library of your own bounced elements**
- Build a dedicated sample folder (mirrored inside the DAW's own browser) organized into categories — his are Drums, Effects, Piano, Roads, Strings, Synths, Vocals — and every time a sample gets a piece worth keeping (a counter melody, an accent, a perc loop, a texture), bounce that stem out and file it into the matching category. **Label each saved file with its tempo and key** so it can be found and pitch/tempo-matched fast later, rather than auditioning blind. Stated explicitly as a slow-build habit ("I only got two in here... I'm building on it every time I make a sample") — the payoff compounds over many samples, not from one big library-building session.
- **Worked example — dropping a stash element onto a new progression:** pulled a Rhodes texture from the Synths folder into the F# minor progression from technique 1, tempo-matched it to 80 BPM, then reversed it and pitched it down an octave before printing it into a mixer channel — reverse + octave-down on a texture element straight out of the stash, not further composed, is enough to read as "a new layer" instantly. → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]], [[TEXTURE Slot]]
- **Processing the pulled-in texture:** a heavy bandpass EQ pushed hard enough to sound "super drowned out" — a deliberate near-total narrow-band carve rather than a gentle tonal EQ move, keeping the texture as a background wash under the main elements. *(general starting point: a narrow bandpass centered in the mids, pushed hard, is the qualitative description given — no Hz/Q numbers stated)* → [[EQ]], [[BED Slot]]
- **Separately, the original piano part got its own short chain:** a Waves J37 tape stage first (owned-lane equivalent already established elsewhere in this vault: [[Kramer Tape]]), then EQ cutting some low end, then a compressor (captioned "Evolve Compressor" — name unclear/likely garbled auto-transcription, no plugin confidently identified) purely to tighten the part up. *(general starting point — no exact settings given for any of the three stages)* → [[Kramer Tape]], [[EQ]], [[Compression]]
- **Habit: always fade a texture layer's volume all the way down/in by hand** — stated specifically to avoid an unwanted vinyl-crackle-style artifact bleeding through audibly at the start/end of a texture layer, a manual gain-riding step rather than a plugin fix. → [[Automation]], [[Ambient Texture and Field Recording]]

**3. Learn ONE scale on piano deeply, then use a root-remap trick to "play" any key**
- Stated directly: learning piano — even just partially — made samples more consistent to build and the process more hands-on/enjoyable than clicking MIDI notes in by hand. Recommendation: pick and internalize **one scale** (his is C minor) well enough to run up and down the full keyboard comfortably in it and freely play counter-melodies, rather than trying to learn every scale/chord.
- **The root-remap trick for playing in an unfamiliar key using only the familiar scale shape:** on the loaded instrument (Arturia Analog Lab here), open its Settings panel and right-click the keyboard's root-note field to change it — set to F# for an F# minor sample — so playing the familiar C-minor shapes on the physical keys actually SOUNDS in F# minor. This means never having to actually know or shape-shift into F# minor; the muscle memory of C minor carries over completely. *(Analog Lab isn't owned — see routing below; the underlying idea is general enough to check for on any owned instrument's own settings/utility panel, or to bake in after the fact via a clip's Transpose control once a part is recorded.)* → [[Melody Writing]], [[Scales]]
- **Why this beats programming MIDI by hand:** playing live and reacting in real time ("just kind of freestyling and seeing what happens") is faster and reads as more musical than the click-in/delete/retry loop of hand-programming notes — framed as functionally similar to clicking MIDI in (neither is "real" theory knowledge of the target key) but far less tedious and quicker to iterate. → [[Melody Writing]]

**4. The beat-block starter: seed a new, empty project with an old counter-melody**
- When staring at a blank project (beat block), don't insist on starting from total silence — bounce out an existing counter-melody, texture, or drum element from the stash folder and drop it straight into the new, otherwise-empty FLP as a starting seed, then build reactively around it. Explicitly framed as a repeatable habit, not a one-off trick, and a deliberate reversal of a self-imposed "always start from scratch, no additional extras" rule he used to follow.
- **Worked example, live:** bounced the counter-melody built in technique 3 into the stash (folder: Synths → "beat block ATF minor"), pitched it down to C minor in a brand-new project, and freestyled a completely different chord progression around it — landing on a chord progression that feels nothing like the F# minor sample it came from, despite reusing the exact same counter-melody and sound patches. Demonstrates the whole loop end-to-end: chord-jack (technique 1) → stash it (technique 2) → seed a blank project with it (technique 4) in well under a minute of screen time. → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- Conceptually the same "seed a blank session from an existing piece of material" idea behind this vault's own [[Song Seeds Index]] / [[Sample Seed Pool]] system — same underlying logic (a fragment beats a blank page), applied here to the producer's own past work rather than a library one-shot.

**5. The counter-melody's finishing chain (reverb → mono delay → hi-cut → cassette)**
- After the counter-melody from technique 3 was written, it got a short, deliberately-ordered 4-stage finishing chain rather than a single effect: **reverb first**, then **delay** (kept **single/mono** rather than stereo, time set to **1/8**, **feedback pushed up**, the delay's own **wow & flutter pulled down**, its internal **EQ pulled down**, and overall **mix pushed up** for a heavy/wet result), then a **high-frequency cut** (captioned "good hertz low high" — likely a garbled auto-caption for a generic hi/lo filter move rather than a named plugin; described qualitatively as cutting off some high end), and finally a **cassette-style crunch/distortion stage** "to make it a bit more crunchy and distorted" — the same finishing-crunch role [[Cassette]] plays repeatedly elsewhere in this vault's loner-video coverage. → [[Delay]], [[Reverb]], [[Cassette]], [[Saturation and Distortion]]
