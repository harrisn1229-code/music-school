## Synopsis
Part 2 of a two-part jazz-guitar improvisation lesson from Jared Borkowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared), teaching a drilled method for **improvising freely over a tune that changes keys often** — worked start-to-finish over the Miles Davis standard "Solar," which packs five different key centers into a 12-bar form. Part 1 (not yet in the vault) sets up the prerequisites: a service-level pass on the melody, determining which scale covers each chord/zone, mapping all those scales as close together as possible in one fretboard position, and drilling each scale alone to fluency. This video is where it becomes musical: a fixed set of practice constraints ("rules of the game") that turn "just noodle over the changes" into a pass/fail drill, then six numbered exercises (5 through 10, continuing Part 1's numbering) escalating from out-of-time counted eighth notes, to a real backing track in time, to free phrasing, to soloing each key zone in isolation, to fully free improvising over the whole form, and finally back to the tune's actual melody — which the video claims reads far more clearly once every note in it can be placed against a specific, just-drilled scale/key-zone. Two bonus tips close it out: never memorize a fixed note-path through the drills (the single biggest failure mode he sees from private students), and a baby-step variant that runs the same exercise progression on just two strings for anyone finding the full six-string version too hard. The teaching is clean, systematic, and thoroughly practice-oriented — heavy on concrete constraints and named concepts ("money note," "getting caught in a rut") rather than abstract theory, and the video is explicit that the underlying method (not the tune) is the actual point: any tune with a known chord-scale map works.

## What's taught

### Prerequisites carried over from Part 1
1. **Melody first, at a "service level"** — enough to recognize and hum it, not a full transcription. Revisited at the end (Exercise 10) once the scale work is done.
2. **Determine the scale for each chord/zone of the tune.** On Solar: 2 measures **C melodic minor** → 4 measures **F major** → 3 measures **E♭ major** → 2 measures **D♭ major** → 1 measure **C harmonic minor** (12 bars, then repeats). Some zones have more than one workable scale option; this is one workable path, not the only one. → [[🎵 MILES DAVIS - Solar]]
3. **Map every zone's scale as close together as possible on the fretboard**, ideally in one position, so a key change means finding the *nearest* correct note rather than jumping to a memorized shape elsewhere on the neck. On Solar, four of the five zones sit in the same position; only D♭ major needs a small shift.
4. **Play each scale up and down in time, flawlessly, 10 times in a row, one scale at a time** — a mastery gate before combining them.
5. **Play the scales back-to-back in the tune's actual zone order** (still out of time) to start hearing the progression's shape.

### The rules of the game
A fixed constraint set for the drilling, so practice has a clear pass/fail instead of just vibes:
1. **Constant notes, no rests, no phrasing** — but durations can mix (quarter/eighth/triplet/sixteenth), an evolution from an older, stricter "eighth notes only" version he found less musical.
2. **Move by step only** (whole or half step) — both within a scale and across a key-change boundary. No leaps.
3. **Continuous direction** — keep going one way until hitting the edge of the mapped shape; only then turn around.
4. **Optional half-step-back exception** — if the closest correct note at a key change is a half step behind the direction of travel, stepping back that half step (then resuming direction) is explicitly allowed because it sounds good.
5. **Don't look at the chord chart**, even an app that highlights the current chord (he uses iReal Pro). Watch your hands, not the changes.
6. **On a mistake:** stop, restart the backing track, and begin again on a **different note** than last time.
7. **Grace notes and mordents allowed** as pure ornament (a chromatic slide or fast neighbor-tone alternation), as long as the chromatic note has no rhythmic value of its own.
8. **Mastery bar: 10 clean passes through the whole tune in a row**, any mistake resets the count. Called "the hardest exercise in this whole thing but 100% worth it" — can take weeks to months.

**Avoid "getting caught in a rut":** landing back on the exact same note you started on when the form repeats means you've drifted into a memorized path instead of the live decision the drill is training. Fix by switching the rhythm or starting note the moment it happens. Called out twice — once as a rule, once as Bonus Tip 1 — as the most common failure mode from his private students, who drill hard enough to accidentally memorize "one giant lick" instead of the actual skill.

### Exercises 5–10
- **5 — Out-of-time eighth notes.** Steady counted eighths ("1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and…") but free tempo (pause/slow/speed up allowed). Count out the right number of beats per zone, and **deliberately pause at each key change** to consciously solve "what's the closest correct note in the new scale, same direction." That pause is the actual target, not the running scale. Introduces the **"money note"** — the note right at a key change that is *not* shared between outgoing and incoming scale, which "sounds amazing" when landed on (not every key-change note is one; sometimes the connecting note is common to both scales). Demonstrated live including hitting his own rut (landing on the same starting note again) and deliberately restarting on a different note per the rule above.
- **6 — In time, over a backing track, any tempo.** Same rules, played to tempo — demoed at 120 BPM via iReal Pro, looped 10 times so there's no counting needed and rule 5 (don't look at the chart) can actually be followed. Described as "a beast" — the hard jump from solved-out-of-time to solved-live-at-tempo.
- **7 — "Try this": free phrasing.** Same accuracy requirement, but any rhythm/phrasing now allowed instead of constant notes — this is the moment "audibly running scales" turns into "sounding like music." Lighter, no 10-in-a-row requirement.
- **8 — Solo over each zone in isolation, looped.** Loop one key zone alone and make sure it sounds musical entirely on its own before worrying about connecting it to the next. Points to a separate 12-video series (his own) on improvising over specific chord types as the deeper resource for this per-chord vocabulary.
- **9 — "You're doing it": free playing over the whole form.** Any phrasing, any note order — the only constraint is correct scale at the correct time. Framed as the arrival point: real improvising over the tune's changes, built entirely from the constrained drills above.
- **10 — Revisit the melody.** Play the actual tune melody again in the same mapped scale position. Demonstrated live on Solar: every melody note now reads as clearly coming from a specific, just-drilled scale/key-zone, which (per the video) cements the melody in memory and improves phrasing and singing it.

### Bonus tips
- **Bonus 1 — never memorize a fixed note path** (restated from the rut warning above, called out as the single most common trap he sees in private students — memorize the *decision process*, not a sequence of notes, or you're not practicing improvisation).
- **Bonus 2 — baby-step entry via two strings.** If the full six-string version is too hard to see/hear cleanly, restrict the *entire* exercise progression (up/down scales, out-of-time eighths, in-time, free phrasing…) to two strings at a time — the middle two strings (G and D) are the recommended starting pair — or even one string. Full skill transfer, much lower physical difficulty; skip it if six strings already feels fine.
