# Etude-Based Vocabulary Practice

Part of [[Song Practice]]'s practice-methodology material — a four-part method for absorbing vocabulary from a **written comping/soloing etude book** (Barry Galbraith's *Comping Etudes* is the source here, but the method generalizes to any notated jazz etude collection), distinct from [[Internalizing a Tune Through Repetition]] (which is about learning a *song*, not a composed practice etude) and a cousin of [[Backward Chaining Practice]] and [[Hub-Latching Approach]] (other named practice-order methodologies in this strand).

## 1. Treat the etude like a transcription — play first, deconstruct after
Don't read the chord symbols. **Play the etude precisely and memorize it entirely** before doing any harmonic analysis — treat it as "a comprehensive comping solo," the same discipline applied to soloing transcriptions. Only after it's under the fingers and memorized do you go back and pick it apart: analyze the voicings, the rhythms, the overall feel. Same play-before-analyze sequencing already documented on [[Internalizing a Tune Through Repetition]] (steps 1-2 there: play the tune off the page before noticing/naming what's happening in it) — this is the etude-specific version of that same ordering principle.

## 2. Play along with the recording, not just the page
A method book with audio (Galbraith's comes with recordings of Galbraith himself comping the etudes alongside bassist Milt Hinton) is a resource beyond the notation — **play along with the recording until you're completely locked in with the rhythm section.** The stated payoff is feel and time, not just correct notes: hearing (and playing against) two masters at work teaches pocket and rhythmic confidence that reading the page alone can't. Don't underestimate this step or treat it as optional polish.

## 3. One etude at a time, worked for months — patience over pace
**Only work on one etude at a time.** Dedicate real time to it — described as often *months* per etude in the source's own practice — absorbing its nuances daily until the etude's vocabulary genuinely becomes "what professional jazz comping feels and sounds like," not just something you can play through once. Explicitly **not a race**: the framing is deep assimilation over coverage. This is a patience/depth instruction, not a pacing suggestion — resist the urge to move to the next etude before the current one is fully internalized.

## 4. Drill rhythmic precision with a rhythm app, not a plain metronome
Practice the etude against a dedicated rhythm/groove app (named tools: **Drum Genius, Pro Metronome**) rather than a bare click — these let you isolate and reinforce the specific rhythmic concepts the etude is built on (e.g. [[Jazz Comping — Rhythmic Motif and Walking Bassline]]'s Charleston-rhythm placements), sharpening timing precision so the comping "feels solid and grooving" rather than merely rhythmically correct on paper.

## Why this order
Steps 1-2 build the etude into the hands and ears (play it right, then play it *with* real time-feel from the recording) before step 3 asks for sustained months-long depth on that material — front-loading correctness and feel before committing the long-haul repetition, the same "get it right before you drill it in" logic underlying [[Backward Chaining Practice]]'s clean-takes-before-expanding rule. Step 4's rhythm-app work can run alongside any of the other three steps rather than as a separate final stage — it's a precision tool applied throughout, not a capstone.
