# Make Your Rhythm Guitar Sound Like A Record

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n26TGOjkS6E)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n26TGOjkS6E)

## Synopsis

Jack Ruch's third note in this vault ([[YouTube Notes Index|29 videos]]) — despite the title reading like a mixing/production breakdown, this is an **arranging lesson, not a signal-chain one**: no gear, mics, or plugins are discussed at all. Over a simple four-chord backing track (A major–E major–F♯ minor–D major, a I–V–vi–IV progression in A), Ruch builds up **three separate rhythm-guitar parts layered on top of each other**, and the whole video is a demonstration of how a small set of closed-position **triad shapes on three adjacent strings** — reused across a handful of neck positions and string groupings — generates all three parts without ever needing a different chord vocabulary. The throughline connecting this to [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]] (Ruch's other triad-zone video already in the vault): both lessons treat triad shapes as a small, memorizable set of physical "landmarks" that get relocated around the neck for different purposes — soloing there, multi-part comping/arranging here. The most transferable idea for a home recordist layering guitar parts: **each layer gets deliberately separated by pickup selection and effect, not by writing genuinely different material** — same triads, different tone, so the parts sit in their own space when stacked rather than needing separation to be built afterward in the mix.

## Techniques

- **Song setup:** a simple bed/backing track first, built from triad shapes on the **D, G, and B strings**, one position at a time, over **A major, E major, F♯ minor, D major** (I–V–vi–IV in A). This bed is the harmonic foundation every subsequent layer reuses.
- **Part 1 — the bed/arpeggiated part:** simple triads on the D-G-B string set, arpeggiated rather than strummed. **Tone: middle pickup position (both pickups engaged) + a light tremolo effect from the pedalboard** — Ruch states this pairing "works really well for a sort of arpeggiated chord part." For the verse section, where the arrangement drops dynamically, the *same four triads* are played from a different position further up the neck — same shapes, same chords, relocated, giving the verse a different color without writing a new part. Two positions total for this one part, jumped between by section.
- **Part 2 — R&B backbeat accents:** triads on the **top three strings**, played as rhythmic accents landing on the backbeat (the classic 2-and-4 emphasis). Busier/denser in the earlier section, deliberately thinned out ("space it out") as the arrangement drops dynamically later — a rhythmic-density arc used as its own dynamic-contrast tool, independent of what the other parts are doing. **Tone: switch to the bridge pickup, tremolo off** — "that's going to give our second part its own sonic space," stated explicitly as the reason for the pickup swap.
  - **Voicings used (all derived from common full-chord shapes, played on just the top three strings):** A major = an open-D-major-chord shape moved up the neck, root on the **10th fret of the B string**; E major = the top three strings of the familiar E-shape bar chord; F♯ minor = the top three strings of the F♯m bar chord; D major = the top three strings of the D-shape bar chord. The pattern: instead of inventing new triad shapes, take the top slice of chord shapes already known as full bar chords.
  - **Section variation:** the following section reuses the identical four chords but drops to a different, lower position on the neck for a different flavor — again, same harmony, different physical location, deliberately varied rather than repeating the exact same voicing throughout the song.
- **Part 3 — melodic double-stop line (Curtis Mayfield / Jimi Hendrix R&B double-stop style):** a melody, still built from the same triad shapes, played on the **neck pickup** — "to give it its own sonic space" (the same explicit reasoning as Part 2's pickup swap, applied a third time).
  - **Double-stop device #1 — add the 6th above the triad:** starting from the triad's 3rd-5th-root, add the 6th of the chord by moving from the **5th fret up to the 7th fret on the B string** (the chord's 5th moving up to its 6th/13th) — a melodic ornament layered on top of a held triad shape rather than a new shape.
  - **Double-stop device #2 — drop the 3rd to the 9th:** from the same root-3rd-5th shape, drop the **3rd down a whole step to the 9th (2nd)** — a second, contrasting color move off the same triad grip. Both devices are named as "a really common double-stop move to use in this style."
  - Applied across the progression: the A-major double-stop line above, then the **same top-part-of-the-triad shapes from Part 2** revisited for E major and F♯ minor, then a **D major triad on the A, D, and G strings at frets 5-4-3**.
  - **Later variation, top two strings only:** simpler double stops built with **hammer-ons within the A major triad shape itself** (hammering into a note of the same triad, no new pitch introduced from outside it), then the same device carried to E major (a slide into the equivalent top-two-string shape), then F♯ minor and D major from the shapes already established in Parts 1–2. A second full pass through the form using this leaner two-string version as one more textural variant.
- **The generalizable lesson (stated directly):** a full-band triad vocabulary spans **four different string groupings** (top three strings in two different chord-family derivations, D-G-B, and the top two strings alone) and **three different neck positions** for the same handful of chords — and any of those shape/position combinations can be freely recombined. Small, closed triad shapes ("these little morsels... these little pieces of music") are specifically valuable for **layering electric guitar parts because they don't take up much space or fill up much sound** — deliberately lean voicings, not full chords, are what makes stacking three guitar parts work without the arrangement turning to mud.
- **Backing-track/practice-material logistics (video-page-only, not a reusable technique):** tabs, notation, and multiple mix versions of the backing track (drums+bass+pad only, then with guitar parts layered in) are provided via Ruch's Patreon — external resource, not something to route further into the vault. Ruch also plugs his "Magic of Triads" course for a deeper dive into the same shape/position system.
