# Open Triads in solos - Spread Triad Guitar Lesson

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FkXA0Xeto48)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FkXA0Xeto48)

## Synopsis

A jazz-guitar lesson from Jens Larsen on using **open (spread) voice triads** — root-5th-3rd triad
shapes stretched across a wide interval with a deliberate string skip, as opposed to close-position
triads where the three notes sit on adjacent strings — as **soloing/improvising** material, the
companion piece to an earlier Larsen lesson (not in the vault) on using the same shapes for comping.
Larsen frames the whole lesson as two layers: first a pair of **technical exercises** to get the
picking hand comfortable with the large interval jumps and string-skipping the voicing demands (inversions
of a single triad, then a full diatonic run of root-position open triads up the neck), then a **theory
section** on which triads are actually usable over each chord of a jazz **251** (worked in the key of
F: Gm7 → Gb7/C7 altered → Fmaj7), and finally **four fully worked example lines** over that same 251
demonstrating the triads in melodic context, mixed freely with arpeggios, sus sounds, pentatonic runs,
and diminished-scale material rather than used in isolation. The triad-selection logic Larsen walks
through — pull a triad straight out of a seventh chord's own 3-5-7 stack, or the stack plus an added
9th/11th/♯11 — is the **same four-rule system** already documented on the vault's [[Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords]] page (from a different Jens Larsen video), so this lesson mostly functions
as a live, in-context re-application of that existing theory, in a different physical voicing (spread
instead of close-position), plus a genuinely new addition: pulling extra triads out of the **diminished
scale** a minor 3rd away from the chord's own triads, over the dominant chord specifically. Auto-captions
on the fast worked-example section are somewhat garbled (fast spoken run-throughs while playing), so
those four lines are captured qualitatively (which devices, in what order) rather than as exact
note-for-note transcriptions — flagged inline.

## What's taught

- **Core concept — "open" triads are *spread*, not close-position.** Same root-3rd-5th (or inverted)
  triad content as a normal triad, but voiced across a **wide interval with a deliberate string skip**
  — described as "huge, string-skipping, large-interval" shapes. The payoff for improvising specifically:
  grouping large melodic leaps into a triad (a strong harmonic unit) makes a wide-interval line easier
  to make sound intentional and melodically strong, rather than just leaping around. *(00:00–01:00
  approx.)*
- **Exercise 1 — major and minor triads through their inversions, worked in the open voicing.** Worked
  on **B♭ major** (the key's IV chord, key of F) and **G minor** (the key's ii chord) through root
  position, 1st inversion, 2nd inversion. Two callouts while drilling: (1) each open triad has **one
  note per string plus exactly one string skip** somewhere — the defining physical trait of the voicing,
  and the main technical challenge; (2) the **same three notes have more than one playable open-voice
  fingering** — Larsen demonstrates two different ways to finger the identical B♭ major triad, notes
  it's worth learning both since one will sometimes suit a given melodic context better than the other.
- **Exercise 2 — root-position open triads harmonized diatonically up the whole neck.** Since the
  lesson's progression is a 251 in F, Larsen harmonizes the **F major scale** in root-position open
  triads and runs the resulting seven triads consecutively all the way up the neck, **keeping the same
  spread/string-skip configuration the whole way** (root, string-skip up to the 5th, up to the 3rd)
  rather than re-fingering per position — the point is a fretboard overview of where the triads sit, not
  positional tidiness. Notes this is hard to confine to one neck position by nature, and that other
  configurations (starting from an inversion instead of root position) are worth trying too, just not
  covered here.
- **Which triads are usable over each chord of a 251 — the theory section, done "fast" because it's
  already covered in Larsen's own triads lesson and his lesson on superimposing diatonic arpeggios (both
  referenced but not linked/named further in this video).** Progression: **Gm7 → Gb7 (equivalently, C7
  altered via tritone substitution — same notes, different name) → Fmaj7.**
  - **Gm7 (ii):** root-position Gm7 already contains a **G minor triad** and (reading the top three
    notes, 3-5-7) a **B♭ major triad** — the chord's own upper-structure triad. Add the **9th** (A) for
    a **D minor triad**. Add the **11th** (C) instead and the resulting stack reads as an **F major
    triad** (the tonic itself, usable as color over the ii chord).
  - **Gb7 / C7 altered (V, reached via the tritone-substitution trick from Larsen's other lessons —
    play/think the dominant as the chord a tritone away):** root-position Gb7's 3-5-♭7 reads as a **B♭
    diminished triad**. Add the **9th** (A♭) for a **D♭ minor triad**. Add the **♯11** (C) for an **E
    augmented triad** — the chord's altered-extension color.
  - **Fmaj7 (I):** **F major** (the chord itself), **A minor**, and **C major** all usable directly —
    Larsen frames the whole method as "find a triad already sitting inside the chord's own stack of
    thirds, then try adding one or two extra notes and listen to how each addition sounds."
  - **Extra dominant-chord source — triads pulled from the diminished scale a minor 3rd away, used in
    at least two of the four example lines.** Larsen doesn't derive the full diminished scale here
    (says he covers that elsewhere), just uses the practical shortcut that the scale's symmetric major
    and minor triads move in minor 3rds — over the C7 in this lesson he specifically names **E♭** (a
    minor 3rd up) and **A** (a minor 3rd down) as extra usable triads over the dominant, on top of the
    four chord-derived triads above.
- **Four worked example lines over the same 251** *(auto-captions garbled here — devices/order captured
  qualitatively, not exact pitches)*:
  1. **B♭ major open-voice triad** to open → runs down a **G minor** line → an **E augmented / C
     augmented triad** (same symmetric triad, either name works) over the dominant → a **Gb7 arpeggio
     in an inversion** → resolves into Fmaj7 with a shape built a **stacked 4th above the 9th** → moves
     into what Larsen calls an **"A sus" triad**, a shape he specifically likes for major-7 color
     because it voices the chord's **3rd, 7th, and 13th** together — "a rich sounding major 7 sound."
  2. A **D minor 7 arpeggio** with two notes encircling the 3rd (an enclosure-style approach into the
     chord tone), resolving, then running down the scale → **Gb major open-voice triads** into a
     **D♭/C♯ minor triad** → on the Fmaj7, resolves to the **9th** via a **G sus** sound, then a
     **three-note-per-string pentatonic run**. Stated intent for this line: cover a **wide overall
     range**, starting low and ending high (with the ending landing on a D more by coincidence than
     design).
  3. **G minor 7 arpeggio**, an **inversion of a D minor 7 arpeggio** → on C7, a **B♭ triad** moving
     into an **E♭ major triad** → encircling the **9th of Fmaj7** → on the Fmaj7 itself, a **D sus
     ("D7sus4, up an octave")**-flavored line. Called out as one of the examples using the
     **octatonic/diminished sound**.
  4. Opens with a **"minor comp pattern"** (Larsen's own phrase — essentially a G minor idea) → an **F
     major triad** resolving its **11th down to the 3rd** (chosen because that resolution works better
     melodically than resolving up) → on C7, the **diminished sound again**: an **E diminished triad**
     into an **A major triad reinterpreted as A minor** → on Fmaj7, resolves first to the **major 7th**,
     then continues up to the **9th**.
- **Practice philosophy, stated explicitly at the end.** (1) **Blend, don't isolate** — mix open triads
  into everything else being played (scales, arpeggios, other devices) rather than treating them as a
  separate mode to switch in and out of, "so it sounds more natural" and isn't something you can
  visibly "turn on and off." (2) **Write your own lines, don't just replay the examples** — playing
  Larsen's worked lines is fine as a start, but the real learning is composing original short lines over
  the same progression and testing them by ear ("does this sound good to me, does it work"); doesn't
  need to be written down, just played and evaluated.
- **Resources mentioned:** a companion lesson (not in the vault) on using open triads for **chords**
  rather than solos; a separate lesson on **triads** generally and one on **superimposing diatonic
  arpeggios**, both referenced as covering the triad-selection theory in more depth; a downloadable **PDF
  of the lesson's music examples** on Larsen's own website (jenslarsen.nl), gated behind his newsletter.
