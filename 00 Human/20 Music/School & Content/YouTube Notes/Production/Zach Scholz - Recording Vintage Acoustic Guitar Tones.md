# Zach Scholz - Recording Vintage Acoustic Guitar Tones

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CQuuXtnyZy8)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CQuuXtnyZy8)

## Synopsis
Zach Scholz chases one specific reference point — the "sweet," "old-school" acoustic guitar tones of 1970s records — across an eleven-minute session built around a 12-string and a 6-string tracked together. He opens with a gear-agnostic philosophy (the tone comes from the playing, not a $1,000 mic) and finds mic placement by ear rather than by formula, landing a Warm Audio WA-87 R2 about 45° off-axis and roughly 10 inches from the end of the fretboard. The centerpiece is a two-stage vintage-character signal path: first a deliberately "wonky" pre-EQ (a wide mid scoop plus a low-shelf and a high-shelf boost) that exists specifically to provoke more reaction out of an in-the-box tape-saturation plugin (Drive/Wear/Noise/high-pass character) that follows it, and then — as a separate, later, more consequential decision — a print through his own physical reel-to-reel tape machine, which he frames as genuine commitment rather than another undo-able plugin pass. He also demonstrates a vintage varispeed trick (tracking deliberately fast, then dropping tempo and pitch together afterward via Flex Time) and, on a second full example, uses match-EQ discipline to keep the tape stage from overwriting a pre-tape mix balance he already liked. He's explicit throughout that this is a deliberate, extreme character choice for period-correct material — not a default acoustic guitar recipe.

## Techniques
- **Reference target set explicitly up front: "sweet," 1970s-era acoustic tone** — states the whole session is chasing that era's character before touching a mic; pulls two instruments for it, a 12-string and a 6-string, tracked together (his 6-string is the role Nate's own [[Yamaha FG730S]] fills — no 12-string in Nate's own gear).
- **A real reel-to-reel tape machine is visible and earmarked from the very start of the session** — model not shown/named on camera, but it's the same print-out-and-back workflow as Nate's own [[Sony TC-630]] loop, not an afterthought bolted on at mixdown.
- **Explicit gear philosophy: "most of that is going to come from up here" (the playing), not the mic** — direct advice against buying an expensive mic chasing one record's sound; perfect the part on what you already own first, then explore gear.
- **Mic: Warm Audio WA-87 R2**, a U87-style large-diaphragm cardioid condenser — same mic class/role as Nate's own [[Neumann TLM 102]], the closest owned equivalent for this exact job.
- **Finds placement by ear, physically moving the mic while playing the actual part, rather than starting from a measured formula** — settles only once a spot sounds right for that specific guitar/room/part.
- **Settled placement: ~45° off-axis from the end of the fretboard, about 10 inches out** — lands squarely inside the vault's own `[[1 Acoustic Guitar Track|Acoustic Guitar Track]]` chain, which specs [[Neumann TLM 102]] at 12th–14th fret, 8–12", ~45° toward the neck/body joint — direct validation of that mic spot on [[Yamaha FG730S]]-style guitars.
- **Vintage pitch-drop capture trick: record the take deliberately a little fast, then slow the whole thing down afterward** — "something they would do a lot" in the reference era; tracked the 12-string + 6-string together at 94 BPM.
- **Executed in Logic's Flex Time using Speed FX mode specifically, not Polyphonic** — Speed FX links pitch and tempo together the way a real tape-speed change would, dialed back by ear to roughly 88 BPM (~6 BPM down, matching his own on-the-fly "5 or 6 BPM" estimate) — exactly the move Nate's own [[Varispeed 3.0]] exists to do live (its Speed knob: "down for a pitched-down drag"), just executed here after tracking instead of at record time.
- **Mix layering: 12-string set as the prominent guitar** — mixed louder and panned closer to center than the 6-string, decided before any tape processing.
- **Light, unspecified compression on both guitar channels pre-bus, then the two channels bussed together** — no ratio/threshold given, just "a little bit of compression." → [[Compression]]
- **Deliberately "wonky"-sounding pre-tape EQ: a broad scoop across roughly 200 Hz–5 kHz plus a separate low-shelf boost and high-shelf boost** (no dB or exact corner frequencies stated beyond that range) — sounds thin and odd soloed by design; hits [[BED Slot]] and [[MIDS Slot]] on the cut, [[AIR Slot]] on the high-shelf boost, and only makes sense once the next (tape) stage is engaged.
- **States the specific reason for the low-end shelf: it's there on purpose to drive more distortion out of the tape stage that follows** — his word is "strategic" — pushing low end into a saturation stage deliberately to provoke a bigger reaction is the transferable lesson, independent of his exact numbers.
- **In-the-box tape-saturation plugin stage runs before the real tape print** — captions render the name as "tape Mofi" / "tap mow file"; the control set described (Drive, Wear, Noise, a high-pass filter) lines up closely with [Baby Audio TAIP](https://babyaud.io/taip-plugin), an "AI tape saturator" literally designed to be pronounced "tape" — plausible ID, not confirmed on screen.
- **High-pass filter on that plugin stage: singled out as a favorite, engaged deliberately** — no frequency given, just that he "really likes" its effect here.
- **Drive control: hots-up the incoming signal so the stage distorts more** — audibly still driving the signal even with its on/off toggle reading "off"; demonstrated by ear by flipping it on/off, no numeric amount stated.
- **Wear control: toggled on/off to demonstrate; audibly bakes in some noise/hiss, kept on purpose** — "sometimes I like to bake noise into my sound" — a deliberate lo-fi noise-floor choice, not a flaw to filter out. → [[TEXTURE Slot]]
- **A "DVE" function is named alongside Wear/Drive as something he likes at this stage, but the transcript never explains what it actually does** — flagged rather than guessed at; likely a caption-garbled term.
- **The physical tape print is a separate, later, more consequential step — not the same move as the plugin stage** — "I think we are ready for tape" comes only after the plugin pass is dialed in; result described as "crunchy," "smooth," adding "polish," and specifically praised for how it compresses the signal — direct validation that the optional tape-print step in `[[1 Acoustic Guitar Track|Acoustic Guitar Track]]` §2 (through the [[Sony TC-630]] loop) is a real, distinct finishing stage worth doing even after ITB tape modeling, not a redundant extra.
- **"Tape is commitment"** — his explicit framing for why the outboard tape pass matters: unlike ITB mixing's endless tweakability, once a part is printed through tape and back, the decision is locked — the reasoning behind treating a [[Sony TC-630]] pass as a late, deliberate step rather than an early exploratory one.
- **Practical warning: tape returns don't always sync back up sample-accurately** — had to manually cut and nudge the returned tape recording to re-align it with the rest of the track — a concrete operational gotcha that extends the [[Sony TC-630]] note's existing "print it, don't monitor live" guidance: budget time to realign after every return.
- **On his favorite result from the session, auditioned several different input balances/levels feeding the tape before settling on one** — no specific levels given, just that multiple balances were tried.
- **Used a "match EQ" process afterward to pull the tape-returned signal back toward the pre-tape mix's tonal balance on purpose** — the pre-tape mix was already good and he didn't want the tape stage to drastically change it; tool unnamed in the transcript, but this is exactly the job Nate's own [[Ozone 9 Match EQ]] does (currently documented there as a mastering-stage tool — this is a legitimate earlier-stage/track-level use of the same idea).
- **Describes the transformation as going from "boring, characterless" straight to something that "could be straight out of the '70s"** — flags it himself as "a little extreme" with "a lot of stuff going on," a deliberate heavy-processing choice he's happy with, not an accident.
- **Confirms slide-guitar parts from the same session were also printed to tape, not just the chordal/strummed guitars** — the tape treatment isn't limited to one part-type within a song.
- **Frames the plugin pass and the physical tape print as two necessary, non-redundant stages rather than belt-and-suspenders overkill** — his own analogy: the plugin stage is like shooting flat S-Log3 footage, and the physical tape print is the color grade that turns it into the finished look.
- **Explicitly not a universal default: "not the guitar tone for every mix"** — a deliberate character choice for period-correct material, reached for on purpose rather than applied to every acoustic part; closing philosophy is that once a chain like this is understood, "every sound becomes a tool in your toolbox," down to a cheap battery-powered toy synth.
