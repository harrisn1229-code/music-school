## Synopsis

### The core thesis: formulas over tune-count
- **Reframe practice from "how many tunes do I know" to "how many formulas do I recognize"** — nearly every jazz standard is built from a short list of recurring harmonic devices, and once those are internalized a new/unfamiliar tune stops looking arbitrary. Explicitly not an argument against learning songs ("you absolutely should learn songs — that's how you actually get out and play music with other musicians") — the formulas are what make learning *each individual song* fast, not a replacement for repertoire.

### Formula 1 — the 5–1 (V7–I)
- **The 5–1 (V7→I) is named as the single most powerful harmonic movement in jazz, creating the tension-and-release engine of a song**, and it works in **two distinct ways** that both need to be recognized on a chart: (1) as the **actual V7 of the key** resolving to the **actual I** of the key, and (2) as a **temporary/secondary dominant** — a V7 chord pulling toward some chord *other than* the tonic. → [[Secondary Dominants]]
- **Worked secondary-dominant examples in C major:** **V7/ii** = A7 → Dm (the five-seven "of the two chord"); **V7/IV** = C7 → F (the five-seven "of the four chord"). Standard V7/x naming, confirmed independently from a fourth source in the vault.
- **A guitar-specific shortcut for instantly finding the V7 of any chord: a power-chord shape on the low two strings.** Fret the shape so the **bottom note is the root of the chord you're starting from**; the **top note of that same power-chord shape is its V** (a perfect 5th up). Worked example: to find the secondary dominant of Am (the vi of C), fret the power chord with A on the bottom — the top note is E, so **E7 is V7/vi in the key of C**, i.e. "5-7 of 6." Framed explicitly as a fast, no-math way to find any chord's dominant on the fly, live, without stopping to count scale degrees. → [[The ii-V-I]] § *Guitar shortcut: the power-chord finder* (full writeup + the reverse-direction use, below)

### Formula 2 — the 2–5 (ii–V)
- **The 2–5 has the same real/secondary duality as the 5–1.** As the real progression: in C major, ii = Dm, V = G7, resolving to I = C. As a secondary 2–5: instead of a bare secondary dominant ("5 of 4"), the full unit can be fronted by its own ii ("2-5 of 4") — this secondary motion is described as happening "all the time" in real charts.
- **When a 2–5 resolves to a minor chord, the ii becomes minor 7 flat 5 (half-diminished)** rather than plain minor 7 — matches [[The ii-V-I]]'s existing minor-key derivation (Dm7♭5→G7♭9→Cm7 in C minor) from a fourth independent source.
- **The same power-chord shape, read the opposite direction, instantly finds the related ii of any dominant 7th chord.** This time think of the **bottom note as the dominant (V7) chord's root**, and the **top note as the root of its related ii**. Worked example: C7's related ii is **Gm7** (bottom note C, top note G — the ii-V pair Gm7→C7, which resolves to Fmaj7). Same physical shape as Formula 1's trick, just relabeled — one shape, two lookups (chord→its V, or dominant→its related ii). → [[The ii-V-I]] § *Guitar shortcut: the power-chord finder*
- **Two named rhythmic forms of a 2–5, worth having vocabulary for:** the **short 2–5** — both the ii and the V share a single one-measure/four-beat bar, usually splitting it in half (two beats each); and the **long 2–5** — each chord gets its own full four-beat measure, doubling the time available to develop a line over each chord. Matches and extends [[Turnarounds]]'s existing two-bar/one-bar compression section with the named "short/long 2-5" terminology used on real charts.
- **Turnarounds are framed here as two 2–5's stacked.** In C: a **2-5 of 2** (i.e. a secondary ii–V targeting the ii chord — Em7→A7 targeting Dm7) moving into the **normal 2-5** (Dm7→G7) back to I. Either half can be played short or long independently. ### Formula 3 — the backdoor dominant
- **Definition: a dominant 7th chord built on the flat-7 of the key, resolving to I** — in C, that's **B♭7 → Cmaj7**. Named "backdoor" because it's a harmonically different route from V to I than the front-door 5–1.
- **It's almost always paired with its own ii, making a backdoor 2–5.** Using the same power-chord-shape trick (bottom note = the dominant's root, B♭; top note = its related ii's root, F), B♭7's related ii is **Fm7** — so the backdoor 2–5 in C is **Fm7 → B♭7 → Cmaj7**. Matches and gives the derivation for [[Turnarounds]]'s existing **Backdoor** row (`Cmaj7 Am7 Fm7 B♭7`).
- **Why it trips players up:** normal 5–1 or 2–5 vocabulary doesn't sit right over a backdoor progression, because the harmonic relationship (root movement, guide-tone resolution) is different from a real dominant cadence — players who reach for their usual V7→I licks here get a wrong-sounding result.
- **The soloing fix Borton says he's found: play off the ii chord of the backdoor 2–5, and resolve that same material down a half step onto the *iii* chord of the key instead of thinking about the I chord at all.** Concretely: play a line/arpeggio over **Fm7**, then repeat that exact idea a half step down over **Em7** — never explicitly targeting Cmaj7. This works because **Em7 functions as a tonic substitute for Cmaj7** (Em7 = E-G-B-D, all but one note shared with Cmaj7 = C-E-G-B), so resolving Fm7's line down a half step onto Em7 reads as landing on "the one chord" even though the chord actually being played is the iii. Two stated variants: play the literal Fm7/Em7 arpeggios back-to-back, or compose a melodic idea over Fm7 and repeat the identical idea over Em7 unmodified. → [[Turnarounds]] § *From videos*, [[The ii-V-I]] § *When the I doesn't arrive* (tonic-substitute framing already documented there for other cases)

### Formula 4 — the sharp-4 walkdown
- **Not a single formula but a chain reaction of resolving 2–5's, combining the major and minor 2–5 patterns already covered.** Starts on the **♯IV degree of the key as a minor 7 flat 5 chord** — in C, that's **F♯m7♭5**. From there: a **minor 2–5 down** from F♯m7♭5 (i.e. F♯m7♭5 → B7) resolving to the **iii chord** (Em7), followed by a **turnaround** (Formula 2's stacked-2-5 device) back to I. Typically every 2–5 in the chain is a **short** 2–5.
- **The practical soloing method: treat the whole chain as a string of individual 2–5's, not one long, intimidating progression, and recycle vocabulary already built for Formulas 1–2 over each link.** Three stated approaches, usable individually or mixed within one chorus:
  1. **Copy-paste 5–1 ideas over every single 2–5 in the chain, deliberately ignoring the ii chord of each unit** — think purely "5, 1, 5, 1, 5, 1…" straight through the walkdown.
  2. **Mix and match different 5–1 licks link to link** for variety, rather than repeating the same one.
  3. **Play minor 2–5 vocabulary over the first unit specifically** (F♯m7♭5→B7 resolving to Em7 is a genuine minor ii–V–i), then shift to major 5–1/2–5 language for the rest of the turnaround that follows.
  - General framing, stated directly: "thinking about these as individual two-fives rather than one long scary progression is the common and most practical way musicians approach this sharp-four walkdown" — the same chunking principle already documented from a different source on [[Chord Progressions]] ("break a long, non-diatonic progression into small chunks... drill each chunk in isolation, then connect them").
