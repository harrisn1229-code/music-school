# Depth and Space

**What it is:** placing elements front-to-back (depth) and giving the record a coherent acoustic world (space). Width gets all the attention; depth is what makes a mix three-dimensional.

## The four depth cues (how the ear judges distance)
1. **Level** — closer = louder. The fader is a depth knob.
2. **High-frequency content** — air absorbs highs; darker = further. LPF a repeat/element and it *recedes* ("your phone bouncing away down the street"). This is why returns are always filtered duller than their source.
3. **Transient sharpness** — close sounds snap. Transient attack up = toward your face; down = backed off. A [[Transient Master]] is a depth knob that doesn't touch the fader.
4. **Wet/dry ratio + pre-delay** — more reverb relative to dry = further away. Long pre-delay (20–40 ms) keeps a wet element *close* (big room, singer near you); zero pre-delay pushes it into the wall.

## Building the depth field (this template)
- **Front:** lead vocal, kick, snare — dry-ish, bright, transient-intact, slap delay rather than reverb.
- **Middle:** guitars, keys, backups — room send, moderate filtering.
- **Back:** pads, harmonies, textures — darker EQ, more send, softened transients.
- One **shared room** ([[Reverb Send Room]]) = everyone in the same world; the **long tail** ([[Reverb Send Long]]) is the "big moment" space, ducked under the dry vocal so size never costs intelligibility.

## Space discipline
- Returns run 100% wet, band-limited (HPF 200–300, LPF 4–8 kHz) — space without spectral cost ([[Masking and Carving]]).
- Delay before reverb (echoes then enter the room, like reality).
- Low end gets no reverb, no widening, and lives in mono <100–120 Hz.
- Contrast is the tool: a dry verse makes a wet chorus enormous. Automate sends per section instead of finding one static setting.

- **Let elements "stick out" instead of gluing everything into one sonic world — some parts intentionally too loud, others buried, as a confidence move rather than a mix flaw.** Explicit reference: Lindsey Buckingham's *Tusk*, cited for mixing home-studio and expensive-studio elements in obvious contrast, wild panning, and sudden mono-to-stereo drops rather than smoothing every element into a consistent "produced" world. - **Skip the reverb plugin — replay a drum stem through two very different real-world speakers and re-record each with a mic, panned to preserve the original stereo picture, for a wide but deliberately "unnatural" spatial image.** Overmono's signature move: once a drum arrangement is locked, the stem is played back out through two very different transducers — a 4×12 Marshall guitar cab and a ~100-year-old railway station announcement speaker rebuilt with a period Goodmans driver — each re-recorded on its own mic, panned hard to opposite sides (elements were pre-panned before the reamp so each speaker only received its intended side). A dry copy of the kick is blended back in underneath. The result reads as wide and spatial but not like any plugin reverb — the "unnatural" transducer coloration is the point, not a flaw to fix. → [[Layering and Stacking]] — Overmono (Tape Notes), [[TN 187 Overmono - Full Video Episode]]
- **When a plugin reverb keeps sounding "too processed" on a vocal that still needs to feel like it's "in a space," replace it with a real small room and a real speaker instead of chasing a plugin setting.** Overmono, on a vocal for *Night in Shining Prada*: after trying "so many different types of reverb plugins" without getting the right feel, they set up a vintage Tannoy speaker in a small stone/concrete storeroom next to the studio and replayed the vocal through it, moving the mic closer to or further from the speaker to dial in natural room depth by ear — chosen specifically because it didn't sound like it had reverb on it while still sounding like it was in a space. → [[Reverb]] — Overmono (Tape Notes), [[TN 187 Overmono - Full Video Episode]]
- **Gradually narrow a layer's stereo width over several bars during a breakdown, then snap it back to full width at the next section change — an arrangement-length width automation used as a "closing in" cue.** On a Prophet breakdown section, Overmono progressively pan multiple detuned synth layers narrower as the section plays (inspired by an unnamed Stephen Stills record), so it feels like the sound is physically closing in on the listener; the sudden return to full width at the next section reads as an opening-up release. A width automation arc treated as a structural/arrangement tool, not just a mix-polish detail. → [[Width and Stereo]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]] — Overmono (Tape Notes), [[TN 187 Overmono - Full Video Episode]]
- **Depth breaks down into separable, mostly-numeric levers: level (baseline), and four distinct reverb parameters.** Louder = closer, quieter = farther — the most basic depth tool, everything else refines it. Reverb: **RT60 (decay time) greater than the source's natural decay = farther away**; **wet/dry ratio** is a direct depth dial (more wet = farther); **pre-delay** (time between dry signal and reverb onset) is itself a depth lever, not just a clarity tool — hearing the direct signal first affects perceived distance; **reverb times under ~1 second add amplitude/character/realism WITHOUT significantly contributing to depth** — useful for room-ish thickness without pushing something back in the mix. Delay can also create depth via slapback, but only **above ~130 ms** — shorter than that, the ear fuses it with the original into one louder (not farther) signal; reverb-based depth reads more believable than delay-based depth generally. → [[Reverb]], [[Delay]] — Sage Audio, [How to Create Depth and Width with Reverb and Delay](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XGSytDiGJl0)
- **Reverb is the single most important localization cue, and it works best applied BEFORE amplitude/timing/frequency panning, because that's the order a real sound encounters its environment.** Real sound interacts with a physical space before the ear ever receives amplitude, timing, or frequency differences — so a reverb stage placed ahead of a panning chain (rather than after) gives the most convincing sense of a source's placement and the space's size/shape. The minute variations and reflections in the reverb are what let the ear estimate a space at all — combining reverb with the other three localization cues (amplitude, inter-aural delay, high-frequency loss toward the far ear) produces meaningfully more realistic placement than amplitude panning alone. → [[Width and Stereo]], [[Reverb]] — Sage Audio, [Psychoacoustics: The Least Talked About, Most Important Part of Music Production Pt.1](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SZ_2yIPp4g4)
- **Leave space for the listener's imagination rather than maximizing density/loudness — a direct trade-off, stated as a deliberate preference over "slamming it to the very ends of the sonic earth."** Jack Antonoff, on Sabrina Carpenter's "Manchild": "recording needs to leave a lot of space for the imagination... we could have a few things going on and then treat them in ways to make them feel like the most special thing in the world, because that leaves the ability for myself and other people to imagine it bigger than it actually was — which is more powerful than slamming it... where there's no room for a human being's ear or body to squeeze into that field." Delivered on a track that keeps its lead vocal nearly undoubled and its chorus stack to just four voices rather than piling on more layers to read as "bigger." → [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]], [[Masking and Carving]] — Mix with the Masters (Jack Antonoff), [[Behind the Track｜'Manchild' by Sabrina Carpenter]]
- **The felt-not-heard test, plus a stated mix-balance philosophy and a confession about the failure mode of ignoring it: put the vocal on "the same stage" as the band, not way out in front of it.** Greg Wells traces his whole vocal-FX approach to hitting stop mid-mix on sessions with Chris Lord-Alge, Spike Stent, and Michael Brauer and being surprised by reverb/delay tails he'd never noticed while the track was playing — the working test for a correctly-set effect is that it "hugs and holds and supports" the vocal rather than reading as its own event (see [[Reverb]] (effect), [[Delay]] (effect)). His stated balance goal: "I want the vocal to not be lost, but I want the vocal to not get in the way of the accompaniment either... I want everyone to kind of be on the same stage" — explicitly against a Frank Sinatra-style mix where "it's all vocal and Nelson Riddle or Count Basie are way in the background conducting the band." Subtle vocal effects (not fader level) are named as the actual mechanism for holding that balance. He also names the opposite failure mode from his own history: "I think I was guilty of under-mixing vocals for a long time when I was younger, because I was so obsessed with what the hi-hat was doing" — a bias worth watching for in anyone mixing from the instrumental side first. → [[Reverb]] (effect), [[Delay]] (effect), [[Compression]] (effect), [[1 Main Vocal Track]] — Greg Wells (Mix with the Masters), [[Bringing vocals out of the speakers with Greg Wells]]
- **Main-mic distance as a literal, steppable depth control — from fully dry to pushed all the way back in the room — a physical framing of the wet/dry depth cue above.** "If I want everything to sound a little bit farther away, well, let me move the mic off the instruments a little bit... I can push it all the way back in the room, or I can make it completely dry." Demoed inside a shared convolution scoring-stage engine (Vienna MIR Pro): moving the *virtual mic* rather than turning a send knob is a clarifying way to think about depth cue #4 above (wet/dry ratio) on any reverb — the send fader IS the mic-distance control. Same session also demonstrates rotating a source within its virtual placement changes its brightness — "the mics that are farther away don't have quite as much high end" — the same high-frequency depth cue (#2 above) produced by orientation instead of filtering. → [[Reverb]] (effect), [[Hybrid Reverb]], [[Width and Stereo]] — Alan Meyerson (Mix with the Masters), [[Using reverb to create space with Alan Meyerson]]
- **Push a vocal chop "far away" with mono + pan, not just reverb — and double up a second texture layer in the exact same stereo spot for extra distance.** A melodic Splice vocal chop gets filtered ambient, heavily reverbed to "drown it out," then explicitly forced to mono and panned right — the mono-then-pan combination (rather than a wide stereo chop left centered) is what reads as pushed back/distant. A previously-built reversed-guitar ambient layer is placed in that same stereo position alongside it "for the whole loop," compounding the far-away effect with a second element rather than one. → [[Reverb]] (effect), [[EQ]], [[Sound Selection Guide]] — loner, [making a soulful afrobeats beat for rema & wizkid from scratch (use voice notes to start ideas!?)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=e-KvnLXHAy4)
- **The quickest way to increase perceived depth: a very subtle maximizer.** Raising the level of quieter ambient details just enough to clear the masking threshold gives the listener a reference point for background relative to foreground — once those details are audible at all, the foreground/background relationship this note's own depth cues describe starts to read. A second, related lever: **subtle amplification of the side image in the mid-frequency range** also increases a sense of depth — a width-adjacent move that reads back as depth rather than just width, worth trying alongside the maximizer move. → [[Critical Listening for Mastering]], [[Oxford Inflator]], [[Width and Stereo]] — Sage Audio, [Before You Master Music, Watch This](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tEqcGkgHgqc)
- **Isolate a reverb return to the mid frequencies to make an instrument blend in — the opposite of the bright/present move.** Band-limiting a reverb return to the mids keeps it from reading bright or boomy and lands it in an already-dense part of the mix, so it disappears into the arrangement instead of adding a distinct new layer — pair with tempo-timed decay (`60,000 ÷ BPM`, see [[Reverb]]) for the best blend. The deliberate opposite move — boost highs/dip lows on the return — makes a reverb stand out as an audible effect instead. → [[Reverb]], [[Masking and Carving]], [[MIDS Slot]] — Sage Audio, [Reverb Masterclass](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9G1ME30K2ro)
- **Sustained, held notes take a reverb send more naturally than short, plucked notes — use that as a first filter for what earns a send** — a part that holds long notes (a legato guitar line, a sustained piano part) absorbs reverb smoothly: the tail expands into the reflected space and evolves subtly over time without drawing attention to itself, unless deliberately exposed by stopping and restarting the transport. A part built from short, quickly-decaying notes reveals the same reverb far more distinctly by comparison, since there's more silence between hits for the tail to be heard in isolation — confirms the existing bass/drums caution above and extends it: sustain, not just instrument category, is the underlying variable worth checking before committing a send. Also confirms the existing house convention that delay should sit before reverb in the signal chain (echoes "enter the room," not the reverse). → [[Masking and Carving]] — Max Konyi, [Creating a Solid First Mix (Specific Process)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dXBNtKHt2r0)
- **Automate the wet/dry ratio itself as an active transition ("wet/dry drop"), not just a static distance setting — plus the stereo-width side effect it carries.** Depth cue #4 above (more reverb relative to dry = further away) can be driven as a live transition tool: automate a delay/reverb send and its return's Wet/Dry progressively wetter approaching a section change, then pull both back to dry once it lands — the section-to-section contrast reads as "opening up a new space" without any tonal/filter change. This wet-vs-dry move carries a built-in stereo-width bonus for free: wet sections read noticeably wider, dry sections read more mono/narrow, so the two contrasts reinforce each other — push it further with unison/chorus on the source and a wider stereo field on the return if more contrast is wanted. → [[Grain Delay]], [[Hybrid Reverb]], [[Width and Stereo]] — Pick Yourself, [3 transition techniques pros use instead of risers](https://youtu.be/05A0Ez0IWAE)
- **True silence right before a drop, softened with a small "breath" so it doesn't read as a dropout** — select the region right before the drop, hit **Cmd/Ctrl+L** (the loop bracket jumps to that selection), click the bracket itself to select the same range across ALL tracks, then hit **Delete** to clear every track in that window at once — genuine silence, not just a low mix moment, maximizes the volume contrast into the drop. Total silence for too long reads as uncomfortable/a mistake though, so soften it: drop a short reversed-synth swell into the gap (an "inhale before speaking" — a small pickup sound rather than dead air) plus one extra hit, so the silence feels like a deliberate pause rather than a dropout. → [[Ear Candy and Hiding Synths]] — You Suck at Producing, [You Suck at Producing: Using Silence](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YtT-Lh9uv24)
- **Break up a "wall of noise" by deleting small gaps BETWEEN individual hits, not just around whole sections** — a chord progression or drop section where every hit runs directly into the next reads as one undifferentiated stream; deleting a small gap between each individual chord/hit increases that hit's perceived impact through contrast, the same principle as full-section silence applied at a much smaller scale. The gaps this creates are also free real estate for ear-candy details (a quick pluck, a chop) that would otherwise have nowhere to sit. → [[Ear Candy and Hiding Synths]] — You Suck at Producing, [You Suck at Producing: Using Silence](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YtT-Lh9uv24)
- **Hard-pan a dry DI on one side and a fully-wet reverb signal on the other, for a call-and-response guitar layer** — split one guitar performance into two channels: the raw dry DI signal panned hard one way, and a heavily-reverbed version of the same performance panned hard the other way — panning that extreme reads surprisingly natural rather than gimmicky, and the dry/wet contrast between the two sides gives a single guitar part a call-and-response character against its own reflection. → [[Width and Stereo]] — You Suck at Producing (Underbelly ft. Arbor), [how to turn simple lofi beats into FULL SONGS ft. @prodarbour](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VOMJUqx_rm0)
- **Spread simultaneous layers across different stereo "rooms," thin out how many play at once, and slow the tempo if it's still cluttered** — pan different elements to distinct positions across the field (not just louder/quieter) so simultaneous parts read as occupying separate space rather than piling into one spot; stagger entrances/exits so parts hand off to each other instead of all sustaining together (fewer things sounding at any one instant = more perceived space); and if a section still feels cluttered after both of those, try dropping the tempo — slower BPM gives the ear room to actually register what's playing. → [[Width and Stereo]], [[Stereo Field]] — You Suck at Producing, [Adding Space & Depth To Your Tracks](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y2s6WF1B7ok)
- **Close your eyes to judge a vocal's proximity and placement, not just its processing (Zedd)** — closing your eyes removes the visual bias of plugin GUIs and DAW track colors so you focus purely on where the vocal actually sits in the mix; goal is a vocal that feels right in front of you, not distant. → [[Vocal Mic Technique]] — Sol State, [I watched 300 hours of music production streams, here are my 17 FAVORITE tips](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JQHvtinwQQ8)
