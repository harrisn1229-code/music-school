# Behind the Track｜'The Less I Know The Better' by Tame Impala

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-tAMM6OAXWE)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-tAMM6OAXWE)

## Synopsis

Kevin Parker (Tame Impala) walks through the making of "The Less I Know The Better" — written and demoed in 15-30 minutes on a guitar synth, later expanded with deliberately tight/controlled drums (a reaction against the previous album's washed-out room sound), a signature vocal-doubling technique, and 80s-style reverb, kept deliberately simple production-wise ("very, very little automation").

## What's taught

- **Speed-capture demoing on a versatile guitar synth.** The entire initial musical idea was recorded on a Roland guitar synth specifically because it could instantly produce strings, horns, and many other sounds without needing keyboard skill — chosen for demo speed ("quick, quick, quick, get this down before you forget it"), not for its final-record tone. A useful demoing lesson independent of genre: pick the fastest-to-capture-an-idea instrument in the room over the "correct" one.
- **Deliberate drum-sound pendulum swing between albums.** The previous album's (Currents) drums were recorded "super washed out" in an untreated room, all-natural room reverb; this track's drums were a deliberate reaction the other way — tight and controlled. Framed as an intentional stylistic swing from album to album, not a fixed production identity.
- **Hi-hat timing as a groove-defining detail, stated precisely.** "Nothing less groovy than a hi-hat that's happening just too early" — the target pocket is **just after the grid**, not on it and not ahead of it; called out as a specific, fussy area of attention distinct from snare/kick placement.
- **Vocal doubling as THE signature vocal sound — and why it works better with imperfect pitch.** No harmony on the track — just a single doubled vocal, in the lineage of the Beach Boys, the Beatles, and Fleetwood Mac's doubling tradition. States his own pitch isn't perfectly precise take-to-take, and argues that's *why* the double reads audibly as a double — cites Stevie Wonder as the counter-example: because Stevie Wonder's pitch was so exact across takes, his doubled vocals reportedly didn't sound like a doubled voice at all, just a single louder one. The imperfection is what makes a double sound like a double.
- **80s-style (Phil Collins-influenced) reverb on vocals**, deliberately mixed low in retrospect-regretted balance — the vocal is more "washed out" in the mix than he'd choose now, but it's identified as part of what makes the track read as classic Tame Impala.
- **Signature synth gear used across albums, named specifically:** a Korg Chronos keyboard ("Swiss Army knife," used on every album since *Currents*, rarely gets attention despite being a workhorse) and a Roland JV-1080 synth module for big pad/lead sounds, chosen while chasing a Pharrell Williams/Justin Timberlake-style big-synth sound; both selected partly out of gear scarcity making music in Perth early on ("you just get what you can get").
- **"Dead simple" production as a deliberate value**, not a limitation — very little automation used on the track, stated as something he actively respects rather than something he'd have added more of given more time/resources.
