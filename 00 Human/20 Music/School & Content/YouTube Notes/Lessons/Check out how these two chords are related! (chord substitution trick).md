# Check out how these two chords are related! (chord substitution trick)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V8Td9PWGH3c)

## Synopsis

A single-topic Jared Borkowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared) deep-dive into the dominant-7/diminished-7 relationship, built as a from-scratch guitar-shape derivation rather than a soloing-vocabulary lesson. Jared derives the mechanism twice, in both directions: (1) take **any** voicing of a dominant 7th chord anywhere on the neck, raise the root a half step, and the shape you're left with is **always** a diminished-7 shape — demonstrated on four different physical voicings (root position, and three different inversions across the top-four-string, middle-four-string, and 6th-string-rooted voicing families) to prove it's a universal property of dominant-7 chords, not a one-off coincidence. He explains why every one of those raised shapes ends up identical: diminished-7 is a **symmetric** chord (built entirely of stacked minor 3rds), so it inverts to itself every three frets. (2) He then inverts the whole process: starting from a *known* diminished-7 voicing, **lower any one of its four notes a half step** and you get a voicing of dominant 7 — used explicitly as a **voicing-discovery tool** ("you're gonna find some voicings you may have never played before"), demonstrated by lowering each of the four notes of one diminished shape in turn to generate all four inversions of dominant 7 from a single known grip. The lesson's payoff is the practical conclusion: because a dominant 7 chord's diminished-shape-off-any-non-root-tone gives you a **dominant 7♭9** sound (the diminished chord IS the dominant 7 with the root swapped for a ♭9), you can superimpose diminished-7 shapes over **any** dominant 7th chord to get instant, movable 7♭9 voicings — worked concretely on a live A7 → D minor progression, playing diminished shapes off the 3rd, 5th, and ♭7 of A7 and resolving into D minor. ## What's taught

- **Any dominant 7 voicing, root raised a half step, becomes a diminished-7 shape — demonstrated on four different physical voicings to prove it's universal, not a one-off.** Worked on a root-position A7, a second inverted A7 voicing, a first-inversion A7 (3rd in the bass), and a 6th-string-rooted A7 — every single time, raising just the root note a half step produces a recognizable diminished-7 shape, with no other notes changed. → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Chord Substitution]]
- **Why they all turn out to be the "same" shape: diminished-7 is symmetric (built of stacked minor 3rds), so it inverts to itself every 3 frets/a minor 3rd.** Demonstrated by walking a diminished-7 shape up in minor thirds on the low strings and hearing/seeing it land back on itself — the classic "horror movie sting" sound. Practical payoff: once you know **one** diminished-7 shape on a given string set, you already know all four of its inversions — moving it up 3 frets at a time just replays the identical fingering. → [[Diminished Chords]] § *Only three unique dim7 chords exist*
- **The reverse process, used explicitly as a voicing-discovery tool: take a known diminished-7 shape and lower any ONE of its four notes a half step to reveal a dominant-7 voicing you may not already know.** Worked by taking one diminished-7 grip and lowering each of its four notes in turn (root, then each remaining chord tone) — each lowered note produces a **different inversion of dominant 7**, cycling through all four inversions from a single starting shape. Framed directly as a way to find new dominant-7 fingerings rather than just as harmonic theory. → [[Chord Voicings]] § *Getting these under the fingers*
- **The core substitution rule, derived rather than just stated: a diminished-7 chord shape built off any chord tone of a dominant 7 EXCEPT the root gives you a dominant 7♭9 sound.** Reasoning: since raising the dominant's root a half step produces the diminished shape, the diminished chord can be thought of as "every chord tone of the dominant 7 except the root, plus a ♭9 in its place" — so playing the diminished shape off the 3rd, 5th, or ♭7 of a dominant chord (i.e., NOT off the root) functions as that same dominant chord voiced as a 7♭9. Same "diminished = rootless dominant 7♭9" mechanism already documented extensively on [[Diminished Chords]], derived here from the "raise the root" direction rather than the more commonly-cited "strip the root, stack one more minor 3rd" derivation. → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Chord Substitution]]
- **Worked live: A7 → D minor, using diminished-7 shapes off the 3rd, 5th, and ♭7 of A7 as movable 7♭9 voicings that resolve smoothly into D minor.** Concrete comping application (block chords/voicings, not a soloing arpeggio run): play the diminished shape off the 3rd of A7, then the same physical shape off the 5th (same shape, different starting point — the symmetry means they're interchangeable), then resolve to D minor; repeated with the shape off the ♭7 as well. Described as sounding "so targeted" resolving into the D minor triad. This is a **chord-voicing** application of the device — distinct from the arpeggio/soloing version of the same mechanism already logged from other Jack Ruch and Jared videos on [[Diminished Chords]] and [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]. → [[Chord Voicings]], [[The ii-V-I]]
- **Practical recommendation given directly: learn three diminished-7 voicings (off the 6th, 5th, and 4th strings) and you effectively own every dominant-7♭9 voicing on the neck**, since the symmetric inversion property means those three shapes, moved in minor thirds, cover the whole fretboard. → [[Guitar Positions]]
