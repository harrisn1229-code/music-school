# 8 Ways To Make Your Guitar Solos Sound Cooler

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MaWhOQM2iZ8)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MaWhOQM2iZ8)

## Synopsis

Solo-crafting advice covering structure, a diagonal-pentatonic fretboard pattern, six tension-and-release builders, and seven named anti-patterns. The tension/arc material substantially overlaps the vault's existing, deeper [[Solo Dynamics and Arc]]; the diagonal pentatonic pattern and the specific anti-pattern checklist are the genuinely new pieces, routed to their natural homes below.

## Techniques

- **Solo structure: Start → Climax → Cool-down, climax placed ~2/3 through.** Simple three-part template: don't front-load your best material (save vocabulary for later), place the climax around two-thirds of the way through the solo, and treat the cool-down as a mirror of the opening rather than a new idea. *(A simpler restatement of the same principle [[Solo Dynamics and Arc]] documents in more depth — "save the peak for the peak," reserving the highest note for the actual climax.)*
- **A diagonal minor pentatonic pattern for breaking out of the box, worked in A minor.** A 4-note cell played across the D and G strings, extended with a 5th note (the note E) to complete a 5-note pentatonic unit, then the same shape repeated an octave down and an octave up — producing a diagonal line across the fretboard built from one repeating cell rather than a fixed box shape.
- **Six tension-and-release builders**, several restating [[Solo Dynamics and Arc]]'s existing material from a different angle: sustaining a bend before releasing it (the bend itself *is* tension-then-release); repetition followed by a deliberate break (you can't repeat forever, so stopping reads as release); playing a "wrong" note tastefully/in passing for a moment of shock value; **ascending lines** (rising pitch reads as rising tension — cited example: the "opening a treasure chest" musical sting convention); **silence** (called the strongest of the six tension builders); and **playing fast** (speed itself creates an expectation of an eventual slow-down, and that anticipated release is the tension).
- **Seven named anti-patterns (ways to make a solo sound worse):** sounding too scalar (a fluency gap, not a taste problem — the fix is practicing the scale until it's as automatic as the minor pentatonic box); an out-of-tune instrument (some sympathy given for genuine intonation problems on cheap gear, none for just not tuning up); staying on only 2–3 strings out of habit; flat-out wrong notes (distinguished from tasteful "wrong" notes used deliberately as tension, per builder #3 above); playing with bad timing (rated worse than wrong notes, since a wrong note played in time can still sound intentional); sounding too composed/note-for-note when the context calls for a looser live improvisation; and sounding "boxy" — a fixed, clockwork-predictable pattern of changing ideas every two bars (worked critique: Kirk Hammett's Metallica "Enter Sandman" solo, called out for changing character every two bars like clockwork).
