# Jazz Language Unlocked! Use Wes Montgomery's Lines

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ARfps1Eapbc)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ARfps1Eapbc)

## Synopsis
Nathan Borton's method for turning five short Wes Montgomery licks (pulled from his solos on "Airegin," "Four on Six," "Gone With the Wind" ×2, and "Canadian Sunset") into a general-purpose system for **splicing memorized vocabulary together** rather than playing licks verbatim start-to-finish. The video first maps each lick onto its 2-5-1 role — minor language for the ii chord, dominant language for the V, a resolving "tag" phrase for the I — then walks through four escalating ways to connect any two licks: (1) simple proximity linking (end one lick on a note a half step from where the next one starts), (2) using a diatonic scale (D Dorian, over the ii chord) as connective tissue to walk from one lick's ending area into the next lick's starting note, (3) editing a lick's own rhythm (triplets simplified to straight eighths) specifically to make the walk-up land more cleanly, and (4) using a chord arpeggio (F major 7 or A minor 7, both over the ii chord) as an "on-ramp" that leads into a memorized dominant phrase by walking up or down the arpeggio until landing near the phrase's own starting note. The last section goes a level deeper: fragments of a lick can be **reharmonized and moved to a different chord of the 2-5-1** entirely, because they're built from notes shared across all three chords — Wes's major "tag" (built from C major 7 tones) works equally over G7 and Dm7 because those tones are common to all three; a dominant fragment built mostly from an E minor 7 arpeggio works over any of the three 2-5-1 chords for the same reason. Most valuable idea in the video: **licks aren't fixed objects, they're raw material** — the actual skill being taught is the connective mechanism (proximity, scale walk-up, rhythm edit, arpeggio on-ramp, shared-note reharmonization), not any one of the five specific Wes licks. This is a genuinely new addition to the vault's vocabulary-connecting material — [[Bebop Vocabulary]] and [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]] cover related ground (enclosures, CAGED-position arpeggio splicing) but neither documents this specific toolkit of proximity-linking + scale-connective-tissue + rhythm-editing + arpeggio-on-ramp + shared-note fragment reharmonization as one coherent method — see the new [[Connecting Licks Into Longer Lines]] technique note this video seeded.

## What's taught

- **Five source licks, each assigned a 2-5-1 role.** Lick 1 (from "Airegin") — a full 2-5-1 outline, played "in a very Wes type of way," used later as dominant-chord language (starts on E, built mostly from an E minor 7 arpeggio — see the reharmonization bullet below). Lick 2 (from "Four on Six") — minor language, assigned to the ii chord, ends on a high E in its original form. Lick 3 (from "Gone With the Wind") — dominant language with "great dominant tension that resolves so nicely," starts on the ♭9 of G7 (A♭), assigned to the V chord. Lick 4 (also from "Gone With the Wind") — a second minor-sounding lick "with a more rhythmic feel" (built on triplets), assigned to the ii chord alongside lick 2. Lick 5 (from "Canadian Sunset") — a dominant line with "a great tag to end your lines," the tag itself (built from C major 7 tones) reserved for the I chord as a line-ending device. → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Bebop Vocabulary]]
- **2-5-1 role mapping as the first step, before any connecting: minor language (licks 2, 4) over the ii (Dm7), dominant language (licks 1, 3, 5) over the V (G7), resolving/tag phrases (lick 5's ending) over the I.** States this directly as the prerequisite to everything else in the lesson — "now that you know where these lines fit, let's talk about connecting them together." → [[The ii-V-I]]
- **Device 1 — proximity linking: connect two licks by ending one on a note a half step (or otherwise close) from where the next one starts, no scale or rhythm change needed.** Worked example: lick 2 (minor, over ii) ends on A; lick 3 (dominant, over V) starts on A♭ (its own ♭9) — "just a half step above" — so playing lick 2 then jumping directly into lick 3 already flows because the two endpoints are a half step apart. *(general starting point: scan any two licks you know for a close interval between one's ending note and the other's starting note before reaching for a scale or rhythm fix)* → [[Connecting Licks Into Longer Lines]], [[Guide Tones]]
- **Device 2 — scale walk-up as connective tissue: instead of jumping straight to a lick's high point, walk up the chord-tone-appropriate scale (D Dorian, since it fits the Dm7 the phrase is over) from partway through lick 2, bypassing its original ending note, until landing on the exact note lick 3 starts on (A♭), then playing straight into lick 3.** Explicitly stated as smoothing what a direct jump would otherwise sound like — "I want you to hear how that scale connects these two ideas into one smooth flowing line." Same device demonstrated a second time linking a rhythmically-edited lick 4 into lick 3. → [[Connecting Licks Into Longer Lines]], [[Bebop Vocabulary]], [[Modal Jazz]]
- **Device 3 — rhythm editing specifically to enable a cleaner connection: lick 4's original triplet rhythm is simplified into straight eighth notes so the line can be interrupted at a useful point and walked toward the next lick's starting note.** Framed as necessary, not cosmetic — playing licks 4 and 3 back-to-back unedited "doesn't flow as well," and the fix is rhythmic before it's melodic: "we need to be able to tweak rhythms and use scales as connective tissue between licks, and then we'll be able to take them and make them into our own ideas." A second worked variation combines the edited lick 4 with dominant language from lick 3 tagged onto the *beginning* of the phrase instead of the end, showing the same two licks can be spliced in more than one order. → [[Connecting Licks Into Longer Lines]]
- **Device 4 — arpeggios as an "on-ramp" into memorized language, two worked examples over the ii chord (Dm7): F major 7 arpeggio, and A minor 7 arpeggio, each walked toward a different dominant lick's starting note.** F major 7 arpeggio over Dm7 — chosen because "F major 7 highlights the ninth of our minor chord and shares almost all the same notes of a D minor 7"; walked up until landing near/on E, then pivoted straight into lick 1's dominant phrase (which starts on E). A minor 7 arpeggio over Dm7 — highlights the 9th and 11th of the minor chord; walked *down* until landing near E, then straight into lick 3's dominant phrase (which also starts on E, a different lick than the F-major-7 example targets). Stated payoff: "these arpeggios can act kind of like an on-ramp to Wes's language, or really any language." → [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]], [[Connecting Licks Into Longer Lines]], [[Extended Chords]]
- **Fragment extraction + shared-note reharmonization: a lick fragment built from one chord's tones can be replayed over a different chord in the same 2-5-1 when the two chords share those tones, rather than staying locked to the chord it was originally learned over.** Lick 5's major "tag" phrase, built from C major 7 tones, is stated to also work over G7 and Dm7 (the V and ii of the same 2-5-1) because those chords share most of the same notes with Cmaj7 — played first over the ii chord, then followed by lick 1's dominant language over the V (connected with a Dorian walk-up per Device 2), landing back on the tag fragment re-voiced lower over the I — "suddenly this major line becomes something else." A second fragment (the dominant phrase that opens lick 1) is identified as being "mostly just an E minor 7 arpeggio," and because an Em7 arpeggio shares tones with all three chords of a 2-5-1 in this key, the same fragment is played over the ii chord, linked to dominant language from lick 3, then closed with the major tag fragment re-voiced lower over the I — a full worked line built entirely from recombined/reharmonized fragments rather than any single lick played as originally learned. → [[Connecting Licks Into Longer Lines]], [[Chord Substitution]], [[The ii-V-I]]
- **Overall philosophy, stated as the closing takeaway: "steal his licks, mix them like Legos, and then bend the rules to make them your own."** Explicitly framed as generalizable beyond Wes Montgomery — the video references a companion lesson applying the identical method to Pat Martino's vocabulary. → [[Connecting Licks Into Longer Lines]], [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]]
