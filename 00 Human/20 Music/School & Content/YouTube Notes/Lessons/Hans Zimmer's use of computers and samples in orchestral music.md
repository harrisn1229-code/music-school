# Hans Zimmer's use of computers and samples in orchestral music

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_LHyNYRtwR8)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_LHyNYRtwR8)

## Synopsis

An eight-minute Q&A-style piece with Hans Zimmer (composer — *The Dark Knight*, *Interstellar*, *Dune*), interviewed by Mix with the Masters, on how computers, sampling, and live orchestra fit together in his scoring process. There are no plugin names, EQ numbers, or a device chain here — this is a philosophy/mindset video, not a mixing tutorial — but it's dense with reusable ideas about *how a composer should relate to their tools*. Three threads run through it: (1) **the computer as an instrument in its own right**, demanding the same daily-practice virtuosity as piano or violin, which is why Zimmer calls himself "a better programmer than I am a player"; (2) **building a genuinely custom sample library** — Zimmer started sampling his own recordings in 1994 specifically because commercial libraries of the era couldn't produce what he heard in his head, worked directly with orchestral musicians in London to build player-specific samples (an arrangement he frames as good for the musicians too — better mock-ups meant directors commissioned more real orchestral work, not less), and today has both Spitfire and a dedicated sample engineer, Claudius Brausewetter, doing phase-alignment work that he says makes his library sound better than most commercial ones; (3) **orchestras as a collection of individuals, not an interchangeable resource** — pick specific players for their specific tone the way a guitarist obsesses over pickups and amps, and expect a score to sound different depending on which city's players and which room record it (he writes differently for LA vs. London vs. Paris vs. Vienna). A closing, very reusable line: you don't need to sample a full orchestra to make great sounds — "if you have a rubber band and a cardboard box... and you can think of a really cool way of using that" that beats a bought library, because deep tool fluency ("the guy who really knows these tools") always outperforms library shopping. ## Techniques

- **The computer is a practiced instrument, not a workflow tool.** "You have to start off by realizing that a computer is a musical instrument that needs just as much practice as when you practice piano, or the violin, or the cello... you have to become fairly virtuosic at programming." Zimmer states outright that he's "a better programmer than I am a player" and that if asked honestly what his instrument is, "I will tell you it's the computer." → [[Career & Release]] (Mindset)
- **The historical arc of his own sample-building, told as a timeline: 8-bit samplers → 12-bit samples → 16-/24-bit samples → a personal stack of samplers → building his own samples starting in 1994.** The motive wasn't "making the music sound better" or making the job easier — it was purely so he wasn't stuck all day listening to mediocre mock-up sounds; he wanted to spend his listening time on "slightly better sounds." A reminder that better tools were pursued for the composer's own working experience, not as a marketable feature. → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]]
- **Sampled real session musicians directly to build a personal library — pitched to them explicitly as a trade that would get them MORE work, not replace them.** Mid-90s London: "here's the experiment I'm going to try — if you let me sample you, I will make it so that directors will want more orchestral music, and you will actually get more work out of it, because I'm going to be able to present something where they go, 'wow, orchestra is right.'" Framed against a specific historical backdrop — orchestral music was "thought of as not cool" at the time, and better-sounding sampled mock-ups became the pitch tool that got directors to commission real orchestras. → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]]
- **Giving assistants/other composers access to his sample tools was a deliberate act, not an accident, and named as part of why it "worked out better."** Assistants who started under him (named: Carrick Griggs, John Williams-lineage collaborators, Ramin Djawadi, John Powell) had access to the same custom sample tools Zimmer used, so their mock-ups could sound "just as fabulous, or as grand," which then won them their own jobs recording with real orchestras. → [[Career & Release]] (Mindset)
- **Treat every orchestra member as an individual with a distinct sound, not an interchangeable section — pick players deliberately, the way a guitarist obsesses over gear.** "You need to think of the string section [as] individuals that come together and give you their everything... each individual player's technique, the sound of the instrument... if you go to London, or Paris, or Berlin, they will have different quality instruments, different-sounding instruments. I mean, guitarists go mad about pickups and strings and amps — that's just one guitarist. You should treat every member of the orchestra the same way." → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]]
- **The room and the players change the writing itself, not just the recording — Zimmer composes differently for LA vs. London vs. Paris vs. Vienna because he's writing for the specific room and specific players who'll perform it.** "I will write very differently here in America, because I have a different room to record and with different players, than I will write for somebody in London, or somebody in Paris, or somebody in Vienna... study who the individuals are that are actually going to make this performance." He cites Berlin and Iceland currently producing distinctly different orchestral sounds from America/London, driven by the individual players, not the tech. When he writes a cello line, "I know who I write it for... I see that person in front of me." → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]]
- **Each project opens with a dedicated "safari of sampling" — hunting down and building custom sounds before writing starts, not reaching for an existing library first.** "Once I have an idea of what I want to do, then it becomes this safari of sampling — we're hunting down sounds, we're making sounds. Each movie has a load of custom sounds in it." Names his current sample-building partners: Spitfire ("very helpful because they can do things") and Claudius Brausewetter in Germany, a specialist who does phase-alignment work on his samples specifically — "he manages to phase-align things and make samples sound amazing... our sampler still sounds better than other people's samples." Framed the same as his relationship with musicians: "I have talented guys in technology who are absolutely our partners in making a piece of music." → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]]
- **Built his own sample tools because commercial ones weren't sufficient to what he heard in his head — a stated reason to bring in outside technical help rather than settle for the available library.** "I just found that the tools available at the time... weren't sufficient to what I was hearing in my head, or what I wanted to do, so I had to get somebody in to help me build some of these things." → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]]
- **You don't need to sample a full orchestra to make a great custom sound — a found object, sampled with imagination, is a legitimate source.** "I'll make my own samples — it's okay, you don't have to go out and sample a huge orchestra... that's going to be really expensive. But if you have a rubber band and a cardboard box, and you can think of a really cool way of using that, and sampling that, and making a score out of that..." → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]], [[Ambient Texture and Field Recording]], [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **Tool depth beats library breadth — the closing thesis of the whole video.** "I don't understand why people don't make sounds more themselves, why people don't really [learn] how their computers work. But people keep — and I'm guilty of that too — of buying [something] and going 'ah, it's a bit boring' and never actually really exploring it. That's why I'm not really going 'this is a good library, or this is a bad library' — it's always the guy who really knows these tools who can make amazing music with it. So, what do you need to make great sounds? You need the thing that suits you, and that you really learn." A direct restatement of "computer as practiced instrument" from the top of the video, now framed as a warning against library-shopping as a substitute for depth. → [[Career & Release]] (Mindset)
