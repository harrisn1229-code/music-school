# Lucy In The Sky With Diamonds — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

Cut from the same cloth as "Tomorrow Never Knows" and "Strawberry Fields Forever" — less subtle than either, no less ingenious. The shifting key structure and the alternation of 3/4 and 4/4 do to the music what the imagery does to the words. Pollack's real claim is that beneath the surface chaos the song runs on **one closed loop built from root motion by thirds**, a Möbius strip in which every station is re-heard in the next key ([[HAR-C10 — Tonicization vs modulation]]). Each third-move throws off a cross relation for free ([[POP-C11 — Chromatic mediants and pop modulation]]).

**Form.** Verse, bridge, refrain three times through, except that **the bridge is dropped before the final pair**. The 4-bar intro ostinato contains the complete design of the verse inside it. The verse's two long vocal phrases lie across a limping accompaniment quatrain of **4 / 5 / 4 / 6** bars. The bridge *should* be 16 bars and starts out that way, but where its fourth phrase would begin it's foreshortened by the switch to 4/4, **the quarter note of the 3/4 bars becoming an eighth** — a section cut short without sounding cut. The refrain is an unusual 7 bars.

**Harmony.** Each section has its own key and profile. The verse is essentially a chromatic filling-in of the tonic chord over a descending bass, whose last step implies **♭VI** ([[HAR-C08 — Borrowed chords and modal interchange]]) and eventually gets its head as V of the bridge's key. The bridge's key looks remote and isn't — it's the parallel major of the relative minor of the one before. The refrain is deliberately the ballast, plain I–IV–V three times, ending on a chord that **pivots to hand back to the verse**; in the outro that same chord is left hanging unresolved instead.

**Melody.** Kept exceedingly thin given what it's riding on — hum it unaccompanied and there's very little there, which is the point. The bridge is stuck almost monotonously on one repeated note that rides roughshod over the chords beneath it, making a gratuitous ninth on one and a thirteenth on the next; it reads as enervation. The thirds motif governs the melody as well as the harmony: triadic outlining in the verse, a minor-third slide in the bridge, and parallel thirds harmonising the refrain. A song can be unified by an interval instead of a theme.

**Arrangement.** The opening ostinato is an organ on a celeste-like stop, Baroque in construction — one line implying a complete four-part texture. Paul's bass is the standout and its density is **scheduled section by section**: downbeats only in the verses, every beat in the bridges, running eighths in the refrains.
