# Learn These Beautiful Altered Chords!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IzOrrHbh7lE)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IzOrrHbh7lE)

## Synopsis

A focused 14-minute guitar lesson from Jack Ruch on **altered dominant chord voicings** — dominant 7th chords with extensions borrowed from the altered scale (♭9, ♯9, ♯11, ♭13) — built entirely around a single practical frame: start from a root-3rd-♭7 shell voicing of the dominant chord, add one altered-scale color tone on top, and use that same color tone as a moving voice that walks chromatically into the next chord in a ii–V–I. The lesson stays in one guitar position throughout (E7 as the V of a 2-5-1 in A major: Bm7–E7alt–Amaj7) but the harmonic content — the altered-scale derivation, the four extension "flavors," the chromatic voice-leading device, and the root-omission comping trick — is fully transposable and reusable vocabulary for any dominant chord. Ruch also shares two personal go-to comping voicings (one his own, one credited to Joe Pass) built by dropping the root and voicing ♭7-3rd-♭13-(♯9 or ♭9) instead. ## What's taught

- **Dominant 7th = major triad + ♭7.** E7 shell voicing (used throughout the lesson) = root, 3rd, ♭7 — the 5th is dropped entirely, consistent with standard shell-voicing practice (see [[Chord Voicings]]/[[Guide Tones]] — 3rd and 7th are the guide tones, root is optional, 5th is the first thing to go).
- **Altered extensions only really work over a *functioning* dominant** — i.e. a dominant chord acting as the V in a ii–V–I, not a static/non-resolving dominant. Demonstrated on a major 2-5-1 in A: Bm7 (root-3-7) → E7 (root-3-♭7 + alteration) → Amaj7 (root-3-7).
- **The altered scale, spelled and derived practically.** E altered scale = E F G G♯ A♯ C D (E). Ruch's mental model: buried inside those seven notes is the plain dominant-7 shell (root E, major 3rd G♯, ♭7 D) — everything left over is an altered extension sitting on top of that shell: **F = ♭9, G = ♯9, A♯ = ♯11, C = ♭13.** (He notes this scale is identical to F melodic minor — the standard jazz-theory equivalence.) → [[Altered Chords]], [[Scale Modes Reference]]
- **E7♯9 called out as the single most common altered-dominant voicing** — shell (root-3-♭7) + ♯9 on top; used as the entry point before the other three colors.
- **The chromatic voice-leading trick (the video's core "aha"):** take each of the four altered extensions in turn as the *top note* of the V chord and track that same voice through all three chords of the 2-5-1 — it walks chromatically:
  - **♭9 (F)** — Bm7 with F♯ on top → E7♭9 with F on top → Amaj7 with E on top = **descending** chromatic line F♯–F–E.
  - **♯9 (G)** — Bm7 with F♯ on top → E7♯9 with G on top → Amaj7 with G♯ (the major 7th) on top = **ascending** chromatic line F♯–G–G♯.
  - **♯11 (A♯)** — Bm7 with the root (B) on top → E7♯11 with A♯ on top → Amaj7 with the root (A) on top = **descending** chromatic line B–A♯–A.
  - **♭13 (C), voiced down an octave inside the chord rather than on top** — B (root) low in the Bm7 → C (the ♭13) in the E7 → C♯ (the major 3rd) in the Amaj7 = **ascending** chromatic line B–C–C♯.
  - Same device works for every alteration: pick the color tone, find where it sits a half-step from something in the ii chord and something in the I chord, and the progression grows a built-in chromatic inner voice.
- **Why altered dominants work: tension that's *designed* to resolve.** Standalone, a dense altered chord (e.g. E7 with ♭9 *and* ♯11 stacked) sounds "dissonant and ugly" in isolation — the payoff only lands because of the chromatic pull into the next chord. That controlled tension→resolution is what elevates "a plain, boring progression" into something with more color and depth. *(General principle, transcript-stated, not tied to a specific numeric setting.)*
- **Guitar shapes, described (no tab/frame data available):** from the E7 root-3-♭7 shell, barre across roughly six frets to add the ♭9 on top; barre further to reach the ♯11; use the pinky to grab the ♭13 on top instead. From the same base shape, sliding the pinky to different spots swaps between ♭9, ♯9, and resolving into the major 7 — i.e. one hand position, several extensions reachable by moving a single finger.
- **Root-omission comping trick.** Since a bass player usually already has the root, Ruch routinely drops the root from these dense altered voicings entirely — freeing a finger/note-slot for another color tone and avoiding a cluttered-sounding chord. Applied to the E7 shape: drop the root, voice **♭7 in the bass, 3rd above it, ♭13 above that**, then either the **root** or the **♭9** on top → **♭7-3-♭13-♭9** (or ♭7-3-♭13-root). Notable reuse: this exact shape, played over a *different* bass note, is the same physical grip as a **Dm7♭5** voicing — same fingers, different harmonic identity depending on what's underneath. Direct example of "one shape, multiple chord identities depending on context," worth flagging alongside the existing dim7-symmetry and rootless-voicing entries already on [[Chord Voicings]].
- **A second favorite voicing, credited to Joe Pass:** same root-omission idea but swap the ♭9 for the ♯9 → **♭7-3rd-♭13-♯9**. Described as "beautiful, dissonant, spread-out" sounding. Ruch says he got it directly from listening to Joe Pass recordings/voicings. → [[Joe Pass Guitar Chords - Enrichment]], [[Chord Voicings]]
- **These voicings are mode-agnostic — they resolve equally well to major or minor.** The same four altered-extension shapes over E7 work whether the target is Amaj7 (major 2-5-1: Bm7–E7alt–Amaj7) or Am7/Am9 (minor 2-5-1: Bm7♭5–E7alt–Am7 or Am9) — no change needed to the E7 voicing itself, only the target chord changes quality. → [[The ii-V-I]]
- **Ensemble/comping etiquette.** When comping with other musicians — especially another chordal instrument (a second guitar or a piano player) — stay aware of what the bass and the melody are doing so a colorful altered voicing doesn't clash with a bandmate's competing voicing choice. Stated as a constant, active listening habit rather than a one-time rule.
- **Practice framing:** this is presented as one movable hand position — explicitly encouraged to relocate the whole shape-set around the fretboard and rearrange which extension sits where within the chord, rather than treating the demonstrated fingering as fixed.
