# All I've Got To Do — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

Three things stacked make this sound exotic on an otherwise plain 1963 record: a near-entirely pentatonic tune, constant major/minor byplay, and a belly-dance syncopation that never lets up. Pollack's claim is that the refusal to commit to a key is deliberate.

**Form.** Two bridges with a single verse between them — the harmonic rhythm is sparse enough that a third verse there would sag. The verse is an odd **11 bars** (4 + 2 + 5), stretched by repeating its own material right where the hard syncopation eases off. Two quiet experiments sit on the plain shape: an opening chord that isn't quite an intro, and a wordless hummed final verse that fades out mid-section.

**Harmony.** The key is genuinely undecided between E major and C♯ minor ([[POP-C08 — Relative-key switching]]): verse phrases start on vi, no firm V–I ever lands, and the **only V chord in the whole song** is the B in the bridge's last bar. Working the vi/I pair this way was a standing habit of the period. Late in the verse the major IV gives way to a borrowed minor iv, and the cause is a chromatic inner voice sliding down rather than a colour choice ([[HAR-C08 — Borrowed chords and modal interchange]]). The opening sonority resists labelling — an augmented triad on C♯ over an E bass — but it functions as a surprise IV-substitute into the minor.

**Melody and arrangement.** Almost pure pentatonic ([[SCL-C03 — Pentatonic scales]]), broken only by a couple of D♯ appoggiaturas. Paul's countermelody starts in parallel thirds and converts to parallel fourths simply by holding one note while John keeps moving; his bass plays double stops across the c♯/E alternation, keeping the common tone G♯ on top of both.
