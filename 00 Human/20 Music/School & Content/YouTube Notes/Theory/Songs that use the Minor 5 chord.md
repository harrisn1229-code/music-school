# Songs that use the Minor 5 chord

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OXz57ar0buk)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OXz57ar0buk)

## Synopsis

Two genuinely distinct explanations for why a song's V chord shows up minor instead of the default major: borrowing from Mixolydian (a modal-interchange reading), and — a new concept for the vault — the **secondary supertonic**, where a minor v chord doubles as the "2" of a temporary secondary key center, usually paired with a secondary dominant to form a miniature ii-V heading toward the IV chord.

## Techniques

- **Default vs. altered V**: the diatonic V chord in a major key is always major; using the minor version instead is a deliberate color choice with two different underlying explanations, covered separately below.
- **Mood examples, straight minor-v usage**: "Clocks" (Coldplay, key of E♭) — Bennett A/B's the actual B♭m against a hypothetical B♭ major, showing how much "mellow"/"open" quality the minor version adds versus a version he calls "too bright." "The Tourist" (Radiohead). "Strawberry Fields Forever" (Beatles). "All Things Must Pass" (George Harrison).
- **Switching from major V to minor v mid-song, for a mood shift**: "Yellow" (Coldplay) — diatonic major V throughout the verses, switches to minor v (as a m7) for the somber outro on the same melody. "Angels" (Robbie Williams) — major V throughout, switches to minor v specifically at the guitar solo for a sense of "opening up." "The Darkness on the Edge of Town" (Bruce Springsteen) — a rare case of major V immediately followed by minor v in the SAME phrase (D→Dm), read as underscoring the lyric "darkness."
- **Explanation #1 — Mixolydian borrowing (modal interchange)**: Mixolydian is the major scale with a lowered 7th degree; since the 7th degree is the middle note of the V chord, lowering it turns V from major to minor as a NATURAL, diatonic consequence of being in Mixolydian rather than plain major — no "chromatic alteration" framing needed if the song is genuinely Mixolydian. "Chicago" (Sufjan Stevens, D Mixolydian) — built on I-v-♭VII-IV (D-Am-C-G), where BOTH the minor v (Am) and the ♭VII (C) are fully diatonic to D Mixolydian even though they'd be foreign chromatic borrows in plain D major.
- **Explanation #2 — the secondary supertonic (a genuinely distinct mechanism, new concept for this vault)**: a minor v chord can ALSO function as the "ii" (supertonic) of a temporary secondary key center built on the song's own IV chord — i.e., "the two of four." Paired with a secondary dominant (V7/IV), this forms a complete miniature ii-V heading INTO the IV chord, exactly like a real ii-V-I in jazz, just embedded briefly inside a different key. "Just the Way You Are" (Billy Joel, key of D) — Am7 (ii of G) → D7 (V/IV, i.e. V7 of G) → G (IV) — a full secondary ii-V-I aimed at the IV chord. "From Me to You" (The Beatles, key of C, middle 8) — Gm7 acts as ii of F, leading through F. McCartney's own account (quoted) shows he recognized the harmonic "departure" feeling this move creates even without knowing the technical ii-V-of-IV label for it.
- **The secondary supertonic doesn't strictly need its paired secondary dominant to function** — Bennett demonstrates by removing the D7 from the Billy Joel example: Am7→G directly still clearly "points toward" G, just with a less powerful pull than the full Am7-D7-G sequence.
- **Mechanism**: a secondary supertonic works by introducing a chromatic note that belongs to the temporary secondary key but not the song's main key — in "Just the Way You Are," Am7 introduces C natural (vs. D major's native C♯), which is exactly the one note distinguishing D major from G major, creating a brief, fleeting sense of having modulated to G without a full, committed key change.
- **A common jazz application of the same 2-5-1 logic, cited directly**: "Misty" (jazz standard) uses a minor v chord as a secondary supertonic in its very first line.
- **Because a secondary supertonic naturally sets up a move to IV, minor-v-then-IV is a common pairing even without an explicit secondary dominant in between**: "Kokomo" (The Beach Boys, key of C) — Gm7 (minor v / secondary supertonic) → F (IV). "The Only Exception" (Paramore, key of B) — Fm6 (minor v) → E major7 (IV).
