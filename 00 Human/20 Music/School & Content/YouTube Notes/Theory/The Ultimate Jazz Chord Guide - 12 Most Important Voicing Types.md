# The Ultimate Jazz Chord Guide - 12 Most Important Voicing Types

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JKOILPmU9pU)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JKOILPmU9pU)

## Synopsis

Jens Larsen's systematic walk through 12 jazz chord voicing families, organized around one core idea: almost every voicing type in the video is built by **adding or removing a single note from a shell voicing** (root-3rd-7th), so the twelve types aren't twelve things to memorize independently — they're one thing (the shell) plus a small set of add/remove/drop operations. This exact video is the source article already cited in [[Chord Voicings]] (`jenslarsen.nl/the-ultimate-jazz-chord-guide-12-most-important-voicing-types`) — that page's "named voicing families" table and its shell/drop-2/drop-3/rootless/quartal framing come directly from this content, so most of the theory here is already in the vault. This page exists as the master record and fills in the construction *sequence* Larsen teaches (which type leads to which) more explicitly than the reference table does.

## What's taught

The 12 types, in the order Larsen builds them (each one an operation on the previous):

1. **Shell voicings** — root, 3rd, 7th (Cmaj7 → C-E-B, no 5th, no G). Root playable on the 5th or 6th string. The foundation everything else adds notes to.
2. **Drop 3** — add the 5th back to the 6th-string-root shell. Constructed by taking a close-position 4-note chord, finding the 3rd-highest note, and dropping it an octave. Inversions exist but are rarely used — root position dominates.
3. **Shell-derived voicings** *(Larsen's own coined term — no standard name exists)* — add the 9th to the 5th-string-root shell instead of the 5th, giving a Cmaj9-type sound. Some resulting shapes through the scale are usable, some aren't.
4. **Drop 3 with bass note removed → drop 2 with bass note** — a shell voicing plus two added notes (one on each of the next two strings) becomes a drop-2-with-bass shape. The pedagogical point: you're always just adding notes to the same shell, not learning a new grip from scratch.
5. **Rootless voicings** — take the fifth-string or sixth-string shell voicing and simply remove the bass note. Safe against soloist/bass-player clashes; take the same shell-voicing scale exercises but drop the root.
6. **Rootless drop 3 → triads** — remove the root from a drop-3 voicing and what's left is a triad: Cmaj7 minus C = E minor triad; Dm7 minus D = F major triad; G7 minus G = B diminished triad. This triad-substitution relationship (a 7th chord minus its root = a specific triad) is the mechanism, not just a coincidence — it means a 2-5 progression can be reharmonized using triad inversions instead of full 7th-chord voicings, including a `G7♭9` reading as an **F major♭5 triad**.
7. **Drop 2** — same construction logic as drop 3 but dropping the 2nd-highest note instead of the 3rd. Built the same way: shell → add a note on the next string → add one more. Drop 2 inversions ARE commonly used (unlike drop 3) and give smooth voice-leading turnarounds. Flagged as "too big to cover in one video" — worth a dedicated deep-dive.
8. **Quartal voicings (3-note)** — stack 4ths instead of 3rds: from G, a G-C-F chord (fourth + fourth) instead of a G-B-D triad (third + third). Open, ambiguous sound — doesn't spell out full harmony alone, used in clusters of several quartal voicings per chord to get the sound across. No standard name exists for many of these shapes; fit them to a chord by ear/context rather than by formula.
9. **Spread triads** — apply the drop-voicing octave-move technique to a plain triad: take an F major triad, move the middle voice (the A) down an octave. Gives beautiful open triad voicings with usable inversions, especially over a 2-5.
10. **Cluster-adjacent voicings (2nd-interval voicings)** — a loose category unified by containing a 2nd interval rather than one fixed construction rule. Two go-to sources: the *inversions of shell voicings* (e.g. F major 7 shell inversions), and inversions of the shell type that drops the 3rd and keeps the 5th instead.
11. **4-part quartal voicings** — the 4-note extension of #8, stacking four 4ths. Sound great but are harder to fit into progressions; particularly effective over an altered dominant in a 2-5.
12. **Drop 2-and-4** — from a close-position 4-note chord (e.g. Cmaj7), drop BOTH the 2nd-highest note (G) and the 4th-highest/lowest note (C) down an octave simultaneously. Has an "Allan Holdsworth" open, colorful character — related to spread triads but for 4-note chords. Especially strong as an **upper-structure voicing** — e.g. Fmaj7 → F half-diminished → Em7 read as upper structures over a C major 2-5 with an altered dominant.

**Two practice methods repeated throughout:** (1) take each new voicing type through a full scale/key to build fluency, not just learn one shape; (2) apply each type to a real 2-5 progression to hear it in context, not just in isolation.
