# Modulation by Type and Distance

Part of [[Music Theory]]. [[Modulation (Key Change)]] lists the common *mechanisms*. This note adds
the **second axis** — how far the key moves, in semitones — and works every distance from ±1 to ±6
plus a category most texts don't have at all. From Nick Clark's ~179,000-character study.

## The two axes

Every modulation gets tagged twice: **how** it is executed, and **where** it lands. Most fit more
than one mechanism, which is exactly why one axis was never enough.

**Definitions he sets first** — and immediately admits are fuzzy:

- **Key change** — the tonal centre moves permanently.
- **Modulation / tonicization** — a brief switch that returns. *"How long does a song have to stay in
  the new key center for it to be considered a key change and not just a modulation? I can't answer
  that one."* Cf. [[HAR-C10 — Tonicization vs modulation]].
- **Modal interchange** — borrowing from parallel modes with no change of tonic
  ([[Modal Interchange]]).

### Axis 1 — the seven mechanisms

| Mechanism | Defining trait |
|---|---|
| **Direct (phrase)** | no harmonic or melodic setup at all. The farther the keys, the more "direct" it feels |
| **Common-tone** | one note bridges the keys — **including enharmonically** (G♯/A♭). Since all keys share at least two tones, this reaches *any* key |
| **Common-chord (pivot)** | keys a fifth apart share **4** diatonic chords; two fifths apart, **2**. Extended by borrowed and secondary chords, and by symmetry: one dim7 can stand for six spellings, one augmented triad for six |
| **Chromatic** | a diatonic chord is *altered* to lead out. "The case for a chromatic modulation can just about be made for any non-diatonic movement" |
| **Sequential** | the progression itself is picked up and moved. "I don't think it matters as much to try and relate the two keys to each other" |
| **Chain** | travelling the circle of fifths without resolving — he equates this with **constant structure** (see [[Major Seventh Chords]]) |
| **Parallel (modal interchange)** | the tonic note doesn't move at all |

### Axis 2 — the visual models

Three diagrams, all worth knowing:

1. **The key wheel** (Berklee) — clockwise **brightens** (adding sharps), counterclockwise
   **darkens** (adding flats). See [[Circle of Fifths]].
2. **Schoenberg's chart of the regions** — shows there are usually *several* routes to the same key.
   D minor from C major is either the minor v of V or the relative minor of IV, and those are
   different journeys.
3. **Chromatic mediants as a separate axis** the fifths diagram cannot show — A and A♭ are chromatic
   submediants of C, E and E♭ chromatic mediants, each sharing one note with the tonic triad
   ([[Chromatic Mediants and Pop Modulation]], [[POP-C11 — Chromatic mediants and pop modulation]]).

**Six fifths = six semitones.** The tritone is simultaneously the farthest harmonic and the farthest
melodic distance between two keys — the same coincidence noted in [[The Harmonic Lattice]].

## The idea worth stealing: harmonic vs. melodic key changes

This is his signature contribution and it is genuinely useful.

Past roughly five fifths, the ear stops modelling the move as *fifths* and starts modelling it as a
**bodily transposition**:

> "With C major to D♭ major (C locrian), 5 notes are flattened. With C major to C♯ major, every note
> is raised one semitone."

Same twelve keys on the piano, two entirely different mental operations. He calls the second a
**"melodic key change"** as against a **harmonic** one, and notes it only convinces at a half or
whole step — "because the farther you go away, the less convincing it is." That maps straight onto
the mechanism axis: common-tone and common-chord modulations are harmonic; direct and sequential
ones are melodic. A whole-step move he calls a **"double lift"** — up two semitones *and* two fifths
at once.

**Practical upshot for a pop writer:** the last-chorus lift is not a weaker version of a real
modulation. It is a different device that works by a different mechanism, and treating it as a
transposition rather than a key relationship is why it needs no preparation.

## Distance by distance

| Move | Common tones | Behaviour | Worked examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **+1** (half step up) | 2 (5 fifths apart) | abrupt; the classic last-chorus lift. Usually I→♭II | The 1975, "I Couldn't Be More in Love" · Stevie Wonder, "With Each Beat of My Heart" (G9sus4 slides to A♭9sus4) and "Saturn" · MGMT, "Metanoia" |
| **−1** | 2 | the mirror — a *darker* last chorus | MGMT, "The Youth" (B♭ = IV in F, SubV/IV in E) · Michael Jackson, "Stranger in Moscow" · Dot Hacker, "Floating up the Stairs" |
| **+2** | 5 | "one of the most popular modulations in modern music" — close enough to hear melodically too | Queen, "We Are the Champions" · Tame Impala, "Let It Happen" · Beach Boys, "Good Vibrations" (F♯→G♯→A♯, textbook sequential) |
| **−2** | 5 | darkens | Silk Sonic, "Leave the Door Open" · Jacob Collier, "In the Real Early Morning" |
| **+3** | | resembles major→parallel minor, because the target's relative minor *is* the parallel minor. Usually darkens | Bonnie Tyler, "Total Eclipse of the Heart" · MGMT, "The Handshake" and "Flash Delirium" |
| **−3** | | resembles minor→major; usually **brightens** | MGMT, "It's Working" · Stevie Wonder, "You and I" · Phony Ppl, "Either Way" |
| **+4** | **3** | distant. The tonic triads are chromatic mediants. Sometimes it is really a *diminished fourth* | Celine Dion, "All By Myself" — **G→C♭, a diminished fourth, not a major third** · MGMT, "Alien Days" |
| **−4** | 3 | mirror; usually darkens | Beach Boys, "Good Vibrations" · **Liszt, "Un Sospiro"** (written D♭→A, harmonically D♭→B𝄫) |
| **+5** (up a fourth) | 6 | **"probably the most popular type of modulation"** — one note different, and it **darkens** (the 7 flattens ≈ borrowing Mixolydian). Can pass unnoticed | Queen, "Bohemian Rhapsody" (B♭→E♭ inside 30 seconds) · Panic! at the Disco, "Impossible Year" · Stevie Wonder, "Lately" |
| **−5** (up a fifth) | 6 | **brightens** (the 4 sharpens ≈ Lydian) | Beach Boys, "God Only Knows" · MGMT, "I Found a Whistle" — *two* successive pivots, and the second retroactively flips the first chord's function |
| **±6** (tritone) | 2, one enharmonic → **no common triads** | the farthest possible. Pivots exist only through parallel-key borrowing | Queen, "Bohemian Rhapsody" (end of the guitar solo) · Toni Braxton, "Un-break My Heart" (B♭ as an enharmonic pivot) · **Thundercat, "I Love Louis Cole" — "this might be the only example of a purely direct modulation on this list"** |
| **±0** | — | see below | |

### The category nobody else has: modulating to an enharmonic equivalent

Net displacement **zero semitones**, but harmonically **up an augmented seventh / down a diminished
second**. The song lands back on the same keys and is somewhere else.

- **Mel Tormé, "The Christmas Song"** — C → F → Am → E → D♯ → B♯maj7.
- **Louis Cole, "Phone"** — D♭ → D → C♯.
- **Thundercat, "Pozole"** — the only one that travels the other way (F → G♭ → G𝄫), and **the melody
  is what proves it**: line 2 is line 1 a semitone up, diatonic to G♭.
- **Thundercat, "A.D.D. Through the Roof"** — one key per line, G → A♭ → E𝄫 → C♭ → A𝄫, cycling back
  to an enharmonic G.
- **Liszt, "Un Sospiro"** — D♭ → C♯, exploiting dim7 symmetry down minor thirds.

This is the same phenomenon [[The Harmonic Lattice]] describes as a **comma**: the piece returns
home and home has moved.

## The parallel-key chapters

- **Major → minor** — Queen, "We Are the Champions" · RHCP, "She Looks to Me" (**Asus2 is diatonic to
  both** and is the transition chord — a clean trick) · MGMT, "Brian Eno."
- **Minor → major** — Tame Impala & Thundercat, "No More Lies" (via A♭7 as the backdoor ♭VII7) ·
  M83, "Solitude," which he calls a textbook Picardy cadence ([[Picardy Third]]).
- **Oscillating** — Stevie Wonder, "I Love You Too Much" · Thundercat, "Friend Zone" · James Blake,
  "Retrograde" · Justice, "Saturnine," where he prints **three simultaneous analyses** and declines
  to pick.

## Multiple modulations — the two best cases

**Billy Joel, "She's Always a Woman"** — eight tonal shifts through six keys: B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭, G♭,
C♭. Those are **exactly E♭ Lydian, Ionian, Mixolydian, Dorian, Aeolian and Phrygian**, so the whole
chorus can be re-read as one key with modal borrowing. A modulation chain and a modal-interchange
passage are the same object seen twice — cf. [[Modal Interchange]] and the constant-structure
mode-slide in [[Major Seventh Chords]].

**Michael Jackson, "I Just Can't Stop Loving You"** — C → E♭ → G♭ → F♭ → D♭. Each step is
"modulating to the relative major of the parallel minor," and the net result is a half step up
"without really sounding like a big change is occurring." A half-step lift smuggled in as four
small ones.

Also: **Miles Davis, "Flamenco Sketches"** — five sections, each its own key *and* scale — C Ionian,
A♭ Mixolydian, B♭ Ionian, D Phrygian dominant, G Dorian. *"You can think of each mode as its own
chord."* ([[Modal Jazz]])

## What to keep, and what to translate

**Keep** — the two-axis method; harmonic vs. melodic key change; the enharmonic-modulation category;
the fact that the same passage often supports several key readings at once, and that printing them
all is more honest than picking one.

**Translate** — he insists on unplayable spellings (B𝄫, D𝄫, G𝄫, B♯, C𝄪, A𝄫) to preserve harmonic
relationships, and stacks three to five Roman-numeral analyses over one chord line. That is the
document's most distinctive habit and the hardest to reproduce; in this vault, name the standard key
and put his spelling in a parenthesis.

⚠️ Two terminology notes: he calls a ♭II→I resolution a **"phrygian cadence"** (classically that is
iv6–V), and he uses **"super-lydian"** and **"super-locrian"** for scales built by stacking fifths
past Lydian or Locrian — the ♯1, ♯5, ♯2 side and the ♭1, ♭4, 𝄫7 side, a name he credits to Jacob
Collier. Neither is school vocabulary.
