# You Suck at Producing: Understanding Volume, dB & Clipping

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fp2o_CzG8TA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fp2o_CzG8TA)

## Synopsis
The most fundamentals-level video in this batch (2016): what decibels actually measure, why 0 dB is a hard ceiling rather than a suggestion, why dB is logarithmic and what that means practically, and the difference between peak and average (RMS) metering as shown on Ableton's stock clip volume meters.

## Techniques
- **0 dB is the ceiling, not a target** — volume is measured in dB, with 0 dB as both the reference point and the ceiling; go above it into positive dB and the sound starts to distort (clip). Keep meaningful headroom — the gap between peak levels and 0 dB — at every stage.
- **dB is logarithmic — the same-sized step matters more the closer you are to 0** — the perceived jump in loudness from 0 dB to −6 dB is bigger than the jump from −6 dB to −12 dB, even though both are 6 dB steps. As a rule of thumb, every 6 dB is roughly a doubling (or halving) of perceived loudness. → [[Gain Staging]]
- **Peak vs. average (RMS) metering are two different readings of the same signal** — on Ableton's stock clip volume meter, the thin transparent lines are the peak level (with an exact numeric dB readout), while the solid bars underneath are the average loudness over time (RMS — the "long view" of the signal, not just its highest instant). Watching only one of the two gives an incomplete picture of how loud something actually is.
- **Clipping is audible and visible once you cross 0 dB** — pushing a clip's volume past 0 dB (and again at the master) produces obvious, harsh distortion — a direct demonstration of why the ceiling matters in practice, not just in theory.
