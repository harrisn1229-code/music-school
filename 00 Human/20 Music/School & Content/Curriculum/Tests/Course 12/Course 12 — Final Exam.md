> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **The idea was never bad — it was alone. It's asking for a B section, not a new idea**
> Loop-itis is a misdiagnosis rather than a skill gap, and it's the biggest single reason projects die in the folder.
>
> **Q2.** **No amount of subtraction sustains a song — expect most second-loop attempts to be discardable**
> The discards are the cost of the method, not evidence that it's going wrong. Stopping after the first failed B section is the actual failure mode.
>
> **Q3.** **Duplicate the drums and strip everything · write a complementary part · test the return**
> Complementary means different chords, opposite density and a different mood. The return is the test: coming out of B back into A should feel like arriving home.
>
> **Q4.** **That B isn't finished — different isn't the target, making A better is**
> *Rubric (5):* says a B section that's merely different has only done half the job (2) · says the target is a return that lifts, which means B has to recontextualize A (2) · gives something concrete to change, such as pushing the density, mood or harmony further in the complementary direction, or trying the other harmonic route (1)
> The trap is that "different" is easy to verify and "makes the return land" isn't, so it's tempting to accept the first and assume the second follows. It doesn't. A B section can differ in every measurable way and still leave A sounding exactly as it did before, which means the listener has been given a detour rather than a structure. What to change depends on which direction it's under-committed in. If B is different but comparable in energy, take the density much further down so A comes back as an arrival rather than as a continuation. If the mood is close, push it properly — a loose, sparse, sadder B under a full A. If the harmony is doing nothing, switch routes: try the relative key if you used the withheld chord, or the reverse.
>
> **Q5.** **Relative key guarantees the notes still fit · withheld chord guarantees the arrival is unfamiliar**
> Both solve the same problem of new but not random, from opposite ends.
>
> **Q6.** **Up three semitones from the minor root — identical scale tones, so nothing can clash**
> The relative major of A minor is C. Same seven notes with a different centre, which is why the contrast is real and the way back resolves without planning.
>
> **Q7.** **Arrangement interest is scheduled absence, not added layers**
> The worked example enters hi-hat, then clap, then clap with snares, then kick, then bass, then everything at the hook — minutes of interest from one idea and no new material.
>
> **Q8.** **Reflex is to add · the move is to decide what leaves and when · attenuate the top end instead of muting**
> Pull the kick for a section, or the 808, or all the drums, or the melody. Where a full mute is too much, taking an element's top end away keeps it in the arrangement while it stops competing.
>
> **Q9.** **Start low, build to exactly one apex, pull back after it — holding the peak reads as exhausting**
> A track that reaches maximum and stays there has a plateau, not a peak. Coming down a notch afterward is what tells the listener retrospectively where the top was.
>
> **Q10.** **Three to four elements simultaneously, not gradually — punctuated with a crash**
> Gradual change reads as drift. Simultaneity is what makes the boundary land as a felt jump.
>
> **Q11.** **House is flat 16 bars · pop is proportional, and the chorus gets longer each return**
> The pop template runs roughly a 4-bar intro with verse, pre-chorus and chorus each about double that. The chorus is teased on its first appearance and is longest at the very end.
>
> **Q12.** **The convention has to be well established, or the break reads as sloppy rather than considered**
> You can't deliberately break a rule you can't state. A verse-drop-verse-drop at speed with a 3-bar buildup only works because the listener already knows the pattern being violated.
>
> **Q13.** **Most weren't using risers or swooshes — the technique is automation across the section**
> Filters, detune, sends and wet/dry, automated through the two sections rather than inserted between them. That relocates the transition from an object into a property of the sections either side.
>
> **Q14.** **Micro drop · macro drop · wet/dry drop**
> Micro is an instant filter wet/dry snap with a gradual percussion sweep. Macro goes for maximum contrast via kick filtering, bass removal, slight detune and a timbre shift. Wet/dry contrasts wet against dry and wide against mono instead of contrasting tone.
>
> **Q15.** **One element carried unchanged across the boundary — contrast and connective tissue are separate jobs**
> Splitting them is what lets everything else change completely while the join still holds.
>
> **Q16.** **Automate across the boundary, carry one element through, and strip the layers just before it**
> *Rubric (5):* automates existing devices across both sections rather than inserting anything — filters, sends, detune, wet/dry (2) · carries one recognisable element unchanged through the boundary (2) · strips the added layers in the last bars so the payoff arrives against space (1)
> With the riser gone the join is exposed, which is the useful state to be in — you can now hear what was actually missing. Start by making the two sections reach toward each other: automate a filter opening across the last bars of the outgoing section and closing into the new one, ride a send so the tail of one washes into the head of the other, and let a small detune or timbre move run through the boundary rather than at it. Then pick one thing — the motif, the lead sound, whatever the section is identifiable by — and let it play through untouched, so there's a thread the listener can hold. Finally, clear the added layers in the last bar or two so the new section lands against space rather than against a wall. If a riser still adds something after all three are in place, it's now icing on a join that already works.
>
> **Q17.** **Cut every added layer — the emptiness gives the payoff something to land against**
> Only the most essential element carries through into the boundary. The last gesture before maximum is minimum, and the sudden reduction reads as tension release rather than as a dropout.
>
> **Q18.** **Muting inside a section · stripping before a drop · staggered entrances across the song**
> *Rubric (5):* names subtraction inside a section — muting or attenuating an element, or the five-to-seven-sound referee (1) · names stripping the layers immediately before a payoff (2) · names staggered entrances and scheduled absence across the whole arrangement (1) · says all three work because contrast, not level, is what the listener perceives (1)
> At the smallest scale it's the simplicity referee and the attenuated top end — deciding that a part is competing and removing it or getting it out of the way. In the middle it's the bars before a drop, where everything you spent the build stacking comes out at once so the payoff has somewhere to arrive. At the largest it's the shape of the whole track: entrances staggered so one idea can hold attention for minutes, and an energy curve that starts deliberately low and comes back down after its apex. What they share is that none of them is about how much is playing. Each is about the size of the difference between one moment and the next, because that difference is the only thing a listener actually perceives — which is why the reflex to add is wrong at all three scales, and why deciding what leaves is the arranging skill.
