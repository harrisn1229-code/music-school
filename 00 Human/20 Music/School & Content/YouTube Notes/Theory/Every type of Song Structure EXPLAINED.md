# Every type of Song Structure EXPLAINED

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SDJwg1JoPtY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SDJwg1JoPtY)

## Synopsis

A comprehensive taxonomy of pop/rock song-section and whole-song forms, from the simplest single-section strophic form up through the pre-chorus, bridge, coda and post-chorus, and the 32-bar AABA form inherited from the Great American Songbook — worked with real song examples throughout, and careful to disambiguate terms that are commonly confused (bridge vs. middle eight, modern "chorus" vs. the old show-tune sense of the word).

## What's taught

- **Strophic form (AAA…)**: one section repeated with no contrast — used where a crowd needs to follow along easily with no surprises: church hymns, national anthems, children's songs, folk songs.
- **Binary form (AB, i.e. verse/chorus only)**: two contrasting sections alternating, no third section — "Hotel California," "Foxy Lady" (Hendrix), "Honky Tonk Woman" (Rolling Stones).
- **Adding a third section, three ways**:
  - **Pre-chorus (ABC)**: a transitional section between verse and chorus with its own chords/melody. Clear-cut example: "Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds" (Beatles) — verse in A major, pre-chorus modulates to B♭ major, chorus arrives in yet another key AND time signature, so all three sections are unmistakably distinct. The line can also blur to the point a section could be classified either way: "Hello" (Adele)'s pre-chorus changes chords/melody but keeps the verse's mood/dynamic, so it's equally valid to call the song plain binary (verse+chorus) as it is to name a separate pre-chorus.
  - **Bridge**: a later-song section (usually final third) that deliberately shifts tonally or dynamically to "refresh the palette" before returning. Often marked with a key change specifically for the bridge, sometimes with a second key change back afterward: "Summer of '69" (Bryan Adams, D major → F major for the bridge), "Clocks" (Coldplay, E♭ Mixolydian → D♭ major), "Say It Ain't So" (Weezer, E♭ major → B♭ major).
  - **Post-chorus**: like a pre-chorus but placed AFTER the chorus instead of before it — distinct from both the chorus and a return to the verse. Example: "Smells Like Teen Spirit" (Nirvana).
- **Coda**: an added section tacked onto the very end of the song (Italian for "tail" — the more casual "outro" is really a backwards-formed shortening of the nonexistent word "outroduction"). Can dwarf the rest of the song in length: "Hey Jude" (Beatles) spends far more time on its coda than on the entire verse/chorus body that precedes it; "Layla" (Derek and the Dominos) similarly closes with an extended coda section.
- **32-bar AABA form**: dominant song form of 1930s-50s American Songbook standards/show tunes, four 8-bar sections — A (repeated), contrasting B, then A again. Persisted into some modern songs: "Wake Me Up When September Ends" (Green Day). The B section here is specifically called the **middle eight** — a term often confused with "bridge" but structurally distinct (occupies the SAME structural role a bridge fills in verse/chorus form, but the surrounding sections are AABA's A sections, not verse/chorus). A song can carry BOTH a middle eight and a separate bridge: "Every Breath You Take" (The Police) has 32-bar AABA form (with its own middle eight) plus an additional distinct bridge section layered on top.
- **The old meaning of "chorus" and the vanished show-tune "verse."** In 32-bar AABA songs, the entire AABA structure was traditionally called the chorus, and a separate, slower, freely-tempo'd section BEFORE it — called the "verse" — carried spoken-style narrative setup for the stage musical the song came from ("I Got Rhythm," written for the 1930 musical *Girl Crazy*, opens with exactly this kind of scene-setting verse before the familiar "I got rhythm" material kicks in). Over decades of cover recordings this intro "verse" fell out of use and got cut, and the naming convention flipped along with it — "chorus" now means what was originally the AABA main body, not the theater-sense verse that preceded it.
