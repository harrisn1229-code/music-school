# Outside Blues Lines with Triads!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Os6ETse9Y_s)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Os6ETse9Y_s)

## Synopsis

Jack Ruch teaches one focused device for playing "outside" over a **blues I→IV move** (worked in A: A7 → D7) using the **half-whole (dominant) diminished scale** — the same scale and I-to-IV blues application already documented on this vault from his earlier video [[5 Lines To Get You Playing The Diminished Scale]], but this video isolates just the **triad-pair** angle of that scale rather than surveying five different lick types over a 251. The core idea: because the half-whole diminished scale is symmetrical (built from alternating half/whole steps, repeats every minor third), it contains **four major triads spaced a minor third apart**, and pairing two of those triads together gives a fretboard-friendly way to outline the scale's outside color without having to think in scale-degree terms. Two worked triad-pair licks are given, each demonstrated at two positions a minor third apart (four total fingerings, two underlying shapes) — Ruch credits **Robin Ford** (also naming Larry Carlton, Mike Landau, and Scott Henderson as related players) as the source of this specific "triad pairs over the I-IV blues move" vocabulary. Short, single-concept lesson (9:37) — no tab/notation in the free video (gated to Patreon), so all fingering below is reconstructed from the spoken note names.

## What's taught

- **The half-whole diminished scale, built live in A.** Alternating half step then whole step from the root: **A, B♭, C, C♯, D♯, E, F♯, G**, back to A — an 8-note scale, same construction and same "dominant"/half-whole (as opposed to whole-half/fully-diminished) identity already documented on [[Diminished Chords]] and [[Scale Modes Reference]]. Stated use: play it over **dominant chords**, and in a blues specifically **to get from the I chord to the IV chord** — restates the exact "Robin Ford, I→IV via this scale" framing already on [[5 Lines To Get You Playing The Diminished Scale]] and [[Blues Form and Harmony]], here without the intermediate 251 (straight A7 → D7, no ii chord inserted).
- **Symmetry → four major triads, a minor third apart.** Because the scale repeats every minor third (3 frets), any triad found inside it recurs three more times up/down the neck. Spotted directly in the A half-whole diminished scale: **A-C♯-E = A major triad**; moving up in minor thirds gives **C major** (C-E-G), **D♯/E♭ major** (D♯-G-A♯), and **F♯/G♭ major** (F♯-A♯-C♯) — four major triads, all playable from the same scale, all a minor third (3 frets) apart. Same triads-inside-a-symmetric-scale mechanism as [[Diminished Chords]]'s existing "5 Lines" entry (B♭/D♭/E/G major in that video's key of G), reconfirmed here in a different key (A) and routed straight at a blues turnaround instead of a 251.
- **Lick 1 (low position) — A major + F♯ major triad pair, over A7 → D7.** Ascend an **A major triad**, hit the **♭7 (G)**, then descend an **F♯ major triad from the top down** — root (F♯) on top, then the 5th (C♯) and 3rd (A♯) below it — then drop into a short run of the half-whole diminished scale, then **slide up into the 3rd and root of D**, phrased so the line lands exactly on D's chord tones as the chord changes (A7→D7, the I→IV move). This is the specific device credited to **Robin Ford**: "add tension [over the I chord] and then resolve to the [IV] chord."
- **Lick 1, relocated a minor third up — C major + A major triad pair, same shapes.** Identical fingering pattern, moved up 3 frets: pairs **C major** with **A major** (the scale's next two triads up), outlines them the same way, then again finds D7's chord tones to resolve into. Demonstrates the pair is a **movable shape**, not a fixed-position lick — one fingering pattern covers two of the scale's four triad pairs just by sliding it.
- **Lick 2 (top three strings) — A major + D♯ major triad pair, chromatic enclosure into the 5th of D7.** Ascend an **A major triad** up to the note **C**, then descend a **D♯ major triad**, drop briefly outside the scale, then **walk chromatically up into the 5th of D7 (A)** — Ruch names this closing move explicitly as a **chromatic enclosure** (chromatic notes surrounding the target chord tone, landing on it exactly as the chord changes). Played on the **top three strings** specifically, a different string set from lick 1.
- **Lick 2, relocated a minor third up — C major + F♯ major triad pair, resolves onto D7's 7th instead of its 5th.** Same fingering, moved up 3 frets: pairs **C major** with **F♯ major** (the scale's other two triads), same chromatic-enclosure ending, but this time lands on **C — the ♭7 of D7** — rather than the 5th, since the whole shape (including the target note) has been transposed with it.
- **Practice takeaway, stated directly.** "The whole key to this is knowing your triad shapes all over the fretboard" — same fundamentals-first framing as [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]]. Explicit encouragement to keep experimenting: move these two shapes to **different string sets** and **different fretboard positions** beyond the two positions demonstrated (there are two more minor-third relocations of each pair not shown in the video, since the scale yields four total triads per pair-family).
- **Named reference listening:** Robin Ford (the specific I→IV tension-and-release device), Larry Carlton, Mike Landau, Scott Henderson — "outside licks in a blues setting" players generally. Genres named as applications beyond straight blues: funk, fusion, jazz.
- **Paywalled extras:** tabs, notation, and backing tracks for this lesson (and all of Ruch's lessons) are on his Patreon — not present in the free video or transcript.
