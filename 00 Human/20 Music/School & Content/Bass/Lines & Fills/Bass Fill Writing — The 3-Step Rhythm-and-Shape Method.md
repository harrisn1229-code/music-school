# Bass Fill Writing — The 3-Step Rhythm-and-Shape Method

BassBuzz's dedicated answer to "what do I actually play for a bass fill" — a repeatable **pick rhythm → pick shape → combine** process that deliberately front-loads rhythm over note choice ("even if the notes you play are garbage, if you sell the rhythm it'll still kind of work"). This is the vault's first note built specifically around **fill-writing as its own skill** (as opposed to the connective-tissue chromatic-walk fills on [[Bass Walkup and Walkdown Fills]], or the harmonic-skeleton material on [[Jamerson's Root-Fifth-Octave Exercise]] and [[Root-Fifth Bass Lines and the 3-Step Style Rhythm Formula]]) and it introduces a genuinely new shape to the vault — the **major/minor "triangle" shape** — alongside a fill-specific usage of the already-documented root-fifth-octave shape. Full worked source: [[3 Steps to Killer Bass Fills (for Beginners)]].

## Step 1 — Pick a rhythm

Three questions, in order, before any note is chosen:

1. **Where does the fill go?** The end of a musical unit — typically the **end of a 4-bar phrase**, or the end of a larger section (verse → chorus). Fills are lead-ins/transitions, not decorations dropped mid-phrase.
2. **How much space?** **2 or 4 beats** is the typical fill length.
3. **What subdivision?** Start simple: **two quarter notes**, **eighth-eighth-quarter**, or **four eighth notes**. *(general starting point: pick whichever of the three feels most natural to count first — none is "more correct," they're just increasing note-density options for the same space.)*

**Leveling up:** for a more complex/intense fill rhythm, **imagine a drum fill in that spot and copy its rhythm onto the bass**, then plug shape-notes into it. The rhythm is what reads as "intense," independent of which notes you choose on top of it — proven in the source video with an A/B of the same borrowed rhythm played with two different note sets.

## Step 2 — Pick a shape

Two shapes, chosen by how much you know about the chord under the fill:

### Root-fifth-octave — use when you don't know (or aren't sure) if the chord is major or minor
Quality-blind by design — works identically over major or minor. Full theory/derivation lives on [[Jamerson's Root-Fifth-Octave Exercise]] (ingredient 1: root-fifth-octave as harmonic skeleton) and [[Root-Fifth Bass Lines and the 3-Step Style Rhythm Formula]] (why root-fifth specifically is acoustically stable — the 3:2 frequency ratio); this note's contribution is just the fill-specific application: find the root of the **last chord in the phrase** (the chord the fill leads into/decorates), stack fifth and octave on top, and you have your note pool. → [[BASS Slot]] · [[Chords]] § *Triads*.

### The triangle shape — use when you DO know the chord's quality (NEW shape, not previously documented in this vault)
A three-note shape, positioned differently for major vs. minor, giving a more melodic, Motown-flavored fill than root-fifth-octave in exchange for needing to know the chord quality first:

- **Major chord:** **root = top note** (the highest-pitched note of the three), with **two flavor notes below it**, drawn from the **major scale** of that root.
- **Minor chord:** same physical shape, but **root = middle note at the far end of the triangle**, with **one flavor note below and one above**, drawn from the **minor scale** of that root.
- **Theory-optional:** the shape can be memorized and played by ear without knowing *why* the flavor notes are scale-appropriate — the video explicitly offers "skip the theory, just memorize the shape" as a valid path.
- **What it actually is, harmonically:** on a minor chord the two flavor notes land on the **♭7 (below root) and ♭3 (above root)** — see the worked G-minor example below — making the shape functionally a **rootless partial min7 arpeggio fragment** wrapped around the root. → [[Seventh Chords]], [[Chords]] § *Triads*. The major-chord flavor notes weren't named as scale degrees in the source (only "from the D major scale"), so treat the major side as scale-color rather than a confirmed arpeggio fragment until a clearer example turns up.
- → [[Bass Scale Shapes — Major, Minor, Pentatonic & Blues]] (sibling shape-vocabulary note — add this shape there if a second source confirms the exact degrees).

## Step 3 — Combine: assign shape-notes to rhythm-slots

- Match the number of rhythm slots to notes needed (four eighth notes = 4 notes; eighth-eighth-quarter = 3 notes).
- **You don't have to use every note of the shape, and you don't have to use them in order.** Repeat notes freely (e.g. root-root-fifth-fifth is as valid as root-fifth-octave-fifth).
- **Try it live over the track first** — the video's stated default — and fall back to planning it out on paper only if real-time experimentation feels overwhelming.

## Worked examples

**Root-fifth-octave, over G major (1 bar) → E minor (1 bar) → D major (2 bars), 2-beat fill, four eighth notes.** Last chord = D major → root = D, shape = D-A-D (root-fifth-octave). Two fills tried: **root–fifth–octave–fifth** (D-A-D-A) and **octave–fifth–root–root** (D-A-D-D) — same shape, two different note orderings/repeats, both usable.

**Major triangle, same G–Em–D progression, same four-eighth-note rhythm.** Root = D (top of triangle), flavor notes from D major scale. Fill tried: **D–D–A–B**. *(The source audio's playback readback of this exact fill is garbled in the transcript — the spoken setup line "D D a B" is the reliable read; treat it as such rather than a note-for-note-verified performance.)*

**Minor triangle, over Gm (1 bar) → E♭ major (1 bar) → Cm (1 bar) → Gm (1 bar), same rhythm.** Last chord = G minor → root = G (far-end-middle of triangle), flavor notes from G minor scale: **F below (♭7)**, **B♭ above (♭3)**. Fill tried: **F–G–B♭–F**.

## Knowing when NOT to fill

Named as the next-level skill once the mechanics above are solid, deliberately left as a closing thought rather than a formula: **"sometimes there will be a big glorious space in the music for your awesome bass fill, sometimes there might be another fill in the way."** No rule given — it's a listening judgment (don't stack your fill on top of another instrument's fill in the same space; leave room when the arrangement doesn't need one), not a mechanical check.
