# 10 Hidden Soloing Mistakes and Constraint-Based Fixes

Brandon D'Eon's list of 10 intermediate-level soloing habits, deliberately chosen to skip the commonly-repeated advice (stuck in the box, too scalar) in favor of subtler patterns players usually can't hear in their own playing. The tenth mistake — never practicing with constraints — is the meta-fix for the other nine: convert a diagnosed weakness into a targeted, constrained drill rather than practicing generally.

## The 10 mistakes

1. **Leaving the scale before it's actually learned.** A shaky, unconfident solo usually isn't "too scalar" — it's that the scale shape underneath isn't solid enough yet to even risk sounding scalar. Nail the shape before adding variation.
2. **Escaping the box before mastering it.** Most players know one scale shape and still can't reliably target chord tones from inside it. Drill chord-tone targeting within a single familiar shape (e.g. over Em–G–D–C) before adding new positions.
3. **Starting every line on beat 1.** The instinctive default; deliberately start phrases on other beats/offbeats to build rhythmic variety — a marker of professional-level phrasing.
4. **Always starting/targeting the root.** A byproduct of always practicing scales starting from the root. Practice starting runs from other scale degrees so lines stop gravitating back to root by habit.
5. **Rhythmic sloppiness in single-note lines.** The single worst mistake on the list — not a technique-capability problem but an anticipation problem: improvising means knowing what you'll play a few beats before you play it (like timing a jump onto a moving train car). Playing with intention (pre-decided) rather than uncertainty (searching in real time) is what produces rhythmic precision.
6. **Playing faster than your actual limit.** Find your personal **16th-note-limit tempo** (fastest BPM for clean unbroken 16ths), then derive every other subdivision's safe ceiling by ratio — e.g. a 100 BPM 16th-note limit implies clean 8ths up to ~200 BPM, triplets up to ~133 BPM, sextuplets up to ~67 BPM. Exceeding the derived ceiling for a subdivision is what produces sloppy fast lines.
7. **Abandoning lines/motifs too early.** Most solos never repeat a motif — root cause is usually insufficient fretboard/key knowledge to safely continue an idea without risking a wrong note, so players unconsciously bail. Requires deeper fretboard knowledge, not just willpower, to fix.
8. **No silence.** The easiest fix on the list: deliberately leave space rather than filling every beat.
9. **Restarting/going silent at chord changes (advanced).** Shows up specifically on multi-key progressions where a bar's chords cross a key boundary — players who can't connect the transition often just stop through it ("no man's land"), producing a repeated stop-start pattern that reads as amateurish. Practice targeting the transition beat/chord directly rather than treating it as a gap to survive.
10. **Never practicing with constraints (the meta-fix).** Assign yourself a specific constraint engineered to attack one diagnosed weakness — stay in one position, restrict to only eighth notes, force more space — rather than practicing generally. Framed as the single highest-leverage habit on the list, since it converts a named weakness directly into a targeted drill.
