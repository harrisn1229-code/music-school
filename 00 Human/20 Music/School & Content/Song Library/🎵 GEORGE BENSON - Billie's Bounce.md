# Billie's Bounce — George Benson

> A 12-bar jazz blues in F (Charlie Parker's tune). Jens Larsen calls George Benson's solo on this recording probably the best jazz blues solo he knows, and spends a full video chunk-analyzing four passages of it — the same transcribe → chunk → name-the-device → replay-your-own-version method as [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]]. **Transcript confidence:** auto-captions garbled jazz terminology heavily; read through context, not verbatim — see [[George Benson - This is The Best Jazz Blues Solo I know]] for the full caveat.

## Chords
**Key:** F · **Form:** 12-bar bebop blues (the "Parker blues" reharmonization of a plain 12-bar).

**Bars 1–4:** **F7 (I7)** held, sometimes reharmonized in bar 4 with a secondary **Cm7 → F7 (ii–V of B♭)** to set up the move to IV. **Bars 5–6: B♭7 (IV7).** **Bars 7–8:** back to **F7 (I7)**, often with a secondary **Am7 → D7 (iii7–VI7)** landing the turnaround on ii. **Bars 9–12 (the turnaround):** **Gm7 (ii) → C7 (V7) → F7 (I7) → C7** — closing on the dominant rather than resolving home, the Parker trademark that keeps the chorus rolling into the next one.

Exact substitutions (a diminished passing chord in bar 6, extra ii–V's in bars 4/8) vary chart to chart — this is the commonly cited skeleton. Benson's solo (analyzed below) treats every dominant as a launchpad for the same handful of arpeggio/enclosure devices regardless of which secondary chord is passing underneath. See [[Blues Form and Harmony]], [[The ii-V-I]], [[Turnarounds]].

## Solo concepts by passage (Jens Larsen's analysis)
Four passages, each reduced to a device Benson leans on repeatedly across the whole solo — full write-up on [[George Benson - This is The Best Jazz Blues Solo I know]].

- **Turnaround, C7 → F7 (early in the solo):** a B♭-major-9-type arpeggio/pentatonic idea built off C7's own ♭7 (upper extensions rather than the plain triad), sliding up into an enclosure around the B♭ — then resolving onto F7 with a blues double-stop (sustained D over G-A♭-F, "almost Dorian over F7") possibly borrowed from Wes Montgomery's playing on "No Blues" — then a plain F-major-pentatonic phrase (3rd-5th-6th-root). Bebop language into blues language into major-pentatonic language, back to back in one short passage. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]], [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]]
- **Minor ii-V turnaround into G minor:** rather than a parent scale, Benson treats G minor 7 as a **plain G-B♭-D triad** and decorates it entirely with **chromatic leading (approach) notes** into each chord tone — producing wide, unpredictable interval skips that still resolve correctly because the target is always a chord tone. Two variations of the same idea are played back a chorus apart. → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] (Shapes 1–3), [[Bebop Vocabulary]]
- **His recurring dominant-chord device — half-diminished arpeggio off the chord's own 3rd:** over C7, an E half-diminished-shaped arpeggio (E-G-B♭-D = 3-5-♭7-9 of C7) skipping down to the 3rd and back up to the 7th before resolving — called out as recurring "again and again" across the whole solo whenever a dominant chord passes. A more "inside"/consonant cousin of the full-dim7-off-the-3rd device already documented on [[Diminished Chords]]. → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Chord-Tone Soloing]], [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]
- **Bar 1 bebop cliché + call-and-response, then an F-minor blues cliché reordered:** the 13th/6th-up-to-root cliché, a long chromatic climb from the ♭3 to the 5th, a bebop-scale-style descent to the root — then B♭7 answers with the melodic inverse of that same shape (call-and-response). Later, on B♭7, a standard F-minor blues cliché (A♭-C-G-F, off the chord's 5th) is played with the note order rearranged from how it's usually voiced — the same lick, kept fresh by resequencing. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]], [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]]

## Steal this
- **Half-diminished (not full dim7) arpeggio off a dominant's own 3rd** — a more "inside" alternative to the altered/full-dim7 version of the same "arpeggio off the 3rd" trick, worth having both colors ready. → [[Diminished Chords]]
- **Treat a ii-chord as a plain triad and decorate purely with chromatic leading notes, skip the scale entirely** — bigger, more surprising interval jumps than scalar playing, while still resolving correctly every time because the target is always a chord tone. → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]
- **Reorder a well-worn blues cliché's notes rather than retiring it** — same underlying lick, same harmonic logic, different sequencing keeps it sounding fresh.
- **Call-and-response across a chord change** — answer one chord's phrase with the melodic inverse of it on the next chord.
