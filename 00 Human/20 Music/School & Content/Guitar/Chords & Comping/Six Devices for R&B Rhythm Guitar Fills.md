# Six Devices for R&B Rhythm Guitar Fills

Part of [[Guitar Positions]]. Six concrete, genre-specific tools for filling the gaps between a lead vocal/melody's phrases with rhythm-guitar fills, worked over a three-chord R&B backing track (**A major – B minor – F♯ minor**, all diatonic to A major — I–ii–vi). Where [[Comping Fills]] gives the jazz-comping *philosophy* of fills (fill only when the soloist rests, connect forward to the next chord/section, build simple-then-color) and [[9th and 13th Chord Voicings for Blues Rhythm]] gives two specific fill licks for a blues 9th/13th-chord comping part, this page is the R&B/gospel-lineage **device catalog** — six distinct interval/shape vocabularies (not fill placement theory) any of which can decorate a chord voicing or bridge two chords. The theory-of-when framing from [[Comping Fills]] still applies here (fill in the space the vocal/melody leaves); this page is the theory-of-what.

## The setup

All six devices are demonstrated over the same progression, so the harmonic groundwork is shared: **A – Bm – F♯m**, all three chords diatonic to A major. That means the **A major scale / A major pentatonic** supplies every note for every device below — over the A chord it sounds like plain A major; over F♯m the same A-major-pentatonic notes are heard as **F♯ minor pentatonic** (its relative-minor reframing), which is what gives that chord its different color despite using identical fingerings/notes to the A-major fills.

## 1 — Butterfly fills (fast hammer-on/pull-off licks)

Named for the style's gospel/early-R&B origin — cited players **Curtis Mayfield and Bobby Womack**. Fast hammer-on/pull-off licks pulled from the **A major pentatonic scale**, played *around* the current chord voicing rather than as a standalone lead line — worked directly into the chord shape you're already fretting.
- Over **A major:** hammer from the **5th degree up to the 6th** while letting the open high **A** ring; separately, hammer from the **9th degree up to the major 3rd** with the **5th** ringing underneath.
- Over **B minor** and **F♯ minor:** the same hammer-on/pull-off device applied around each chord's own voicing shape — same technique, relocated to the new chord grip, still pulling only from the shared A major pentatonic pool.
- **Drill:** loop one chord at a time (A only, then Bm, then F♯m) against a static drone/backing track; find the hammer-on/pull-off inside that one chord shape first (don't chase new fingerings — the notes are already under your fretting hand in the chord grip) before switching chords.

## 2 — Triad-grouping fills

Uses the **three-string triad groupings** (top three strings / upper-middle three strings / lower-middle three strings — the same close-position triad system as [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]] and [[Layering Rhythm Guitar Parts with Triads]]) as fill material, either decorating one chord or **alternating between two different triads** for a mini-progression-within-the-fill.
- Worked moves: **A major triad → E major triad** (I → V, a fourth/fifth relationship), and **A major → D major** (I → IV).
- Each chord in the progression (A, Bm, F♯m) has its own set of these three-string triad shapes available in the same neck area — the fill vocabulary is "which triad grouping, which string set," not a fixed lick.
- **Drill:** isolate one triad grouping (e.g., top three strings) across all three chords first, then practice alternating pairs (A→E, A→D) purely as a rhythmic chop, before mixing groupings.

## 3 — Sixths (double stops a 6th apart)

Two notes separated by a **6th** (in A major: A up to F♯ is a 6th), diatonically harmonized and moved through the A major scale under the chord progression. Cited as the classic **Steve Cropper / Memphis Soul** sound. Because the shapes are diatonic 6th intervals inside A major, the same double-stop shape slides scale-degree to scale-degree and stays in key without any per-chord adjustment — move it under A, Bm, or F♯m and it still fits.
- **Drill:** play the 6th-interval shape as a straight ascending/descending run through the A major scale first (no rhythm, just the shapes), then apply it as fills on beats/subdivisions that land in the gaps of the vocal melody.

## 4 — Fourths (double stops a 4th apart)

Two notes separated by a **4th** (E up to A is a 4th in this key). Also a defining R&B double-stop sound. Two named grip pairs slide diatonically:
- **9th+5th → 3rd+6th:** starting on **B with E on top** (9th + 5th of A), slide up to **C♯ with F♯ on top** (3rd + 6th of A).
- **5th+root → 6th+9th(-ish):** **E + A** (5th + root) slides a whole step up to **F♯ + B** (6th + 2nd/9th).
- **Over the minor chords (Bm):** play the **♭7 + minor 3rd** above the chord, then slide that up a whole step to land on **root + 4th** — same shape family, minor-chord-specific starting interval.
- **Drill:** learn the major-chord pair (9th+5th → 3rd+6th) first against the A chord alone, then the minor-chord variant against Bm, before combining both into fills across the full progression.

## 5 — Thirds (double stops a 3rd apart)

Two notes separated by a **3rd** (A up to C♯ is a 3rd), moved diatonically through the A major scale and across different string sets. Framed as a general-purpose device: **any scale (or any scale that fits a chord progression) can be harmonized in 3rds** to generate fills and counter-melodies — this video's R&B application is one instance of a broadly reusable idea (cf. the jazz-side analogue in [[Pentatonic Scale Harmonized in Thirds]], which stacks pentatonic-scale-degree "3rds" rather than diatonic 3rds — same family of device, different scale source).
- **Drill:** pick one string-set for the 3rds shape and run it straight through the A major scale (root to root) before trying to place any of it rhythmically as a fill; then try the same shape on a second string set to compare the fingering.

## 6 — Single-note pentatonic lines

Straight single-note lines from the **A major pentatonic scale** (reframed as F♯ minor pentatonic over the F♯m chord) played in the gaps between rhythmic chord "chunks" — the chord jabs implying the backbeat/snare groove, with single-note fills threaded between them. Three named uses:
- **Call and response** with the melody (echo/answer the vocal's phrase).
- **Counter-melody** running against the melody rather than echoing it.
- **Muted fills** — the same pentatonic material played palm-muted for a percussive texture rather than a melodic one, adding rhythmic color without competing tonally with the vocal.
- **Drill:** first play just the chunked backbeat chord pattern alone (lock the groove), then add pentatonic single notes only in the silences, starting with simple call-and-response (echo a short vocal-melody fragment) before trying counter-melody or muted textures.

## Practice order across all six

The video's own progression is device-by-device, not simultaneous — the natural drill order: (1) loop the A-Bm-F♯m backing track (or any I-ii-vi/I-IV-V diatonic progression) at a slow tempo; (2) work one device at a time until it's comfortable over all three chords; (3) once 2–3 devices are solid, mix them freely within one pass through the form — e.g., a butterfly fill on the A chord, a 6th-interval fill into the Bm, a pentatonic call-and-response line answering the melody over F♯m — since all six draw from the same A-major-scale pool, they combine without needing separate vocabulary per device.
