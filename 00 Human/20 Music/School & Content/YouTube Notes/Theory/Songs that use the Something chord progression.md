# Songs that use the Something chord progression

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AmqiQFyCAGY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AmqiQFyCAGY)

## Synopsis

Answering a viewer question, Bennett traces the descending line-cliché progression from The Beatles' "Something" (C–Cmaj7–C7–F) through several other songs, explains the secondary-dominant mechanism that makes the C7→F resolution satisfying, and defines "line cliché" as a term along the way. This is the source video for the new [[Line Cliché]] reference note, which carries the full technical writeup and song list.

## Techniques

- **The progression**: C – Cmaj7 – C7 – F, the top voice walking C→B→B♭ over an otherwise-static chord before the harmony moves to F.
- **Why C7 resolves to F**: C7 isn't diatonic to C major (its B♭ isn't in the key) — it's a secondary dominant, specifically the V of IV (F's own dominant chord), so the ear feels a strong pull from C7 to F even though we never actually left the key of C.
- **Line cliché, defined**: a chromatic line moving in one voice (top or bottom) while the rest of the chord holds still. Strictly, only the first three chords here (C–Cmaj7–C7) count as a pure line cliché, since the move to F changes another voice (E→F) alongside the line — the melodic device is still audible into the IV chord, just not in the strict textbook sense past that point.
- **The line can run in the bass instead of the top voice** — same chords, same function, different register, audibly different character.
- **Common extension**: IV → iv (minor four) → I — continuing the chromatic-descent logic one more step (by darkening the IV chord's quality) before resolving home, for a melancholic color.
- **Paul McCartney's bassline** on the original "Something" is non-root-motion and exploratory enough that the 2nd chord (Cmaj7) is actually voiced as a 2nd-inversion (C/G), not root position — cited as one of his most celebrated Beatles basslines.
- **Songs**: "Something" (The Beatles, the original); "Something More" (Train, same progression/key, likely a direct homage); "Give Me Love (Give Me Peace on Earth)" (George Harrison, IV chord given a dominant 7th); "Kiss Me" (Sixpence None the Richer, teases the line repeatedly before releasing to IV just before the chorus); "Raindrops Keep Fallin' on My Head" (B.J. Thomas); "Can't Take My Eyes Off You" (Frankie Valli).
