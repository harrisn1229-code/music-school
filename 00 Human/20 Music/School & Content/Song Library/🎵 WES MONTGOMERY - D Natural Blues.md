# D Natural Blues — Wes Montgomery

> A 12-bar jazz blues in D from Wes Montgomery's 1960 album *The Incredible Jazz Guitar of Wes Montgomery*. Jack Ruch uses a short stretch of Wes's solo — all falling over the I chord (D7) or IV chord (G7) — as a worked example of transcribing → naming the harmonic device → building an original line from the same "recipe," the same method documented in full on [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]]. For the tune's full jazz-blues chord progression (not just the I/IV passages analyzed here), see [[The Key To Understanding A Jazz Blues]], which cites this same recording as its jazz-blues example.

## Chords
**Key:** D — a **12-bar jazz blues**, cited directly on [[The Key To Understanding A Jazz Blues]] as the jazz-blues example (alongside Grant Green) contrasted with a plain 1-4-5 blues. A jazz blues keeps the same 12-bar skeleton (I in bars 1-4, IV in bar 5) but plays the tonic bars as **major 7, not dominant**, and closes with a **1-6-2-5 turnaround** rather than a static or dominant-cycled tonic. Transposed to D, that standard jazz-blues form reads:

**Dmaj7 (I)** – G7 (IV) – Dmaj7 (I) – **B7 (VI7)** – **Em7 (ii)** – **A7 (V7)** – (back to Dmaj7)

→ **I – IV – I – VI7 – ii – V7 – I**, the same 1-6-2-5 turnaround worked in B♭ on [[The Key To Understanding A Jazz Blues]] and documented generally on [[Turnarounds]]. Only the **I (D7)** and **IV (G7)** portions are what the source video below actually transcribes solo lines over — the full turnaround above is the vault's standard jazz-blues form applied to this key, not a bar-by-bar transcription of Wes's own comping.

## Solo concepts by passage (Jack Ruch's analysis)
Four short passages, each reduced to a reusable concept and immediately re-played by Ruch as his own line — see [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]] for the general method.

- **Over D7 (I), passage 1:** D7 scale (D Mixolydian: D major with a ♭7) → on the change to G7 (IV), target chord tones directly (3rd → root → ♭7). → [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]], [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]
- **Over D7 (I), passage 1 continued:** a **chromatic walk-down from the 5th to the 3rd** of D7, into a **D7♭9 arpeggio**, then chord-tone targeting of G7 again on the change — the single device Ruch calls out as what "makes this solo special." → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]], [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]
- **Over G7 (IV), passage 2:** chord tones + chromatic passing tones, followed by the **D tonic diminished scale (whole-half/half-whole diminished, starting on C♯)** used as a connector to bring the line back from G7 into D7. → [[Scale Modes Reference]], [[Diminished Chords]]
- **Over D7 (I), passage 3:** a **C major 7 arpeggio** played over D7 — gives ♭7-9-11-13, the chord's full upper-extension stack, from one familiar shape a whole step below the root — followed by **D minor pentatonic** as a bluesy cadential move into the IV chord. → [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]], [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]], [[Extended Chords]]
- **Over D7 (I), passage 4:** an **A minor 7 ("five minor") arpeggio** over D7 — gives the 5th, ♭7th, 9th and 11th from one shape — decorated with chromatic connective notes. → [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]], [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]

## Steal this
- **C major 7 over a dominant a whole step below** as a one-shape way to grab ♭7/9/11/13 all at once — reusable over any dominant 7 chord, not just D7.
- **Minor pentatonic on the I as a cadential move into the IV**, rather than as changes-outlining vocabulary — a bluesy "punctuation mark" use of the pentatonic distinct from running it through the whole form.
- **Whole-half diminished scale as connective tissue between IV and I**, not a static "correct scale for the IV chord" — framed as a bridge, the same way [[Secondary Dominants]] and approach-chord material gets used elsewhere in this vault's jazz strand.
- **v-minor (A minor 7 over D7) arpeggio** as a quick way to imply the 5-♭7-9-11 stack over a dominant chord — the minor-7-off-the-5th trick, distinct from the C-major-7-off-the-♭7 trick above.
