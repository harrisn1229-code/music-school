> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **The melody's shape over time — strong means balanced, an arc with one clear peak**
> Independent of the actual pitches. What goes up comes back down, rather than drifting in one direction or sitting flat.
>
> **Q2.** **Fix an explicit top and bottom note first · after stepwise movement, take a leap**
> About an octave and a third is a reasonable starting band. Steps read as smooth, leaps as dramatic, and a line that's all of one reads as one-dimensional.
>
> **Q3.** **Repetition · sequence · inversion · diminution**
> *Also accepted:* interval contraction · interval expansion · augmentation · rhythmic metamorphosis · imitation
>
> **Q4.** **Root settled · 3rd colours it · 5th stable but neutral · 7th leaning · 9th open and floating**
> The root is where a phrase goes to end. The 3rd is brighter or sadder without changing the harmony. The 7th wants to move. The 9th adds colour without changing the chord's function.
>
> **Q5.** **It agrees with the harmony on every downbeat, so nothing is ever in tension**
> *Rubric (5):* says landing on the root confirms the chord rather than creating anything unresolved (2) · says with no tension anywhere there is nothing for the line to resolve, so it reads as the harmony restated (1) · says to retarget the strong-beat landings onto 3rds, 7ths and 9ths (2)
> This is the single most common reason a melody sounds boring, and it survives every other fix because it doesn't look like a mistake — each note is correct, in key, and consonant. The problem is that correctness on every downbeat leaves the line with nothing to say. An arpeggio and a melody can be built from identical pitches; what separates them is whether the strong beats confirm the chord or argue with it. Move the landings onto the 3rd, the 7th and the 9th and the same notes start to read as a line, because each strong beat now carries something that has to go somewhere.
>
> **Q6.** **Over changes the harmony supplies it · over a static loop the melody must generate its own**
> Over changes you can hold one note and let the chords recontextualize it. Over a loop that doesn't happen, so the movement has to come from rhythm, contour and phrasing.
>
> **Q7.** **The static loop tolerates more — and a topline written over changes collapses in it**
> The listener is already in a looped frame, so a repeating figure reads as a hook. A line that was relying on the chords for its motion has nothing holding it up once the chords stop moving; nothing was wrong with it, the thing moving it went away.
>
> **Q8.** **Where the chorus sits relative to the verse**
> Higher reads as more intense, and much of what makes a chorus feel like one is that it's above what came before. Moving it up a third often does more than new notes, so it's the cheapest thing to rule out first.
>
> **Q9.** **Climb · shorten the note values · ascending sequence · withholding the tonic keeps it unresolved**
> Rising register and busier note values make the verse gather; repeating a fragment in ascending sequence makes it feel like it's accumulating. Never landing on the root means nothing has resolved, so the chorus doing it becomes the release.
>
> **Q10.** **Strip everything back before the chorus so it arrives against space — loop-based production**
> In a genre where the drop is the arrival, subtraction beforehand widens the gap at the boundary, where building into it leaves nowhere to go.
>
> **Q11.** **Repetition · rhythmic distinctiveness · singable range and clear contour — repetition outranks the rest**
> It has to come back, and be short enough to hold after one pass.
>
> **Q12.** **Tap it with no pitches — it's being tested for surviving being remembered badly**
> If the rhythm alone is still recognisable it's rhythmically distinct, and that's the form a listener will actually recall it in.
>
> **Q13.** **Sparse, high, short and rhythmically distinct — contrast is doing most of the work**
> *Rubric (5):* makes the hook contrast with the verse — sparser, higher, simpler (2) · keeps it short enough to hold after one pass and brings it back (1) · gives it a distinct rhythm that survives being tapped, and a singable range with a clear contour (2)
> Against a busy, low, dense verse almost every decision is decided by contrast. Take the register up, because the verse has established a floor and the lift is free intensity. Take the density down, because a sparse figure after a crowded one lands harder than a denser one could. Keep it to a couple of bars so it can repeat inside the section rather than being heard once. Give it a rhythm that isn't the verse's rhythm — something with a rest in it, or a syncopation the verse never used, so that tapping it out would still identify the song. Then keep the range singable and the contour a clear arc, because a hook that can't be sung back isn't one. Simplicity is not a compromise here; the hook is the part that has to survive being remembered badly.
>
> **Q14.** **Change the rhythm · widen it · move where it sits against the chord · add space**
> After those: displace the phrase off the downbeat, and reharmonize underneath it — the same melody over different chords means something entirely different.
>
> **Q15.** **Rhythm — reached for last because pitch feels like where melody lives**
> Most dull melodies are dull rhythmically, circling even note values on the beat, but the instinct when a line isn't working is to hunt for better notes.
>
> **Q16.** **Rhythm first, then widen, then retarget the landings — stop at whichever one solves it**
> *Rubric (5):* runs the three in the right order and stops at the first that works (1) · ties widening to Lesson 1's contour and range material, naming a leap or a higher top note (2) · ties retargeting to Lesson 2's landing notes, naming 9ths and 7ths in place of roots and 5ths (2)
> Step one draws on Lesson 1 only indirectly and on nothing else: rewrite the rhythm alone, displacing notes off the beat, varying the note values, cutting a rest into a line that currently runs straight through. Play it back before changing a single pitch, because this is the fix that resolves most dull melodies and it costs the least. Step two is Lesson 1's material — contour and range. The line is probably orbiting three notes inside a band it never leaves, so add a real leap and push the top note above anything it has reached, which gives the arc a peak it didn't have. Step three is Lesson 2's — the landing notes. Find where each phrase comes to rest and move those landings off the root and the 5th onto 9ths and 7ths, so the strong beats stop agreeing with the harmony. Working in that order matters more than the individual moves, because each one is cheaper than the one after it and the first is the one most likely to be the actual problem.
