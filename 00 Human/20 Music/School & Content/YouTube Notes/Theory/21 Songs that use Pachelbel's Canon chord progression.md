# 21 Songs that use Pachelbel's Canon chord progression

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_PC6jwoHyOk)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_PC6jwoHyOk)

## Synopsis

The 8-bar chord loop from Pachelbel's "Canon in D," traced through pop covers both faithful and altered, with an explanation of the harmonic-sequence mechanism that makes it work and why it was unusual for its own classical era.

## Techniques

- **The progression**: I–V–vi–iii–IV–I–IV–V (D–A–Bm–F♯m–G–D–G–A in the original key), 8 bars, entirely diatonic — stays completely within the key the whole way through.
- **Why it works — harmonic sequence in the bass**: the first 6 bars repeat the SAME interval movement (a descending 4th) three times from different starting points (D→A, then B→F♯, then G→D) — the repetition-with-variation is what makes the progression feel logically "meant to go together" rather than arbitrary. The final 2 bars deliberately break the sequence with a IV–V turnaround, because a pure sequence has no natural way to loop back to the start — you need a real cadential move for that.
- **Unusual for its era**: basing an entire classical piece on one repeating chord loop is closer to how modern pop is structured than how Baroque composers typically wrote — one reason the progression ports so cleanly into pop songwriting centuries later.
- **Common pop alterations**: the first 4 bars are usually kept intact ("that's where the character comes from"); changes cluster in bars 5–8 — swapping the iii chord for a major III, or replacing the penultimate IV with a ii chord (minor or major) instead.
- **Songs in the unaltered original form**: "All Together Now" (The Farm); "Love in the First Degree" (Bananarama) — producer Pete Waterman (Stock Aitken Waterman) has described "Canon in D" as almost the godfather of their production sound.
