# Is Karma Police in E minor or A minor?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IgjmSoSmaoc)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IgjmSoSmaoc)

## Synopsis

A case study in genuine, unresolvable modal ambiguity (a collaboration with the YouTube channel Listening In). Radiohead's "Karma Police" has three sections — a verse whose root note is legitimately disputed (A Dorian vs. E minor, among musicians and academics alike), an unambiguous G major chorus, and an outro the presenter hears as B minor but that others hear as its relative major, D.

## Techniques

- **The general mechanism behind "which note is the root" disputes: relative modes share 100% of their notes, so "which mode is this" reduces entirely to which note CONTEXT treats as home — and context can point in more than one direction at once.** The verse's seven notes fit all seven modes of G major equally well on note-content alone; nothing about the pitch set itself picks A Dorian over E minor (or any other mode). Two competing sources of context pull in different directions here: the chord progression starts on A minor (setting A as the first-heard tonal center) then moves through D→E minor (pulling toward E as a resolution target); the vocal melody, examined in isolation with the chords stripped away, resolves down to A and — critically — never uses the 6th scale degree at all, which is specifically the ONE note that distinguishes A Dorian from plain A natural minor. So harmony and melody can each supply a genuinely different, independently-defensible answer for the same passage.
- **A useful test for whether a modal-ambiguity dispute is actually resolvable: does any passage state the ONE distinguishing note?** The verse briefly substitutes F natural for the otherwise-consistent F♯ — but this doesn't settle the A-vs-E argument, because it's consistent with BOTH readings (A Dorian→A natural minor if A is root; a brief dip into E Phrygian if E is root). A genuinely undecidable case, as opposed to a case where a single clearly-audible note would settle it (contrast with the "same-notes, different-root" RESOLUTION test used for "Hey Jude" in [[How The Beatles use Modes]], where substituting a competing chord as tonic and listening for resolution DOES settle the question).
- **A structural principle for distinguishing genuinely tonal writing from modal writing, worth keeping as a general framing**: tonal music (clear cadences, strong V-I pulls) usually leaves the root essentially undebatable — described as "a realist portrait." Modal music, lacking that same directional pull, is instead compared to "an optical illusion" — the same notes can perceptually flip between two valid root readings depending on what the listener's ear locks onto, and this isn't a listener error on either side; it's an inherent property of how modes work, not a bug to be resolved.
- **A mode CHANGE (not a key change) between sections, distinguished carefully from an actual key change**: verse→chorus doesn't introduce any new notes — it's still a mode of G major throughout — it just shifts which note reads as root (A or E in the verse, G unambiguously in the chorus, established by a D→F→G melodic climb over IV-V-I). Because no new pitch content enters, this is properly a *mode* change, not a *key* change, even though it's diagrammed as a "key change" for clarity.
- **Relative major/minor (and by extension, relative modes generally) are close enough to "the same key" that resolving an ambiguous case between them is often not worth forcing**: the outro's B minor-vs-D major dispute is functionally moot since B minor is D major's relative minor — identical notes, identical key signature, genuinely two sides of one coin. General takeaway: a mode dispute between RELATIVE pairs (sharing a key signature) is lower-stakes to resolve than one between unrelated keys, since the practical difference (what to actually play) is often nil.
