# Peter Bernstein Comping Masterclass/Lesson

## Synopsis

A 24-minute guest masterclass jazz guitarist **Peter Bernstein** recorded in collaboration with Nathan Borton's channel, structured as an interview rather than a shape-by-shape lesson — Bernstein talks through his own comping philosophy in his own words, with Borton prompting follow-ups (a PDF of written-out examples and onscreen chord diagrams accompanies the original video but isn't captured here; only the spoken content is extracted). It's the most philosophically dense comping source in the vault: rather than a catalog of voicings, it's an hour of *how a master jazz comper actually thinks* — melody-first chord selection, radical economy of notes (three-note voicings over four), a lifelong habit of cataloging voicings by which note sits on top (credited to Ted Dunbar), deliberately breaking textbook voice-leading rules ("never double the third") when the ear says otherwise, and a Thelonious Monk anecdote about stripping a dense altered-dominant voicing down to just three notes that gets held up as the central lesson of the whole talk: *the notes you leave out matter more than the ones you play.* Bernstein also talks at length about *how* he built this vocabulary — stealing comping patterns the same way soloists steal licks, specifically from piano players (Hank Jones is named directly), and treating boredom with your own chords as the practical engine of growth. A worked real-tune example (Days of Wine and Roses, the F7→E♭ transition) shows the philosophy in action: substituting B♭minor for E♭7 to "milk" tension on the way back to a G minor destination, justified entirely by ear and function rather than by a textbook substitution rule. The through-line, stated explicitly near the end, is that **ears come before rules** — theory explains why something works after your ear has already said yes, not the other way around.

## What's taught

- **Melody drives chord choice, not the reverse — "the chord dresses the note."** When comping a tune like "one-six-two-five," don't think of the changes first and add a melody note on top as an afterthought; think of the melody line *first* and let it drive which chord voicing you reach for. Framed with a vivid image: the top note is "a naked note," and the chord underneath has to "give it something to wear" — comfortable, appropriate, but also giving the note "an attitude," a sense that it's special at that moment. → [[Guide Tones]], [[Chord Voicings]]
- **A concrete voicing rule that follows directly from the above: if the melody note is the chord's root, that's redundant — reach for a ♭5 instead to make the note interesting.** Worked example: melody note is C over a C chord — playing C-on-C is flat/redundant, so voice a ♭5 underneath instead to give that note tension worth hearing. General instance of "give the note an attitude" above, stated as an explicit rule rather than just a philosophy.
- **Deliberately choosing a "strange"/unexpected melody note, then finding the chord that makes it work, as a compositional exercise.** Rather than always harmonizing the obvious diatonic note, challenge yourself to pick an odd melody note and see what chord choice makes it land — the difficulty of the exercise is the point; it forces genuinely new voicing decisions instead of defaulting to comfortable ones.
- **"Melodic guide tones" — using the classic 3rd/7th guide-tone pair not just to identify chord quality but as connective material between chords, especially valuable comping solo or in a duo (no second harmony instrument to lean on).** When you're the only comping instrument, thinking of the guide tones as a line to connect — small intervals, occasionally bigger leaps — turns two static harmonic anchor points into something you can embellish and build small melodies around, rather than just landing on the "right" notes. Extends the standard 3rd/7th definition already on [[Guide Tones]] into an explicitly linear/melodic device. → [[Guide Tones]]
- **Economy of notes: three-note voicings, not four, as the deliberate default — "these kind of four-note voicings have done everyone a disservice."** Reasoning given directly: four-note "block to block, grip to grip" voicings (what "the old guys call grips") carry "too many pockets" — too much fixed material to move around, which makes voice leading clunky. Fewer notes in the chord means more freedom to move the individual voices independently. Cites watching **Jim Hall** play small chords up close: the sound read as *bigger* than fuller four-note voicings specifically because the voices were moving, not sitting static. Demonstrated on a three-note voicing skipping the A string (playing the D and G strings together) as one concrete shape. → [[Chord Voicings]] § *Which notes actually matter*
- **"Throw away the boxes" — chord shapes memorized as fixed fingerings ("cord boxes") are a trap; think of the shape as movable/derivable material, not a box you carry around.** Directly tied to the note-economy point above: carrying fewer notes per chord means less "box" to drag from grip to grip, which is what actually frees up rhythmic flexibility too — once the hand isn't locked into full block shapes, there's more mental bandwidth left to think about rhythm rather than just landing the right notes.
- **A three-note diminished voicing used purely for internal movement inside a static harmony, demonstrated on a passage resembling "Confirmation" (Charlie Parker) — described qualitatively rather than named precisely due to caption garbling.** Bernstein plays a three-note diminished shape and moves it in a way that implies a minor chord/minor-major-7 inversion relationship, explicitly framed as "not thinking to think of that minor-major-7 as just part of" the diminished movement — the exact chord spellings are unclear in the transcript (auto-caption artifacts around "this can minor" / "minor major 7"), so this is captured as a general device (small symmetric/diminished shapes generating movement inside otherwise-static harmony) rather than a literal fingering. *(Low-confidence, qualitative only — do not treat the exact notes as transcript-verified.)* → [[Diminished Chords]]
- **Catalog voicings by which note sits on top — a lifelong organizing method credited to Ted Dunbar ("store your voicings").** Rather than memorizing a chord shape by its root, Bernstein organizes his vocabulary by *the top note* — e.g. "F major with the C on top" as one entry, "F major with the major 7th on top" as a different entry — because on a chorded instrument that top note is functionally "the melody," and it's the note a listener (or the player choosing what to reach for) actually tracks. Worked examples given: an open, spread "straight fourths" voicing as one big-sounding option with a given top note; a smaller three-note voicing with the 3rd on top as a tighter alternative for the same top note. The mental exercise is framed visually — like being able to name, at a glance, which piano-hand finger would land that specific top note — and framed as an ongoing "how do I open that sound up, how do I conceptualize my options" question asked chord by chord, not a one-time drill. → [[Chord Voicings]]
- **Once the top note is fixed, work out what the middle voices should do — "think of each string as a person that has an instrument, and you're giving them a part."** For any chord (his example: C7♭9), the top note might dictate a diminished shape by default, but the real work is asking what *other* options exist for the same top-note/root pair so it doesn't always sound like "someone's tied to the railroad tracks" (i.e., the same predictable diminished color every time) — e.g. opening it up into a "triadic sound" instead. Framed as a functional-but-creative constraint-satisfaction exercise, not a fixed formula.
- **Deliberately breaking a textbook voice-leading rule ("never double the third") because the ear preferred it.** Bernstein recounts hearing a specific major-7 voicing with the 3rd doubled played live (by an unnamed guitarist, captions garble the name), being told in music-theory class that voicing was wrong, and concluding "it sounds better to my ears than this" — leading him to deliberately build both major and minor chord voicings using the doubled-3rd idea from then on. **Direct statement of the underlying philosophy: rules should follow the ear, not precede it** — "the rules come when your ears tell you yes... then you might make a little rule about it," and "there's many asterisks for any every rule." Explicit warning against rule-first playing: "sometimes you hear someone playing all the right notes and it's wrong." → [[Chord Voicings]]
- **A specific voicing-restructuring trick: move the 5th (A) below the 3rd/major-7th interval instead of above it, freeing the major-7th interval to actually ring.** Comparing two close voicings of the same notes: in the "contracted"-sounding version, the A sits above the D and F♯/C♯ stack, physically blocking the major-7th interval from ringing clearly (the interval is "in the way"); moving that same A note to sit *below* the D changes what Bernstein calls "the whole social equation of these notes" — now the A-D relationship reads as a clean 4th, and the major-7th interval (F♯ to something a half-step below the octave, per the transcript's C♯) is left free to ring on top, unobstructed. Described as making the guitar feel like it's "stretching out" rather than "contracting." A concrete, ear-led counter-example to conventional close-voicing wisdom — reorder a voicing's internal note stack, not just its outer register, when a chord feels cramped. → [[Chord Voicings]]
- **The central Monk anecdote — reducing a dense altered-dominant chord to three essential notes.** Bernstein describes discovering that Thelonious Monk played a B♭ dominant chord with the 9th in the melody using only **three notes: two 5ths and a 3rd** (i.e., stripping away the root, the 7th, and other "filler" tones and keeping only the interval Monk actually wanted to hear — framed as "chiseling," throwing away the notes that "end up on the ground"). Bernstein contrasts this with the standard six-note barre-chord version of the same B♭9 shape ("a horrible sounding... six notes... what a mess") and notes Monk achieved the sound with careful touch/attack on each remaining note (each note given "a different feeling... root, 7th, 9th") rather than any unusual technique — "people say Monk didn't really have technique," but the precision was in *how* each of the three notes was struck. **Stated general lesson: guitarists tend to assume they can't play enough notes on a chord, when in most cases they're playing too many** — a chord could theoretically carry 13-14 notes; choosing three, deliberately, is the actual skill. "The notes you leave out really matter... it's all about what's ringing." → [[Chord Voicings]] § *Which notes actually matter*
- **Taking notes out of a chord voicing as a controllable choice, not just an emergency simplification — applied specifically to diminished shapes ("not always play the full diminish shape").** Reinforces the Monk point as a general practice habit: deliberately audition partial/reduced versions of a familiar full voicing (a full dim7 shape reduced to a triad-plus-color-tone, e.g.) rather than treating the "full" version as the only correct one — framed as having "control" and "options for exploring this tone" instead of reflexively grabbing the same complete shape every time. → [[Diminished Chords]]
- **"Notes in a chord are like fish in a tank — they have to live together harmoniously."** A stated philosophy, not a technique, but the organizing metaphor for everything above: you can't just add a note because it's individually "pretty" (a "pretty fish") if it clashes with what's already sounding — every note has to make every other note in the voicing "happy." Directly ties Bernstein's whole note-selection process (economy, doubled 3rds, the Monk reduction) back to one ear-first test.
- **Steal comping vocabulary the same way soloists steal licks — and steal specifically from piano players, not other guitarists.** Direct statement: "people develop their soloing language by stealing a few licks... it's the same thing with comping, you have to steal comping patterns." Bernstein explicitly points comping vocabulary-building toward piano, not guitar: piano players "have more of an orchestra to work with," the piano is inherently more of a percussion instrument, and the greatest jazz piano compers were "so funky in where they put the stuff and how they phrase." **Named source to transcribe from specifically: Hank Jones's comping** ("who cares about the solos, just listen to what's going on there"). Jim Hall, Kenny Burrell, and Wes Montgomery are named as great guitar compers too, but explicitly framed as secondary to the piano tradition as the real template. → [[Transcribing and Playing by Ear]]
- **Comping is "spontaneously writing a chart with backgrounds and send-offs" in real time — described as a heavier task than soloing.** Bernstein's framing: comping means simultaneously accompanying the soloist AND responding to the soloist in real time, functionally composing arrangement-level material (backgrounds, phrase-ending "send-offs") on the fly rather than just playing changes. Stated directly as arguably harder than soloing because of this dual, reactive demand. → [[Comping Fills]], [[Ensemble Listening and Comping Restraint]]
- **Practice motivation and method: boredom with your own chords is the actual engine of growth — practice comping chorus after chorus on a real tune until you get sick of your voicings, then deliberately work out a different way through the same spot.** Stated bluntly: "the biggest motivator is boredom — you practice comping and get sick of your chords... work out another chord, what's another way to get from here to here." Framed as a repeatable habit (done tune by tune, spot by spot), not a one-time exercise — the boredom itself is the signal to go looking for an alternative path through a specific harmonic transition. → [[Transcribing and Playing by Ear]], [[Etude-Based Vocabulary Practice]]
- **Worked reharmonization example over "Days of Wine and Roses": substitute B♭minor for E♭7 (the F7→E♭ transition) to "milk" tension on the way to a G minor destination.** The tune's real book chart calls for a straightforward F7 to E♭ move; instead of playing E♭7, Bernstein plays **B♭minor** in its place — justified functionally (B♭m relates to E♭7 the way a ii chord relates to its V, so substituting the ii sound for the V still points toward the same destination) and by ear/intent (getting to B♭m first is "getting me closer to G minor," the tune's real destination a bit further on, so the substitution deliberately delays and intensifies the arrival — "I want to milk that tension that gets me there"). Explicitly framed as motivated by the "biggest motivator is boredom" practice habit above: this is what working out "another way to get from here to here" produces in a real tune. → [[Chord Substitution]] § *Interpolating a ii in front of any dominant*
- **A sharp-11 reinterpretation example: voicing a written chart chord differently to give a specific melody note "attitude."** The real book might say a plain G minor 7, and a straightforward comp would play a plain D minor voicing under it — Bernstein calls that combination "tattered, ill-fitting" for a specific melody note sitting on top, because the plain chord gives that note nothing to react against. Instead, voicing the same functional spot so the melody note reads as the **♯11** gives it "attitude" — the identical melody note, felt completely differently depending on which chord tone it's voiced as. A second concrete instance of the "chord dresses the note" philosophy from the top of the video, now shown against a real lead-sheet chord symbol rather than an abstract example. → [[Guide Tones]]
- **Harmonic-rhythm awareness as a constraint that actually helps comping decisions, not just a form-analysis fact.** Working out alternate paths through a specific chord-to-chord transition (like the Days of Wine and Roses F→E♭ example above) is made easier, not harder, by a fast harmonic rhythm — "there's not that many ways to go from F to E♭... that's actually a nice thing in this tune because the harmonic rhythm is [fast]." Fewer beats between chord changes narrows the realistic substitution options, which Bernstein frames as a practical aid to decision-making rather than a limitation.
- **Momentum across chorus boundaries: don't over-emphasize beat 1 of a new chorus, but never skip it either — let momentum carry you back around.** Comping practice tip for multi-chorus tunes: you don't need to "hit that first [beat]" of a new chorus hard or make "a big thing" out of landing squarely on it, but don't drop it out of the comp entirely — "you're basically coming back around for another lap," and the sense of returning to the top of the form should arrive on its own, driven by the overall feel/momentum of the chorus that just finished, rather than being manually announced. Framed as "starting strong the next chorus" through momentum rather than a deliberate accent. → [[Jazz Comping — Rhythmic Motif and Walking Bassline]]
- **Comping tension is a deliberate, portioned resource — not something to apply uniformly under every soloist.** Restraint framing tied directly to the reharmonization/tension ideas above: "you're not gonna throw those [tense/substituted] chords under every soloist all the time" — the tension devices above (the milked B♭m-for-E♭7 substitution, the ♯11 reinterpretation) are described as things to reach for selectively, giving the comper "a sense of space" that isn't rigidly dictated, while still hitting the harmonic landmarks clearly enough that everyone in the band knows where they are.
- **Guitar's physical note-limit as a hidden advantage over piano, not just a constraint.** Bernstein observes that guitar's inherent limitation (a finite number of fingers, no free finger always available for one more note) forces a guitarist toward the same reduced-note discipline Monk arrived at deliberately — while piano players, who *can* play many more notes at once, sometimes end up not really hearing every note they're playing because there's no physical cost to including it. States this directly: guitarists "walk around thinking we can't play enough notes when in many cases we play too many notes in our chords" — reframing the instrument's constraint as an ear-training asset rather than purely a limitation.
- **Long-term fretboard fluency has no shortcut — described as roughly 20-25 years of sustained attention, but the *quality* of that attention (not just time logged) is what changes outcomes.** Closing framing: "you have to stare at this fingerboard for about 20-25 years" before it makes real sense — but the more useful point buried in that line is that HOW you stare at it matters: staring in a way that improves where your fingers actually go, versus staring without a specific question in mind ("do I need to do that, or what am I trying to play"), produces very different rates of improvement for the same amount of time spent. Credits time spent around **Jim Hall** specifically for teaching this — "it's in the details... every little choice."
