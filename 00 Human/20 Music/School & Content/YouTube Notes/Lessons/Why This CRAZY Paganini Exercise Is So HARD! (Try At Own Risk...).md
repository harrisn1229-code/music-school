# Why This CRAZY Paganini Exercise Is So HARD! (Try At Own Risk...)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=biil9g_Tn1Q)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=biil9g_Tn1Q)

## Synopsis

Adapts several measures of Paganini's "Moto Perpetuo" to guitar as a pure alternate-picking accuracy exercise — explicitly not a speed exercise (capped at 120 BPM straight 16ths). The value is that classical composition places string transitions and finger-position shifts in places a guitarist would rarely choose voluntarily, breaking the habit of writing/practicing only guitar-friendly picking patterns.

## What's taught

- **Why a classical piece, specifically:** guitarists writing their own fast lines tend to (consciously or not) place string transitions and position shifts where they're mechanically comfortable. Paganini's violin composition has no such guitarist-friendly bias, so adapting it exposes picking-angle and fingering problems a self-composed exercise would likely never surface.
- **Explicit non-goal: this is not a speed exercise.** The fastest demonstrated tempo is 120 BPM continuous 16th notes with zero rests, zero economy picking — the stated goals are accuracy, even note-spacing, and hand synchronization, not raw tempo. Framed as deceptively harder than its modest BPM number suggests specifically because of the accuracy bar.
- **Named technical problem points, worked in detail:** a same-fret double-stop-adjacent passage requiring a deliberate finger-roll to prevent the two notes ringing together; a fixed four-finger-to-four-fret assignment that shifts up one fret partway through, requiring a clean position change with no audible slide between the two hand positions; a multi-string passage demanding continuous alternate picking through several consecutive string changes, named as the hardest single section — notes must not overlap, and the listener should not be able to hear the fingering/string-transition difficulty at all.
- **Practice-quality bar stated directly:** the piece should sound like "one effortless stream of 16th notes" — any audible strain, unevenness, or transition noise defeats the exercise's purpose even if the notes are technically all correct and in time.
