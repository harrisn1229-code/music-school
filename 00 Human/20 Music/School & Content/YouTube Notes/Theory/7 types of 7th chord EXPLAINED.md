# 7 types of 7th chord EXPLAINED

## Synopsis

A complete taxonomy of 7th chord types, each built from a labeled triad-plus-7th recipe, with notation symbols and functional context for each.

## What's taught

- **Dominant 7** (`C7`, no "dominant" spelled out — assumed by default) = major triad + ♭7. The only degree where a dominant 7 occurs diatonically in a key is the 5th (hence "dominant"), typically resolving V7→I (a perfect/authentic cadence). Blues music breaks this rule deliberately, putting dominant 7ths on I and IV too, for a grittier sound.
- **Major 7** (`Cmaj7` or `C△`, the triangle symbol) = major triad + natural 7 (the major scale's own 7th degree). Described as having a serene quality. Example: Erik Satie's *Gymnopédie No. 1*, built extensively on Imaj7→IVmaj7 motion.
- **Minor 7** (`Cm7` or `C-7`) = minor triad + ♭7 (the natural minor scale's own 7th degree) — described as lush/smooth, adding a jazzy sophistication to a progression.
- **Minor-major 7** (`CmMaj7`) = minor triad + natural (major) 7 — completes the 4-way combination of {major/minor triad} × {♭7/natural 7}. Doesn't occur diatonically in a major or minor key (requires a chromatic alteration), giving it a mysterious, "doesn't quite belong" quality. Most common real-world context: the second chord of a **line cliché** (e.g. Am → AmMaj7 as the top note steps down chromatically from the root).
- **Diminished 7** (`C°7`) = a full stack of **four minor 3rds** (returns to the starting pitch class an octave up) — commonly confused with a half-diminished 7th; the "extra" note added on top of a diminished triad looks like it could be a 6th but is actually a diminished 7th (a whole step below what a natural 6th would be). Mysterious/unstable, rarely lingered on — functions as a passing/resolving chord.
- **Half-diminished 7** (`Cø7` or, more commonly written, `Cm7♭5`) = diminished triad + ♭7 (one semitone higher than the fully-diminished 7th). More common than the full diminished 7 because it occurs **diatonically** in a minor key, on the ii degree — the ii-V-i in a minor key (e.g. Bm7♭5–E7–Am) is a staple move in jazz, soul, and funk.
- **Miscellaneous variants** (not treated as fully separate chord families, but worth recognizing): **7sus4** (a dominant 7 with the 3rd suspended up to the 4th); **dominant ♯5** (an augmented triad + dominant 7 — called "♯5" rather than "augmented 7" specifically to avoid ambiguity about which note is altered).
- **Practical stakes**: misreading a chord symbol (e.g. playing Cmaj7 when the chart says C7/dominant) creates audible clashing tension with the rest of the band — precise symbol reading matters, not just approximate chord-family recognition.
