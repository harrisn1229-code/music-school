# 10 Years of Bass Advice in 10 Minutes

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D_PeUEPJMZw)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D_PeUEPJMZw)

## Synopsis

BassBuzz's Josh distills "10 years" of self-taught bass practice-methodology into six principles, delivered with an unusual proof-of-concept structure: he sets himself a real, on-camera **two-week guitar challenge** (learning AC/DC's "Back in Black," 20 minutes a day, applying every principle in the video as he goes) specifically to show the advice works on an instrument he's genuinely weak at, not just the one he's already good at. The throughline across all six is **why the same player got great on bass but stayed mediocre on guitar despite comparable time invested** — the answer isn't talent or hours, it's *how* those hours were spent. The six, in order: (1) noodle strategically — schedule unstructured, purely-for-fun playing at the **end** of a session rather than banning it, using the psychological "peak-end rule" to make the whole session feel good in memory, which drives you to come back; (2) practice consistency — frequent short/distributed sessions beat rare "mega" sessions, and life doesn't always allow mega sessions anyway, so the goal is showing up with the time you actually have, not the time you wish you had; (3) focus practice on the actual weak points holding a goal back, not just repetition — repeating something badly just makes you better at doing it badly; (4) get feedback on technique, because bad technique has real consequences and you can't fix what you can't see, illustrated with a genuinely memorable anecdote (his upright-bass teacher's "sharp pencil under the elbow" trick) and a short cameo from guitar teacher Alex Leach giving Josh live technique notes; (5) when a teacher isn't available (time, money, access), build your own teacher-level self-assessment skill via recording your practice and reviewing it against specific criteria — backed by naming self-taught legends (Billy Sheehan, Paul McCartney, Tina Weymouth) who had no formal teacher and so necessarily built this skill themselves; (6) don't get stuck in scales/theory/exercises forever — actually learn whole, real songs, since they teach feel and how music works in a way isolated drilling can't. This is a companion piece to the vault's other BassBuzz self-teaching video, [[How to Start Learning Bass - Self Taught!]] — that one is the *goal-setting/coverage-category* framework (what to practice), this one is more about the *psychology and feedback loop* of practicing well (how to sustain and correct it) — genuinely different angles on the same "self-taught, no external teacher required" thesis, with real complementary, non-redundant content in each.

## Techniques

- **[0:00–0:47] Setup.** Josh has played bass and guitar for the same ~10 years but is clearly much better on bass than guitar. The video frames its six lessons as the explanation for that gap, and sets up a live two-week guitar-improvement experiment as proof they work.
- **[0:48–2:20] Lesson 1 — Noodle strategically, and put it at the *end* of the session (the peak-end rule).** Noodling (aimless, exploratory, no-agenda playing) is reframed as valuable, not wasted time — cited examples: Slash wrote the "Sweet Child O' Mine" riff from a random noodle, jam bands like Phish and the Grateful Dead built careers on noodling entire sets, and The Offspring's guitarist performs under the stage name "Noodles." The catch: "with great noodling comes great responsibility" — Josh noodled *carelessly* on guitar but *strategically* on bass, and the strategic part is **where in the session it happens**. Cites the **peak-end rule** (a real psychological finding, attributed loosely here to "psychologists"): people's memory/evaluation of an experience is disproportionately shaped by its peak moment and, especially, how it *ends* — same reason a mediocre movie with a great ending feels better in memory than a good movie with a bad one. Applied to practice: **do the structured/hard work first, then finish every session with unstructured noodling** — you still get the productive work done, but the session's last impression (and thus the memory you associate with "practicing") is the fun part, which pulls you back to practice again tomorrow. Explicitly framed as a deliberate scheduling choice, not "noodle whenever" — noodling ungated at the *start* of a session is the "careless" version that doesn't get this benefit.
- **[2:16–4:30] Lesson 2 — Practice consistency: frequent distributed sessions beat rare mega sessions.** Names the common "practice multiple hours every day" advice as unrealistic for anyone with a full life (his own list: full-time work, gigging, wedding planning, mowing the lawn). Concedes mega-time practice *did* work when he first built his bass skills and had few other obligations — "if you're doing quality practice and keeping your life together, more time does equal more progress" — but names the real failure mode: when mega-session time isn't available, people who tie their practice identity to "long sessions" often just stop entirely for weeks or months (his own guitar pattern) rather than downshifting to something smaller and consistent. **Named research claim: frequent distributed practice beats infrequent mega sessions**, with two stated mechanisms — the rest between sessions helps consolidate memories, and sleep specifically helps the brain finish learning new motor skills *(qualitative claim from the transcript, not a specific cited study — treat as a general spaced-practice/motor-learning finding rather than a sourced statistic)*. His own bass practice has stayed "rare that I don't pick it up at least a few times a week" for years, vs. guitar going weeks/months untouched in the closet. **The rule, stated directly: "put in the time you have, not the time you wish you had, and just show up as often as you can."** He then commits to this on camera: **20 minutes a day, every day, for two weeks**, learning AC/DC's "Back in Black" on guitar — explicitly capped at 20 min/day specifically to prove the principle is about consistency, not hidden mega-time.
- **[4:03–4:27] Song choice + why it's a real technique test.** "Back in Black" is chosen because, while AC/DC basslines are typically easy, the *guitar* part is genuinely hard for him — it will force him to clean up string crossing and picking technique, not just serve as an easy motivational win.
- **[4:28–6:48] Lesson 3 — Focus on the right stuff: identify and fix the actual weak points, not just repeat.** Names this as where "a lot of people flounder for years" even *with* goals set (his own example: setting a slap-speed goal to match a Victor Wooten track, on bass, worked — but naively repeating a goal piece on guitar, like his Bach "Bourrée," did not: "this is exactly how it sounded a year ago... I've made no real progress"). **The mechanism named directly: focusing on the right stuff isn't "pick a song and smash your head against it" — it's identifying and fixing the specific weak points blocking that goal.** The sharpest line in the video, worth quoting for the technique note: **"if you practice playing crappily... you just get better at playing crappily."** Mindless repetition of a piece you're already playing badly reinforces the bad version, it doesn't fix it — repetition alone is not the same as deliberate practice.
- **[5:53–7:09] Lesson 4 — Fix bad technique; feedback is what tells you what to fix.** Frames Lesson 3 and 4 as directly linked: you can't know *what* the "right stuff" to focus on is without feedback on your own technique. Illustrated with a personal anecdote from Josh's upright-bass study with Karen Zimmerman (Santa Rosa Symphony): upright technique requires keeping the elbow up, and Karen would place a sharp pencil just below where his elbow should sit — not to stab him, but so that *dropping* his elbow meant stabbing himself. Framed as extreme but effective: "bad technique has consequences... sometimes that's your teacher [with a pencil], but most of the time it's that you have to struggle way harder to play anything" — the takeaway is that bad technique has a real, felt cost even without a dramatic enforcement mechanism, and at some point you have to face and fix it rather than work around it forever.
- **[7:09–7:29] Guest clip — guitar teacher Alex Leach gives Josh live technique feedback.** Three concrete tips delivered on camera: (1) **when gripping a chord, be already thinking about the next chord** — anticipate the upcoming shape rather than reacting to it after landing the current one; (2) **tighten up picking-hand movements** — reduce excess motion in the picking hand; (3) **find a fixed anchor point on the guitar for the picking hand** — a consistent physical reference point rather than a floating hand. Presented as a real, concrete example of what "expert technique feedback" actually looks like in practice, immediately followed by the video's pivot to what to do when that kind of feedback isn't accessible.
- **[7:29–8:57] Lesson 5 — Build your own self-assessment skill when a teacher isn't accessible.** Names the honest limitation directly: most people don't have a teacher's access, time, or money (Josh admits he's "just been lazy" and wants to learn guitar without talking to anyone). Pushes back on the common framing that a private teacher is unconditionally "the gold standard" over YouTube/courses: while there's real truth to direct feedback's value (he's taught "thousands of one-on-one lessons" and seen it work), he counters with **self-taught legends who had no formal teacher at all** — Billy Sheehan ("king of all shredders," self-taught in bars and clubs), Paul McCartney (self-taught on bass out of necessity), and Tina Weymouth (self-taught in "just a few months," Talking Heads). **The stated logic: those players *must* have had some self-feedback method, or they'd still be stuck with bad technique and rhythm** — which means the underlying skill, "assess your own playing critically and improve it," is the same skill everyone eventually needs, teacher or not. **A good teacher's real value, reframed: they help you build your self-assessment skill and give you criteria to assess with — they can't play the instrument for you.** Concrete method Josh commits to for his own guitar challenge: **record his practice sessions, then watch them back and self-assess against the specific criteria Alex Leach gave him** (chord anticipation, tighter picking-hand movement, a fixed anchor point) rather than assessing vaguely.
- **[9:09–10:00] Lesson 6 — Learn whole, real songs, not just scales and exercises.** Named as "maybe the most missed" piece of advice in traditional music education, and deliberately saved for last. Josh's own contrast: on bass, alongside scales and finger exercises, he's "played in real life thousands of songs" he can't even count; on guitar, despite years of playing, he realizes on camera he may never have played a **complete** song start-to-finish the way it's actually recorded. **The stated reason songs matter beyond repertoire: "they teach you more than just your instrument — they teach you how music works."** Closing instruction: do what every musician you admire has done — learn real songs, whole, not just fragments or drills.
- **[10:00–10:30] Payoff + outro.** A quick "did the two weeks work" reveal ("I think I suck a little less") — the specific before/after guitar footage isn't detailed further in the transcript beyond that line. Points to BassBuzz's free "Seven Days to Learning Bass" method as a next step, and closes with a course plug for "Beginner to Badass" (mentioned twice earlier in the video too, tied respectively to consistency and to self-teaching feedback skills).
