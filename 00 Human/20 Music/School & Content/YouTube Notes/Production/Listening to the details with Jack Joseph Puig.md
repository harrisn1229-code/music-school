## Synopsis

A short Mix with the Masters ear-training clip — Jack Joseph Puig (Goo Goo Dolls, No Doubt, John Mayer, Fergie) runs a live A/B/C comparison of his own stereo-bus summing options (an API-flavored path, an all-tube path, and digital summing), then a second A/B between the API path and adding a Neve stage, closing with a strong personal take on sample-rate choice. The throughline is a single listening discipline: don't judge a processing/format choice by the obvious tone of the source itself — judge it by the *tail* (the ring of a snare's reverb/echo/compression decay), because that low-level information is where the resolution difference actually lives. He frames critical-listening skill as something that compounds: the better trained the ear gets at identifying quality/resolution/emotion/distortion/harmonics, the more it starts reliably identifying source format (tape vs. digital, and even specific sample rates) purely by ear. ## Techniques

- **Judge an A/B by the decay tail, not the source tone.** When comparing two processing/summing paths on the same material, don't listen to "the sound of the snare drum" itself — listen to **the ring**: the tail of the reverb/echo/compression riding the snare. The tail is where low-level resolution differences actually show up; the source hit itself sounds similar across paths, but how long and how clearly the decay continues to read is what separates them. A generally reusable A/B-listening habit, not specific to this gear. → [[Critical Listening for Mastering]]
- **Analog summing character, three-way A/B: API-flavored stereo bus vs. all-tube stereo bus vs. digital summing.** Tube reveals the most of that low-level "ring" information — "the tube again has even the highest resolution, it's even more than the solid state" — the decay note is audibly longer and clearer. Digital summing reveals the least of it — the tail reads comparatively short/thin. Running API **and** tube together (his own stereo-bus routing) reveals the most of all three. Stated purpose isn't "more ring = better" as a rule — it's that the ring is the diagnostic: it's the low-level detail you have to listen to in order to actually hear a difference in the highs/lows and the length of the notes. *(qualitative only — no dB/settings given, a live hardware A/B)* → [[Saturation and Distortion]] (effect), [[Critical Listening for Mastering]]
- **API vs. Neve stereo-bus color, direct A/B.** API stereo bus: **"very focused, really nice in the mid-range."** Adding a Neve stage: **"low bottom end, like warm, thick"** — a character some call "fuzzy," some call "glue." Framed as a genuine either/or tonal choice for the mix bus, not a right-vs-wrong call — "it's just choosing different things, do I want the Neve, what kind of stereo bus do I want." → [[API-2500]], [[UAD Neve 1073]]
- **Ear-training compounds: as critical-listening skill improves, source/format identification gets reliable.** Stated directly as the interesting part of ear training — the better you get at identifying sound quality, resolution, emotion, distortion, and harmonics, the more you start reliably hearing things like whether a take was recorded to tape or digital, and even which sample rate (44.1/48/96) was used — "you can actually hear every one of those." Framed from direct mixing-room experience, not a claim about golden ears in the abstract. → [[Critical Listening for Mastering]]
- **Sample-rate preference, a strong stated personal opinion with reasoning.** **48 kHz — avoid:** "I don't think 48k sounds very good," describes it as consistently "a little strident, a little hard... not as clear, very digital" — "out of all the different choices, 48k is the most digital sounding to me, I hear it every single time," and explicitly the format behind sessions where the record "doesn't come together" for him. **44.1 kHz — preferred, most-musical:** "sounds so much more musical to me, so much easier to work, everything likes it — the analog gear likes it, the digital gear likes it." **96 kHz — also excellent:** "it loves 96." **88.2 kHz — the weak middle option:** "always sounds kind of bland, just kind of sits there." He acknowledges the technical counterargument (48 kHz is exactly half of 96 kHz, same relationship as 44.1:88.2) and sets it aside — the claim is purely from what he consistently hears over a decade of sessions, not from the math. *(strong stated opinion — no blind-test methodology described, worth treating as one experienced engineer's ear rather than settled fact, but concrete and specific enough to log)* → [[Session Templates]], [[Master Chain]]
