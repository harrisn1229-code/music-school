# Happiness Is a Warm Gun — The Beatles

> Three distinct sections spliced by Lennon, described as 'three songs stuck together.' Complex shifting time signatures throughout. 95 takes to complete. Each section references a different era of rock history.

## Chords
**Key:** shifts between A minor and C major · famously changing time signatures (4/4, 3/4, 9/8…)

**Part 1 ("She's not a girl who misses much"):** Am7 – Am – Em – Am – Dm – Am
→ **i7 – i – v – i – iv – i** (A minor)

**"Mother Superior jump the gun":** Am – C – Am – G7
→ **i – ♭III – i – ♭VII7**

**Chorus ("Happiness is a warm gun"):** C – Am – F – G – G7 – C
→ **I – vi – IV – V – V7 – I** — a straight ’50s doo-wop turnaround (now in C major) under the shifting meter.

## Production & Arrangement
A **suite** — three unrelated song fragments stitched into one ~2:45 track — and a masterclass in making stitched sections feel inevitable. The band reportedly ran ~70 takes to nail the tight ensemble through the **constantly shifting time signatures** (the "Mother Superior" section is famously hard to count).

- Ends in a **50s doo-wop pastiche** ("bang bang, shoot shoot") — a sudden genre shift that resolves all the tension.
- Tight, dry band performance; the complexity is in the *arrangement/meter*, not the mix.

## Steal this
- **Suite form:** stitch 2–3 unrelated fragments into one song; use tempo/feel/genre contrast as the "sections."
- **Shifting meters** for unease, then a **simple pastiche section** (doo-wop) to release it.
- **Rehearse the ensemble tight** (or comp meticulously) so a complex arrangement still grooves.

## Pollack's analysis

*Extracted from [[AWP — Happiness Is A Warm Gun]] — Alan W. Pollack, "Notes On…" #136.*

**Form.** His term for it is a **"teleological medley"** — a run of non-recurring fragments that reads as one piece because the sequence is engineered to escalate. It is the first Beatles track built this way; the Abbey Road medley is the mature version, the difference being that those sections could each stand alone whereas these lean on immediate repetition to feel like sections at all. **He counts four parts, not three** (Lewisohn says three). Part 1 has two sub-sections, the second a quatrain of iv–i phrases running **5, 4, 4 and 4½ bars** — the two identical middle phrases sit at positions 2 and 3, the one placement that destroys symmetry ([[ARR-C06 — Section-length conventions, and breaking them on purpose]]). Part 2 is an eleven-bar phrase stated twice, internally 3 + 4 + 4. Part 3 is three phrase pairs, the second of each running one beat longer. Part 4 is the only part with an ordinary full progression. What binds them is a rising intensity curve plus **meter change as the recurring motif** in place of any recurring tune.

**Harmony.** `Part 1a: i (Am7) – v (Em), ×2` · `Part 1b: iv – i, ×4 (uneven)` · `Part 3: i – III · i – ♭VII` · `Part 4: I – vi – IV – V (repeated) · iv (≈4 bars) · I – vi – IV – V (×2)`. Three parts drone in A minor in modal rather than functional terms, then it lands in C for the finish — and **the opening Am7 contains both the A-minor and C-major triads at once**, so the destination is embedded in bar 1 ([[POP-C08 — Relative-key switching]]). The doo-wop finale's one deviation is a **minor iv** held about four bars before the last two loops ([[HAR-C08 — Borrowed chords and modal interchange]]).

**Notable.** Part 3 runs a **hemiola** in the first bar of every phrase ([[RHY-C04 — Polyrhythm and hemiola]]); part 2 puts a quarter-tone-flat blues bend on several E naturals; the riffs repeatedly outline both triads together, which is why the arrival in C doesn't sound arbitrary. At the very end the engineer pushed the faders up as the last chord died.

**Against this page.** The `key:` field here says A major. **Pollack reads C major, converged upon from A minor** — close to this page's own Chords line ("shifts between A minor and C major"). Nothing above has been changed.
