# How to play odd meters WITHOUT counting

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d3XZG3wx_Cg)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d3XZG3wx_Cg)

## Synopsis

A practical method for staying inside an odd time signature by internalizing a repeating anchor rhythm ("clave"/ostinato) instead of counting beats — one worked example pattern for each odd meter from 5/4 up through 17/8.

## What's taught

- **Core method**: pick a repeating rhythmic pattern (an ostinato/"clave") that fills exactly one bar of the odd meter, and lock onto *that rhythm* rather than mentally counting "1-2-3-4-5" etc. — described as far more musical and natural than a counting scheme, and the underlying pattern can still be varied/embellished as long as its overall shape (and therefore bar length) is preserved.
- **5/4** — "long-long-short-short" = two dotted quarter notes + two regular quarter notes (the *Mission: Impossible* theme's rhythm). Trivia: this exact long-long-short-short pattern is Morse code for "MI" (M = dash-dash, I = dot-dot) — presumably the intended pun on the theme's title.
- **7/8** — quarter + quarter + three eighth notes (variant: eighth + eighth in place of the opening quarters).
- **9/8** — three quarter notes + three eighth notes (avoid the flatter "4/4 plus one extra 8th note" framing — grouping the extra beat as three 8ths at the end reads as more natural/flowing).
- **11/8** — treat as 12/8 (four groups of 3) missing the final eighth note: three groups of 3 + one group of 2.
- **13/8** — two options: (a) five quarter notes + three eighth notes (the video's general preferred shape for odd meters — as many quarter notes as possible, capped with eighth notes as a "bookend" that signals the reset point); (b) 12/8 (four groups of 3) plus one extra eighth note thrown in, which never lets the pattern settle.
- **15/8** — treat as two bars of 4/4 (=16 eighth notes) missing one eighth note: three groups of 4 + one group of 3.
- **17/8** — two options: (a) two bars of 4/4 plus an extra eighth note: three groups of 4 + one group of 5; (b) prioritizing 3s instead of 4s: five groups of 3 + one group of 2.
- General pattern across the higher odd meters: frame them as a familiar, mostly-even grouping (12/8, or two bars of 4/4) with one small deviation (missing or added eighth note) rather than as an unfamiliar number from scratch — the deviation is what gives the odd meter its distinctive "almost settles into a normal groove, then gets thrown off" feel.
