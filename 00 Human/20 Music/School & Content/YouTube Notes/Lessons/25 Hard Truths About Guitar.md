# 25 Hard Truths About Guitar

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=m0-nDbssvsA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=m0-nDbssvsA)

## Synopsis

A rapid-fire, comedic list of 25 blunt observations about guitar practice and the music industry. Most are pure mindset/motivational commentary with no actionable technique content (being in a band isn't cool, nobody wants to hear your original music, guitar gets harder as you age); a genuinely practice-relevant subset is extracted below.

## Techniques

- **Truth 2 — Your rhythm playing is probably worse than you think.** Distinguishes "rhythm" as literally playing in time with a metronome from the more common usage (comping chords for someone else to solo over). Direct diagnostic: ask a guitarist with years of experience to play a simple strum pattern against a metronome — many can't do it cleanly, regardless of years played.
- **Truth 10 — Most practice isn't practice, it's just playing.** Distinguishes intentional, goal-directed practice (pursuing a specific improvement) from noodling on material you already know. Framed as neurologically and practically distinct activities, not a matter of degree — sitting down "to play guitar" isn't the same action as sitting down to practice a specific weakness.
- **Truth 11 — You aren't as good as you sound to yourself; record to find out.** Direct recommendation: film yourself playing your best material to close the gap between perceived and actual skill level, since self-perception here is reliably inflated (compares to the well-known "most drivers rate themselves above average" bias). *(Same core method as the vault's [[Recording Yourself for Self-Diagnosis]] — no new mechanism, just independent corroboration of the same practice.)*
- **Truth 13 — Performance conditions cost real, measurable skill.** A rough, informally-reported figure: playing for another person in the moment can cost roughly half your practiced skill level (one of his own students self-estimated the drop at closer to 80%) — attributed to the change in environment/judgment context rather than a loss of actual ability. Practical implication: build performance-condition practice deliberately (playing for others, recording live, unfamiliar environments) rather than assuming solo-practice fluency transfers automatically.
- **Truth 15 — Fixing an ingrained bad habit temporarily feels like regression, and that's expected, not a sign of failure.** Personal example: two separate pick-grip changes in his own playing, both of which felt like "one step backward" and both of which recovered to prior level (and beyond) within a few months. Named as the actual cost of correcting deeply automatic technique — a real but bounded and temporary setback.
- **Truth 19 — "Finding your own voice" too early is usually a way of avoiding the study of established players.** Personal admission: avoided transcribing/studying established jazz players early on specifically to "find his own voice," and now considers that a mistake — the actual sequence is study the language first (transcription, vocabulary), then original voice follows from having something to draw on, not from avoiding influence.
- **Truth 21 — "I just play by ear" as an exclusive claim is often theory-avoidance framed as a virtue, not a superior method.** Distinguishes playing by ear as one legitimate skill among several (fine on its own) from treating it as the *only* legitimate way to play — the latter is called out as a coping frame for players who tried theory and gave up, or never tried, rather than a real methodological stance.
- **Truth 22 — Splitting practice time across many instruments simultaneously dilutes skill on all of them.** Recommendation: build genuine depth on one instrument first, then move to the next, rather than developing several at once — framed with the "chasing five rabbits catches none" analogy. Multi-instrumentalist claims (e.g. "I play five instruments") are treated skeptically by experienced musicians as a likely sign of shallow competence across the board rather than a flex.
