## Synopsis

A general survey of inversion uses in real songs — tonic-in-first-inversion and dominant-in-first-inversion as common connective moves, third inversion (only possible on 4-note chords), a direct McCartney-influenced-by-Beach-Boys anecdote about bass-driven inversion, the cadential 6/4, and a rundown of the (genuinely messy, non-standardized) competing systems for labeling inversions in Roman-numeral analysis.

## Techniques

- **Tonic chord in first inversion, a common connective move**: "Use Somebody" (Kings of Leon) uses it to climb from a root-position tonic to a root-position IV. "Uptown Girl" (Billy Joel) and "Thinking Out Loud" (Ed Sheeran) use it the same way.
- **Dominant chord in first inversion**: "American Pie" (Don McLean) and "Free Bird" (Lynyrd Skynyrd) both use V/3rd-in-bass to create a smooth stepwise connecting line between chords.
- **Third inversion — only possible on 4-note (7th) chords**, since a plain triad only has two inversions before you're back to the root note an octave up. Adding a 4th note (a 7th) opens up a third inversion, prized for a "rich" quality from the 7th and root sitting right next to each other. "Ain't No Mountain High Enough" (Marvin Gaye & Tammi Terrell) opens on a Bm7 in third inversion.
- **A song renowned for inversion use throughout**: "God Only Knows" (The Beach Boys) — nearly every chord in the verse is inverted, and Bennett plays an un-inverted version side by side to demonstrate how much character/voice-leading is lost without the inversions, even though the underlying progression still "works" harmonically.
- **A direct lineage from Beach Boys bass writing to a specific Beatles bassline**: Paul McCartney has said the Pet Sounds basslines (deliberately NOT following the chord roots) were what first showed him a bassline could change a chord's effective inversion. Applied on "Something" (George Harrison/Beatles): rather than playing the root under a G chord, McCartney plays the chord's 5th (D) in the bass, putting it in second inversion and creating a smooth descending bass line from the E♭ chord down to the C tonic — a subtle but real difference from the plain root-position version.
- **The cadential 6/4**: a passage closes with I(6/4)→V→I(root position) — an extended, drawn-out version of a plain perfect cadence, common in the Classical period but also found in pop: "Falling" (?), "Part of Your World" (*The Little Mermaid*), "Don't Think Twice, It's All Right" (Bob Dylan) are all cited as pop-context uses.
- **Competing, genuinely non-standardized systems for labeling inversions in Roman-numeral analysis** — worth knowing all three exist since different sources use different ones: (1) a plain abbreviated label after the numeral (Bennett's own preferred system, "the most straightforward"). (2) **Figured bass numbers** (6 for first inversion, 6/4 for second inversion) — derived from Baroque-era figured bass, describing the intervals above the bass note; genuinely ambiguous in practice, since "I6" in the key of C could mean either "C major, first inversion" (classical reading) or "a C6 chord" (jazz/pop reading) — the same symbol, two different chords depending on the reader's background. (3) **Lowercase letters** (b = first inversion, c = second inversion) — simpler, but still collides with existing notation (lowercase b already means "flat").
