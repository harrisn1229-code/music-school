# Songs that use the Blues Rock Chord Progression

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kVxA0EouxkQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kVxA0EouxkQ)

## Synopsis

A deep dive on I–♭III–IV–I ("1-flat3-4"), the workhorse of blues-rock and hard rock — why mixing a borrowed minor-scale ♭III with an otherwise-major-scale I and IV is what gives it the "blues" sound, several real-song variations (starting halfway through the loop, all-minor "School's Out" version, the climb-to-♭VI variant), and a genuinely useful closing insight: the progression is "guitar-native" because its chords fall directly out of the CAGED open-chord system and the harmonized pentatonic scale, which is why it barely obeys standard major/minor-scale theory yet feels completely natural on guitar.

## Techniques

- **The progression: I–♭III–IV–I** — in A: A–C–D–A, all major chords. ♭III is borrowed from the parallel minor (modal mixture) rather than being the diatonic major-scale iii — mixing a major I/IV with a minor-borrowed ♭III chord is explicitly named as the mechanism that produces the "bluesy, gritty" sound, since it puts both the major 3rd (from I) and a minor-3rd-flavored chord (♭III) into the same harmonic context.
- **Straight uses**: "Are You Gonna Be My Girl" (Jet, key of A). "Free Bird" (Lynyrd Skynyrd, extended outro, key of G). The Beatles used it multiple times (all Paul McCartney songs): "Back in the U.S.S.R." (verse reorders the chords, chorus uses the straight I-♭III-IV-I order), "Magical Mystery Tour" (key of E), "Helter Skelter" (key of E, verse vamps on I before moving to ♭III-IV).
- **A likely influence chain**: "Purple Haze" (Jimi Hendrix, key of E) uses the same progression plus a **"Hendrix chord"** (a ♯9 stacked on a dominant 7 — the natural 9th/G-natural and sharp 9th/G-sharp sounding simultaneously over an E7) — Bennett suggests this may have influenced McCartney's "Helter Skelter" use of the same base progression.
- **Starting halfway through the loop (♭III–IV–I instead of I–♭III–IV)**: "Get It On" (T. Rex, key of E) starts on ♭III (G) and resolves up through IV to I. "Born to Be Wild" (Steppenwolf, key of E) does the same but at a faster harmonic rhythm. "Seven Nation Army" (The White Stripes, key of E) builds ♭III→IV before its riff/chorus resolves back to I.
- **All-minor variant, darker/more serious tone**: "School's Out" (Alice Cooper, key of G) plays i–♭III–IV (G minor–B♭–C) instead of the major I — shifts the color from "mixed major/minor blues" to a fully minor/Dorian sound, losing the major-chord brightness that defines the standard version.
- **Soul-genre crossover** (blues-influenced genre beyond hard rock): "Hold On, I'm Coming" (Sam & Dave). "Sliced Tomatoes" (The Just Brothers, key of G) — notable as the source sample for Fatboy Slim's "Rockafeller Skank."
- **Pedal-point variant**: "Let Me Entertain You" (Robbie Williams, key of F) plays F-A♭-B♭-F in the verse/intro over a sustained F bass pedal (bass ignores the chord roots), building tension that releases in the chorus when the bass finally follows the moving chord roots.
- **Historical framing — vamp, not walked progression**: in genuine old blues, this shape functioned more as a one-chord vamp with the ♭III/IV as brief melodic detours rather than a "progression you walk through." "On the Road Again" (Canned Heat) and "La Grange" (ZZ Top, power chords/5ths rather than full triads) both essentially vamp on I and use ♭III-IV as a turnaround hook to break the monotony.
- **Climb-to-♭VI variant**: after I-♭III-IV, continuing up to ♭VI instead of resolving. "Gimme Some Lovin'" (The Spencer Davis Group, key of E: E-G-A-C). "Welcome to Paradise" (Green Day, power chords: E-G-A-C). "Love Shack" (The B-52s, key of C: C-E♭-A♭). **Named device**: this variant is a musical **sequence** — the same minor-3rd melodic motif (root climbing a minor 3rd) repeated starting a 4th higher the second time, giving the variation a structural symmetry beyond just "one more chord."
- **Why it's "guitar-native"**: no single major or minor scale contains I, ♭III, and IV together — it requires blending two scales (modal mixture) to justify on paper. But on guitar, the five CAGED open-chord shapes (C, A, G, E, D) map directly onto the notes of the A (or E) **pentatonic scale**, and playing major chords on all five pentatonic degrees hands you exactly this progression's chords for free, with zero modal-mixture theory required — a genuinely different, guitar-fretboard-first way to arrive at the same sound theory calls "borrowed." Reinforces why the progression is central to blues (built on the pentatonic scale) and why it "shouldn't work" on paper but feels obvious in hand.
