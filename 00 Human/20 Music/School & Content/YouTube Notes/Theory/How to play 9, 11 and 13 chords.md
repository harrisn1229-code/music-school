# How to play 9, 11 and 13 chords

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BpWPUQPTMYE)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BpWPUQPTMYE)

## Synopsis

Two-part lesson on upper chord extensions (9ths, 11ths, 13ths): a "slash-chord trick" for voicing them without having to think in 5-7 note stacks, and a set of practical guidelines for where in a progression they actually belong.

## What's taught

- **The slash-chord trick**: any 9th/11th/13th chord can be reduced to a simpler, more familiar chord in the right hand over a bass note in the left. Worked example: **G13** = a fully-stacked 7-note tertiary chord, but functionally the same as **F major 7 over G** (drop the 5th, and often the 3rd, from the full stack — it's too dense to be useful voiced literally). A further example: **G13♭9** reduces to **F minor-major 7 over G**. On a full band, this splits naturally — a guitarist plays the "over" chord, the bassist plays the bass note, and together they sound the full extended chord without either player needing to think in extension-chord terms.
- **C dominant 9** = **E minor 7♭5 over C** — since extensions by definition go past the octave, they're usually easier played across two hands (or two instruments) than crammed into one, which also frees the root to be voiced somewhere more open/interesting.
- **Where dominant extensions belong**: "dominant" always means built on the 5th scale degree — so a G9/G11/G13 in the key of C functions the same as a plain G7, wanting to resolve back to C, even when altered (e.g. G11♭9 still pulls toward I).
- **12-bar blues upgrade**: swap the standard I7-IV7-V7 dominant chords for their 9th equivalents (C9-F9-G9 in a C blues) for a "lusher" but still functionally identical blues sound.
- **Major/minor 9th upgrades**: in a Imaj7-IVmaj7 progression (e.g. Cmaj7-Fmaj7), upgrading both to Cmaj9-Fmaj9 adds richness without changing function; the same upgrade works on minor ii-v-type motion (Dm7-Gm7 → Dm9-Gm9).
- **Major 11 chords are dissonant by default** — a Cmaj11 (Cmaj7 + 9th + 11th) has a rub between the 3rd and 11th plus a tritone between the 7th and 11th, giving it a hazy, unresolved quality. **Fix: sharpen the 11th** (Cmaj9♯11) — turns the tritone into a perfect 5th, removing most of the dissonance while adding a Lydian-flavored ♯4 color. Suggested practical use: tack a maj9♯11 onto the final tonic chord of a piece as an easy, low-risk way to add a jazzy color at a cadence, since there's nothing left to clash against by that point.
