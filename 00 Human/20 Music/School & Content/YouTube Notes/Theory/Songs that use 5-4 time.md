# Songs that use 5/4 time

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KQ76-WiFTlo)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KQ76-WiFTlo)

## Synopsis

The densest of the odd-time-signature survey videos in this batch — a wide catalog of 5/4 songs across grouping styles (3+2 vs. long-long-short-short), a real notation-choice case study (5/4 vs. 10/8), several genuine polymeters built on top of 5/4, and a practical guide to when to notate the same rhythm as 5/4 vs. 5/8.

## Techniques

- **5/4's two dominant grouping shapes, both real and both common**: 3+2 ("Take Five," Dave Brubeck — alternating-feel 3/4+2/4, "wonky waltz"; demonstrated by A/B against a straight 3/4 version of the same material, which loses the unevenness) vs. long-long-short-short (2 dotted quarters + 2 regular quarters — the Mission: Impossible theme, derived by Lalo Schifrin directly from the Morse code for "MI"; same slow-slow-fast-fast shape recurs in Hozier's "From Eden").
- **A band publishing the same song in two different meters, letting a direct A/B replace guesswork**: Radiohead's "Morning Bell" (5/4, *Kid A*) was re-recorded in 4/4 for *Amnesiac* — comparing the two shows each note keeping its relative beat position while longer notes get shortened to fit the tighter meter.
- **5/4 and 10/8 are mathematically identical, and the choice between them is about which grouping makes the accent pattern feel intuitive**: Taylor Swift's "tolerate it" is officially published as 5/4, but co-writer Aaron Dessner calls it 10/8 — the finer 10/8 subdivision lines up better with where the song's actual accents fall.
- **Multiple distinct polymeters built on top of a 5/4 base, in the same handful of songs**: Gorillaz' "5/4" (guitar 5/4 vs. drums 4/4, a true 5-against-4 polymeter). Muse's "Animals" stacks several: a crash cymbal marking every 2 beats against the band's 5/4 (5-against-2), and a later section where drums drop to half-time 4/4 under the rest of the band's continuing 5/4. Muse have said "Animals" was directly inspired by Queens of the Stone Age's "Hanging Tree," which does the same 5-against-4 guitar/snare polymeter in its guitar solo.
- **A practical, non-arbitrary rule for choosing 5/4 vs. 5/8 notation**: mathematically interchangeable (5/4 = two bars of 5/8), so the choice is about performer legibility — use 5/8 when the felt beats are too fast to comfortably count as quarter notes (the presenter's own "The Longest March," felt as "one" per whole bar despite being 5/8), or when the piece needs to swap cleanly into a 4/4 section without an awkward tempo-halving instruction (Sufjan Stevens' "Come On! Feel the Illinoise!" — written in 5/8 specifically because a later section moves to 4/4).
- **Deliberate juxtaposition against a straight meter to sell the "oddness" of 5/4**: Cream's "White Room" opens its intro in 5/4 specifically to build atmosphere/anticipation, priming the ear for the satisfying "click" into solid 4/4 when the verse begins.

## Catalog (additional named examples)

Holst's "Mars" (*The Planets*) — a 5/4 ostinato deliberately distorts an otherwise march-like rhythm into something foreboding, matching the movement's war theme (one of the few pre-20th-century classical 5/4 examples) · Nick Drake, "Riverman" (meandering 5/4 + major/minor harmonic swing) · Sting, "Seven Days" (snare on beat 4, splitting the bar 3+2) · Radiohead, "15 Step" (2-bar rhythmic motif anchoring the whole groove).
