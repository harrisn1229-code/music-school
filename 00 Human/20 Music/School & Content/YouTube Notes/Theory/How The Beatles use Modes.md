# How The Beatles use Modes

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zY6mtCK9OF4)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zY6mtCK9OF4)

## Synopsis

Six Beatles examples of modal writing, opening with an explicit disclaimer that the band wasn't consciously using mode theory — the point is using their songs to understand what a given mode is *for*, perceptually. Five of the six examples are Mixolydian; one (Eleanor Rigby) is Dorian. Companion/sequel to an earlier video by the same channel on the same topic (referenced but not yet in this vault).

## Techniques

- **Drone-plus-melody Mixolydian, two songs, harmony almost entirely absent.** "Tomorrow Never Knows": a static C drone throughout; the vocal entering on E establishes C major (C-E = major 3rd), then a later B♭ in the vocal shifts the color to C Mixolydian — explained functionally: Mixolydian's flattened 7th is only a whole step (not a semitone) from the root, so it lacks the strong pull-to-resolve of a real leading tone, producing Mixolydian's characteristic "float, don't resolve" quality, which the video ties directly to the song's hypnotic/transcendental effect. "Within You Without You": one chord (C♯5) for the ENTIRE song — with no chord changes at all, the only source of tonal color is which scale degrees the melody touches, demonstrated via an A/B edit showing the same one-chord backing reharmonized as major just by changing which notes the melody hits.
- **Mixolydian stated only in the harmony, melody staying plain major — the reverse of the drone examples.** "She Said, She Said": opens on a B♭7 chord (ambiguous — a dominant-7 opening chord this way is also a common blues-tonality signal, not automatically Mixolydian) but the following A♭ chord confirms B♭ Mixolydian; the vocal melody itself mostly stays in B♭ major, only briefly touching the A♭ at a verse's end — Mixolydian color here is a harmony-only "hint," not the song's primary flavor. "Hey Jude" outro: shifts from F major into F Mixolydian via an E♭ chord (the ♭7); addresses a specific listener misconception head-on — the same seven notes also spell B♭ major, so some listeners hear the outro as "really" in B♭. The video's ROOT test for resolving this: extend a B♭ chord over where the F chord should be — it still sounds unresolved/tense; only the F chord itself resolves fully — confirming F (not B♭) is the actual tonic, i.e. genuinely F Mixolydian, not a B♭-major reading in disguise. Same E♭-chord device demonstrated by substitution: swapping in a diatonic F-major chord instead noticeably flattens the outro's excitement, isolating exactly what the borrowed ♭7 chord contributes.
- **A practical "same notes, which is the mode?" resolution test, generalizable beyond Hey Jude**: when two readings share an identical note set (e.g. F Mixolydian vs. B♭ major — same 7 notes, different starting degree), the deciding factor is which note the music actually treats as home — test by substituting a competing chord as if it were the tonic and listening for whether tension resolves or persists.
- **Dorian, the one non-Mixolydian example.** "Eleanor Rigby": chords stay strictly in E Aeolian (Em, C major — nothing modally unusual there), but the vocal sings C♯ over the Em chord — Dorian's raised 6th — adding brightness/interest over an otherwise static minor chord, and specifically making the following C-natural chord land as more dramatic by contrast once the ear has adjusted to the lighter C♯. A/B removal (C♯→C natural) makes the song read as more serious/less whimsical, isolating the raised-6th's specific emotional contribution.
- **A subtler Mixolydian "moment" rather than a sustained mode — "Norwegian Wood."** Melody stays in plain E major for nearly the whole phrase, touching a single D natural (E Mixolydian's ♭7) only in a closing melodic turnaround, doubled in the accompanying B minor chord. A/B removal (restoring D♯) makes the melody read as more nursery-rhyme-predictable — the brief modal touch is specifically what supplies the "folky, whimsical" character on an otherwise fully diatonic tune.
