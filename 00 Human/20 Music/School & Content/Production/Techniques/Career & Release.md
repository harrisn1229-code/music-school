# Career & Release

The business side — getting paid, getting distributed, getting signed. Deliberately thin: this exists so
career/industry videos have a destination instead of each one sitting siloed in its own YouTube note. It is
**not** a production note; see [[Mix Handoff]] for the handoff craft and [[Sample Clearance]] for rights.

Relevant context: mixes go out to a mixing engineer (see [[Mix Handoff]]), and mastering is prep-only —
so the "getting paid / getting heard" questions here are about **releasing**, not about selling services.

## Getting paid

- 🎬 *From video:* engineers doing genuinely creative work on a record should ask for **royalty points**, not
  flat-rate-only session pay — the flat fee prices the labour, not the contribution, and points are the only
  mechanism that pays out if the record works. Ask **before** the session, not after — Alex Tumay,
  [[Alex Tumay: Know Your Worth and Get Paid (When To Ask For Points In The Record Industry)]]

## Portfolio income sources

- 🎬 *From video:* four less-obvious income streams for a working musician, from someone who's run a full-time portfolio career for 7+ years — the throughline is diversification, not any single source paying the bills alone. **Library/production music**: record and publish tracks to a licensing database (no commission, no advance) so TV/ad music supervisors can license pre-cleared music on tight deadlines; play to your actual strengths (his: solo piano) rather than chasing every genre; royalties arrive later via a PRO statement and you rarely learn where a track landed (his: a bin-collection-schedule radio ad, Australian X Factor); accept a low hit rate (~1 in 20 tracks earns anything) as the normal economics of the format, not a sign it's not working. **Remote session work**: home-recording + the internet turned session work into something doable entirely asynchronously for clients you'll never meet in person — spans passion-project singer-songwriters up to film scores; get comfortable quoting/delivering over back-and-forth remote revisions rather than in-room sessions. **Care homes as a gig venue**: an underused daytime/midweek slot (fills calendar gaps other venues can't), a uniquely engaged audience (an "event," not background music), and a real audience-connection effect worth knowing as a performer — American songbook standards can audibly reach non-responsive dementia patients. **YouTube**: ad revenue is the least interesting lever — for a growing channel, a viral video's real value is as a promotion/marketing engine that generates inbound bookings and opportunities for the rest of a career, not a self-contained income stream; monetization stacks (ads, Patreon, sponsorships once an audience exists, merch); cover songs get flagged for copyright but can often still partially monetize under YouTube's scheme. → [[Sample Clearance]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[4 less obvious ways to make money as a musician]]

## Distribution

- 🎬 *From video:* DistroKid walkthrough — flat annual fee, unlimited releases; the add-ons that actually matter
  are **Shazam ID** and **YouTube Content ID** (the latter is what catches your audio in other people's uploads).
  Set metadata carefully at upload; it propagates everywhere and is painful to correct later —

## Mindset

- 🎬 *From video:* a demo reel of high-quality performance/production footage posted online can land unsolicited work — Bennett landed a gig at Abbey Road after the hiring songwriter found a performance video he'd posted with no idea it would be seen; being capable across more than one instrument/role is a similar edge, making you the flexible pick when a job needs coverage beyond the one thing you're known for (most of the source video is gigging-musician logistics — driving, calendars, networking by phone — genuinely specific to a live-performance career and not carried over here) — David Bennett Music Theory, [[27 tips from a professional musician]]
- 🎬 *From video:* the computer is a **practiced instrument**, not a workflow tool — "a computer is a musical instrument that needs just as much practice as when you practice piano, or the violin, or the cello... you have to become fairly virtuosic at programming." Hans Zimmer calls the computer his actual instrument (self-rated a better programmer than a player), and closes on tool depth beating library-shopping: "it's always the guy who really knows these tools who can make amazing music with it" — buying a library and never really exploring it is named directly as the failure mode. He also deliberately gave assistants (Ramin Djawadi, John Powell, among others) access to his own custom sample tools early in their careers, framed as part of why it "worked out better" for everyone, not just him — Hans Zimmer, [[Hans Zimmer's use of computers and samples in orchestral music]]
- 🎬 *From video:* tool-fluency over gear-chasing — once it's actually time to work, grab the tool you know fastest rather than exploring new plugins, because "you don't want to waste the inspiration time saying, how do I do this specific thing." Paired mindset point from the same video: a large toolbox (many genres you're capable of) makes it HARDER to know what to make today, not easier — his fix is an external anchor (digging records first, see [[Sampling and Sound Selection]]) that narrows the choice before he sits down to build — Young Guru, [Starting a new production with Young Guru](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QDvQ8x0QyFI)
- 🎬 *From video:* a real cross-DAW production handoff between two collaborators — production starts in an MPC, exports into Ableton for tricks, then gets shared with a production partner who finishes the record in Pro Tools; a working example of a two-person team (one an artist/producer hybrid) splitting a record across three different tools rather than staying in one DAW end to end — Young Guru, [Starting a new production with Young Guru](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QDvQ8x0QyFI)
- 🎬 *From video:* **part two of the same handoff, on camera** — once the Pro Tools idea session is where he wants it, Young Guru emails the whole session to his production partner Volley, who "will sit at home and construct a song around the beat" and arranges the actual song structure (what plays when) rather than just writing on top of it; States a compatible-collaborator trait that makes the pairing work: Volley "understands his voice" and sends vocals back already carrying textures/treatment, not a raw dry take. Full staggered-entrance arrangement example that comes back from this handoff is logged on [[Arrangement and Song Form]]. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]], [[Layering and Stacking]] — Young Guru (Mix with the Masters), [[Taking your ideas to the next level with Young Guru]]
- 🎬 *From video:* the "perfect home studio" is explicitly **not** the gear — Laidback Luke started at 15 with
  no equipment; the constraint that matters is finishing, not acquiring —
  [[What Makes The PERFECT Home Studio? (Jauz & Laidback Luke Debate)]]
- 🎬 *From video:* the one real idea in an otherwise-comedy entry: **clear distractions** (phone, socials, tabs)
  before a session — [[You Suck at Producing - How To Stay Focused]]
