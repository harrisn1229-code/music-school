# Songwriting Tip from In My Life by The Beatles (songwriting lesson)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jK0IlXkWnsI)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jK0IlXkWnsI)

## Synopsis
A short, focused songwriting-technique lesson (not a full song breakdown) using The Beatles' "In My Life" as the single worked example: John Lennon's verse takes the extremely common **I–V–vi–IV** loop and, instead of landing on IV plainly, inserts a "funky alternative chord" between vi and IV — the **I chord respelled as a dominant 7th in third inversion** (root's own ♭7 in the bass), which functions as a **secondary dominant into IV (V7/IV)**. Jared frames this explicitly as a reusable songwriting device — not just a fact about one Beatles song — and spends most of the video walking the exact same chord/progression through **five keys** (E, A, D, G, C, i.e. up a 4th each time / the CAGED keys reordered) so the viewer learns to hear it as a **function** (a scale-degree relationship) rather than memorize one fixed shape. He closes by directly comparing the same slot in "Let It Be" (which plays the expected plain I–V–vi–IV with no substitution) to highlight what the extra chord buys emotionally. This is the same A7/G chord already documented on [[🎵 THE BEATLES - In My Life]] and cross-sourced on [[Chord Inversions]] from a separate, earlier Jared video (referenced but not linked here) — this video adds the *general songwriting-device* framing and the five-key drill that neither of those pages had.

## What's taught
- **The core device — turn the I chord into its own dominant 7th, third inversion, as a pivot before IV.** Over a I–V–vi loop, instead of resolving straight to IV, respell the I chord as a **dominant 7th with its own ♭7 in the bass** (3rd inversion) — e.g. in the key of A: `A – E – F♯m – A7/G – D` (I–V–vi–**I7/♭7**–IV). Functionally this is a **secondary dominant, V7/IV** ([[Secondary Dominants]]) — the ♭7-in-bass voicing just makes the bass line rise a half step out of vi and fall a step into IV instead of jumping. *(00:00–02:00)*
- **Why it works, stated directly:** "the bass note goes up a half step from the six chord" (vi's root → the new chord's bass note, its own ♭7) "and then traditionally [that dominant chord] would resolve to [I]" — except here it's redirected to resolve to IV instead, since the chord IS the V7/IV. The half-step bass rise out of vi is what makes the ear register the chord as a deliberate lift rather than a random insert. → [[Chromatic Bass Lines]], [[Secondary Dominants]]
- **The "outside the key" framing (why it's spicy):** built from the diatonic menu of a key (I ii iii IV V vi vii°), this chord adds a note that ISN'T in the plain major scale of the key — "we are taking a note outside of just straight up the notes that come from the scale... and that's one of the reasons it's an interesting sounding chord and spices things up a little bit."
- **Practice method — walk the identical progression through 5 keys around the circle of 4ths (E → A → D → G → C):** stated reason: thinking of chords as **Nashville numbers within a key**, not fixed chord names/shapes, is a top-priority music-study habit — "you'll see how I go about teaching and internalizing a song... so we can use it creatively on our own." He explicitly recommends drilling any new/unusual chord choice through multiple keys this way to internalize the *function*, not just the shape, and calls out that E-A-D-G-C is the same five keys the CAGED system is built from, just started from E instead of C. → general study habit, reusable beyond this one chord.
- **Guitar voicings worked per key (all "add the ♭7 in the bass" applied to the tonic-as-dominant-7 shape):**
  - **Key of E** (used for the teaching walkthrough; song's recorded key is A): E7 3rd inversion — 1st finger on the 3rd string/1st fret, play the top four strings (an open-D-based E7 shape with the bottom two strings dropped).
  - **Key of A** (the song's actual recorded key): open-A-shape based, A7/G — "our main focus is on that funky one chord dominant seventh third inversion" — same shape family as the 6-chord (F♯m) with the pinky note raised a half step to the ♭7. Song's IV chord (D) is also noted to become a **borrowed minor iv (Dm)** before resolving back to I — the [[Borrowed Chords]] move already documented on the song's own page.
  - **Key of D:** open-D-chord-based, third finger moved to fret the ♭7 (C) — explicitly compared to the "Sweet Home Alabama" D-power-chord-to-D/C move as a familiar reference shape.
  - **Key of G:** G7/F, fingerpicked (not strummed) — low E-string root note plus three open strings (low E, D, G, B — skipping A and high E), described as G major-7-ish shape reduced to root + the ♭7 a half step above F♯.
  - **Key of C:** open-C-chord-based, rearranged fingers to add the ♭7 (B♭) under the C shape — "nice crunchy sound," resolves to F (IV).
- **Direct comparison to "Let It Be" (also recorded in C):** Let It Be plays the **plain, expected I–V–vi–IV** in the same slot with no substitution chord — Jared uses it as the control case: "it actually goes to that four chord, it just [In My Life] delays it by giving us this tension chord first, giving it a little spiciness." Framed as Lennon vs. McCartney, same era, same key, same starting progression, different color choice.
- **Two big takeaways, stated explicitly at the close:** (1) **learn chord numbers/scale-degree function within a key**, and drill the *same* progression/idea across at least these five keys to internalize the functionality rather than memorize one shape; (2) **keep the "half-step-up-from-vi, tonic-as-dominant-7-third-inversion" move as a standing songwriting tool** — reach for it when a normal I–V–vi–IV (or similar loop) feels too expected and you want "an interesting twist... something that doesn't sound like the other song you just wrote."
