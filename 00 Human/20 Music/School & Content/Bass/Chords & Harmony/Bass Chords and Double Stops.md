# Bass Chords and Double Stops

Playing two or more notes together on bass — pulling the instrument out of its usual single-root-note role and into a melodic/harmonic one — via two distinct mechanisms: a fretted chord shape (register-gated, see below) or an open-string pedal tone under a melodic line (root-note-gated, see the open-string section).

## The core mechanic: register makes or breaks it

**Bass chords played down low sound muddy and gross; the same voicing played higher up the neck sounds clean and "guitar-ly."** This is the whole technique in one line — the chord shape itself isn't the hard part, the register choice is. Low strings/low frets have too much fundamental energy and not enough separation between the chord tones for a stacked voicing to read clearly; moving the same shape up the neck (into a register closer to a guitar's normal range) gives the notes enough separation to sound intentional rather than smeared.

## The tenth — a voicing that avoids mud by note choice, not register

A second, genuinely different mechanism for getting a clean bass chord: instead of relying on register to keep a full triad from smearing (the core mechanic above), **build a purpose-designed two-note voicing that reads clean anywhere on the neck, including low.** Derivation, straight from the source video: start from a standard triad (root-third-fifth). **Remove the fifth** — it adds thickness but not color, so it isn't needed for this voicing. That leaves root + third, which is *still* muddy because the two notes sit too close together. **Invert the third up an octave** (up two strings, up two frets from where it was) and the result is called a **tenth** — because counting through the scale a second time (1 through 8, then 9, 10), the third-up-an-octave lands on scale-step ten. Root + tenth "gives instantly pretty chords with no mud to worry about anywhere on the bass" — unlike the register rule above, this voicing doesn't depend on playing high up the neck to sound clean.

- **Major tenth shape:** middle finger on the root, pinky on the third-up-an-octave (two strings up, two frets further along than the root) — worked from A (5th fret, E string): middle on the A, pinky on C♯ (6th fret, G string).
- **Minor tenth shape:** identical shape with the pinky note dropped a half step — both fingers land on the *same fret number* (e.g. 5th fret on both the E and G strings for an A-rooted chord) — "a nice sad sounding chord."
- **Recommended fingering: middle + pinky for both shapes, always** — reusing the same two fingers regardless of root or shape means the hand never has to relearn fingering moving around the neck, and it leaves the **index finger free to rest on and mute the A and D strings** so only the intended two notes ring.
- **Both shapes are fully movable** to any root, anywhere on the neck, low or high — the whole point being they don't need the register workaround the core mechanic above relies on.
- **Plucking:** thumb on the low (root) note, index/middle/ring (picker's choice) on the high note — thumb+index, thumb+middle, or thumb+ring all work; pinky is too short to pluck reliably.

**Worked song examples (two-chord/simple vamps, BassBuzz's own reharmonizations — not transcriptions of what the original bassists played):**
- **"For What It's Worth" (Buffalo Springfield)** — E major ↔ A major. E uses the **open** low E string, so the major tenth shape adapts: the high note lands on the 1st fret of the G string (no fretted root to count two frets up from). A uses the standard major tenth (5th fret E string root, 6th fret G string pinky). Played as straight whole notes.
- **"Dreams"** — F major ↔ G major, both roots on the low E string (1st and 3rd frets), plain major tenth shape on each. Upgraded from whole notes to **copying the original bassline's actual rhythm** — moving root notes on the bottom while the tenth note holds on top.
- **"Fallin'"** — E minor ↔ B minor (12th and 7th frets, E string) using the **minor tenth** shape. In **6/8 time** (two strong beats of three eighth notes each); the tenths are played to the song's actual verse rhythm rather than plain whole notes, to preserve the original feel.

**The diatonic sequence, mapped onto bass tenths — a songwriting/jamming tool, not just a voicing trick.** Every major scale has seven notes, so seven diatonic chords come out of it: **I, IV, V are major · ii, iii, vi are minor · vii is diminished** (same pattern [[Chords by Key]] tables) — true in every key, and a progression built only from this sequence is **diatonic**. For a beginner, sidestep diminished theory entirely: **use the minor tenth shape as a stand-in for vii°.** Worked demo in G (chosen to stay within fret reach), walking straight up the major scale on the E string: **G (I, major tenth) → A minor (ii, minor tenth) → B minor (iii, minor tenth) → C (IV, major tenth) → D (V, major tenth) → E minor (vi, minor tenth) → F♯° (vii, minor-tenth stand-in) → back to G (I).** Practice the full walk-up in as many keys as the bass's frets allow.

**Four-step method for writing an original chord progression with tenths:**
1. Pick a key (worked example: G major).
2. Pick chord numbers from the diatonic sequence, starting on I to establish the key, then any others (worked example: **I, vi, iii, IV**).
3. Find each chord's root on the E string and plug in the matching tenth shape — major tenth for major-quality numbers, minor tenth for minor/diminished ones (worked example: G → E minor → B minor → C major).
4. Pick a rhythm and plucking pattern to fit the groove — demoed as straight whole notes, alternating plucks, and copying a reference kick-drum rhythm, all over the same I-vi-iii-IV progression.

A progression built this way can anchor a jam or a song outright: write a melody over it, loop it and solo over it with the matching major scale, or accompany a singer with it — the tenths double as a compact songwriting tool, not just a chord-voicing fix. → [[Chords by Key]], [[Chord Progressions]] (the diatonic-sequence theory this maps onto bass), [[Bass Fretboard Note Memorization System]] (finding roots fast on the E string is exactly this method's step 3) — BassBuzz, [[Idiot-Proof Bass Chords (2 Easy Chords for Any Song／Jam)]]

## Worked examples

- **Mark Hoppus (Blink-182), "Carousel":** uses bass chords as the song's signature melodic device — described as giving the track a Cure/New Order-adjacent character, compared directly to Peter Hook's bassline on "Love Will Tear Us Apart." Played up high enough on the neck to stay clean rather than muddy.
- **Fat Mike (NOFX), "The Decline":** combines chords with wide register jumps in the same passage — playing a chord, dropping into a low riff, then leaping up high — treated as an example of the bassist refusing to stay anchored to a fixed low-end role at all, chords being one tool among several for breaking out of it.

## Thundercat's three hacks — extending the register mechanic

Where the core mechanic above is "get the register right and any chord shape can read clean," Thundercat's approach (BassBuzz artist study) adds two genuinely new levers beyond register, plus a structural precondition most bassists don't have:

1. **Design the song around the bass chords, rather than fitting them into an existing arrangement.** Most bassists have to voice chords around pre-existing vocals/guitars/synths that are already using up the song's sonic space; because Thundercat is his own lead singer and songwriter, he can build the arrangement *around* a chord-heavy bass part from the start. The video's own caution: dropping the same chord voicing into an already-busy existing arrangement (full drums/guitar/synths) would just be mud — cool in isolation, wrong for the song. This is a songwriting-role precondition, not a technique you can apply on someone else's song, but it explains why his chord voicings can be denser than a typical session bassist's.
2. **An extra (sixth) string buys chord-tone spacing a 4-string can't reach in one hand position.** The core mechanic above already establishes that a low-register 4-note chord on a standard 4-string reads as mud — that's exactly why most bassists cap themselves at 2–3 simultaneous notes. Thundercat's signature 6-string sidesteps that cap directly: the same 4-note chord that would cram together (and mud out) on 4 strings can spread across a wider string set, giving the ear more separation between chord tones without having to climb as far up the neck to get it. Worked example: the "Tron Song" chord (written for his cat, Turbotron) — cramped and okay-sounding on 4 strings, clearer and more "guitar-like" spread across 6.
3. **A live octave-up effect doubles the chord an octave higher, on top of getting the register/string-count right.** This is a genuinely different mechanism from either of the above — not a fretting-hand or instrument choice but a signal-chain move: an octave-up effect takes every note of the chord and duplicates it an octave up, pulling the *effected* copy into guitar register regardless of where the *fretted* voicing actually sits. This means the underlying chord shape doesn't have to fight as hard for clarity on its own — the effect does part of the register-clarity job that hack 1 (register) and hack 2 (extra string) do acoustically. See [[Bass Tone Chain Priority]] (Link 7 — Pedals) for the general "effects reshape what the ear can parse out of a bass part" framing this slots into, and [[Playing Fast on Bass — Efficient Technique, Setup, and Bridge Plucking]] for this same video's other extracted technique.

**Why this matters for the core mechanic above:** it reframes "register makes or breaks it" as one lever among (at least) three — register, string count, and live doubling — that all attack the same underlying problem (not enough separation between simultaneous low-frequency notes for the ear to parse them as distinct pitches), rather than the only lever. → BassBuzz, [[7 Reasons Thundercat is Funkin' Awesome (+Lesson Tips)]]

## A second mechanism: open-string pedal tone under a melodic line

A different way to get a chord-like, harmonically fuller bass sound, gated on a different condition than register — **whether the passage's root note happens to be one of the bass's open strings.**

- **The move:** let an open string (E, A, D, or G in standard tuning) ring continuously while playing higher melodic notes — drawn from the scale that relates to that root — on another string. The open string supplies a sustained low-end pedal tone under a moving melodic line, giving a chord-like fullness without the mud risk of a fretted low-register chord.
- **Worked example — Mike Dirnt (Green Day), "Longview," closing bar:** leaves the open D string ringing under higher melodic notes on the last bar of the riff, for a fuller, more harmonically interesting finish than a single melodic line would give (which the source video says would leave "the track feel empty on the bottom"). Named sibling songs using the same device: "All Right Now" (Free), "Lopsy Lue," "Love Will Tear Us Apart" (Joy Division/Peter Hook — also the direct comparison point for the fretted-chord mechanism above, via Blink-182's "Carousel").
- **Execution detail:** no change to fretting-hand fingering vs. the rest of the riff — just leave the target string open/unmuted. **Use all downstrokes on a closing pedal-tone passage** so every note lands with even strength (the same downstroke-for-consistency logic [[Pick vs. Fingers on Bass — Tone and Technique Tradeoffs]] documents for on-beat notes, applied here to a whole closing bar).
- **The gating condition, stated directly by the source:** this only works when the chord/passage's root is one of the bass's open strings — there's no equivalent move for a root that isn't an open string, unlike the fretted-chord mechanism above, which works anywhere on the neck (just better up high).

## Why it's rare

You don't hear bass chords in that many songs precisely because the register trade-off is easy to get wrong — a bassist who tries it down low (where a bass "naturally" lives) gets mud, concludes the technique doesn't work, and drops it. The songs that use it well are deliberately playing higher up the neck than a bassline typically sits.

## When to reach for it

Use bass chords when you want the bass to function as a **melodic/harmonic voice** in its own right rather than a low-end anchor — a bridge, a hook, a moment where the bass should compete for the listener's attention instead of supporting underneath everything else. Not a default technique; it's a deliberate register-and-role choice for a specific passage.
