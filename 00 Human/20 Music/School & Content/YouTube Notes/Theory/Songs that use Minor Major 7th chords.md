# Songs that use Minor Major 7th chords

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u_RroREHVXY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u_RroREHVXY)

## Synopsis

The minor-major 7th's two natural homes: film-scoring tension (Bernard Herrmann's "Hitchcock chord," used across Vertigo/Psycho/Taxi Driver) and, far more commonly, as a stepping-stone chord inside a line cliché. Closes with three different valid ways to notate the same chord and a neat reframing: a minor-major 7 is really an augmented triad over a bass note.

## Techniques

- **Definition**: minor triad + major 7th (Am/maj7 = A-C-E-G♯). Stark, mysterious, borderline-dissonant in isolation; needs the right context to land.
- **Opening a song cold with it (rare)**: "Life in a Glass House" (Radiohead) opens directly on Am(maj7), repeatedly dropping to a plain Am7 and back.
- **As a passing/arpeggiated color chord**: "Us and Them" (Pink Floyd) — an arpeggiated motif rooted on D moves through Dsus2, Esus2/D, G/D, and Dm(maj7).
- **Film scoring — "the Hitchcock chord," more accurately "the Bernard Herrmann chord"**: Herrmann used the minor-major 7 constantly across his Hitchcock scores — "Vertigo" (opens on an E♭m(maj7) arpeggio, sometimes with an added 6th/C natural for extra tension), "Psycho." The nickname undersells Herrmann's range — he used it in non-Hitchcock work too, including his LAST score, "Taxi Driver" (both minor-major 7 and minor-major 9 appear, e.g. in the cue "Hitting the Girl").
- **Minor-major 9 (mM7 + the 9th)** — a related, slightly softer-tension chord: the famous FINAL chord of the James Bond theme.
- **Most common real-world context — a stepping stone inside a line cliché**: a minor chord with its top note walking down chromatically naturally passes through the minor-major 7 on its way from a plain minor triad toward a minor 7th chord (Am→Am(maj7)→Am7...). "Butterflies and Hurricanes" (Muse) — Dm→Dm(maj7) as the first step of a descending line cliché. "I Just Called to Say I Love You" (Stevie Wonder) — walks down AND back up through the same device. More Stevie Wonder uses: "Golden Lady," "Don't You Worry 'Bout a Thing."
- **Strong jazz association via line clichés**: "In a Sentimental Mood" (Duke Ellington) is built from two separate line clichés (Dm→Dm(maj7)..., then Gm→Gm(maj7)...) — also the source of the standard jazz-notation triangle symbol (△) for "major 7."
- **Standalone jazz use, not part of a line cliché — a direct lineage between two standards**: "Nica's Dream" (Horace Silva) opens on B♭m(maj7), moves down a tone to A♭m(maj7), and back — likely inspired by "Chelsea Bridge" (Billy Strayhorn), which opens with the identical B♭m(maj7)→A♭m(maj7) move.
- **Three valid notations for the same chord**: the jazz triangle symbol (△7), a capital M (distinguishing "major" from lowercase "m" for minor), or spelling it out unambiguously as "minor(major7)."
- **A useful reframing**: the upper three notes of a minor-major 7 chord (3rd-5th-7th) form an AUGMENTED triad — so Am(maj7) can also be thought of/labeled as "C augmented over A."
