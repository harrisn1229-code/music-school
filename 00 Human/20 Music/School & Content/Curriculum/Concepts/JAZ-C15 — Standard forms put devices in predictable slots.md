# JAZ-C15 — Standard forms put devices in predictable slots

**Q.** Name the two 32-bar containers, say what the first half of a 16+16 tune must not do, and say what knowing the form buys you before you read a single chord.

> [!question]- Answer
> **AABA** — four 8-bar sections, the same A three times around a contrasting bridge, as in Rhythm Changes. And **16+16** — two halves, the second repeating most of the first with a different ending.
>
> The first half of a 16+16 **must not resolve to I.** It has to reach V, or a V-substitute, so the second half has somewhere to launch from; the second half's ending is the tune's real cadence.
>
> Knowing the shape tells you how much of the chart is genuinely new material, and puts the recurring devices in known slots: a secondary ii–V to vi early, one to IV soon after, IV → iv → I around the IV chord, V7/V in the last line of the first half, and a turnaround in the final bars.

## Why it matters

The point is not memorising one chart. It is that unrelated standards by different composers keep reusing the same device in the same *position*, so a new chart in a known form reduces to "which variant of the usual devices is this one" rather than 32 bars of arbitrary chords.

That is also how to memorise one: chunk it the way a phone number is chunked. Name the form first, then name the cadences and functional units — "a 2-5 to six", not "half-diminished, then dominant, then minor" — and once a chunk is learned once it is recognised everywhere else it appears.

## Drill

Take a standard you do not know, mark the form and the section boundaries before reading a single chord, then fill in the chunks by name rather than by chord symbol. Count how many distinct things you actually had to remember.
