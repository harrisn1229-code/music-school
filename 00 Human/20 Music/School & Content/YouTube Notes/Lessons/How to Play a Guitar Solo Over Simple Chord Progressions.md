# How to Play a Guitar Solo Over Simple Chord Progressions

## Synopsis

A viewer-question response (from "Eric," who found a plain I–vi–IV–V triad progression too "limited" to solo over) answered with a 7-step scale-layering practice ladder — a companion, not a duplicate, to the vault's existing [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] practice ladder: that note escalates *targeting precision* over a fast jazz turnaround (ii-V-I style), this video escalates *which scale/vocabulary layer* to reach for over a slow, simple diatonic triad progression, worked in the key of E over I–vi–IV–V (E–C♯m–A–B).

## Techniques

- **The 7-step scale-layering ladder, in order:** (1) major pentatonic — "the most typical spot," works but colorless; (2) add the blue note → blues scale; (3) the full parent major scale — more color/extension notes "by accident" just from following the scale, described as tastier but riskier; (4) chord tones only, one triad per chord, in a single fretboard position — flagged as *harder* than seventh-chord tones ("there's less connections"), but the payoff is melodies that "write themselves," a more classical/targeted sound; (5) the awareness drill — switch back to playing *anything* (pentatonic, blues, whatever) while internally tracking which chord you're on at every moment, so a "free" phrase can still land precisely; (6) deliberately add 7ths and chromatic passing tones on top of the awareness drill; (7) full freedom — play any scale/vocabulary you want, zoomed out, but occasionally zoom in to target a specific chord tone, landing on it like "a master plan you had the whole time."
- **Harmonic-function-aware phrasing structure (AABA), the standout concrete takeaway:** play a short melodic idea, repeat it (near-)identically three times in a row over the I, vi, and IV chords — because in a major key those three chords share the same **tonic-group harmonic function** (see [[Chord Progressions]] § *Harmonic function (T/SD/D)* — I/vi/iii share 2 of 3 scale-degree tones and read as interchangeable) — then deliberately break the pattern and target a chord tone of the V chord specifically, since V is the one harmonically distinct chord in the loop. This gives a phrase structural logic for free: the ear hears "same, same, same, different" mapped directly onto the progression's own tonic-vs-dominant contrast.
- **Framing for why "limited" harmony isn't actually limited:** there are no wrong notes over a simple progression, only varying amounts of tension/dissonance and resolution — you can play maximally consonant (all chord tones, "very classical"), maximally colorful (pile on 7ths/chromaticism), or anything between; the emotional payoff in music generally comes from *change/contrast* (e.g., overstaying on the blues scale, then resolving onto a plain chord tone) rather than from any single scale choice being inherently better.
