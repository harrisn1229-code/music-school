# 2 Super Satisfying Chord Progressions And How To Write Your Own

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MAAssFBwvUY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MAAssFBwvUY)

## Synopsis

Two progressions that turn out to be the exact same four chords starting at different points, both explained by the same underlying mechanism — circle-of-fifths root motion — then generalized into a standalone writing tool: moving any chord a 5th up or down is close to foolproof, and you can chain several such moves even outside the starting key.

## Techniques

- **I–vi–ii–V** (C–Am–Dm–G) — jumps away from home first (C→Am, a 3rd), then walks back in a chain of perfect 5ths (Am→Dm→G→C). Character: starts at home, wanders, returns.
- **vi–ii–V–I** (Am–Dm–G–C) — the same four chords, started one position later; a pure 5th-chain the whole way through. Character: starts away from home, works steadily back.
- **Why both feel satisfying**: the perfect 5th is the simplest consonant interval past the octave (a 3:2 frequency ratio) — stepping around the circle of fifths one hop at a time reads as "correct" even when a step briefly leaves the diatonic key.
- **Extended/jazzed-up version**: "Isn't She Lovely" (Stevie Wonder) dresses the vi–ii–V–I form up with extensions in the key of C♯: C♯m7 → F♯7 (a secondary dominant — the V of V) → B11 or B9sus4 → C♯.
- **General writing tool**: from any chord, moving a 5th up or down is close to foolproof. Demonstrated live: starting from Am–F, moving up a 5th to C sounds correct (circle-of-fifths step); moving down a 5th to B♭ instead — not diatonic to A minor — still glues together because the individual interval is consonant; chaining further (B♭→E♭) keeps working the same way. The tradeoff: the further you wander from the starting key, the more deliberately you have to plan the 5th-move back home to resolve when you want to.
