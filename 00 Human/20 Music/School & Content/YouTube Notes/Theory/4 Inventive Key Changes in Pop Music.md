# 4 Inventive Key Changes in Pop Music

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oQ81Sz38Acw)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oQ81Sz38Acw)

## Synopsis

Four pre-1980s pop key changes chosen specifically as counter-examples to the "abrupt truck-driver key change for the final chorus" cliché — each modulation is woven into the song's structure closely enough to be "hidden in plain sight" rather than announced. Closes noting all four examples predate the 1980s and that this style of modulation has largely disappeared from modern pop.

## Techniques

- **"Uptown Girl" (Billy Joel) — modulates on EVERY chorus, not just the last one.** Verse: diatonic E major. Pre-chorus: **chromatic modulation** — the V chord of E (B) slides up a semitone into the new tonic, C major (works smoothly because the two chords are only a semitone apart), then a I-vi-ii-V in C establishes the new key. Chorus: **ii-V-I** perfect-cadence modulation back to E major, except the expected v chord (B minor) resolves as B **major** instead, functioning as V of E — the melody telegraphs this shift by moving from D natural (under B minor) to D♯ (under B major, E major's leading tone), which pulls the ear up into E for the chorus.
- **"Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds" (Beatles) — pivot-chord modulation reinforced by a bass line.** Verse in A major; the recurring D minor chord (already diatonically ambiguous-sounding against A major) becomes the **pivot chord** into the new key of B♭ major, with a C added in the bass creating a walking D→C→B♭ bass line into the new tonic — C isn't in A major but is in B♭ major, so the bass note itself primes the ear for the destination key before the chord change lands. From B♭, the progression runs I (B♭) → II (C, functioning as a **secondary dominant**) → V (F) → I, until C major reappears WITHOUT resolving to F — instead pulling toward G major (also foreign to B♭), with the melody dropping from D to B natural to confirm the departure from B♭. Lands on a D major chord that (with the sustained D melody note throughout) briefly implies **D Mixolydian** before resolving into the true destination, G major (I-IV-V ×3, unambiguous). This key change is doubled by a simultaneous **metric modulation**: 3/4 → 4/4 exactly as the key lands on G. Final pivot back to the verse (A major) via the shared D major chord, which is V of G (where it came from) and IV of A (where it's going).
- **"We've Only Just Begun" (Carpenters) — a melody note bridges an otherwise-jarring chord jump.** Verse diatonic in A major; bridge starts with a **major-third-relation jump**, V(E) → F♯ major as new tonic, stays there 4 bars (I-IV, F♯-B), then another major-third jump to B♭ major for 4 more bars — then, on the bridge's penultimate bar, an abrupt, unprepared jump to B minor (a chord with almost no relation to B♭ major). The jump works because the vocal melody holds a D note across the transition: over the preceding E♭ major chord, D functions as the major 7th; over the new B minor 7 chord, that same D is a chord tone — the ONE pitch shared by both harmonic contexts is exactly the note the melody is already sitting on, so the ear tracks the melody through the "wrong" chord change. A deliberately extra 9th bar of B minor 7 (rather than the expected 8-bar bridge) is added purely to let the destination key (A major) settle in the listener's ear before the verse re-enters.
- **"God Only Knows" (Beach Boys) — a key change that never fully resolves into a single announced key, staying genuinely ambiguous between three closely related keys (A major / D major / E major) for most of the song.** Intro implies A major, then a D♯ (diatonic to E major, not A) briefly suggests E; verse moves to a D major chord (foreign to A, native to D) — a B♭6 (=B minor add G♯) in bar 2 argues for A major, but a G natural just before the verse begins (native to D major, foreign to both A and E) tips the balance toward hearing the verse as genuinely in D major. Eight bars in, an E major chord plus a D♯ in the melody (foreign to both A and D, native to E) suggests a shift to E — reinforced by an F♯m-B7 **ii-V-I** into E major that establishes E as a real tonic, not just V of A, further reinforced by a C°7 (near-identical to B7) repeating the approach. Chorus lands clearly in A major for 4 bars via an A♯m7♭5 (= C♯m/A♯, a shared pivot between E and A) — then returns to the D-major-flavored verse, complete with the same orienting G natural. Explicit takeaway: the song's tonal ambiguity — genuinely hearable as more than one key at multiple points — is the point, not a flaw to resolve.
