## What's taught

- **The core device: ♯IV° as a suspension of the I chord, not an approach to it.** Distinct from the already-documented "sharp-4 diminished arriving off the V" reading (`G7 → F♯dim/C → Cmaj7`, on [[Diminished Chords]] and [[Passing Chords]] § 5) — here the diminished chord goes at the very END of a full 2-5-1 cadence, in the spot where the I chord itself would land, delaying the resolution rather than approaching it. In F: **Gm7 – C7 – [B°/F] – Fmaj7**, i.e. play the whole 2-5-1 as normal, but instead of landing on Fmaj7 on beat 1 of the "1," insert B diminished (F in the bass) first and resolve to Fmaj7 one beat/bar later. Voicing used: **B diminished with F in the bass, voiced like an E major triad resolving into Fmaj7** — deliberately chosen so the voice leading points somewhere other than where the preceding C7 already pointed, so the ear gets a genuine "wait, we're not home yet" moment before the actual arrival. → [[SUB Slot]] n/a (guitar/theory, not production) — see [[Diminished Chords]] § *Usage — passing chords* for the shared "sharp-4 diminished" family this extends.
- **Two use cases, explicitly separated: written into the tune, or dropped into a solo as a variation.** (1) It already appears as a composed part of some standards' harmony (see song examples below) — there it's not optional, everyone needs to play it. (2) It can also be applied mid-solo, purely as an improviser's substitution over an existing static or cadencing chord — a variation on "the chord operation you're playing on," not a written change. **Stated caveat for case 2:** it needs bandmates who are alert enough to hear it coming and not clash against it, and it's "not that effective" played against a fixed backing track, since nothing in the track will move with the substitution.
- **Harmonic-minor-flavored scale for soloing over the ♯IV° itself.** Take the key's major scale (F major here) and raise two scale degrees to fit the B diminished chord inside it — the resulting scale is described as a **harmonic minor** sound (the captions garble the exact degrees raised; treat as qualitative, not an exact note-by-note spelling). → [[Scale Modes Reference]] § Harmonic Minor.
- **The minor-ii-V reading: many standards harmonize this exact sound as a real minor 2-5 into the tonic, not a bare diminished chord.** In the key of F, that's **B half-diminished (ø7) → E7♭9 → F** — the ♯IV° and this minor-ii-V share the same underlying color because a half-diminished chord a half step above a dim7's root is close kin to it. **Practical soloing consequence: think "E7" specifically when building lines over the diminished-chord slot**, rather than thinking generically "diminished chord" — that's the mechanism behind the ♯9-on-harmonic-minor lines in the worked examples below. Named standard where this exact minor-ii-V shows up: "I Remember You." → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Secondary Dominants]] (same "borrow the relative-minor's own ii-V" logic as a secondary-dominant/tonicization move, here applied to a passing/suspension slot instead of a diatonic target).
- **Altered-dominant color-tone trick, stated as a general habit, not just a one-off:** when soloing over that implied E7, split the dominant 9th into **both** the ♭9 and the ♯9 in the same harmonic-minor-derived line — "not completely theoretically sound, but it usually sounds okay." A pragmatic ear-first justification for stacking both altered 9ths rather than picking one.
- **Three named standards where the device is already written into the tune, given as reference points before improvising it "at random":** **"If I Should Lose You"** (a longer cadence into B♭: a 2-5 to B♭, then instead of landing on B♭ the tune inserts **E diminished with B♭ in the bass**, THEN resolves to B♭maj7 — the identical ♯IV°-suspends-I mechanism, one whole step transposed, key of B♭); **"I've Got You Under My Skin"** (same device, unspecified spot); **"I Remember You,"** 2nd bar (paired with the minor-ii-V reading above). **Explicit reason to learn the standards first:** hearing the sound with a real melody/reference attached makes it far easier to place by ear than trying to drop it in "at random" over a progression where it isn't already implied — you need to hear it fairly strongly before you can use it freely.
- **Three worked improvised lines over the Gm7–C7–[B°/F]–Fmaj7 cadence**, each demonstrating a different melodic path through the same three chords and each resolving onto a different F major 7 chord tone:
  1. **Line 1** — over Gm7: an encircling figure around the G minor triad's root (a small-scale/chromatic-neighbor move, not a straight arpeggio). Over C7 (altered): the same encircling idea, relocated onto the C minor triad shape instead (the "altered dominant via the minor triad a half step... Over the B° diminished chord: a dim-triad shape with an **added 9th-equivalent color tone** on top (captions: "dim triad with an added E"), then a short scale fragment climbing up to land on the **3rd of Fmaj7** — with the ♭9/♯9 harmonic-minor split described above folded into the climb (from thinking E7 under the diminished sound).
  2. **Line 2** — opens with an **open B♭ major 7 arpeggio**, a short connecting scale fragment, then over C7 a line built from **two stacked triads a tritone-ish interval apart** (captions: "C♯ and F♯," parsed by Larsen on-camera as effectively a C-minor/F-triad-pair altered-dominant sound). Over the diminished chord: the same triad-pair movement pattern relocated, again using the added color tone from Line 1's approach, resolving this time onto the **5th of Fmaj7** instead of the 3rd.
  3. **Line 3** — opens on a chord-tone "stack," moves through a short **D minor**-flavored fragment, then a C7-altered line built around the note C that resolves into what reads as an **E minor7♭5 shape** (again = thinking E7 under the hood) — a leading tone climbs up to **A♭/G♯**, then descends through the diminished arpeggio to resolve onto the **5th of F**, same target chord tone as Line 2 but reached by a different route.
