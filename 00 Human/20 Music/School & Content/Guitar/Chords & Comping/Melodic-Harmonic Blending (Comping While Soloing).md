# Melodic-Harmonic Blending (Comping While Soloing)

Part of [[Jazz Harmony]] and the guitar side of [[Chord Voicings]] — how to get a **pianistic, self-accompanied fullness** out of a single guitar line by continuously deciding, in real time, how much harmony the moment needs and filling it in yourself rather than relying on a rhythm section. Distinct from [[Chord Melody Arranging]]: that page's method is for arranging a **fixed, known melody** ahead of time (or reading one off a lead sheet); this page is for **improvised soloing** where the "melody" doesn't exist yet and the harmonic support has to be invented on the fly, in the moment, as part of the line itself. Also distinct from [[Jazz Comping — Rhythmic Motif and Walking Bassline]], which is comping *behind another soloist*; this is comping *behind your own solo, as you play it*. [[Adding Chords to Single-Note Lines]] is the earlier-stage, more structured on-ramp to this same idea — chords placed deliberately at phrase-ending target notes, drilled on one static chord, before the alternation dissolves into the continuous blend described below.

## The governing question: how active does the harmony need to be right now?
The whole technique reduces to one continuously-asked question: **is the harmony active enough in the space right now, or does it need more support?** When a melodic line alone feels sufficient, keep soloing single-note. The moment it feels like it needs support — more harmonic information, more fullness — stop the line and fill out the harmony with a chord, then either hold it or launch back into a line. There's no fixed rule for when to switch; it's a continuous read of the musical space, re-decided constantly rather than planned in advance. *(This is the same underlying judgment call as [[Chord Melody Arranging]]'s "don't harmonize every note" section — both pages land on "space, not density, is the goal" — but that page applies it to a known melody being arranged, this one to an improvised line being built in the moment.)*

## Two-stage development: split first, blend second
**Stage 1 — deliberately split the two tasks.** Play a melodic line, stop, support it with a chord (or a few), then play another line, stop, support that with a chord — a visibly alternating back-and-forth between "soloing" and "comping for yourself," thought of and executed as two separate, sequential actions. This is the practice-stage version — necessary before the blend below is reachable, not a shortcut to skip.

**Stage 2 — let the two blend into one continuous texture.** With repetition, the alternation stops being two separate decisions and starts happening inside a single phrase: play a melodic line, then — without a hard stop — play the notes *around* the melody note that belong to the underlying chord, supporting it as the line keeps moving, so a single held or passing tone reads simultaneously as "the melody" and as "part of the current chord's support." At this stage you're tracking the chords underneath the melody (not the melody as an isolated line) and constantly reaching for the chord-tone notes around wherever the melody currently sits to reinforce it as you go. This can lead directly into spontaneous reharmonization — a chord choice that departs from the "correct" one, arrived at from wherever the melody happens to be sitting at that instant rather than planned ahead. → [[Chord Substitution]], [[Reharmonization]]

## The practice drill that surfaces this technique's weak spots: force a chord on every beat
Practicing the blend above exposes **unfamiliar territory on the neck** — chord shapes and voicings you don't have ready in positions you don't already know well. The fix is a dedicated drill, not more soloing:
- **Take a simple, familiar tune and force yourself to play a chord on every single beat** — a stride-like constraint (harmony on every beat, not just downbeats or bar-starts) rather than free comping.
- On a tune with a slow harmonic rhythm, this constraint routinely produces **two beats of the same written chord in a row** — which is the point: you're forced to find a second voicing for the second beat rather than repeating the first.
- **That second voicing is where passing chords live.** Given a static two-beat chord like `B♭ | B♭` moving to `C7`, instead of repeating the B♭ voicing on beat 2, find a chord that voice-leads *toward* C7 — a passing chord sitting harmonically between the two — so the bar becomes `B♭ → (passing chord) → C7` rather than `B♭ → B♭ → C7`. *(The exact passing-chord identity is qualitative in the source — the transcript garbles the specific voicings named — but the mechanism is precise: treat every repeated-chord beat as a voice-leading problem to solve, not a beat to fill.)* → [[Chord Substitution]] §4 (interpolating extra harmony without changing the underlying path is exactly this move, applied here as a discovery drill rather than a written-out reharmonization)
- **The payoff loops back into soloing.** Once a tune's passing chords are found this way, the same passing-chord vocabulary becomes available as color inside melodic lines too — a line built with harmonic awareness can imply those same passing chords without ever stopping to voice them, producing "a more modern kind of sound" than lines that stick strictly to the written changes.
