# 3 Songs with unique chord progressions

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mnSxFSgHh9Y)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mnSxFSgHh9Y)

## Synopsis

Three chord progressions Bennett believes are each essentially one-offs, used in only a single well-known song apiece: "Black Hole Sun" (Soundgarden), "You and Whose Army?" (Radiohead), and "All the Young Dudes" (David Bowie/Mott the Hoople).

## Techniques

- **"Black Hole Sun" (Soundgarden) verse**: G6–B♭6–Fmaj–E♭maj... a blend of **parallel G major and G minor** (not relative major/minor — same tonic, different mode) — tonic chord is G major (major key confirmed), but the *very next* chord (B♭) is borrowed from G minor, followed by another minor-key borrow (F). Sixth-chords added to the first two chords for extra color. A consistent common tone (G, the tonic note itself) glues the major/minor-blended chords together across the semitone voice-leading drop (Em→E♭maj needs only E and B to drop a semitone, G stays put). Ends with a Dsus4 (not D, avoiding the F♯ that would clash with the minor-key borrows) and a **Phrygian dominant** turnaround (major tonic + a ♭2 chord).
- **"You and Whose Army?" (Radiohead, from *Amnesiac*)**: chords sound locally logical moving chord-to-chord, but resist unifying under one static key — because the progression is actually a chain of **ii–V–I's marching through different keys** (the same device as jazz's "Giant Steps," cited explicitly), changing key roughly every two bars: a ii–V–I lands on C♯m, which then serves as the ii of the *next* ii–V–I landing on Bm, which serves as the ii of a third landing on A major, and A major moves smoothly back to C♯m (only one note different) to loop the whole thing back to the start. Whole-progression analysis only makes sense heard as consecutive local relationships, not one static key center.
- **"All the Young Dudes" (David Bowie, made famous by Mott the Hoople)**: starts as a straightforward descending-stepwise progression (harmonized walk down the C major scale — Bennett's own separate 40-song descending-stepwise video covers this device generally), but at the halfway point deliberately detours: instead of the expected next scale step (F), it goes to G, continuing down in an unusual way, landing on B♭, then a deliberately irregular 3-chord turnaround (a bar of 3/4 spliced into an otherwise 4/4 song). **Two competing readings of the second half**: (1) a literal key change to B♭ major — the second-half chords (Gm–F–E♭–B♭–F–B♭) are entirely diatonic to B♭ and not to C major; OR (2) — Bennett's preferred reading — **parallel major/minor blending, same device as "Black Hole Sun"**: first half solidly in C major, second half borrows from C minor (Gm = v of C minor, E♭ = III, B♭ = ♭VII), which fits better because the **vocal melody stays entirely within the C major scale through the second half** — a genuine key change would typically show up in the melody too, and it doesn't here.
