### 2. Ambiguous-root dominant resolved with a suspension-style voice-leading move

The chord following #1 is described as readable from two different roots simultaneously — a tritone-related pair (he checks it "from a D♭ perspective" and "from an E♭ perspective" and gets a consistent altered-dominant reading either way) — resolving into a **C-rooted chord voiced as something close to a slash chord** (an inversion, not root position), landing where the melody's C sits. ### 4. Deliberately reversing the expected chromatic bass direction, as a surprise device

**The clearest, highest-confidence point in the video.** Larsen states directly that in tonal music, a **descending chromatic bass line** is the expected/typical move — so one reliable way to make a passage sound surprising is to **run the bass line UP chromatically instead**, in a spot where the ear expects it to fall. He builds the second cadence's continuation this way: starting from a chord he names as a **♯4 minor 11** (explicitly *not* a half-diminished reading, despite the same root — he hears it as "basically the great [i.e. natural] 5th," even though he's not sounding the 5th itself) and walking the bass **upward** through the passage rather than down, landing eventually on a dominant voiced as a **7♯5-type chord with an added upper-structure tone**, which then resolves back to the tonic. For soloing across this stretch he names **Dorian** and a **whole-tone**-flavored option over different chords in the passage, and over the plain G7 spot notes players can freely choose **C harmonic minor, C melodic minor, or nothing more exotic than C major** depending on how far out they want to go. → [[Chromatic Bass Lines]] (routed — direct match for this page's descending/ascending mirror-principle), [[Reharmonization]]

### 5. Less reharmonization where the form already delivers its own surprise

Going into the bridge, Larsen explicitly reharmonizes **less** there than in the A sections — his stated reasoning: the bridge already represents a big harmonic/textural shift on its own (new key area, new melodic material), so it "asks less" for chordal reharmonization to also feel surprising; piling a heavy substitution on top of an already-big shift is redundant. This is a form-awareness rule distinct from (but adjacent to) [[Reharmonization]]'s existing "save the out moves for a later pass" guidance — here the governing factor is *how much surprise the raw form itself is already delivering*, not *which pass through the form it is*. → [[Reharmonization]] § *When not to*

### 6. Parallel/planing chromatic voicings as a bridge-to-final-A transition

To get back from the bridge to the final A, rather than the common textbook move (add a ii and a subdominant substitute to walk back to the tonic), Larsen instead **harmonizes the melody by moving one fixed chord-voicing shape chromatically underneath it** — parallel harmony/planing, using the same interval structure at every step rather than functionally-derived chords. He notes this trick works especially well **when the shape's 3rds share the same quality relationship** at each step, and names it as something that can sound "boring fast" if overused but works well as an occasional device — citing the standard **"Squeeze Me"** *(caption-uncertain title read)* as a tune that lends itself especially well to this treatment. He keeps a moving bass line under the parallel voicings rather than a static one, and uses minor-quality chords within the passage to keep the line's motion coherent. *(The exact chord sequence of this passage is too garbled in the transcript to spell out reliably beyond this qualitative description.)* → [[Reharmonization]], [[Chord Substitution]]

### 7. Ending cadence: avoiding a plain dominant resolution on purpose

At the very final cadence, a plain dominant-to-tonic resolution isn't available/desired given how he's set the melody up, so he substitutes **other harmonic elements** instead of forcing a V7. He mentions leaning toward a **stacked-5ths/quartal-flavored sound** in this arrangement generally (something he says he tried to use more here than usual) as part of how he gets a convincing "ending" quality without a textbook V–I. → [[Reharmonization]], [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]

### 8. General framing: reharmonization is theory + personal chord vocabulary, not a derivation

Stated directly as the video's closing thesis: reharmonizing a tune "doesn't really work like turning it all into ii–V–I's" — there isn't always a single unifying logic connecting the bass-line choices and the chord choices at every point; sometimes there's "one logic in the bass and another logic in the chords" and they simply coexist. Larsen frames his own process as pulling from **chord sounds and vocabulary he's personally familiar with and likes the sound of**, then testing them against the melody, rather than deriving substitutes from a formula — an explicit statement of the taste-plus-theory framing that opens the video. → [[Reharmonization]]
