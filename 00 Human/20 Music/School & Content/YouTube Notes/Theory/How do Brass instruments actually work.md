## Techniques

See [[Brass Section Arranging]] for the complete breakdown — summary here: pitch comes from buzzing the lips against the mouthpiece (embouchure) to resonate the air column inside the tube; tube length sets which harmonic series is available, and valves/slides change that length to switch between series, while embouchure alone moves between notes *within* a series (demonstrated cleanly on the valveless bugle, which only ever accesses one series). A typical orchestral brass section: 4 French horns, 2-3 trumpets, 2 tenor trombones, 1 bass trombone, 1 tuba. Range extends well beyond an instrument's "official" ceiling for a skilled player via embouchure control. Cylindrical-bore instruments (trumpet, trombone) sound brighter; conical-bore instruments (French horn, tuba, euphonium, flugelhorn) sound more mellow — mutes further shape tone/volume. Writing for brass shares strings' monophonic-voicing rule (share a chord across the section) but adds transposition — most brass instruments read/think in a different key than what actually sounds, so the same fingering produces the same written note across differently-tuned instruments in a family, at the cost of live mental transposition (especially acute in jazz). Flutter-tonguing is the standout specialist technique. The saxophone, despite its material, isn't a true brass/labrophone instrument by the sound-production definition — though instruments like the alphorn and didgeridoo technically are.
