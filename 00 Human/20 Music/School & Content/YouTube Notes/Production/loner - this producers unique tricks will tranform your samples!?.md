# this producers unique tricks will tranform your samples!?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=INQDFQLR-h4)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=INQDFQLR-h4)

## Synopsis

A short two-part video: loner hosts guest producer **London Rayne**, who walks through three sample-making tricks over his own worked example, then loner tries to recreate all three tricks himself from scratch on a different sample (a free Kontakt piano bank), narrating the recreation as he goes. London's three tricks: (1) capture the natural room reverb of virtual instruments by re-recording them through real studio monitors with a 3-mic (L/R/center) setup instead of relying on a plugin reverb; (2) smooth the seams between 4–8 bar sample "blocks" with a short melodic walk-up into each new section, rather than letting instruments start cold on beat one; (3) build a sample's hook from ONE strong melody carried across several different instruments (piano → pluck synth → voice) instead of writing several competing melodies. loner's recreation is the more concretely useful half of the video for technique-mining: lacking real speakers, he improvises a phone-speaker reamp (bounce → email to self → play back through phone speaker into a mic) as a workable substitute for London's monitor-reamp trick, uses a Cassette plugin's "crush" setting sparingly (only on isolated moments, not throughout) as automated processing on the piano, and closes out a vocal accent with a distinctive chop chain: sample rate dropped to ~2000 Hz → reverse → pitch up an octave → heavy reverb → a "shaper" device to sidechain/duck it. Most valuable here: the phone-speaker reamp workaround (useful whenever real monitors/room reverb aren't available), the walk-up-transition and one-melody-many-instruments arrangement principles (small, cheap, broadly applicable), and the specific low-sample-rate + reverse + pitch-up vocal-chop recipe.

## Techniques

- **[0:30–1:40] Speaker reamp for natural room reverb** — play the MIDI part, route it out to real studio monitors, and record it back in with THREE mic positions: left of the speakers, right of the speakers, and center. Turn off input monitoring on the interface first or the mic/speaker loop feeds back. In the mix: keep the center recording panned center, pan the left and right captures hard L/hard R, then EQ all three with a basic cut-lows/boost-highs curve to widen everything out. Stated jumping-off points for further experiments: blend the ambience recordings in under the original dry virtual instrument rather than replacing it, vary the mic-to-speaker distance, or try the same reamp on a totally unrelated VST just to hear what it does. → [[Ambient Texture and Field Recording]], [[Stereo Field]], [[TEXTURE Slot]]
- **[1:40–2:40] Walk-up transitions between sample "blocks"** — sample-based compositions are usually built from obvious 4–8 bar blocks that read as disconnected because nothing bridges them; give the incoming instrument (piano, in the worked example) a short 1–2 note melodic walk-up BEFORE the downbeat of the next block instead of having it start cold on beat one. The walk-up is what makes the transition read as one continuous, "played" performance instead of separate loops stitched together — framed explicitly as "escaping the prison of the grid lines." → [[Arrangement and Song Form]], [[Pocket and Microtiming]]
- **[2:40–3:20] One melody, many instruments (not many melodies)** — writing several different melodic hooks in the same sample tends to dilute the composition (too many competing ideas, nothing sticks). Instead, commit to ONE strong melody and repeat it across different instrument voices in sequence — worked example: piano states the main melody, a plucky synth restates the exact same melody, then a vocal restates it again. Reusing one melodic idea across instrument timbres reads as cohesive and reinforces the hook in the listener's ear rather than confusing them with new material each pass. → [[Melody Writing]], [[Layering and Stacking]]
- **[4:00–4:20] Phone-speaker reamp as a no-studio-monitors substitute for the room-reverb trick** — loner's recreation of London's trick #1 without access to real speakers: bounce the part to audio, email the file to himself, then play it back out of his phone's built-in speaker while recording it back in on a mic, and blend that captured take back on top of the dry source. A usable fallback version of the reamp-for-room-character move when studio monitors aren't available — the phone speaker's own coloration becomes part of the texture rather than a compromise to fight. → [[Ambient Texture and Field Recording]]
- **[4:50–5:10] Automate a lo-fi cassette crush so it only hits isolated moments, not the whole part** — running the piano through a Cassette-style plugin's "crush" setting and specifically automating it so the degraded/crushed character only appears in "little parts here and there" rather than colouring the entire performance — the automation is the point; printing the crush statically across the whole part would have been a much blunter, less useful effect. → [[Cassette]], [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[Automation]]
- **[5:10–5:30] Pre-empt a section switch-up by bringing in the new chords early** — reapplying London's walk-up-transition principle at the arrangement level rather than the note level: instead of cutting hard into a new section, bring the incoming section's chords in slightly ahead of the actual switch so the listener isn't caught off guard by the "sudden change" — record the transition-softening chords in before committing to the new section fully. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **[5:40–6:05] A specific vocal-chop-into-texture chain: downsample to ~2000 Hz → reverse → pitch up an octave → heavy reverb → shaper for sidechain-style ducking** — after recording a vocal accent, the chop is pushed hard into texture territory: drop the effective sample rate down to roughly 2000 Hz (a Redux-style bit/sample-rate reducer), reverse the resulting crunched audio, pitch it up an octave, drown it in reverb, then run it through a shaper device to give it a sidechain-style ducking/pumping motion. Each step compounds on the last — described as landing distinctly ("this one sounds fire") compared to the earlier, more conservative reverse-only vocal accent used a minute earlier in the same build. → [[Redux]], [[Shaper]], [[Reverb]], [[Sampling and Sound Selection]]
- **[6:30–7:00] Reapply the recycling method (walk-up transition / one-melody-many-instruments) to add a flute** as the last layering pass on the recreation, rather than inventing new material for the added instrument — the same technique #2/#3 principles get reused a second and third time within one short build rather than being one-off tricks. → [[Melody Writing]]
- **Sound source note:** the recreation's starting instrument is a free Kontakt bank, **"Claustrophobic Piano" from Piano Book** — worth flagging as a known free/cheap piano source. → [[Sound Selection Guide]]
