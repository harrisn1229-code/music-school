# Course 01 — Derivation Rules

Key signatures, intervals and enharmonics as **rules you can apply to any key**, rather than facts to look up.

**What it assumes**

- Nothing. This is where the school starts.

- Scale construction: spelling a mode from a parallel root is interval arithmetic and nothing else.

## The lessons

### Lesson 1 — Two Names for One Pitch

**Read**
- [ ] [[Notes & Pitches]] → *Sharps & flats*, *Half-steps & whole-steps*

**The material**
- Two spellings, one sound — that's what [[FND-C01 — Enharmonic equivalents|enharmonic]] means. C♯ and D♭ are the same key on a piano and the same fret on a guitar.
- Which name is *correct* depends on context: the key you're in, and which letter the music needs next. There is always a right answer; it just isn't a property of the sound.
- The edge cases are the whole trap. **F♭ is E** and **E♯ is F**, because there's no black key between E–F or B–C — the only two white-key pairs without one.
- Said the other way round: every white-key pair has a black key between it *except* E–F and B–C. Same fact, and it's the one worth being able to state from either direction.

**Concepts**
- [[FND-C01 — Enharmonic equivalents]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — name the enharmonic of C♯, F♭, E♯, B♯, G♭, A♯. Then say which of the six are white keys.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — on guitar, play one pitch in two positions and name it both ways: 6th fret of the low E as A♯ and as B♭. Do six pitches up the neck. Then find E♯ and F♭ on a piano and confirm they land on F and E.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 01 — Lesson 1 Test]]

### Lesson 2 — The Order, and Reading It Backwards

**Read**
- [ ] [[Circle of Fifths]] → *Sharp/flat order mnemonic*
- [ ] [[Key Signatures]] → *Full table*, *The pattern*

**The material**
- Two sequences, and the second is the first reversed — that's [[FND-C02 — Order of sharps and flats|the order of sharps and flats]]. **Sharps: F C G D A E B.** **Flats: B E A D G C F.**
- Knowing four of seven is the usual stopping point and it isn't enough, because the reading rules are indexed off the **end** of the list.
- [[FND-C03 — Reading a key signature (sharps)|Reading sharps]]: take the **last sharp** and go up a semitone. Last sharp G♯ → A major.
- [[FND-C04 — Reading a key signature (flats)|Reading flats]]: the **second-to-last flat names the key**. B♭ E♭ A♭ D♭ → A♭ major.
- **One flat is the exception** and you just memorise it: F major. There's no second-to-last flat to point at.
- Both rules run in reverse too, once you have the full order — that's the half people miss. Knowing "four flats is A♭" is useless if you can't then produce B♭ E♭ A♭ D♭.

**Concepts**
- [[FND-C02 — Order of sharps and flats]]
- [[FND-C03 — Reading a key signature (sharps)]]
- [[FND-C04 — Reading a key signature (flats)]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — given 2, 3, 4 and 5 sharps, name the key. Given 1, 2, 3 and 4 flats, name the key *and* list the flats.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — play I–IV–V in E, A and B on guitar, naming each key's sharps aloud *before* you start. Then set an Ableton Scale device to A♭ major and name all four flats from the second-to-last-flat rule before you look at what it highlights.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 01 — Lesson 2 Test]]

### Lesson 3 — Intervals as a System

**Read**
- [ ] [[Intervals]] → *Interval quality: Perfect/Major/Minor/Augmented/Diminished*

**The material**
- Three rules that turn interval questions from recall into arithmetic.
- **[[FND-C05 — Interval inversion|Inversion]]** — flip an interval and the numbers sum to 9 while the quality flips: major↔minor, augmented↔diminished, perfect stays perfect. A major 3rd inverts to a minor 6th, and 3 + 6 = 9. Derivable under pressure, which is the point.
- **[[FND-C06 — Interval quality names|Quality is not optional]]** — it's easy to name intervals by number alone and never say major or minor, because C→F is unambiguous either way. The cost lands in ear training, where "a 3rd" with no quality attached is half an answer. Number tells you *how far*; quality tells you *which colour*.
- **[[FND-C07 — Letter distance decides the interval number|Letter distance decides the number]]** — C→A♯ *sounds* like a minor 7th and *is* an augmented 6th, because C D E F G A spans six letters. Count letters first, then fix the quality.
- That last rule is the one that unlocks chord spelling. It's why a diminished 7th is spelled G B♭ D♭ F♭ and not with an E: the chord needs four alternating letters, and E would break the pattern.

**Concepts**
- [[FND-C05 — Interval inversion]]
- [[FND-C06 — Interval quality names]]
- [[FND-C07 — Letter distance decides the interval number]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — invert M3, m6, P5, M2, m7. Then name C→F♯, C→G♭, D→B, A→F, and for each say *why* the number is what it is.
- [ ] **Technique** (~5 min) — [[Interval Ladder]] · guitar · 60→120 bpm. Minor 2nd to the octave, one string then across strings — the same intervals under the hand instead of on paper.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — take the opening vocal line of [[🎵 THE BEATLES - In My Life]] and label every leap by number **and** quality. For each leap:
  1. Play the two notes of the leap **in the order the melody actually goes** — ascending leaps play low-to-high, descending leaps play high-to-low. Direction doesn't change what the interval is; "bottom note" and "top note" are about which pitch is lower/higher, not which one you play first. To name it, always sort the two by pitch: whichever is lower is the bottom note for the shortcut in [[FND-C06 — Interval quality names]], regardless of play order.
  2. **Build the inversion**: take the bottom note (by pitch, not play order) and move it up one octave (12 frets on the same string, or the same pitch class an octave up on a different string) so it now sits *above* the original top note. The original top note is now the bottom note of a new interval.
  3. Play that new pair **ascending — low to high** (the original top note, then the original bottom note an octave up). This step doesn't follow the melody's direction from step 1; it's a fresh drill on the new interval, and ascending is just the clearest way to hear it. Name it — number and quality.
  4. Check: the two interval numbers add up to 9 (2nd↔7th, 3rd↔6th, 4th↔5th), and the quality flipped per [[FND-C05 — Interval inversion]] (major↔minor, augmented↔diminished; perfect stays perfect).
  Worked example: G up to B is a major 3rd. Move the G up an octave — now B sits on the bottom, G on top. B up to G is a minor 6th. 3 + 6 = 9, major flipped to minor. That's the check, every time.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 01 — Lesson 3 Test]]

### Lesson 4 — Past the Octave

**Read**
- [ ] [[Intervals]] → the table, top to bottom

**The material**
- A 9th is a 2nd plus an octave, an 11th is a 4th, a 13th is a 6th. [[FND-C08 — Compound intervals|Subtract 7]] to get the simple interval.
- "A 2nd or a 9th" is a half-answer that's technically true — the useful skill is naming the *simple* interval underneath, because that's what tells you the colour.
- This is the bridge to [[Extended Chords]]. A B♭13 is only confusing until 13 reads as "the 6th, an octave up."
- The octave matters to the *sound* even though it doesn't change the name: the same note voiced up top is a colour and voiced in the middle is a clash. That's why extensions live at the top of a voicing.

**Concepts**
- [[FND-C08 — Compound intervals]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — give the simple interval for a major 9th, a perfect 11th, a major 13th, a minor 9th. Then spell each above C.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — on piano, voice Cmaj9, C7♯11 and Cm13 with the extension in the top voice. Name the compound interval you're playing and its simple equivalent, then drop that note an octave and hear the colour flatten.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 01 — Lesson 4 Test]]
