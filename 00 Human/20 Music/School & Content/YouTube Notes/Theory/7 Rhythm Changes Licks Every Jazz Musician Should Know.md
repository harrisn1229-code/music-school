# 7 Rhythm Changes Licks Every Jazz Musician Should Know

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=56w2jM6Sb38)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=56w2jM6Sb38)

## Synopsis

Nathan Borton's 13-minute lesson is a **soloing-vocabulary tour of [[Rhythm Changes]]**, organized as seven escalating "concepts," each demonstrated with a lick and then generalized into a reusable simplification strategy for the form. The throughline, stated explicitly up front, is that **most players over-chase the changes** — trying to outline every one of the fast-moving chords in the A section — and get discouraged; every concept in the video is instead a way to **collapse the busy changes into a smaller number of playable sounds**: first blues, then the major tonic sound, then the dominant (V) sound, then ii–V vocabulary (including "long" two-bar ii–Vs), then a chromatic walk-up lick usable over both a static key center and each bridge dominant treated as its own mini key, and finally two "advanced sub" concepts — a diminished-scale/arpeggio sound over the first four bars (traced to Ben Webster's Cotton Tail solo) plus the ♯IV° that follows the IV chord, and a "cycling dominants" descending-dominant-chain concept for the same four bars, paired with the Coltrane 1-2-3-5 pattern for soloing over it. This is the vault's **first video dedicated specifically to rhythm-changes vocabulary/soloing strategy** (as opposed to the form's harmonic analysis, already on [[Rhythm Changes]], or general chord-tone practice methodology, on [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]) — it slots directly on top of both.

## What's taught

- **Concept 1 — Blues sound over the A section.** Think of the entire A section (bars 1–8, all the fast I–vi–ii–V motion) as one static **B♭7 / B♭ blues** sound rather than chasing each chord — the simplest possible reduction, and the video's recommended starting point precisely because most players skip past it in a rush to sound "correct" on every change. A second blues-language lick over the same reduction is credited to **Kenny Burrell**. → [[Blues Form and Harmony]], [[Diagonal Pentatonic Blues Soloing]].
- **Concept 2 — Major (tonic) sound over the A section.** The first four bars of rhythm changes are just **I–vi–ii–V repeated twice**, and every one of those chords is diatonic to B♭ major — so instead of blues language, play straight **B♭ major** sound (scale/arpeggio) across the whole four bars. Framed as "a common way that jazz musicians handle lots of chords moving really fast," i.e. a named instance of treating a fast turnaround as a single prolonged tonic function. → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] § *Prolongation*.
- **Concept 3 — Dominant (V) sound over the A section, tonic vs. altered.** Instead of the I-chord (tonic) sound of Concept 2, play off the **V chord of the key** (still one static sound over the whole four bars) — works over any diatonic progression, not just this one. Explicitly distinguishes two dominant colors: a **tonic dominant** (built from the notes of the underlying key — stable, doesn't pull anywhere) vs. an **altered dominant** (deliberately unstable, wants to resolve to its own I) — the lesson uses the *tonic* dominant sound here, and plays the altered version audibly as a contrast so the difference is heard, not just described. Also demonstrates **mixing tonic-dominant and major language in one line** — major-sound opening, tonic-dominant continuation an octave down, connected into a single phrase. → [[Altered Chords]], [[The ii-V-I]].
- **Concept 4 — ii–V vocabulary, short and "long."** Standard short ii–V licks, deployed right before the IV chord and over the turnarounds back to I at the end of each A. Over the **B section (bridge)**, use **"long" ii–Vs spanning two full bars per dominant** instead of one — licensed because *every dominant chord can also be read as its own ii chord* (D7 ≈ Dm7, i.e. its own relative ii). The same long-ii–V logic **also collapses the A section**: instead of tracking I–vi–ii–V, think of the first four bars as **two long ii–Vs — Cm7–F7, Cm7–F7** — a further simplification on top of Concepts 2–3. → [[Rhythm Changes]] § *The B section*, [[The ii-V-I]], [[Turnarounds]].
- **Concept 5 — The walk-up progression (chromatic connector, three uses).** A named "walk-up progression" lick that (1) enhances rhythm changes' own first couple of bars, (2) works standalone over a **static key-center chord** (e.g. a plain B♭ major chord with no other motion), and (3) transfers onto the **bridge**, where each dominant chord (D7, G7, C7, F7) is treated as its own temporary key center and the same walk-up lick is replayed starting from that key's root (e.g. starting on G for the D7 bar, since D7 functions as V of G). → [[Rhythm Changes]] § *The B section*, [[Chromatic Bass Lines]].
- **Concept 6 — Diminished sound over the first four bars, plus the ♯IV° after the IV chord.** Players commonly play **B♭ diminished** straight through rhythm changes' first four bars — a device the video traces directly to **Ben Webster's solo on "Cotton Tail"** — used to build tension toward the arrival of the IV chord. Generalizes to soloing with **any material from the B♭ diminished scale**, or **triads pulled from the diminished scale**. Separately, the recurring **♯IV° chord that follows the IV chord** in the form (this page's [[Diminished Chords]] "subdominant diminished" family) gets its own line: beyond the obvious diminished arpeggio, a specific lick credited to a trumpet player (transcript renders only "Smith" — likely Louis Smith, unconfirmed from the auto-captions; flagged low-confidence). → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Rhythm Changes]].
- **Concept 7 — Cycling dominants over the first four bars, plus the Coltrane pattern.** A "cycling dominants" reduction of the same first-four-bars slot: instead of the diminished sound's *ascending/building* tension into the IV chord, start on the **♯5 of whatever key you're playing rhythm changes in** and play a chain of dominant chords, each resolving into the next **every two beats**, until landing on the IV chord — described as creating a *downward*-feeling pull rather than the diminished concept's buildup. For soloing over this specific cycling-dominant reduction, layer in the **Coltrane pattern** — **1-2-3-5** of each chord in the chain — as a ready-made melodic device that tracks the fast root movement without needing a fresh lick per chord. → [[Rhythm Changes]], [[Chord Substitution]] § *Coltrane changes*.
- **Framing note, stated up front and worth keeping:** the video opens by naming the trap directly — most players try to nail every single chord of a fast-moving progression and get "discouraged or frustrated." All seven concepts are offered as **progressively more advanced ways of NOT doing that** — starting from the simplest reduction (blues, one static sound) and ending on the two "advanced sub" concepts (diminished, cycling dominants) that the video explicitly flags as **only worth using once the simpler sounds are already solid** ("would only be used if you can clearly play each sound").
