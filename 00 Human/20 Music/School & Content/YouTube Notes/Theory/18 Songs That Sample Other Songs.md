# 18 Songs That Sample Other Songs

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eO5hYlb3uWE)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eO5hYlb3uWE)

## Synopsis

A hidden-samples catalog, with two genuinely important technique threads: why sampling just a **drum beat** (no melody/chords) is the least restrictive kind of sample to build on — including the two most-sampled breakbeats in music history — and Fatboy Slim's "Praise You" as a worked example of building a full track from several unrelated, disparate samples.

## Techniques

- **Why an isolated drum break is the most flexible kind of sample**: a pure drum beat carries no key or tonality (no chords, no melody), so the artist sampling it isn't constrained by the original song's harmonic content and can build in any musical direction. This is why drum-only samples get reused across wildly different songs/genres/keys, unlike a melodic or harmonic sample.
- **The Amen break — likely the single most-sampled drum recording in music history**: from "Amen, Brother" by The Winstons (1969), sampled from the song's *drum break* (a moment where every other instrument drops out, leaving just drums — functionally a mini drum solo). Too many songs sample it to list.
- **The Funky Drummer break — the other most-sampled breakbeat**: from James Brown's "Funky Drummer" (1970), same drum-break mechanism.
- **Two Isley Brothers samples of the same song, different parts, both successful**: "Footsteps in the Dark" was sampled melodically/harmonically by Ice Cube for "It Was a Good Day" (1992); 25 years later, Thundercat sampled the *same original recording's opening drum groove only* for "Them Changes" — a clean illustration that a single source recording can supply two completely different kinds of sample (melodic/harmonic vs. rhythmic-only) to two completely different artists without either being "used up."
- **Building an entire track from disparate, unrelated samples in different keys/tempos/grooves, woven together — Fatboy Slim's "Praise You"**: at least six samples from unlikely sources, including (1) an a cappella vocal hook from Camille Yarborough's 1975 "Take Yo' Praise" — being unaccompanied made it superimposable over any backing track; (2) a two-bar piano vamp lifted not from a song at all but from a 1973 JBL audio-electronics test/demonstration recording about drum-and-bass alignment; (3) a "la la" vocal from the theme song of the 1970s cartoon *Fat Albert and the Cosby Kids*. The reusable move: an a cappella vocal is uniquely easy to re-contextualize precisely because it has no fixed harmonic bed of its own to clash with a new one.
- **Other confirmed samples cataloged**: Britney Spears' "Toxic" splices two melodic fragments from the 1981 Bollywood film song "Tere Mere Beech Mein" for its hook. Lil Nas X's breakout hit reorders melodic fragments from a banjo-instrumental sample of Nine Inch Nails' "34 Ghosts IV" (Trent Reznor publicly approved of the reworking). Eminem's "My Name Is" is built on Labi Siffre's 1975 "I Got the..." (the original recording's guitarist/bassist were Chas & Dave, known for an entirely different, distinctly English music-hall style). Eminem's "Like Toy Soldiers" sped up a sample of Martika's 1989 "Toy Soldiers." Dr. Dre's "What's the Difference" slowed down the orchestral intro of Charles Aznavour's 1966 "Paseo de Gracia."
