# Bebop Vocabulary

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Jazz Harmony]]. The **melodic** side of the strand — how jazz lines are built, as opposed to how the chords underneath them work.

Useful here mostly for writing: these devices are what make a MIDI lead sound played rather than programmed, and they transfer directly to writing horn lines, guitar fills and vocal runs.

For hands-on practice rather than reading, [[Bebop Vocabulary Practice (Joe Pass Method)]] works this material against Joe Pass's *Guitar Style* — a graded blues/rhythm-changes/3-4-blues solo sequence capped by a full, chord-symbol-free transcription of Pass's own "For Django" solo.

---

## 1. The bebop scales — why an eighth note

A bebop scale is a normal seven-note scale with **one chromatic passing tone added**, making eight notes.

That's the entire idea, and the reason is rhythmic rather than harmonic. Play a seven-note scale in straight eighths starting on the downbeat and by the next bar you've shifted: chord tones start landing on offbeats. **Add an eighth note and the chord tones land on the beat again, every bar.**

| Scale | Formula | Added note |
|---|---|---|
| **Bebop dominant** | Mixolydian + natural 7 | passing tone between ♭7 and root |
| **Bebop major** | Major + ♯5 | passing tone between 5 and 6 |
| **Bebop dorian** | Dorian + natural 3 | passing tone between ♭3 and 4 |
| **Bebop melodic minor** | Melodic minor + ♯5 | passing tone between 5 and 6 |

**Bebop dominant on G:** G A B C D E F F♯ G. The F♯ is the added tone. Run it descending from the root in eighths and G, E, D and B — the chord tones of G7 — all land on downbeats.

**The practical rule:** the extra note goes wherever it makes chord tones fall on strong beats. If your line's chord tones are landing on offbeats, you need a passing tone; if they're already on the beat, you don't. → [[Scale Modes Reference]]

## 2. Enclosures — approach a note from both sides

Surround a target note before playing it. This is the most transferable device in the whole vocabulary and it works in any genre.

Target: **C**

| Type | Notes | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Chromatic above/below | D♭ – B – C | Tight, classic bebop |
| Diatonic above, chromatic below | D – B – C | Smoother |
| Below then above | B – D♭ – C | Slightly more angular |
| Double chromatic | D – D♭ – B – C | Longest, most obviously "jazz" |

**Where to use them:** on the note you actually want the listener to hear — usually a chord tone on a downbeat. An enclosure delays arrival by two or three notes and makes the arrival feel intentional. Applied to a plain melody it instantly reads as improvised.

**Where not to:** on every note. Enclosures are punctuation. Wall-to-wall, a line stops having a shape.

**A chord-tone-independent drilling variant** exists: apply enclosures systematically to every note of a scale form in descending order, with no regard for which notes are chord tones — a simpler entry point than targeting chord tones directly, worked out step by step on [[Enclosures Through the Scale]].

**A beat-driven sizing rule** also exists, orthogonal to the shape table above: which of the two/three-note enclosure types to use is decided purely by which beat the target lands on (downbeat → two notes, upbeat → three), reused to drill diatonic arpeggios and to build ii–V connective lines — full rule on [[Downbeat and Upbeat Enclosures]].

## 3. Approach notes

The simpler cousin. Precede a chord tone with the note a half-step below (or above, or a scale step away). One note, not three.

- **Chromatic from below** is the default and the strongest — it borrows the leading-tone pull.
- **Chromatic from above** is softer and slightly melancholy.
- **Scale step** is the most neutral and the most usable in non-jazz contexts.

**A pivot-tone variant** pedals (repeats) one tone and uses it as the launch point for a chromatic approach to a second tone, rather than approaching from a static position — minor chords pivot the 5th into the 6th (which becomes the 3rd of the following V7 in a 2-5-1), major/dominant chords pivot the root into the 5th (approached from the 3rd, or the reverse). Also covers applying the half-step-approach idea to every tone of an arpeggio (not just one target), and to comping voicings treated as single-note-line targets. Full device → [[Pivot Tone Lines]].

## 4. Target the guide tones

The structural principle underneath all of the above: **good lines aim at 3rds and 7ths, not roots.**

Landing on the root of each chord states the obvious and sounds like an exercise. Landing on the 3rd or 7th states the chord's *quality*, and because guide tones move by step between chords, a line built on them is automatically smooth across changes. → [[Guide Tones]]

Over `Dm7 G7 Cmaj7`, a line landing on F, B, E on the three downbeats is already a good line before any decoration.

## 5. Digital patterns

Numbered scale-degree cells transposed through the changes — `1-2-3-5`, `1-3-5-7`, `3-5-7-9`, `5-3-2-1`. Mechanical to generate, which makes them good raw material and bad finished writing.

Their real use is **as a grid to decorate**: generate a 1-2-3-5 through the changes, then enclose the strong notes and add passing tones. That's a workable method for writing a horn line from nothing.

## 7. Line closers — stock cadential tails

A **line closer** is a short, largely fixed phrase kept in memory purely to *end* a line — the mirror image of the freely-built middle of a phrase. New device for the vault (not previously named here, though several `## From videos` bullets above describe cadence-shaped tags without naming the category).

**The default shape: arpeggio up, line closer down.** Start on a chord tone (often the 3rd) of the dominant, walk up its arpeggio (with extensions optionally thrown in — 9th/11th, a sound [[🎵 GRANT GREEN - Cool Blues|Grant Green]] and Wes Montgomery lean on), then drop into a memorized closer that resolves down into the target chord — worked example: start on the 3rd of F7 (A), walk up the F7 arpeggio, then a closer starting on F7's ♭9 (G♭) walking down to the 3rd of the I chord (B♭). This is the textbook 5–1 bebop shape.

**The arpeggio doesn't have to hand off smoothly.** You can jump straight from wherever the arpeggio lands to the closer's own starting note — the closer is a fixed unit, not something that has to be voice-led into.

**Flipped: closer first, arpeggio second.** The same closer that normally *ends* a phrase can instead *open* it, with the arpeggio then extending the line outward rather than resolving it. This reads as **[[Modal Jazz|tonic dominant]] language** — a dominant chord treated as its own stable sound rather than tension wanting to resolve — useful for stretching a dominant line before an eventual resolution, or for soloing over a static dominant that isn't moving (the B section of [[Rhythm Changes]]).

**Practice loop:** memorize a small bank of closers, then combine them freely with any arpeggio/enclosure pattern from §1–2 and finish every combination with one of the closers — a fast way to generate "correct-sounding" lines without composing a full cadence from scratch each time.

## 8. CESH — chromatic embellishment of static harmony

Take one note — usually the root of a chord that's about to sound, or that's currently sitting still — and move it **chromatically up or down through the changes**, independent of the underlying harmonic rhythm. The harmony can be genuinely static (one chord held for a while) or just slower-moving than the chromatic line riding underneath it; either way the term describes the chromatic line, not the chord.

**Where it differs from an approach note or an enclosure (§2–3):** those target a single arrival on a single chord tone. CESH is a **sustained chromatic line across several beats or bars**, more like a bass-line device borrowed into the melody than a one-note ornament.

**Practical use:** pick a note that matters (a chord root, a guide tone) and walk it by half step under or against otherwise-static harmony — it's a way to generate forward motion without changing the chord itself, useful anywhere a vamp or a held chord risks going static. → [[Chromatic Bass Lines]], [[Non-Harmonic Tones]]

## Transferring this outside jazz

Three things here earn their place in modern production:

- **Enclosures on a lead melody.** Take a two-note synth hook, enclose the second note. Costs two 16ths and stops the line sounding quantized-from-scratch.
- **Chromatic approach into a section.** A single half-step approach into the downbeat of a chorus is the cheapest lift available.
- **Bebop-scale passing tones in a bass line.** The added eighth is exactly what makes a walking-ish bass line land its chord tones on the beat. → [[Bassline Writing]]

What doesn't transfer well is density. Bebop's note rate is a genre convention, not a principle, and importing it wholesale into a trap beat just sounds busy.

- **A top-level framework for how vocabulary gets acquired and internalized in the first place, distinct from any single lick or device documented elsewhere on this page.** Three acquisition methods, in order of directness: (1) **aural transcription of canon solos** over common progressions (ii–V–I named specifically) — Charlie Parker on "Donna Lee," Miles Davis on "So What," Grant Green on "Cool Blues"; (2) **written transcription-study resources** — Joe Pass's *Guitar Style* (owned, [[Joe Pass Guitar Style - Enrichment|Joe Pass Guitar Style]]), Barry Galbraith's solo-method book, and "Omnibook"-style written-solo collections, used to visually study how players tackle those same progressions; (3) **jazz-improv books that explain the *why* behind the vocabulary**, not just notation — Jerry Coker's *Elements of Jazz Language* named.
