# How Elton John uses Inversions

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JSoJlw0Fgtg)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JSoJlw0Fgtg)

## Synopsis

A single-artist case study in chord inversions as a piano-writing habit — smooth bass-line construction, pedal point (including bass-imposed inversions the piano part alone doesn't show), and a genuinely useful craft explanation for WHY inversions show up constantly in piano writing but rarely occur to a guitarist: piano offers near-unlimited voicing freedom for a given chord, while a guitarist's chord vocabulary is bounded by a small set of fixed, memorized shapes.

## Techniques

- **Basic definition**: an inverted chord keeps the same notes but puts something other than the root at the bottom — E major first inversion (G♯ in the bass) is notated E/G♯, same chord, different bass note, different character/tone.
- **Inversions for a smooth connecting bass line, single example**: "Candle in the Wind" — a plain I→IV move (E→A) gets a first-inversion E/G♯ inserted, creating a smooth bass line between the two chords rather than a plain root-to-root jump.
- **Inversions for a smooth connecting bass line, extended across several chords = a line cliché**: "Rocket Man" — a chord sequence that would jump around in register if played in root position instead voices each chord so the bass notes form a stepwise line, moving by only a semitone or tone at a time. Bennett names this device explicitly as **line cliché**, tracing the term to Jazz-era songwriting where it became so common it was considered clichéd — reused constantly by Elton John regardless: "Don't Let the Sun Go Down on Me" (chorus), "Can You Feel the Love Tonight" (chorus — an inverted F chord glues B♭ to Gm; an inverted C7, with E in the bass, lifts from E♭ up to F by a semitone; more inversions stitch together a fast chord-change passage at the end of the chorus).
- **Pedal point**: a sustained bass note held constant while the chords above it change — turns the moving chords into a de facto set of inversions over the fixed bass. "Your Song" (piano intro) holds E♭ in the bass under a changing chord sequence — Bennett A/B's this against a version where the bass instead follows each chord's own root, which sounds noticeably less grounded/cohesive by comparison. "Tiny Dancer" (piano intro) does the same trick holding C in the bass. (Etymology note: "pedal point" comes from organ playing, where a sustained bass note is literally held down with a foot pedal while the hands play changing chords on the manuals above.)
- **Bass-imposed inversions the piano part doesn't show on its own**: just before the chorus of "Rocket Man," the piano keeps playing a static F chord while the BASS climbs to different notes underneath — the bass alone is what changes the effective inversion the listener hears, independent of what the piano is doing.
- **Pedal point as a tension device, not just a grounding one**: the verse of "I'm Still Standing" pedals on the root (B♭) in the bass throughout, despite the chords above changing — building tension that only releases when the bass note finally moves.
- **Why inversions are so central to Elton John's sound specifically — a piano-vs-guitar structural explanation**: on guitar, a given chord (e.g. open E major) is essentially ONE default shape a player reaches for automatically, with only a handful of alternate voicings available anywhere on the neck — a guitarist stringing together E-C-G typically isn't thinking about inversion at all, just defaulting to the standard open shapes everyone uses. On piano, by contrast, the same three-chord progression can be voiced dozens of different ways with two independent hands and no fixed "default shape" — which makes inversion an active, constant compositional choice rather than something that has to be deliberately sought out. Elton John has said directly that he writes differently on piano than on guitar for exactly this reason.
