# Burt Bacharach's Clever Key Changes

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iTrkALm2JNE)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iTrkALm2JNE)

## Synopsis

Four Burt Bacharach songs, all built on the same core device: shift key by swapping exactly ONE note (the note that distinguishes two closely related keys), rather than changing several notes/chords at once. Closes noting Bacharach deliberately ignored conventional songwriting rules (phrase-length pressure from record executives) in favor of trusting his ear.

## Techniques

- **The core device, stated once and demonstrated four times: a "related key" differs from the current key by exactly one sharp/flat — swap that single note and the key shifts, without ever needing an abrupt or obvious pivot.** "Walk On By": A minor → D minor, the only difference being B natural vs. B♭ — swapping the B is the entire modulation. Bacharach reinforces the arrival with a borrowed D MAJOR chord (not the diatonic D minor) right before the shift: on the surface it colors the passage as A Dorian (F♯ instead of A minor's F natural), but its real function is setting up a perfect cadence (D major→G minor) into the next key center — doing double duty as both a color choice and a functional pivot chord.
- **"Do You Know the Way to San Jose": C major → E minor** (again exactly one note different: F natural vs. F♯), lasting only ~6 bars before reverting. The return trip is smoothed by a C half-diminished chord, which — foreign to E minor but diatonic to C major — leads the ear back up into a D minor chord, re-establishing C major as home.
- **"Close to You": a deliberately AMBIGUOUS one-note key relationship, exploited rather than resolved quickly.** C major and E minor (and their shared relative, G major) differ only by F natural vs. F♯ — until that F actually sounds, the ear has no way to commit to either key, so an opening passage using no F at all is genuinely tonally undecided, resolved only by context (a repeated C major chord) until the real F♯ arrives and confirms E minor. **A production-level detail worth keeping**: the Carpenters' famous recording ADDS a piano intro establishing C major as tonic before the vocal enters — absent from Richard Chamberlain's original recording, where the song launches straight into the ambiguous vocal phrase with no such anchor. The Carpenters' arrangement choice is what makes the "is this C or E minor" ambiguity audible as a deliberate compositional device rather than something that flies by unnoticed in one bar.
- **"This Guy's In Love With You": D♭ major → A♭ major mid-chorus** (one note: G♭ vs. G natural — both keys share every other note), then the G♭ is reintroduced right before the chorus ends specifically to resolve the harmony back into D♭ major for the close.
- **Compositional philosophy worth keeping as context for the technique above**: Bacharach describes actively resisting industry pressure to conform phrases to a standard 4-bar length, crediting that resistance (eventually producing his own records "out of self-defense") as the reason his catalog is full of these odd, ear-led modulations and phrase lengths rather than more conventional writing.
