# String Crossing and Plucking Patterns

A BassBuzz coach's live-coaching fix for a bassist whose self-diagnosed weakness was finger speed/accuracy when improvising fast 16th-note lines — specifically fumbling *which string* to hit next, not fretting-hand errors. The root-cause framing: stumbling happens when a fast line pulls the plucking hand **outside a pattern it has actually rehearsed**, so the fingers hesitate ("wait, which finger goes now?"). The fix is to pre-drill a small set of string-crossing patterns in isolation so that, when improvising, the hand is drawing on rehearsed muscle memory more of the time instead of guessing live.

## The core method: break improvisation into rehearsable chunks

Fast improvised playing is too complex to drill directly — break it into simpler, isolatable pieces first ("that's working... now this is working... now this is working"), get each piece solid on its own, then let them recombine into the complex skill. The specific pieces to isolate here are **how many plucks you play per string before crossing to the next one**, and **which picking-hand fingering you use across the crossing**.

## Drill 1 — N-notes-per-string, ascending/descending across the string set

Pick a fixed number of plucks per string and walk it up and down the strings (can be done on open strings, or on any fretted note — the drill is about the plucking-hand pattern, not the pitches):
- **2 plucks per string** — two, two, two, two, working up and down the set. The easiest version; a good starting point before adding the others. *(general starting point)*
- **3 plucks per string** — noticeably harder than 2; work it the same way.
- **4 plucks per string** — named as one of the two or three counts (2, 3, or 4 notes per string) that cover **most of the crossings you'll actually need in real playing**, so these three counts are the priority set, not an arbitrary progression.
- **1 pluck per string** — the other end of the range; every string is a crossing, so it's the purest test of the finger-choice decision below.

**Finding the productive tempo (edge-of-ability loading):** for each pattern, notice there's a speed where it's easy, and a faster speed where it starts feeling stumbly — that boundary is the practice edge, not a flaw to avoid. With a metronome: find the tempo where you're nailing the pattern **100%** of the time, then creep the tempo up until you're missing it roughly **10% of the time**, and drill at that edge rather than either the too-easy tempo or maxing out (see [[Interleaved Practice, Chunking, and Deliberate-Practice Drills#Practice at the edge of your ability ("deep practice")|Practice at the edge of your ability]] for the same threshold-pushing principle applied on guitar).

## Drill 2 — decide rake vs. alternate on every direction change

This is the piece that actually fixes the stumbling, and it's presented as the more important half of the method. Going **one direction** across the strings, the plucking hand naturally alternates fingers the whole way (e.g. index-middle-index-middle…) — there's no decision to make. Coming **back the other direction**, there's a real fork, and an un-rehearsed hand will stumble exactly at that fork because it hasn't decided which option to take:
- **Alternate the whole way** — keep strictly alternating fingers through the direction change (e.g. up: index-middle-index-middle; back down: continue index-middle-index-middle without a repeat).
- **Rake** — repeat the same finger across the string change instead of alternating (e.g. up: index-middle, index-middle; back down: middle-middle, middle-middle — the finger that lands on the last string of the first pass "rakes" into the first string of the return pass rather than alternating).
- Drill **both versions**, and also the **flipped starting finger** for each (start on middle instead of index) — that's 1-note-per-string alone yielding four distinct rehearsed patterns (alternate/rake × index-start/middle-start).

**Why indecision, not speed, is the actual cause of stumbling:** the flub happens specifically when the hand reaches the direction-change fork without having already decided rake-or-alternate — the fingers freeze mid-choice and a finger ends up somewhere it shouldn't. The fix isn't "practice more reps," it's **pre-deciding** which option a given line uses and drilling that specific choice slowly until it's automatic, the same way you'd pre-decide up-stroke vs. down-stroke on a guitar pick rather than leaving it to chance in the moment (conceptual analog, not the same mechanism — see [[Directional Alternate Picking System]]).

**Worked example on a real fretted riff, not just open strings.** A separate BassBuzz daily-practice riff (a one-finger-per-fret pattern spanning frets 2–6) names its own two rake spots directly: coming **down from the octave, G string → D string**, reusing the plucking finger there, and again **D string → A string**. Presented as one concrete option against **strict alternate** (index-middle the whole way) as the simpler default — same alternate-vs-rake fork as Drill 2 above, just anchored to specific string crossings in a real exercise rather than an open-string drill. Recovery tip if the fingering falls apart at speed: drop tempo back down until the plucking is clean, then rebuild toward the target BPM. Full exercise: [[One-Finger-Per-Fret 5-Level Daily Drill]]. → BassBuzz, [[Practice THIS Daily (10 Mins)]]

## The parity math hack — predicting rake vs. alternate without trying both

A companion BassBuzz "versus battle" video adds a fast heuristic for the same rake/alternate fork Drill 2 drills, letting you predict the winner **before** working out both fingerings by hand: look at how the string-crossing notes group.
- **Even-numbered groups (twos, fours) → strict alternating wins.** An even group matches the even number of plucking fingers (two: index, middle) — e.g. an octave-fifth-root line played two-notes-per-string alternates cleanly (I‑M‑I‑M‑I‑M) with no decision needed.
- **Odd-numbered groups (ones, threes) → raking wins.** Raking is specifically what lets an odd-numbered group land cleanly on the two-finger rotation — a 1-note-per-string line raked gives an easy all-index or all-middle option; a 3-note group raked lets the same short pattern (e.g. I‑M‑I) repeat identically every time instead of drifting out of phase.
- **Mixed basslines get decided chunk by chunk**, not with one global rule for the whole song — apply the parity check per string-crossing section.
- Often more than one fingering works equally well for a given chunk — when that's true, there's no single "correct" choice; pick a favorite and stay consistent with it rather than re-deciding every time you play the line.

**Three worked rounds, each a real bassline scored against the hack:**
- **"Just What I Needed" (The Cars) — even grouping, alternating wins.** Chugging eighth notes, crossing A string → E string. Alternating keeps I‑M‑I‑M straight through the crossing; raking reuses the middle finger on the crossing and flips the rest of the bar to M‑I‑M‑I. Raking adds a finger-lead switch (more coordination) for **no efficiency gain**, since the notes are evenly grouped — named as the case where raking is actively pointless rather than merely unnecessary.
- **"My Friend of Misery" (Metallica) — odd/mixed grouping, raking wins clearly.** Alternating the riff's second bar gives an unbroken I‑M‑I‑M‑I‑M‑I‑M; raking gives I‑I‑I‑M‑M‑M‑I‑I — far fewer finger changes, and the version that actually delivers the riff's flowing, ringing-notes feel. Genuinely hard to play clean with strict alternating (had to be practiced specifically to demo, starting at half speed).
- **"Johnny, Kick a Hole in the Sky" (Red Hot Chili Peppers, *Mother's Milk*) — a real draw, split within one riff.** Bar one (notes grouped in twos, fast tempo): alternating wins, keeping I‑M rocking with no change, vs. raking's mid-bar flip (I‑M‑M‑I) that adds complication for no gain. Bar two (notes grouped in **threes**): raking wins — I‑M‑I repeats identically on every group, vs. alternating drifting out of a clean pattern. The proof case that the parity hack applies **per chunk**, not per song — see the mixed-basslines note above.

**No universally "correct" technique — most pro bassists use both, chosen per line.** The video explicitly debunks bass-forum wisdom that certain named players "only strictly alternate" (the cited examples turn out to rake themselves) and that raking is inherently slower (countered with fast players who rake regularly). The practical takeaway isn't the specific names — it's that standardizing on one technique for everything is the wrong instinct; the parity hack above is how to make the per-line call quickly instead of guessing.

**The plucking trap — why beginners must drill clean alternating even on lines simple enough to fake.** At slow tempos almost any plucking habit "works" — most simple basslines are even playable with a single finger — so sloppy alternating (a finger repeating on a string it shouldn't) or lazy raking (raking when the line calls for strict alternating) goes unnoticed. Push the same habit to a faster tempo and it causes stumbling, and it's easy to miss in the moment because attention is usually locked on the fretting hand rather than the plucking hand. **The fix is to deliberately drill clean, decisive alternating even on basslines simple enough to get away with sloppy technique** — skipping that discipline at slow tempo is what causes stumbling later at speed. Same root-cause shape as Drill 2's "indecision at the fork" diagnosis above, just from the tempo-tolerance angle rather than the decision-fork angle. Raking can also become a **crutch for bad alternating technique** rather than a deliberate choice — one stated reason a couple of go-to reference tracks for raking done right are worth knowing: raking is called essential for funky rake patterns ("Strut," The Meters) and funky ghost notes ("Girls on Film," Duran Duran). → BassBuzz, [[Are You Plucking the Wrong Way? (Raking v. Alternating Showdown)]]

## The failure mode to eliminate

**Never let one finger end up jumping to play multiple notes in a row** — that's the concrete symptom of an undecided rake/alternate choice colliding with a fast line, and the tell that a pattern needs to be pulled out and drilled in isolation via Drills 1–2 rather than pushed through at speed.

## Applying it back to real playing

Once the isolated patterns are solid, bring the fix back into an actual riff/line: play it a notch **slower** than performance tempo, and shift attention specifically to **the plucking hand and the feel of the fingertips on the strings** rather than the fretting hand — de-emphasizing the fretting side on purpose so the plucking-hand decision-making gets full attention while it's still being consolidated.

## Four plucking approaches to any bassline (drill-as-vocabulary framing)

A second BassBuzz lesson ("String Crossing Crusher," exercise two of a three-exercise beginner routine — see [[Fretting-Hand Finger Independence Drill]] for exercise one, [[Bass Note Muting Technique]] for exercise three) reframes the same alternate/rake × index-start/middle-start choice from Drill 2 above as **four named, reusable plucking approaches** worth being consciously fluent in on *any* bassline, not just as a stumbling-fix drill. Worked example — a string-only pattern crossing A-D-G-D-A:
- **Alternate, index-start** — index-middle-index-middle-index. Called the plucking-hand default on bass.
- **Alternate, middle-start** — middle-index-middle-index-middle.
- **Rake, index-start** — index-middle-index-index-index (repeating a finger on the string-crossing-back-down move, for efficiency).
- **Rake, middle-start** — middle-index-middle-middle-middle.

The point isn't picking one "correct" approach — it's building comfort with all four so the choice becomes **intuitive per-bassline** rather than a fixed habit. Practice value: running an unfamiliar bassline through more than one of these four exposes which one actually fits it best, which a single default approach never reveals.

## A finger-*count* axis: one-finger plucking vs. alternating (consistency vs. speed)

A distinct choice from everything above — Drills 1–2 and the four-approach vocabulary all assume **two-finger alternating** as the baseline; this is about whether to use **one** plucking finger at all, on lines simple enough to allow it. Standard teacher advice pushes alternating index/middle specifically so you can keep up with fast material. The counter-case: some pros still favor **one-finger plucking** for its **consistency of attack** — every note gets hit by the exact same finger, doing the exact same motion, which removes the index-vs-middle tonal/volume mismatch this note's own **Dynamics control** section above is built to train out. **The catch, stated directly by the teacher himself despite liking one-finger plucking:** beginners still have to force themselves to consistently practice alternating, or they'll never get comfortable with it — and even moderately fast one-finger plucking is genuinely hard, so skipping alternating practice creates a real speed ceiling later. **Verdict: alternating is still the default to build first** (same "don't let a simple line's forgiveness hide sloppy fundamentals" logic as this note's plucking-trap warning above); one-finger plucking is a legitimate tone-consistency choice to reach for deliberately once alternating isn't a crutch you're avoiding. → BassBuzz, [[The 10 Rules of Bass Playing (That Pros Break)]]

## Finger roll technique (pinky) for root-fifth-octave shapes

For the **fretted** side of the same exercise (not the plucking hand): instead of the default index-ring-pinky-per-group fingering, a **finger roll** — index, pinky, pinky — is offered as a more efficient alternative for common **root-fifth-octave** bassline shapes. Mechanics: the pinky **fingertip** frets the first of the two pinky notes (e.g. the 5th on the D string); for the second pinky note (e.g. the octave on the G string), **straighten the pinky's end joint and/or rock the arm back**, fretting closer to the **first knuckle** instead of the tip — effectively using two different contact points on the same finger to hit two adjacent strings without a full hand shift. Flagged as genuinely challenging at first and dependent on individual finger length/hand geometry — expect to experiment with the joint-straighten/arm-rock balance to find what works for your hand. Optional add-on to the plucking-pattern choice above, not a replacement for it — combine any of the four plucking approaches with either the standard index-ring-pinky fretting or this finger-roll alternative.

**A second finger-roll variant — index finger, knuckle-to-fingertip, across adjacent strings at the same fret.** Distinct from the pinky root-fifth-octave roll above (different finger, different geometry, and rolling *across strings at one fret* rather than *up strings across two frets*): in the 21 Guns chorus (Green Day, bassist Mike Dirnt), the riff asks for the 1st fret of the A string immediately followed by the 1st fret of the E string, with no time to reposition the whole hand. Fix: fret the A-string note on the **back of the index finger's knuckle**, then **roll the pressure forward toward the fingertip** to land the E-string note at the same fret with the same finger, instead of physically jumping the fingertip between strings. Explicitly optional — jumping the fingertip works too, "just a little bit sloppier." **This is the free-tier demonstration of a move [[5 Beginner Bass Riffs (You Can’t F*** Up)]] only named without showing** — that video's "Under Pressure" section offers an index-finger finger roll as one of two valid fingerings but couldn't demonstrate the mechanics (gated behind BassBuzz's paid course there); this 21 Guns riff is where the free-tier BassBuzz catalog actually teaches it end to end. → BassBuzz, [[3 Beginner Bass Riffs (You Can’t F*** Up)]]

## "Finger syncing" — the anti-pattern of mirroring plucking fingers to fretting fingers

A separate BassBuzz lesson names and bans a specific beginner habit: **"finger syncing"** — matching plucking-hand finger choice to fretting-hand finger choice (fret with index → pluck with index; fret with middle → pluck with middle), because it feels natural when you're new. The video is blunt about it: "never ever think of bass fingering that way — just stop it." It's a super inflexible approach that breaks the instant a line needs to pluck the same note twice in a row, or fret with the ring/pinky — both of which happen in basically every real bassline.

**The fix: treat fretting fingering and plucking fingering as two fully independent systems that don't relate to each other.** Work out each hand's pattern on its own terms rather than deriving one from the other:
- **Worked example** — a riff mixing open strings with 2nd/3rd-fret notes: fretting uses index for every 2nd-fret note and middle for every 3rd-fret note (`open, open, index, middle, middle, index, open, open, middle, index, middle, open`), while plucking is simply **straight alternating** (`index, middle, index, middle...`) for the entire line **regardless of what the fretting hand is doing** — including the open strings, which have no fretting-hand equivalent to sync to in the first place. Syncing the plucking pattern to the fretting pattern here would force inefficient same-finger repeats for no reason.
- This is the same underlying decouple-the-two-hands logic as the rake-vs-alternate fork above (Drill 2) — both are ways the plucking hand's pattern is chosen independently of what the fretting hand happens to be doing — but "finger syncing" is the more fundamental, earlier-stage myth to bust: it targets beginners who haven't yet separated the two hands into independent systems at all, before rake-vs-alternate decisions are even on the table.

## Dynamics control — even volume in alternate plucking

A distinct plucking-hand skill from the rest of this note: not *which* finger crosses where, but whether alternating fingers deliver **equal volume**. New to alternate plucking, the index and middle fingers typically don't yet — most audible on a repetitive **"chugging"** driving-note pattern, where uneven volume between the two fingers reads as an obvious wobble rather than a clean, driving line.

**Prep drill (BassBuzz):**
1. **Intentionally play uneven first** — loud, soft, loud, soft on alternating fingers. The point: uneven isn't inherently *wrong*, it's just not what you want in this context — playing it on purpose builds awareness of what uneven actually feels/sounds like before trying to eliminate it.
2. **Flip the pattern** — soft, loud, soft, loud — so both fingers get practice on both dynamic roles rather than one finger always being "the loud one."
3. **Push further to same-dynamic pairs** — loud-loud and soft-soft on consecutive plucks (breaking strict alternation) to isolate each individual finger's weak spot rather than only ever testing them in an alternating pair.
4. **Switch between all the patterns** to build conscious, on-demand volume control per finger, rather than accepting whatever volume difference happens to fall out of habit.

This is a training-wheels step, not the destination — done, the plucking hand should be able to produce even volume on a chugging line *by default*, with the loud/soft control now available intentionally rather than being an unmanaged inconsistency.

## A fifth plucking approach: the back-and-forth strum (Geddy Lee)

A distinct right-hand motion from every pattern above — none of Drills 1–2's alternate/rake string-by-string plucking is strumming. Covering Chris Squire's fingerstyle-friendly bass parts, BassBuzz names Geddy Lee's actual approach to playing the "Roundabout" (Yes) riff fingerstyle: rather than picking individual notes with alternating or raked fingers, Geddy plays it as a **back-and-forth strum with a single finger (the index)** — closer to a miniature strumming motion than a per-note pluck. Offered alongside a second, more familiar option for the same riff — **clacky downward plucks** (the same approach BassBuzz documents for Steve Harris, and one Geddy also reaches for often) — as two named alternatives for a fingerstyle player working through a pick-oriented riff without a pick. Not mapped onto this note's four-approach vocabulary above (alternate/rake × index/middle-start) because it isn't a per-string plucking pattern at all — worth treating as a fifth, separate tool in the plucking-hand toolkit for riffs with a strummy, chordal-adjacent feel rather than a clean single-note line. → [[🎵 YES - Roundabout]], [[Pick vs. Fingers on Bass — Tone and Technique Tradeoffs]] — BassBuzz, [[Why This Bass Line Effin’ Kills It → Roundabout by Yes (+Tab)]]
