# Vocal Comping and Takes

**Everything that happens before the vocal chain.** `Production/Chains/1 Vocals/` has 22 chains and every one of them describes processing a vocal that already exists — this is the part that decides whether there's a good performance to process.

## How many passes

There's no correct number, but there is a wrong shape: **too few and you have no options; too many and the singer is worn out and you're comping fatigue.**

A workable default:

1. **Two or three full passes** while the voice is fresh, top to bottom, no stopping. These usually carry the performance.
2. **A pass for the sections that aren't landing** — chorus only, or the bridge.
3. **Punch-ins** for specific lines that are close but not right.

**Track the full passes first, always.** A vocal assembled entirely from punch-ins never has a through-line, because the singer never had one.

**Label as you go.** `Vox T1`, `Vox T2`, `Vox chorus T4`. Ten unnamed takes an hour later is a comping session that starts with archaeology.

## Comping

**Comp by phrase, not by syllable.**

The instinct is to take the technically best fragment of every word. That produces a take that's perfect and dead — a Frankenstein comp that's measurably better than any single pass and worse to listen to, because the performance stops being continuous. A phrase carries intention; a syllable doesn't.

**What you're listening for, in order:**

1. **Delivery** — which take actually *means* it. This outranks everything below.
2. **Pitch** — but see [[Vocal Tuning]]; some of what sounds off is expression.
3. **Timing** — where it sits against the beat. Often fixable by nudging (see [[Pocket and Microtiming]]).
4. **Tone** — brightness and weight, which drift as the voice warms up and tires.

**Where comps give themselves away:** the seams. Breaths and consonants at edit points are audible if you cut carelessly.

- **Cut on a breath** wherever you can — it masks the join.
- **Keep breaths.** Removing every breath sounds unnatural and unsettling; a vocal with no breathing reads as inhuman. Ride them down if they're loud, don't delete them.
- **Watch consonant collisions** — cutting mid-word leaves a doubled or clipped consonant.
- **Crossfade every edit.** Short, but present.

## The rule worth remembering

**A comp should be better than the best single take. If it isn't, use the best single take.**

That's a real check, not a platitude — assemble the comp, then A/B it against your strongest full pass. If the single take feels more alive, you over-comped, and the fix is to comp in longer chunks.

## Punch-ins

Recording over a specific section rather than re-taking the whole part.

- **Give a run-up.** Start playback several bars early so the singer arrives in the phrase rather than starting cold on it.
- **Match the energy**, not just the notes. A punch-in recorded at a different intensity to the surrounding lines is more obvious than a pitch problem.
- **Watch the room and the mic position** — if the session resumes another day, a moved mic or a different distance makes the punch obvious. Photograph the setup.
- Central to rap workflow, where the whole verse is often built line by line. See [[Rap Vocal Production]].

## From videos

- **Trained precision doubling as a substitute for comping: sing every take start-to-finish, never punching in, and let the doubles land in near-alignment naturally.** Arlo Parks doubles/triples/quadruples vocals by re-singing the entire song from scratch each time rather than comping sections together, landing in close alignment without correction tools — Baird calls it "extremely crazy," having "never experienced this with another singer." The habit traces to not knowing comping was an option as a teenager, so every home recording had to be perfect start to finish; the constraint became a trained skill. → [[Arlo Parks Vocal Chain]] — Arlo Parks & Baird (Tape Notes), [[TN 183 Arlo Parks & Baird - Full Video Episode]]

- **Deliberately NOT comping a vocal, and rejecting a technically-better re-cut, to preserve a specific emotional/physical state.** Myles Smith / Peter Fenn's *My Mess* bridge: recorded one continuous take, unpolished on purpose, at the tail end of a multi-day writing camp when the singer's voice was genuinely worn out — the vocal exhaustion is described as matching the song's content. They tried recutting fresh in both the original and a different key and reverted to the original take both times specifically because a technically cleaner vocal didn't carry the same emotion. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]] — Myles Smith / Peter Fenn (Tape Notes), [[TN 184 Myles Smith & Peter Fenn - Full Video Episode]]
- **The comp-by-take discipline applies to a featured instrumental solo just as much as a vocal — and the performer can drive their own comp.** Eddie Van Halen's "Beat It" guitar solo was tracked, then he came back later with his own trusted engineer specifically to comp the final performance together from the original takes — the artist and engineer chose which sections of which takes made the edit, rather than the producing team assembling it independently. Separately, casting the right performer for a part is itself a comparison exercise: several session guitarists were fully tracked on the same song before the production settled on the one whose combination and personality actually fit. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]] — Anthony Marinelli & Matt Forger, [[Inside the Guitar Sessions for Michael Jacksons Beat It]]

- **Capture vocal takes from the moment a lyric idea appears during writing, rather than treating "doing vocals" as a separate, announced event — the earliest, least self-conscious takes are frequently the keepers.** Jack Antonoff: turning vocal recording into its own formal moment makes performers self-conscious, "especially if you're a songwriter... some part of you gets scared." His fix: keep a mic permanently set up in the writing room (no dedicated booth) so an idea gets sung and captured immediately, imperfect lyric and all — "the mic is right here, and as we're working or writing, it's just like, oh, let's put it in." His stated experience: the very first vocal takes captured this way are keepers "9.99999 out of 10 times," because they aren't self-conscious performances. → [[Vocal Mic Technique]] — Mix with the Masters (Jack Antonoff), [[Sonically enhancing the meaning of songs with Jack Antonoff]]
- **Comp across inconsistent early takes for the specific standout MOMENTS, rather than discarding takes that don't match each other in tone or polish — a named case study on Lorde's "Liability."** A repeated "just put it in, put it in" writing process produced multiple takes with different vocal character/tone from each other, no single clean pass. Engineer Laura Sisk's method, quoted directly: identify which specific moments across the different takes carry the real "heart and soul," accept that they'll sound different from each other, and comp/blend those specific moments into one performance rather than picking one take wholesale or re-cutting for consistency — "we have the heart and soul here... this has to become one thing because this is perfect." → [[Vocal Mic Technique]] — Mix with the Masters (Jack Antonoff), [[Sonically enhancing the meaning of songs with Jack Antonoff]]
- **Build a singer's confidence/looseness before the take that matters, by getting an easy layer down first.** "It's nice to build a layer of confidence before you have to do the vocal — like, oh, this would be a cool backup, great, let's get it, and let's get eight stacks of it... getting someone to use their voice before they have to get the perfect take." → [[Layering and Stacking]] — Mix with the Masters (Jack Antonoff), [[Sonically enhancing the meaning of songs with Jack Antonoff]]
- **Choose the keeper take by mood/feeling, not by how in-tune or in-time it is — stated as the core take-selection philosophy for a full-band rock take, not one phrase-level edit.** Nick Launay: "we'll choose the take that we like best from the mood that it creates, or the feeling it gives us, rather than whether it's in tune or in time — or in fact, sometimes the most accurately played takes actually don't have such a great feeling as the ones that were a bit sloppy," and a tempo shift between verse and chorus can be exactly what makes a take work emotionally. Contrasted with his own earlier career (pre-Pro-Tools, no editing/time-stretch available) when chasing maximal accuracy was the whole goal — a stance he says he's moved away from. **Textbook example: the Rolling Stones' "Satisfaction."** An audible guitar-pedal-switch flub on the chorus transition (late click, missed first riff of verse two) stayed on the released record and is now part of its charm — "a lot of people would fix that [today]... and then the charm's gone." Same underlying principle as this note's own "kept an audibly fatigued take on purpose" (Lola Young, below) and "full takes over punch-fixing" (070 Shake, below) entries, generalized beyond vocals to a whole band's performance. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]] — Mix with the Masters (Nick Launay), [[Parallel distortion by Nick Launay]]
- **Pull a vocal moment straight from the demo instead of re-cutting it, specifically because it already sounds right — a take-selection call, not a processing one.** On one line in a second verse: "we realized that we love how that sounded in the demo, so we pulled that in from the demo — him doing [it] — but it's really compressed, it's really pushed, and it sounds like it has vocoder on it as well." The demo's baked-in character (heavy compression + pitch artifacts reading as vocoder) was the reason to keep it rather than replace it with a "clean" re-tracked pass — the same instinct as comping for delivery over polish, applied to a whole borrowed take rather than a phrase within one. → [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]] — Kuk Harrell (Mix with the Masters), [[Creating vocal stacks with Kuk Harrell]]
- **Diagnose a "weird" edit by naming the actual missing/wrong phoneme — false leads first, correct answer by process of elimination — then fix it by pasting a donor consonant copied from elsewhere in the session, run as a separate pass per track.** An edit "sounds pretty much like we're missing the 'b' of baby. Or maybe it's an 'm' of maybe... it's actually supposed to be 'hey there.' And so we're missing the 'h.'" Once identified: "take that 'h' that's there and let's put it on these tracks here... copy that, maybe we should get that on a beat" for the background vocals, then explicitly repeated as its own pass on the lead: "let's do the same thing for the lead vocal... let's get that 'hey' on the lead voice as well" — fixing one part is never assumed to fix another sharing the same lyric. Worth the general lesson: when an edit reads as subtly wrong, try naming candidate missing/doubled consonants against the actual lyric rather than re-editing blind, and check the pasted fix lands on the beat grid before committing. → [[Tony Maserati Vocal Chain]] — Tony Maserati (Mix with the Masters), [[Vocal chain with Tony Maserati]]
- **Don't stop a singer to chase "the perfect take" — let them sing the same phrase repeatedly (stated as seven times) without commentary, unless there's something genuinely specific to correct, and record continuously rather than starting/stopping per pass.** Andrés Torres and Mauricio Rengifo, discussing Luis Fonsi's sessions: "there are singers that you don't want to start correcting and looking for the perfect take because it starts to stress the singer out... it becomes a beautiful process if you make it something mechanical, singing that part seven times without needing to correct it, unless you need to tell them something specific." If the singer "has the magic," the right take arrives on its own — repeated generic notes ("asking them, asking them, asking them, asking them") are framed as actively worse than just letting more passes happen. Paired with a dual-channel "auxiliary input" tracking setup (a live-processed monitor feed on one channel, the actual comped take on another) that keeps the control room quiet during takes instead of interrupting the singer to react to each one, and a stated tradeoff: this whole approach takes LONGER overall than recording to a single mixed channel, because of the extra comping pass it creates — worth it anyway for how it treats the singer. → [[Latency-Free Tracking]], [[Vocal Mic Technique]], [[1 Main Vocal Track]] — Mix with the Masters (Andrés Torres and Mauricio Rengifo), [[Vocal production with Andrés Torres and Mauricio Rengifo]]
- **Kept an audibly fatigued, imperfect lead vocal take on purpose, as part of a full-band whole-take philosophy, because the imperfection reinforced the song's theme.** Lola Young was losing her voice by the time "Messy" was recorded and eventually said she couldn't keep singing; the verses were kept from that fatigued take rather than redone or comped around. Paired with a full live-band tracking approach — drums, acoustic guitar, bass, synth, and lead vocal recorded together, and the entire take (not a comp) used: "I think we used the whole take... the imperfections were kind of part of it," called a "raw, organic way of working." The "slight imperfections" are described as "massively" making the song what it is, and tied directly to the song's own lyric theme (too messy / too perfect) — imperfection kept as more "enticing and interesting" than a clean-cut take. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]] — Mix with the Masters (Lola Young, Jared Solomon), [Behind the Track｜'Messy' by Lola Young](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=02QKFlkbmYY)
- **The same comp-many-takes-for-the-accidental-keeper logic applied to a live instrumentalist, not a vocalist.** Jack Antonoff's 'Modern Girl' (Bleachers): "what I like to do when I'm recording is just have him play like eight takes and I'll grab moments" — describing fiddle player Bobby Hawk. Same discipline this page documents for vocals (many passes, comp for the phrase/moment that has "it"), stated here as a general recording habit, not vocal-specific. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]], [[Layering and Stacking]] — Mix with the Masters (Jack Antonoff), [Behind the Track｜'Modern Girl' by Bleachers](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5Y1PQt5imXo)
- **Full continuous takes over comping — no punch-fixing, redo the whole take instead.** 070 Shake records "the whole verse through" or "the whole song through" in one pass rather than assembling from fragments, explicitly to preserve "one expression and one flow"; if a line doesn't land she'll go back and redo the FULL take rather than punch it, because — like speech — "I can't go back and fix how I said it to you." Reinforced on camera by a clip from the film *Naked Lunch* (Burroughs/Kerouac/Ginsberg debating whether "to rewrite is to deceive and lie") used to frame their own philosophy: reworking/editing a vocal risks betraying the honesty of the original delivery. Paired with a companion move on the same video: near-zero vocal FX ("it's really not that much stuff going on, it's just really how she sounds") and autotune pulled back further than ever to lean into that raw character — see [[Vocal Tuning]]. → [[1 Main Vocal Track]] — Mix with the Masters (070 Shake & Dave), [Behind the Track｜'Elephant' by 070 Shake](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OKBT8-0l9AI)
- **Deliberately teach an artist to vocal-engineer and comp their OWN takes, and physically leave the room while they record.** Finneas on Billie Eilish: "I've spent the last couple years teaching her how to vocal engineer herself because she's very particular and loves to do a million takes and loves to comp them herself, and everybody wins if she's doing the work." He bounces in and out of the room rather than sitting over her shoulder for every take. → [[Vocal Mic Technique]] — Finneas, [[Behind the Track - 'BIRDS OF A FEATHER' by Billie Eilish]]
- **Being genuinely alone (not just "comfortable") while tracking is what let the take's most extreme vocal delivery moment happen — the same psychology as singing in the shower or the car alone.** Not feeling watched lets a performer "fail harder," i.e. commit further past their normal comfort zone; on this song, Finneas was out of the room entirely (upstairs getting water) when Billie tracked the take in question, and she brought it to him afterward looking "sheepish" — it turned out to be the keeper. Worth remembering as a reason to leave a singer alone for high-stakes takes rather than staying present "for support." → [[Vocal Mic Technique]] — Finneas, [[Behind the Track - 'BIRDS OF A FEATHER' by Billie Eilish]]
- **Keep only the strongest short fragment of a take and copy/paste it to rebuild the rest of the phrase, instead of a full retake.** Recording his own melodic vocal for a sample: only the first three notes of a take landed the way he wanted, so that fragment alone was kept, copy-pasted into a second slot to reuse it, and just the remaining second half of the run was re-sung separately — a faster, more targeted move than scrapping the whole phrase and starting over. Paired with a wordless "ooh" vocalizing habit (stated directly as a workaround for not being a strong singer — easier to actually hit the notes than on full lyrics) and one-ear-off monitoring while tracking with live auto-tune (see [[Vocal Mic Technique]]). → [[Vocal Mic Technique]], [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]], [[Sampling and Sound Selection]] — loner, [how to add your own vocals to your r&b samples (in depth tutorial)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NP27-3YFlDk)
- **Consolidate a vocal-chop stem and trim its remainder/tail before any further chopping.** A quick two-step cleanup pass on a downloaded community vocal-chop stem — consolidate the clip, cut the leftover tail — done specifically to make the sample "much easier to work with" before applying any processing (double-time, reverse, pitch shift). → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]] — loner, [turning your melodies into crazy full samples!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=M8saMeKUdTE)
