# Jamerson's Stupidly Simple Exercise (for Killer Bass Lines)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JXYdib2PaGE)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JXYdib2PaGE)

## Synopsis

BassBuzz reverse-engineers a single practice exercise James Jamerson (Motown's uncredited house bassist with the Funk Brothers, now in the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame) taught one-on-one to Ron Brown, documented in the *Standing in the Shadows of Motown* book — and unpacks it into a repeatable three-ingredient formula for writing basslines in any style: **root-fifth-octave** as the harmonic skeleton, a **chromatic approach walk** (three frets below a target, working up into the fifth or octave) as connective tissue, and **syncopation** as the rhythmic engine that gives the notes their tension-and-release. The most valuable part of the video isn't the exercise itself (which is genuinely simple, hence the title) but the demonstration that Jamerson's *own* recorded basslines are close-to-literal applications of it — worked out note-by-note on "Bernadette" (Four Tops), "For Once in My Life" (Stevie Wonder), and "Darling Dear" (Jackson 5) — followed by a from-scratch writing demo applying the formula to a new chord progression, and a genre-hopping victory lap showing the same devices surfacing in The Beatles, Stone Temple Pilots, and Rush. ## What's taught

**The core exercise:** over a static C, root-fifth-octave (C–G–C) as the skeleton, then a chromatic walk starting **three frets below** the target note, stepping up chromatically into the fifth, with the fifth re-inserted partway through the walk-up into the octave (rather than a plain chromatic run) to "hammer home" the target. Played in **swing eighths** — offbeat notes land two-thirds past the beat rather than the even halfway point of straight eighths, which is why the exercise sounds "lumpy" rather than metronomic when first heard. Recommended to practice in **all 12 keys**, not just C — BassBuzz notes the *Standing in the Shadows* book only writes it out in a handful of keys, so he made a companion PDF covering all 12 (linked in the video description) and promises a follow-up video walking the fingerings in every key.

**Ingredient 1 — Root, fifth, octave.** Root = the home-base note for the current key/chord; octave = the same note name an octave up; fifth = a strong supportive note that adds movement without adding new harmonic color. Framed as the most generic possible note choice — "the bread and butter of bass playing" — and the reason today's bassists still default to it is largely because of how thoroughly Jamerson normalized the device in the '60s with the Funk Brothers.

**Ingredient 2 — Chromatic approach (tension and release).** Chromatic = drawing from any of the 12 available notes rather than staying inside a 5-or-7-note scale. Played with no destination, chromatic notes just sound wrong; played *toward* a target note, they create tension that resolves the instant the target lands — explicitly tied to "tension and release is a huge part of what makes life non-boring" and specifically credited as what gives Motown's bass parts their depth/journey quality.
- **"Bernadette" (Four Tops)** worked example: riff moves E♭ → D♭. Root-fifth-octave on E♭ = E♭-B♭-E♭; on D♭ = D♭-A♭-D♭ — between the two chords that covers nearly every note, plus each chord's third. The one leftover note, a **D natural**, only makes sense as a chromatic approach tone connecting the two chord centers.
- **"For Once in My Life" (Stevie Wonder)**, key of F (F = root/octave, C = fifth): described as almost literally copy-pasted from the exercise — heavy root-fifth-octave use, a three-fret chromatic walk up to the fifth (with a small interruption), then a three-fret chromatic walk straight to the octave.

**Ingredient 3 — Syncopation.** Not written into the exercise itself, but called the most central ingredient to Jamerson's actual sound. Syncopation = emphasizing off-beat notes (the "e"/"a" of a beat) rather than on-beat ones; non-syncopated notes (quarter notes, on-beat eighths) sound straight and tension-free ("chugging along"), syncopated notes build tension that resolves only when the line lands back on a strong beat — the same tension/release logic as ingredient 2, applied to rhythm instead of pitch. Distinguished explicitly from **swing eighths** (a timing/feel choice — the exercise itself is played with swing eighths, syncopation is a separate device layered on top in real playing).
- **"Darling Dear" (Jackson 5)** worked example: across two bars, essentially every note after the initial C lands on an "e" or "a" (maximally syncopated), resolving only when the line lands on beat 1 of the F chord — and the same two bars also carry ingredient 1 (root-fifth-octave on the C chord) and ingredient 2 (a chromatic walk to the fifth on the F chord), making it the clearest all-three-ingredients-at-once example in the video.

**Gear/technique tricks for the tone (sound selection):** Jamerson **plucked with one finger**, on a **Fender Precision Bass (P-bass)**, with **flatwound strings** and **high action** — named directly as part of "getting this sounding right" before the writing demo.

**Applied writing demo:** a new chord progression, **G major7 – B minor7 – A minor7 – D7 – G major** (a 1‑3‑2‑5‑1, soul-flavored, framed as a "What's Going On"-style band exercise), worked from a plain root-note-per-chord starting point up to a full Jamerson-formula bassline: G chord = the exercise directly (root-fifth-octave + chromatic walk); chromatic walk into B's root; octave-fifth-root motion on B; chromatic walk into A; more root-fifth-octave; chromatic walk into D; repeat; chromatic walk back into G. Backstory aside: the "What's Going On" bassline itself is framed with the (widely told) anecdote that Marvin Gaye had to find a too-drunk-to-stand Jamerson in a bar and record him lying on his back in the studio.

**Cross-genre reach (why Jamerson is in the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame, not filed only under Motown/soul):**
- **The Beatles — "Nowhere Man" (Paul McCartney):** verse, over an E chord — chromatic walk up to B (the chord's fifth, and the root of the next chord) plus heavy syncopation.
- **Stone Temple Pilots — "Plush" (Robert DeLeo):** walk up to the four, then a three-fret chromatic walk to that chord's fifth, plus heavy syncopation.
- **Rush — "The Spirit of Radio" (Geddy Lee):** a chromatic walk leading into the A, plus syncopation leading into each chord change.
- **Stated takeaway:** starting from a plain, generic root-note-only bassline over any track, layering in chromatic walks plus a few fifths/octaves is what makes it "spicier" — the formula travels across genres because it's a set of devices, not a style-specific ornament.
