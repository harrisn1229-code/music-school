# BEST Music Theory Trick | Use The Circle Of Fifths For SOLOS!

## Synopsis

Same core system as [[Circle Of Fifths - Why Guitarists Need It In Their Life]] (one-note-change chain through the circle of fifths) — restated as the learning drill, but this video's real payoff is a distinct practical application: **modulating an existing lick to a new key by editing its accidentals IN PLACE, without moving the lick to a different fretboard position.**

## What's taught

- **Circle of fifths mechanics, restated (see [[Circle Of Fifths - Why Guitarists Need It In Their Life]]).** C major (0 sharps/flats) as the starting point; moving up a perfect fifth per step through the sharp keys, then down through the flat keys back to C; relative minors follow the identical perfect-fifth chain a third below their major partner. Practice drill: play a small C major scale fragment, then step through the circle changing exactly one note per key (F→F♯ for G major, then C→C♯ for D major, etc.) while always starting the fragment on the same note (C) so the pattern shape stays constant and the single-note differences are directly audible.
- **New application: transpose an existing lick to a new key by editing its accidentals, not by relocating it on the neck.** The default guitarist habit when a song modulates is to find a new scale box/arpeggio shape whose root sits in a convenient area and move the whole lick there — often landing in a fretboard area the player didn't actually want to play in. Instead: keep the lick in its EXISTING fretboard position and simply raise/lower the specific note(s) that the new key's accidentals require (worked example: a C major lick moved to G major needs only F changed to F♯, played in the identical physical position) — the lick's shape/position stays put, only the pitch content updates to match the new key.
- **Stated payoff for improvisation specifically:** this in-place modulation approach reads as less predictable than "moving a pattern to fit a new chord/key," since the physical motion doesn't telegraph the key change the way relocating to a new box does. Explicitly flagged as requiring solid fretboard note visualization first (points back to the octave-triangle material for players not yet comfortable with that).
