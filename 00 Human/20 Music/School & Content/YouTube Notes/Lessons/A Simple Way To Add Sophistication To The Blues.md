# A Simple Way To Add Sophistication To The Blues

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vcwey5cUOjM)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vcwey5cUOjM)

## Synopsis

Jack Ruch teaches the **ii–V–I (2-5-1)** as a "connect the dots" device for a 12-bar blues, worked entirely in the **key of A**. Two insertion spots are used, back to back, inside a single 1-4-5 blues (A7-D7-A7-E7-D7-A7): a 2-5-1 **targeting the IV chord** (Em7 → A7 → D7) and a 2-5-1 **coming back to the I chord** (Bm7 → E7 → A7) — the same "insert a 2-5-1 wherever a target chord is about to land" device already documented on [[The ii-V-I]] and [[Blues Form and Harmony]] from several other Jack Ruch videos, but this is the first instance in the vault that works **both spots of the same blues chorus with the target being a dominant I chord (A7), not a major 7**, and drills them as a matched pair of movable arpeggio shapes rather than a single insertion point. Ruch is explicit that these lines are a **soloist's private reharmonization**: the bass never plays the inserted chords, only holds the plain 1-4-5, and the lines still work because "we're kind of superimposing the sound of these chords over what's happening in the music." A companion rhythm-guitar demonstration shows the same two insertions comped as actual chords (not just soloed) over a 13/9-chord blues rhythm part. Sibling video, same channel, same core device (2-5-1 into a blues's I→IV pivot), more lines/more decoration → [[If Your Blues Solos Feel Stale, Try This!]].

## What's taught

- **The core device: a 2-5-1 is just "a cadence, a cycle of chords that resolves to a different place" — and it can be dropped in front of ANY target chord in a progression, not just the tonic.** Plain 2-5-1 in A major would be Bm7 → E7 → Amaj7, but "in the case of a blues it's pretty common to have a dominant chord as the one chord," so the resolution target becomes **A7** instead of Amaj7 — Bm7 → E7 → A7. Moving the same device to resolve to the **IV chord (D7)** instead: the "2" and "5" are built relative to the *new* target, a whole step and a fourth above it respectively — **Em7 → A7 → D7** — named directly as "a 251 of the four chord." → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Blues Form and Harmony]]

- **Rhythm-guitar (comping) demonstration — plain blues voicings first, then the same two insertions played as chords.** Base comping: **A13** for the I chord, **D9** for the IV chord, **E9** for the V chord — "pretty basic, common kind of blues voicings for playing rhythm guitar," comped through a plain 1-4-5 with a few fills first. Second pass through the form, the insertions are added as actual chord changes: heading into the IV chord, sneak in **Em7 → A7 (or A13) → D7**; coming back to the I chord, sneak in **Bm7 → E7 → A7**. Same voicing family (13/9-chord blues rhythm shapes) already documented in depth on [[9th and 13th Chord Voicings for Blues Rhythm]] and [[Rootless 9th Chords and Chromatic Movement in Blues Rhythm Guitar]] (same channel) — this video doesn't add new grips, just shows the 2-5-1 insertion played on rhythm guitar rather than only soloed.

- **Line 1 — the 2-5-1 into the IV chord (Em7 → A7 → D7), a descending-arpeggio-plus-tension shape.** Over **Em7**: descend the arpeggio starting on the **5th (B)** → **♭3rd (G)** → **root (E)** → **♭7th (D)** — "the fifth, minor 3rd, root, and flat 7 of an E minor 7 chord." Shift to **A7** (the "5" of this 2-5-1): go **down to the 3rd**, then **up to the 5th**, then the **♭7th**, straight out of the chord voicing/bar-chord shape underneath the line. Add the tension note: play **B♭, the ♭9 of A7** — described as giving "a little kind of diminished sound... a tense altered color" — stated directly that it "doesn't work all the time" but fits well in a blues context. Resolve down to a **chord tone of D** — specifically the **3rd (F♯)** — with a bit of swing feel to fit the shuffle. **Variation offered directly in the lesson:** instead of a straight half-step drop into the 3rd, **walk up to it chromatically** — an alternate fingering/phrasing of the same target note, adjustable depending on tempo and feel (swung vs. straight, slow blues with room to spare vs. an uptempo tune needing the line compressed into a small window).

- **Line 2 — the same shape moved to the coming-back-to-I 2-5-1 (Bm7 → E7 → A7), relocated to a different string set.** Same underlying line, played on lower strings for the second insertion point: **Bm7** — 5th, ♭3rd, root, ♭7th (B's own version of Line 1's Em7 shape) → **E7** — 3rd, 5th, ♭7th, **♭9** (the same altered-tension device as Line 1, now on E7) → a **chromatic enclosure into the 3rd of A7**, described as "the fourth to the 9th walking up to the third." Explicitly framed as movable: "you could finger that [in] all different ways, all over the fretboard... one of the cool things about the guitar is how we can take the same ideas and move them all over the place."

- **Full solo demonstration — the same two lines woven into freer playing across two full choruses.** Ruch plays two passes through the 1-4-5 (A7-D7-A7-E7-D7-A7), using Lines 1 and 2 at their respective insertion points but mixing in other licks the rest of the time, explicitly modeling how a soloist actually deploys this kind of vocabulary in a real solo rather than playing the two lines in isolation. Framed as "not really outside... nothing we're playing is too far out of the basic realm of the blues," a way to "break out of typical scale patterns and typical types of blues licks" without leaving the style. **Stated general takeaway:** try the device on **any progression**, even a plain two-chord I→IV vamp, not just a full 12-bar blues — "anything you learn here you can move around the fretboard... it's also going to set you up and lead you into different possibilities with the next thing that you play."

## Drilling this into your own playing

- **Isolate each insertion point out of time first**, same practice model as [[Woodshedding Changes with a Looper]] and the identical device on [[If Your Blues Solos Feel Stale, Try This!]]: loop just the I→IV bar with a drone/slow metronome, run the Em7→A7→D7 arpeggio (Line 1) until the landing on D's 3rd is automatic on the downbeat, then do the same for the IV→I bar with Bm7→E7→A7 (Line 2).
- **Drill the ♭9-tension-note move on its own**, decoupled from the specific line: over any dominant 7 chord that's the "5" of an inserted 2-5-1, add its ♭9 as a passing color tone before resolving down to a chord tone of the target — the single most reusable device in this video (works over Line 1's A7 and Line 2's E7 identically, and transfers to any V7 in any key).
- **Practice both phrasing variations of the resolution**: straight half-step drop into the target's 3rd vs. a chromatic walk-up into the same note — pick based on tempo/feel (more room on a slow shuffle, tighter on an uptempo blues).
- **Transpose the whole pair of lines to at least 2–3 other keys** — both lines are built from movable ii-V-I shapes tied to scale degrees, not fixed fret positions. E.g. blues in G: IV-targeting insertion becomes Am7→D7→G7, I-targeting insertion becomes Em7→A7→G(7).
- **Comp the two insertions as rhythm-guitar chords, not just soloed lines** — play the A13/D9/E9 base voicings from [[9th and 13th Chord Voicings for Blues Rhythm]], then sneak in Em7→A7→D7 and Bm7→E7→A7 as actual chord changes at the same two spots, matching this video's rhythm-guitar demonstration.
