# Course 04 — Reading & Position Play

Notation as **rules you can apply cold**, and the reason a written line is harder to play on guitar than on piano. Reading is two skills wearing one name: a reference layer you can learn off a page, and a perceptual skill that only comes from reps.

**What it assumes**

- [[Course 01 — Derivation Rules]] — a chart opens with a key signature, so reading one has to be automatic before any of this is useful.

**What this unlocks**

- The `theory` practice lane, which already points at [[Sight Reading]]'s lesson plans. This course and that lane are the same project from two directions.
- Practically: charts, lead sheets and other people's parts stop being a decoding problem and become something you can play from.

> [!info] The one belief worth correcting early
> Guitar sight reading is usually blamed on **ledger lines**. That's the wrong mechanism, and Lesson 2 replaces it — see [[NOT-C07 — Why guitar reading is hard: position]]. It matters because the real cause has a fix and the imagined one doesn't.

## The lessons

### Lesson 1 — The Reference Layer

**Read**
- [ ] [[Notation]] → *accidental scope rules*, *rhythm notation*, *ledger lines and octave signs*, *navigation signs*. Five rules, one note

**The material**
- Five rules that decide whether a chart plays back correctly. None of them are guessable from the page, and all five are in [[Notation]].
- **[[NOT-C01 — Accidentals last one measure|An accidental lasts for the rest of that measure only]]** — and only on that exact line or space. The bar line cancels it, and it does *not* carry to the same letter in another octave. The octave half is the one that catches people.
- **A [[NOT-C02 — Ties vs slurs|tie]] joins two notes of the *same* pitch** — attack the first, hold through the second. **A slur joins *different* pitches** — play legato, which on guitar means a hammer-on or a pull-off. One curved line, two unrelated instructions.
- **A [[NOT-C03 — Dotted and double-dotted notes|dot adds half the note's own value]]**, so a dotted half is 3 beats. **A second dot adds half of what the first dot added** — a double-dotted half is 2 + 1 + ½ = **3½ beats**. Not 4; the second dot doesn't repeat the first.
- **[[NOT-C04 — Octave signs: 8va and loco|`8va` means play an octave higher than written]]**, cancelled by *loco* or by the end of the dotted bracket. `8vb` below the staff is the octave-lower version. Both exist to keep ledger lines off the page.
- **[[NOT-C05 — Navigation: D.S. al Coda|`D.S. al Coda` is three moves]]**: back to **the sign (𝄋)**, play from there to the *To Coda* mark, **then** jump to the Coda. Going straight to the Coda is the standard misread, and it's why the chart scan in Lesson 3 checks the roadmap first.

**Concepts**
- [[NOT-C01 — Accidentals last one measure]]
- [[NOT-C02 — Ties vs slurs]]
- [[NOT-C03 — Dotted and double-dotted notes]]
- [[NOT-C04 — Octave signs: 8va and loco]]
- [[NOT-C05 — Navigation: D.S. al Coda]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — write out the beat values for a dotted half, a double-dotted half, a dotted quarter and a double-dotted quarter. Then find a bar with an accidental in any chart and say which later notes it does and doesn't affect.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — take a chart with a D.S. al Coda out of [[A Modern Method for Guitar - Enrichment|Modern Method]] and trace the whole route with your finger before playing a note. Play it twice following only the navigation signs, then find one tie and one slur in it and play each.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 04 — Lesson 1 Test]]

### Lesson 2 — The Guitar Problem

**Read**
- [ ] [[Reading Notation on Guitar]] in full, then [[Reading Bass Clef]]

**The material**
- Two facts explain why the same page is harder on guitar than on piano, and only one of them is the one usually named.
- **[[NOT-C06 — Guitar sounds an octave lower|Guitar sounds an octave lower than written]].** It's a transposing instrument: written pitch sits an octave *above* sounding pitch, effectively a permanent 8vb. Direction is the entire content of the fact — the reverse is the common wrong answer — and it's why guitar parts fit in treble clef at all.
- **[[NOT-C07 — Why guitar reading is hard: position|The difficulty is position, not ledger lines]].** The same written pitch exists in five or six different places on the fretboard. Notation tells you *what*, not *where*. On a piano the mapping is one-to-one, so the decision never comes up.
- **The fix is the position system** — a fixed four-fret window, one finger per fret, which makes the mapping unambiguous again. That's why Leavitt holds Volume 1 of [[A Modern Method for Guitar - Enrichment|Modern Method]] in first position for 24 pages, and it's the reason [[Sight Reading]]'s lesson plans are built the way they are.
- Bass clef is the same skill on a different set of lines. Learn it here rather than in the moment you need to read a bass part — [[Reading Bass Clef]] is a short note and it removes a whole category of stall.

**Concepts**
- [[NOT-C06 — Guitar sounds an octave lower]]
- [[NOT-C07 — Why guitar reading is hard: position]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — play written middle C on the guitar, find the same *sounding* pitch on a piano, and say which direction the octave went. Then take one written phrase and locate it in three different fretboard positions.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — take one written phrase from Modern Method, play it in three positions, then commit to first position and read it again at tempo. Say which position made the reading easiest and why it did.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 04 — Lesson 2 Test]]

### Lesson 3 — Reading as a Skill

**Read**
- [ ] [[Sight Reading]] in full — including the 15 session-sized lesson plans

**The material**
- **[[NOT-C08 — The 30-second chart scan|The 30-second scan]]** is what happens between being handed an unseen chart and playing the first note. Five steps, in order:
1. **Key and time signature**
2. **The roadmap** — repeats, 1st/2nd endings, D.S., Coda
3. **Range** — highest and lowest note, so you can pick a position that holds the whole phrase
4. **Rhythm hotspots** — tuplets, syncopation, dotted figures, counted silently first
5. **A tempo you can hold through the hardest bar**, not the easiest
- Step 1 is the one everybody does, and on its own it doesn't prevent the derailment. Steps 2 and 5 are what actually save the read: the roadmap stops you playing into a repeat you didn't see, and the hardest-bar tempo stops you outrunning yourself in bar 9.
- Then the rule that saves a performance: **[[NOT-C09 — Keep the pulse when you fall behind|when you fall behind, keep the pulse]].** Drop notes, thin the chord, play only the downbeats, rejoin at the next downbeat. Never stop and never go back — the continuity *is* the skill being trained. "Slow down until I can play it" turns the exercise into a different one and trains hesitation.

> [!warning] This lesson is reps, not reading
> Reading is a perceptual-motor skill and no note will close it. The lesson test measures the *reference* layer; the skill itself comes from the daily lesson plans in [[Sight Reading]] running through the `theory` practice lane. **Treat the test as the floor, not the goal.**

**Concepts**
- [[NOT-C08 — The 30-second chart scan]]
- [[NOT-C09 — Keep the pulse when you fall behind]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — run the five-step scan out loud, in 30 seconds, on a line you've never seen. Then play it once, no second pass.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — cold-read one new line from [[A Modern Method for Guitar - Enrichment|Modern Method]] in first position, metronome slow enough for the hardest bar, no stopping and no going back. Log it against the monthly cold-read in [[Sight Reading]].
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 04 — Lesson 3 Test]]
