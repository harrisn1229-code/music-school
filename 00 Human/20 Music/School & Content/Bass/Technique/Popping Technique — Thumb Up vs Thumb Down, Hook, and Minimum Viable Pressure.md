# Popping Technique — Thumb Up vs Thumb Down, Hook, and Minimum Viable Pressure

BassBuzz's dedicated breakdown of the **pop** itself — the finger-hook-and-pull half of slap-and-pop that [[Teeter-Totter Slap-Pop Technique]] assumes is already in place before it teaches the single-cycle motion that connects it to the slap. Where that note covers *when* the pop happens in the cycle, this note covers *how the popping finger is actually shaped and pressured* — the base hand shape (thumb-up vs. thumb-down), two named variations, one explicit anti-pattern, and the pressure-control concept ("minimum viable pressure") that keeps a beginner's pop finger from getting shredded.

## Base mechanic: hook, rotate, pull — not a finger-only motion

Get the arm into slap position and hook the popping finger **under the target string**, then **rotate the forearm back** (the same forearm-driven motion slap technique uses, not an isolated finger flick) with some tension held in the finger — the string pulls up and off the fretboard, creating the pop/snap sound. **Very little actual finger movement** — like slap, it's the forearm doing the work, the finger just holds its hooked shape and tension while the arm supplies the pull. It's easiest to learn on the **G string** first, since there are no strings above it to catch on the way in.

## Thumb-up popping (Larry Graham, Victor Wooten, Rhonda Smith, Les Claypool)

Hook the **index finger** under the G string, rotate the forearm back with tension in the finger. The string should trace a **diagonal line across the finger, on the edge closest to the thumb** — that diagonal-on-the-thumb-side-edge contact point is the tell that the hand angle is right for this style. This is the style BassBuzz teaches in the slap module of the paid Beginner to Badass course.

## Thumb-down popping (Tim Commerford, Chris Wolstenholme, + a third artist)

Same core mechanic — hook the index finger under the string, take the forearm back — but because the **thumb-down hand angle is different**, the string ends up **centered on the finger** instead of riding the thumb-side edge the way it does in thumb-up popping. *(The video also names a third thumb-down reference alongside Tim Commerford and Chris Wolstenholme — the auto-captions garble the name as "Brian martini dirt lands," which doesn't resolve to a known slap bassist; left unresolved here rather than guessed, per the transcript-grounded-only rule.)*

## Variation 1 — Flea's middle-finger pop

Flea (Red Hot Chili Peppers), in his own words: *"I will slap down with my thumb, and then with my middle finger it will go underneath the D string and pull up."* Using the **middle finger** instead of the index finger to hook and pop can feel more natural specifically **if the slapping thumb sits at an extreme downward angle** — the middle finger reaches the string more naturally from that hand position than the index finger would. This is the same on-camera Flea quote already documented in [[Slap Thumb Technique — Angle, Anchoring, and Muting]]'s "From videos" section (sourced from [[Flea Teaches His Stupidly Simple Slap Drill (For Insane Speed)]]) — this note adds the specific *reason* to reach for it (extreme thumb-down angle), which that source didn't spell out.

## Variation 2 — Louis Johnson's arm-assisted pop

Louis Johnson ("Thunder Thumbs," one of the grandfathers of slap bass) sometimes added **arm motion to snap the string off the fretboard**, layered on top of the normal forearm rotation rather than replacing it. Effect: **more muscle behind the pop and more of a visual performance** (arms flailing) — but at the cost of some **technique efficiency**. Named as a variation to try, not a default recommendation. This is a distinct mechanic from Louis Johnson's hit-force-as-tone-control lesson already documented in [[Slap Bass Dynamics — Contrast, Force, and Restraint]] §2 (that note covers *how hard* he hits for tone; this is *how* he adds extra arm travel specifically to the pop motion) — same artist, two different technique lessons from two different videos.

## Anti-pattern: don't isolate the finger or flex the wrist upward

Two explicitly **not recommended** ways to generate the pop:
- Popping with an **isolated finger motion** (no forearm rotation behind it).
- **Flexing the wrist upward** to produce the pop.

Both **tire the forearm out**, **create muting issues**, and — critically — **break the [[Teeter-Totter Slap-Pop Technique]] cycle**, since that technique depends on the forearm's down/up rotation doing double duty for both the slap and the pop; taking the pop out of the forearm's rotation removes the mechanical link the teeter-totter relies on.

## Minimum viable pressure (MVP)

A beginner learning to slap **will get blisters** — the fix isn't toughing it out, it's using **just enough tension in the finger to get the pop/snap sound, and no more**:
- **Too little tension** → the finger doesn't resist the string on the way up, so it comes off as a normal pluck with no snap sound at all.
- **The sweet spot** → gradually add tension to the fingertip from that too-light starting point until the snap sound appears — that's the target amount, no further.
- **Too much tension** → extra resistance the string didn't need, which wears the finger out faster than necessary for the same sound.
- **Way too much tension** → the pop doesn't happen at all — the finger just holds onto the string instead of releasing it.

**Action height affects how much pressure MVP actually requires:** if the action (string-to-fretboard clearance) is too high, it takes more finger pressure to get the same pop sound, which wears the pop finger out faster than a properly set-up bass would. Quick-check threshold given in the video: **more than ~5 mm of clearance between the E string and the 12th fret wire → get a setup.** This is a slap-specific framing of the same measurement [[Bass Setup — Truss Rod, Action, Intonation, and Pickup Height]] Step 2 already covers in detail (that note's own medium-action starting point is ~2–3 mm on the E string, measured the same way, for general playability — this video's ~5 mm figure is a looser "you're now actively damaging your pop finger" ceiling, not a target to dial toward).
