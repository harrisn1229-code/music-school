# A Complete Guide for Diminished Chord Mastery: What to Know

## Synopsis
Nathan Borton lays out a complete, self-contained method for soloing directly over the SOUND of a diminished chord itself — deliberately scoped apart from the more common "diminished as a dominant substitute/passing chord" use he already has a separate video on (linked from this one, not yet in the vault). The lesson builds three tools in sequence — the diminished arpeggio, the (whole-half/"tonic") diminished scale, and a family of four triads a minor third apart — and then gives one clean decision rule for switching between them mid-line: land on a chord tone of the diminished chord → use the arpeggio or scale; land on a non-chord tone → grab the triad built on that note instead. Each tool gets the same two-part practice method: small one-octave shapes worked off every chord tone around the neck, and larger horizontal patterns that run the full length of the neck off the low-E or low-A string. Because a fully-diminished chord is symmetric, everything taught here — the arpeggio shapes, the scale shapes, the triad set, and any original lick built from them — automatically works over all four minor-third-related roots (C, E♭, G♭, A for the C-diminished worked example) without any extra work, which Borton states explicitly as the payoff of practicing this way. The most valuable single idea is the arpeggio/scale-vs-triad switching rule itself — it's a portable, chord-tone-driven decision framework for building diminished lines on the fly, not just a shape inventory.

## What's taught
- **Diminished chord construction, stated as pure interval stacking rather than a fixed chord-tone spelling exercise.** A diminished chord is minor 3rds (3 frets each) stacked on top of each other: 2 stacked minor 3rds = a **diminished triad**; 3 stacked minor 3rds = a **diminished 7th chord**. Playing the stack broken (not blocked) is a **diminished arpeggio** — root, ♭3, ♭5, 𝄫7 (double-flat 7th). → [[Diminished Chords]]
- **Diminished-arpeggio fretboard practice, method 1 — one-octave shapes off every chord tone.** Worked over C diminished (C, E♭, G♭/F♯, A): play the one-octave diminished-arpeggio shape starting from each individual chord tone (worked example starts from the ♭3, E♭) everywhere that note occurs on the neck. Explicitly notes the shape's fingering **changes** depending on which string set it's played on (higher string sets vs. lower) even though the notes/degrees stay identical — a fretboard-mapping detail worth knowing going in, not a mistake to debug. → [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]] § *Single-chord-type ladder*
- **Diminished-arpeggio fretboard practice, method 2 — larger horizontal patterns.** A longer pattern that runs the length of the neck rather than staying in one octave-box, moved off of every chord tone the same way; the resulting pattern differs depending on whether it's rooted off the low-E string or the low-A string. Drill direction both ways (ascending and descending) and off every chord tone. → [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]
- **The diminished (whole-half/"tonic") scale, constructed live: whole step, half step, repeat, until back at the starting note.** This is the *whole-half* rotation of the symmetric diminished scale (built to sit over a °7 chord itself), distinct from the half-whole/dominant rotation already documented on [[Scale Modes Reference]] (used over a 7th chord, e.g. [[5 Lines To Get You Playing The Diminished Scale]]). Practiced with the identical two methods as the arpeggio above — small one-octave shapes off each chord tone (worked from the ♭3, E♭, over C diminished) and larger horizontal patterns rooted off the low-E or low-A string. → [[Scale Modes Reference]] § *Whole Tone / Diminished*
- **Linking scale to arpeggio to build original lines — two worked examples over C diminished.** Line 1: ascend the one-octave diminished-scale shape from ♭3 to ♭3 (an octave), walk up a diminished arpeggio starting from the ♭3, then walk back down the diminished scale, resolving on a B natural (a color/passing tone outside the strict chord-tone set, used as a melodic landing rather than a harmonic resolution). A second worked example is played but not verbally broken down in the transcript. The stated method, generalized: alternate short scale runs with short arpeggio runs from the same starting chord tone rather than treating scale and arpeggio as separate, unconnected drills. → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]
- **The triad family over a diminished chord — a movable guitar shape, four triads a minor third apart, built off the notes the chord DOESN'T contain.** Shape: the diminished chord's root plus a triad voiced on top of it (worked example over C diminished: a **D triad** on top of the C root). Slide that same triad shape up in minor 3rds and it produces **four usable triads total** (D-rooted plus three more a minor 3rd apart each), and any of the four can be played as **either a major or minor triad** for extra color options — doubling the available palette to eight triad flavors from one movable shape. Explicitly named as the reason these work: **the triad roots land on exactly the four notes that are NOT chord tones of the diminished chord** — the "gap-filling" notes between the diminished arpeggio's own four notes. Same minor-third-triad-family mechanism already well documented on [[Diminished Chords]] from the dominant-substitution side (the diminished-scale-contains-4-triads material); this video derives the identical triad set from the *plain diminished chord itself* rather than from an underlying dominant. → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]]
- **Connecting triads with scales/arpeggios — two more worked lines over C diminished.** Line 1: D triad → diminished scale → arpeggio, in that order. Line 2: the same shape repeated starting a minor third up (the next triad in the family, F). Line 3: D triad → diminished arpeggio → diminished scale (arpeggio and scale swapped from line 1's order) — demonstrating the three tools can chain in any order, not one fixed sequence. Stated explicitly: this whole three-triad/scale/arpeggio combination shape can be moved around the neck in minor 3rds like everything else in the lesson.
- **The core switching rule — the video's central, portable takeaway: chord tone → arpeggio/scale; non-chord tone → triad.** While running the diminished scale, if you land on a note that IS a chord tone of the diminished chord, either continue the scale or drop into the diminished arpeggio from that point; if you land on a note that is NOT a chord tone, grab whichever of the four triads is built on that specific note instead. This is a real-time decision rule for building original diminished lines on the fly rather than a fixed lick — the practical payoff of having built all three tools (arpeggio, scale, triad family) off the same root set. → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]], [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]]
- **Minor-third transposition payoff, stated explicitly as the reason to practice this way at all.** Because a fully-diminished chord is symmetric, ANY pattern/line built over C diminished automatically works, unchanged, over the chord's three other symmetric roots: **E♭ diminished, G♭ diminished, and A diminished** — practicing once off C diminished effectively covers all four. Restates the "only three unique dim7 chords" / four-symmetric-root property already on [[Diminished Chords]], here applied specifically to freshly-composed original lines rather than to chord substitution or modulation. → [[Diminished Chords]]
- **Explicit scope boundary: this lesson is soloing over the diminished SOUND itself, not diminished-as-dominant-substitute.** Borton flags directly that diminished chords are also usable over a dominant chord (the passing/substitution use this vault already has extensive coverage of via Jens Larsen and Jack Ruch on [[Diminished Chords]]), but that use is out of scope here and pointed to a separate video of his (an on-screen card link, not resolvable from the transcript/captions — not yet in the vault, so not linked).
