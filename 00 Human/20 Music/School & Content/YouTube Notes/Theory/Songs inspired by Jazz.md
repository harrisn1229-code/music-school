# Songs inspired by Jazz

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-0cp4PjUQ2E)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-0cp4PjUQ2E)

## Synopsis

A wide-ranging survey (collaboration with jazz musician/educator Amy Neely) of jazz's influence across six decades of pop — Pink Floyd, the Beatles, Radiohead, Stevie Wonder, hip-hop sampling, and several one-off examples — organized around specific, checkable techniques rather than vague "jazz vibes" claims.

## Techniques

- **The jazz-era "B-section key change via ii-V-I, then back again via another ii-V-I" song-form device, demonstrated as a direct Beatles borrowing.** Duke Ellington/Billy Strayhorn's "Satin Doll": A section in C major, B section modulates to F major via a ii-V-I (Gm7-C7-Fmaj7), then reverses back to C for the return. The Beatles' "From Me to You" uses the EXACT same device, less ornamented: A section plain C-major rock-and-roll, B section modulates to F major via the identical Gm7-C7-Fmaj7 ii-V-I. Explicitly named by McCartney as an inherited jazz-songbook habit (his father's jazz band) blended into otherwise-straight rock and roll — argued as the specific ingredient that gives early Beatles songs "a more sophisticated edge" versus their rock-and-roll peers. General takeaway: a ii-V-I is a modular, reusable unit for briefly visiting and then leaving a new key center, not just a cadence formula.
- **A specific borrowed chord traced to its exact source recording**: Pink Floyd's "Breathe" (Dark Side of the Moon) uses a D7♯9 at the end of its B section to guide back to E minor — Richard Wright (keyboardist) directly credits this chord to Miles Davis's "All Blues" (*Kind of Blue*), worked out by ear at home from the record. A concrete example of "borrow one specific voicing from a specific recording" as a compositional move, distinct from borrowing a whole progression or form.
- **The "old-style verse" as a distinct song-form device, mostly extinct in modern pop but deliberately revived by the Beatles.** In 1930s-40s musical-theater songwriting, a "verse" (confusingly NOT what a modern verse means) was a free-tempo, structurally and harmonically UNRELATED standalone intro section that set up the song's premise/topic before the tempo picked up into the main "refrain" — functionally a spoken-to-sung transition device for stage musicals (worked example: Cole Porter's "I Get a Kick Out of You"). By the 1960s even jazz singers were skipping these intros. The Beatles deliberately kept the device alive: "Here, There and Everywhere" opens with a brief standalone verse before the main refrain; "Honey Pie" (a full music-hall/vaudeville pastiche, not jazz per se, but sharing jazz's forebear tradition) opens with an extended free-time verse establishing the song's narrative before the tempo locks in.
- **"Shout chorus" — a named big-band device worth having on hand as vocabulary**: a high-energy full-horn-section passage, typically placed near the end of an arrangement (worked example: the close of Glenn Miller's "Chattanooga Choo Choo"). Stevie Wonder's "Sir Duke" borrows this device directly for its own horn-driven climactic section, on a song that also explicitly name-checks major jazz artists in its lyrics.
- **A second, independent ii-V-I modulation example, confirming the device generalizes well beyond one song/era**: Stevie Wonder's "You Are the Sunshine of My Life" modulates B major→A♭ major for its B section via a ii-V-I, then back to B major via a second, more extended ii-V-I cadence.
- **A grab-bag of borrowing/sampling mechanisms worth keeping as a reference list, each a slightly different device**: melody lift with same songwriting credit retained (Train's "Play That Song" lifts Hoagy Carmichael's "Heart and Soul" melody, both writers credited); style emulation without direct sampling (Christina Aguilera's "Candyman," emulating the Andrews Sisters' vocal-group style); direct sample as a track's harmonic/rhythmic foundation (A Tribe Called Quest's "Jazz (We've Got)" samples "On Green Dolphin Street"; Kendrick Lamar's "Sing About Me" samples Grant Green's "Maybe Tomorrow"; will.i.am's "Bang Bang" samples James P. Johnson's "Charleston"; Nas's "Can't Forget About You" samples Nat King Cole's "Unforgettable"); attempted-emulation-that-drifted ("Pyramid Song," Radiohead — Thom Yorke describes trying to directly copy Charles Mingus's "Freedom," including an early draft with matching handclaps later cut as sounding "naff" — the final track bears little audible resemblance to the source, illustrating that an artist's STATED influence and the audibly perceptible result can diverge significantly); atmosphere-not-notes influence (Radiohead's *OK Computer* channeling the "dark, uneasy atmosphere" of Miles Davis's *Bitches Brew* rather than any specific musical material, per Yorke's own "aim for something you've fallen in love with and completely miss" framing); riff adaptation with deliberate small alterations (Deep Purple's "Burn" guitar riff adapts the first two bars of Gershwin's "Fascinating Rhythm," dropping the melody's G's down an octave before extending it into a longer riff).
