# 8 Things The Beatles Pioneered

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fy9a_ClKxqM)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fy9a_ClKxqM)

## Synopsis

A "firsts" video rather than a technique breakdown — David Bennett surveys eight things the Beatles did before anyone else, with archival interview clips: the first stadium rock concert (Shea Stadium, 1965), the first live international satellite TV broadcast ("All You Need Is Love," 1967), tape-loop composition brought to mainstream pop ("Tomorrow Never Knows"), intentional guitar feedback as a musical effect ("I Feel Fine"), backmasking ("Rain"), Artificial Double Tracking (invented at Abbey Road for them), sitar/Indian classical fusion in Western pop (George Harrison, "Norwegian Wood"), and the album cover as thematic artistic statement (Sgt. Pepper's).

## Techniques

Most of this video is music-industry history/trivia rather than reproducible technique — genuinely useful for context, but there isn't a setting or transferable move behind most of these "firsts." The one with real, reusable studio-technique content is **ADT (Artificial Double Tracking)**: engineer Ken Townsend split a vocal signal into a dry path and a ~100ms-delayed path (achieved via a tape-speed offset) and recombined them, giving the thickening effect of a real double-tracked vocal without a second performance — built specifically because Lennon was tired of the tedium of manually re-singing double takes. Full mechanism now on [[Artificial Double Tracking (ADT)]].

Everything else stays page-only as historical context: tape-loop composition (Paul McCartney ran ~12 simultaneous tape loop machines live into the mixing desk for "Tomorrow Never Knows," a technique he compares directly to modern DJ/dance-music live looping); intentional guitar feedback (John Lennon's amp feeding back by accident on "I Feel Fine," kept in as the first commercial use of the effect); backmasking (reversed vocal on "Rain," 1966); George Harrison's sitar on "Norwegian Wood" as the first use of the instrument on a Western pop record; and Sgt. Pepper's cover as the first album art conceived as a thematic extension of the music itself.
