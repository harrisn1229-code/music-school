# You Suck at Mixing No. 8: Fixing Muddiness

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ISqEnvAq2ao)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ISqEnvAq2ao)

## Synopsis
A short, focused fix for low-mid "mud" — too much frequency content pooling in roughly the 100–200 Hz zone — demonstrated on two different sources to show two different tools for the same problem. On a bass, a static EQ cut risks changing the character of the sound too much, so multiband dynamics is used instead to compress just the muddy notes when they get loud. On a smaller supporting synth, a simple static EQ notch does the job fine. The choice between the two comes down to how much a static, permanent cut would damage the source's character.

## Techniques
- **Diagnose mud as a build-up of low-mid frequencies, roughly 100–200 Hz** — mud is what happens when too many elements' low-mid content piles up in the same narrow zone; the fix is to comb through the individual elements contributing to it and address each one, not to EQ the master bus.
- **Prefer multiband dynamics over a static EQ cut on a sound whose character matters** — a straight EQ cut through a muddy zone can noticeably change the tone and quality of a sound, which may be too heavy-handed. On a muddy bass, use a 2-band multiband compressor instead: turn off the unused high band, solo the mid band, narrow its range down to the muddy zone (around 100 Hz), then pull the threshold down so the compressor only reacts to the muddy notes when they get loud — the sub band underneath stays mostly untouched — and nudge the output back up afterward. → [[Multiband Compression]], [[Masking and Carving]]
- **A static EQ notch is fine on a less identity-sensitive source** — on a smaller supporting synth, sweep a narrow, soloed EQ band up the spectrum by ear until the muddy frequency spikes (commonly clustering in the 100–200 Hz zone), then pull the gain down on that point with a fairly narrow Q — a −5 dB cut was enough in the worked example. → [[EQ]]
- **Pick the tool by how much a static cut would hurt the source, not by habit** — the same muddy-frequency problem gets two different fixes in this video specifically because one source (the bass) is sensitive to a permanent EQ cut and the other (the small synth) isn't; that judgment call is the actual skill, not memorizing one fixed workflow.
