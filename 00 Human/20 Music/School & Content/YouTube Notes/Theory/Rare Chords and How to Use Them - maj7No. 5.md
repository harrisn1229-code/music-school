# Rare Chords and How to Use Them - maj7#5

## Synopsis

First in Max Konyi's "rare chords" series, covering the **major 7♯5** (augmented major 7th, 1-3-♯5-7 — distinct from the far more common dominant-quality augmented 7th, 1-3-♯5-♭7). Walks three composed fragments showing it used as an applied V chord, as a decorated/delayed resolution of the tonic, and — the most useful generalizable idea — built a major 3rd (3 half steps) above ANY target chord's root to function as an unusual-looking applied dominant (effectively a rootless V7♭13) resolving into that target. Frames everything through "rootless voicing" thinking: the chord you see on the page often isn't functioning as its own root at all, but as the upper structure of a different chord entirely. Confidence: high (clean creator captions, spoken lesson with played examples).

## What's taught

- **Chord structure: major 7♯5 (augmented major 7th) = 1-3-♯5-7** — same shape as a plain major 7th (1-3-5-7) with the 5th raised a half step; NOT the same chord as the more commonly documented dominant-quality augmented 7th (1-3-♯5-♭7, aka Caug7/C7♯5), which keeps a flat 7 instead of a natural 7.
- **Use 1 — applied/decorated V**: build the maj7♯5 on a chord's own root (e.g. I) and let it resolve to that key's IV, functioning the same job a dominant 7th would but with a different color. Stripping the root off reveals the remaining triad sliding up a half step into the target chord's triad as one shape.
- **Use 2 — decorated/delayed tonic**: the same chord type can instead decorate the I chord itself (unresolved tension, or resolve the ♯5 up to a natural 5 and the 7 down to the root at whatever pace the phrase wants) rather than functioning as a dominant.
- **Use 3 — the generalizable applied-dominant trick: go up 3 half steps (a major 3rd) from any target chord's root and build a major 7♯5 there.** The resulting chord functions as an approach/applied-dominant chord into that target (effectively a rootless V7♭13 — the target's own 5th sits inside the shape as its top triad, with the target's ♭13/♭6 in the bass), even though it doesn't look like a dominant chord on paper. Demonstrated resolving into a minor i, a IV (via a Neapolitan-adjacent chord), and a minor i again — same mechanic each time.
- **A bass-note-held variant of the same trick**: instead of the whole shape sliding as a block, keep the bass note fixed while the chord above it functions as an applied dominant built 3 half steps above a DIFFERENT target — the "root" of the chord (conceptually its own 3rd) silently changes identity mid-progression from acting as part of one chord to acting as the 5th of the next target.
- **Core conceptual takeaway: rootless voicings and shifting chord identity** — the same notes can perform entirely different harmonic functions (and even sound different) depending on context; most of these maj7♯5 uses are best understood as rootless voicings of some OTHER chord rather than as a chord built on its own literal root. Rootless voicings generally are flagged as a deeper topic for a future video.
