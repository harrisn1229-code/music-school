# Cadences

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Chord Progressions]]. A **cadence** is the last two chords of a phrase, section, or piece — the punctuation mark of harmony. Four types, each with strict criteria for the "perfect" version.

## The four types

| Cadence | Chords | "Perfect" requires | Feel |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Authentic** | V (or vii°) → I | V→I only (not vii°→I), both root position, soprano ends on tonic by step | full resolution, "home" |
| **Plagal** | IV (or ii) → I | IV→I only (not ii→I), both root position, soprano keeps the common tone, ends on tonic | the "amen" cadence — softer landing than authentic |
| **Half** | anything → V | — (half cadences don't have a "perfect" variant) | stops on the dominant, deliberately unresolved |
| **Deceptive** | V → anything but I (almost always vi) | — | the "psych-out" — sets up a resolution, then dodges it |

- Any of the above that misses its "perfect" criteria is labeled **imperfect** (imperfect authentic, imperfect plagal).
- **Phrygian half cadence** — a specific minor-key half cadence: iv6 → V, with soprano and bass moving stepwise in contrary motion, both landing on scale degree 5.
- A deceptive cadence shows up more often **mid-phrase** than as a true ending — in that context it's sometimes called a "cadence-like structure" rather than a true cadence.
- **The three ways an authentic cadence can fail to be "Perfect" and become Imperfect (IAC) instead**, US voicing-strictness terms: (1) both chords root position, but the top voice of I isn't the tonic note; (2) either chord is inverted; (3) V is substituted for the leading-tone (vii°) chord — a rootless dominant 7th standing in for V.
- **Minor plagal cadence ≠ plagal cadence in a minor key** — two different things that sound similar by name. The **minor plagal cadence** is a chromatic borrowing INSIDE a major-key song: iv→I (not IV→I), introducing a ♭6 that pulls strongly down into the tonic chord's 5th — a bittersweet color, arguably more resolving than the plain major plagal despite the "wrong" chord quality. The actual **plagal cadence in the minor key** is just iv→i, no chromaticism, both chords minor as expected.
- **The minor-key perfect/authentic cadence keeps V major.** Diatonically the v chord in a minor key is minor, but a true minor-key perfect cadence borrows the major V from the harmonic minor scale (v→i with both minor is still a real resolving cadence, just not a formally-named classical one).
- **The Super Mario cadence** — ♭VI→♭VII→I (in C: A♭→B♭→C) — a "slang" cadence with no classical pedigree, functionally resolving the same way the named ones do; worth keeping as a case study in how a cadence name gets coined and spreads (a shared cultural reference point standing in for formal terminology). — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Every type of Cadence EXPLAINED]]

- **A full taxonomy for ending a song WITHOUT resolving to I, ranked by how unresolved it reads.** End on V (most obvious unresolved sound): "You're Still the One" (Shania Twain, dragged-out B♭7), "Mr. Brightside" (ends on A♭). End on IV (subtler/"tasteful" — IV contains the tonic note, so the vocal melody can still land on the tonic even though the chord doesn't): "Your Bird Can Sing" (Beatles) goes further, ending on IV in first inversion. A sus4-hybrid pulls the tonic note directly into an otherwise-unresolved V: "We Are the Champions" ends on C7sus4, not plain C7. Chromatic-mediant ending for cinematic unease: "Live and Let Die" ends on E♭m in the key of G minor. **Alternate chords with genuine tonic FUNCTION, not just "not the tonic"**: vi (relative minor) is a second, darker/weaker tonic-function resolution — "Take It Easy" (Eagles) and "From Me to You" (Beatles) both end there instead of I; iii (mediant) is a more debatable, even weaker third option — "Ask Me Why" (Beatles) ends there. → David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that don't resolve at the end]]
- **A key change that never states its own tonic chord — a compact variant of the tonic-avoidance device already logged above.** "Wuthering Heights" (Kate Bush) shifts to B♭ minor for the pre-chorus but never actually plays a B♭ minor chord, instead cycling through iv (E♭m7), ♭VI (G♭), and V (Fsus4) — establishing the new key purely through the chords surrounding the tonic rather than the tonic itself. → [[Chromatic Mediants and Pop Modulation]] for this same song's chromatic-mediant verse progression — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Kate Bush's unique chord progression]]

- **Tonic-avoidance as a deliberate device — never resolving to I at all, the extreme end of "deceptive cadence."** Key is decided by the melody's own scale/resting note, not by the chords, so a song can sit entirely on non-tonic chords and still clearly read as being "in" that key: "Jane Says" (Jane's Addiction, D major) never plays a D chord, only IV and V, yet the melody rests on D. **Simplest form: a IV–V vamp that loops forever without resolving** — "Sure and Certain" (Jimmy Eat World), "Life Like This" (Kurt Vile). One real resolution anywhere in the song breaks the effect — "Dreams" (Fleetwood Mac) is explicitly disqualified for resolving once, in the bridge, despite vamping vi–VII everywhere else. More developed non-vamp examples: "One More Time" (Daft Punk, two chord loops, neither touching the Bm tonic), "Smile" (Wolfpack, a fully composed progression that keeps rerouting around B♭). Effect: reads as "constantly traveling without arriving" — useful anywhere a song wants sustained tension without the release a cadence gives. → [[Chord Progressions]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that never go to the Tonic chord]]
- **The "Aeolian cadence" — a real term with an odd, non-standard origin, useful mainly as a piece of Beatles trivia rather than actual theory vocabulary.** A deceptive/interrupted cadence (V→vi, here D7→E minor in G major) on the Beatles' 1963 single "Not a Second Time" was described this way by Times classical-music critic William Mann in his 1963 review — his reasoning: resolving to the vi chord instead of the tonic gives that passage the color of Aeolian (the mode built on the 6th degree) rather than the song's home major key. **It never became standardized theory terminology** — it's just "a deceptive/interrupted cadence" everywhere else — and John Lennon's own reaction to being reviewed this way was bemusement ("to this day I have no idea what Aeolian cadences are, they sound like exotic birds"). Worth knowing the term exists and where it came from, but reach for "deceptive cadence" (US) / "interrupted cadence" (UK) in normal use. → [[Diatonic Modes]] (Aeolian) — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use the Aeolian mode]]
- **UK/US cadence-naming cross-reference — this page mixes both dialects without flagging it, so worth having as a lookup**: UK "perfect cadence" = US "authentic cadence" (V→I; the "perfect" qualifier in both dialects additionally requires root-position chords with the tonic on top when resolved). UK "imperfect cadence" = US "half cadence" (ends on V, unresolved). UK "interrupted cadence" = US "deceptive cadence" (V→vi). → [[US vs UK music theory EXPLAINED]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [US vs. UK music theory EXPLAINED](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ltO5r5ZZNxo)
- **A dedicated full-song "Bohemian Rhapsody" analysis, independently confirming and extending the minor-plagal-cadence entry below with two more devices from the same song.** Deliberately withholding an expected resolution: after a line cliché sets up a "should be" return to E♭ major via F major→B♭ (a clean perfect cadence), Freddie substitutes F minor instead — a less predictable, more jarring route back to the same destination chord. A circle-of-fifths modulation chain smooths a distant key change into the coda: E♭ major → briefly through the intermediate related key B♭ major → lands on F major, the intermediate step being what makes the E♭-to-F shift read as natural rather than abrupt. Also documents a named recurring tension device ("the Bohemian motif" — trapping an expected resolution note between two chromatic neighbor chords, delaying arrival) that isn't a cadence itself but is the thing these cadences are working against/toward throughout the song. → David Bennett Music Theory, [[A Brief Analysis of Bohemian Rhapsody]]
- **A worked tonic/subdominant/dominant functional-harmony walkthrough on a real song, plus why the minor key normally can't produce this without harmonic minor's raised 7th.** "bury a friend" (Billy Eilish, G harmonic minor): i (Gm) = tonic function, fully at rest; iv (Cm) = subdominant function, some tension, wants to resolve onto the dominant; V (D major — only possible because harmonic minor raises the diatonic v to a major V) = dominant function, maximum tension, wants to resolve back to tonic. The iv→V→i motion is a textbook perfect cadence that plain natural minor (with its weaker minor v) can't produce. Full scale-level rationale → [[Scale Modes Reference]]. → [[Scale Modes Reference]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[What is the Harmonic Minor scale?]]
- **Why all-minor-chord songs are rare — because the three common cadences (perfect V-I, deceptive V-vi, plagal IV-I) all require a MAJOR chord somewhere in the resolution.** An all-minor harmony literally can't use functional cadences, which is why real all-minor-chord songs collapse to one or two static chords instead of a full progression: "505" (Arctic Monkeys), "Get the Party Started" (Pink, one chord the whole song), "Run Through the Jungle" (CCR), "Political World" (Dylan). Genres that embrace this stasis ON PURPOSE, moving the musical interest elsewhere: funk (groove/rhythm carries it), rap (sample loops keep focus on lyrics/flow), modal jazz (vamps on 1-2 chords as key centers rather than functional chords — "So What," Miles Davis; "Impressions," Coltrane, a purer all-minor example) → [[Modal Jazz]]. Even a brief major chord matters: "The Imperial March" (John Williams) is near-all-minor except one E♭ major resolution, cited as giving a moment of "opening up" amid the otherwise unnerving minor harmony. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Why are Minor Chord Songs so rare?]]
- **The minor plagal cadence — iv→I in a major key (a borrowed minor-iv, see [[Modal Interchange]]) — and why it's so much more emotionally loaded than the plain major plagal (IV→I).** Examples: "Bohemian Rhapsody" (Queen) — A♭m→E♭, key of E♭, the closing "nothing really matters" moment. "Nowhere Man" (The Beatles), key of E. **The common extended form — IV → iv → I** (start on the regular major IV, darken it to minor iv, then resolve) is by far the most frequent real-world pattern: "Wake Me Up When September Ends" (Green Day), "Don't Look Back in Anger" (Oasis), "Show Me Love," "Who Do You Think You Are." **Alternate resolution — iv→vi instead of iv→I**: darker, minor-to-minor. "Between the Bars" (Elliott Smith), "Where Is My Mind" (Pixies). **Why the resolution feels so satisfying — a voice-leading explanation**: compare Csus4→C (the 4th/F resolving down a step to the 3rd/E, a classic suspension release) with Fm→C — the same F→E motion is present, but Fm ALSO moves A♭→G in parallel (the minor 3rd resolving down to the major 3rd), doubling the semitone-down resolution. Two voices moving in parallel semitone resolution, both landing on tonic chord tones, is what gives the cadence its "lush, longing" quality — a "supercharged" version of a plain sus4 release. → [[Modal Interchange]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use the Minor 4 chord]]
- **The cadential 6/4 — stretching out a perfect cadence by inserting the tonic in second inversion before the dominant.** I⁶⁄₄ → V(7) → I (root position), used as "musical punctuation" at phrase/section endings: "Manic Monday" (The Bangles), "We Are Young" (fun.), "The Luckiest" (Ben Folds), "A Little Bit of Everything" (Dodie), "New" (Paul McCartney, escalated at the very end of the song). Timing is flexible — "You're the Inspiration" (Chicago) plays it fast, one beat per chord, reading as a quick turnaround rather than a dragged-out cadence. **Extension 1 — splice in a sus4** between the I⁶⁄₄ and V, breaking the cadence's two simultaneously-moving voices into two separate resolving steps for extra drag — the Rainbow Road theme (*Mario Kart*) uses this for extra climactic grandeur. **Extension 2 — approach via a full voice-led descending bass walk** rather than landing on the 6/4 chord directly (each preceding chord independently inverted so the bass steps down scale-degree by scale-degree) — "Edelweiss" (*The Sound of Music*); connects to the descending-stepwise-bass entry on [[Chord Progressions]]. **Home turf**: Baroque/Classical — a Bach chorale, several instances in Schubert's "Ave Maria," including one **interrupted cadential 6/4** where the expected I⁶⁄₄→V7 resolves not to I but to vi instead (a deceptive/interrupted cadence made to land harder by the drawn-out 6/4 buildup preceding it). "Skyline Pigeon" (Elton John) reads as Baroque-influenced partly because of this device. → [[Chord Progressions]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use the Cadential 6 4 chord progression]]

- **A concrete plagal-cadence (IV→I) worked example with exact voicings and voice leading: John Mayer's "Slow Dancing in a Burning Room" A→E move in the key of E.** 5th-position triad voicing on strings D-G-B (4-3-2): A root position (frets 7-6-5 = 1-3-5) → E 2nd inversion (frets 6-4-5 = 3-5-1) — one voice stays the same (the shared 5th/root, E), one moves a half step, one moves a whole step, the textbook smallest-motion voice-leading pattern for a triad-to-triad cadence. The same A→E pairing is also played with **E held as a constant pedal bass note under both chords** (E is the 5th of A and the root of E) — called out in the lesson as "a neighbor IV chord," a bass-pedal variant of the same plagal cadence. Layered on top: a portable **2→3 hammer-on ornament** (hammer onto the chord's 3rd from the major 2nd a whole step below it) that turns the plain cadence into the song's recognizable lick — full breakdown on [[Chord Inversions]]. → [[Chord Inversions]], [[🎵 JOHN MAYER - Slow Dancing in a Burning Room]] — Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared, [Slow Dancing in a Burning Room Chords - Unique John Mayer Guitar Lesson](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bBVcjMUKBpY)
