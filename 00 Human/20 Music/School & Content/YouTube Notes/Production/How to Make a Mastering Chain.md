## Techniques

- **Conceptualizing the chain — routing philosophy.** Each processor affects the signal in the order it's inserted; all buses occur after the last channel-strip processor. Start processing on the channel, add buses if needed, finish on the master output — decide the order deliberately. *(Restates [[How to Setup Your Mastering Chain]]'s "file/waveform → inserts → aux sends/buses → stereo output" routing lesson already on [[Master Chain]]; not re-routed.)*
- **Picking the first insert — subtractive EQ vs. a mastering preamp.** The first insert sets the direction for the whole rest of the chain, so treat the choice as consequential, not a formality. Two candidates named: a **subtractive EQ** (attenuate parts of the spectrum you don't want amplified downstream — the stated personal preference, since it "sets me up well for having a successful chain later on") or a **mastering preamp**. Keep the move subtle — it's mastering, and it's the first insert.
- **Is de-essing needed?** A mixing engineer won't always account for how much more abrasive sibilance reads once the overall level of a mix is raised at mastering — that's the reason de-essing sometimes belongs in a mastering chain even though the mix was already de-essed. Placement: right after the subtractive EQ. Prefer a de-esser over a static EQ cut at the sibilant frequency — a static cut attenuates that frequency *at all times*, not just during the sibilant, where a de-esser only triggers dynamically when it's actually present.
- **Compression, saturation, or both.** Both control dynamics from the peaks down. **Compression** is more flexible — compress from average loudness, shape the behavior with ratio/attack/release, etc. **Saturation** is more program-dependent and reads more natural, while adding harmonics that fill the sound out. Stated personal habit: skip compression on a full mix and reach for saturation instead — but using both is valid: compression first for accurate, specific dynamics control, then saturation for the harmonics and analog-feeling character.
- **Picking a saturation type — transformer vs. tube.** The saturation flavor chosen meaningfully shapes a master's timbre. **Transformer** saturation compresses moderately and can attenuate higher frequencies, reading mellow. **Tube** saturation compresses slightly, extends transients, and emphasizes both the low end and the high-frequency range at once. Stated preference: tube most often, specifically **warm tube** for its **2nd-order harmonic**. Decide by ear per song, not by default.
- **Using reverb when mastering — generally don't.** Reverb on a master typically disturbs the spatial relationships already established at mixing, reading as washed-out or less dimensional. Use your ears and decide per session — not a hard rule. *(This chain already carries a much deeper version of this exact lesson — see [[Reverb on a Master: Everything You Need to Know]] — not re-routed.)*
- **Increasing low-level detail with the Oxford Inflator.** Rather than an upward compressor (measure/compress/amplify the quieter parts), this uses the **Oxford Inflator**, which prioritizes signal content with a positive value over content with a negative value — raising the ratio of signal-to-silence (or high-level-to-low-level signal). **Stated best-use recipe, credited to the plugin's own developers: set Effect to 100, then use Input to dial how aggressive the processing reads** — a distinct operating method from a low-Effect/adjust-to-taste approach.
- **The importance of additive EQ.** After the corrective/de-essing/dynamics stages, add a second EQ to amplify what you want *more* of, rather than only cutting. Concrete moves: boost **3–5 kHz** (where ears are most sensitive — reads as an overall louder mix); boost a super-high range for air; and, via mid-side EQ, narrow or widen the stereo image by amplifying the mid or side independently. Stated personal recipe: amplify the **side image's highs and mid frequencies**, amplify **3–5 kHz on the mid**, and maybe **the kick's fundamental**.
- **Picking the right limiter.** Favor limiters with multiple algorithms so you can cycle through and find the best fit per song. Stated recent go-to: the **Oxford Limiter**, using its **Enhance** feature pushed to a higher level, ~3–5 dB of attenuation. Stated favorite free option: a plugin whose name is unclear from the captions (heard as "CS DSP Factory Limiter 1") — described as having a really aggressive and detailed sound. *(Low confidence on the free-limiter name — flagged rather than guessed; see [[Plugin Wishlist]].)*
- **Automating a mastering chain.** Once the static chain is in place, automate stages in/out to track the song's emotional arc rather than leaving every setting flat for the whole record. Concrete move: **increase saturation/upward processing (and the limiter's Enhance amount) during choruses, or just the last chorus**, to make the ending read as more engaging and differentiated from earlier choruses.
- **Metering and monitoring.** Use a LUFS meter for perceived loudness. Context-specific target for this demo: **−14 LUFS**, chosen specifically because YouTube normalizes the whole video's level (not just the isolated demo passages) — a platform-specific consideration, not a general mastering target. On peaking: not a major concern except at the literal master output, since 32-/64-bit internal processing lets signal exceed 0 dB inside the chain without clipping, as long as it's brought back under before the master output. *(The internal-headroom point restates [[How to Gain Stage Your Master]]'s "most plugins run 64-bit internally" lesson already on [[Master Chain]]; not re-routed.)*
