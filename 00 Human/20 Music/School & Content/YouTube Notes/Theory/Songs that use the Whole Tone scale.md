# Songs that use the Whole Tone scale

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zqDKBKIguuo)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zqDKBKIguuo)

## Synopsis

A construction-and-application survey of the whole tone scale — the "dream sequence"/"flashback" scale — covering why it sounds rootless, why there are only two of them, why it harmonizes badly, and where real songs (Debussy/Ravel-adjacent Impressionists, Stevie Wonder, King Crimson, Pink Floyd, Burt Bacharach) actually use it.

## Techniques

- **Construction**: an octave divided into six equal whole-tone steps (T-T-T-T-T-T). Because it's a symmetrical scale, there are only **two unique whole tone scales** — starting on any note of one collection just restates the same six notes (a mode of itself), same logic as the chromatic scale having only one unique set.
- **Why it sounds rootless**: every note in the scale carries equal weight (no half-steps to create a leading-tone pull toward a tonic), so the ear can't lock onto a "home" note — this symmetry is exactly what produces the floating/dreamlike quality.
- **Harmonizing it is a trap**: stacking thirds on a whole tone scale only produces **augmented triads and major-♭5 triads** — i.e. every chord built from it is already an altered chord. Because these chords are tonally unsatisfying on their own, whole-tone material is used far more as a **melodic color for a brief moment** than as a harmonic foundation for a whole passage — "a potent flavor to sprinkle in, not to dominate the palate."
- **Over an altered dominant chord**: the classic real-world use. An altered 7th chord (e.g. F7♯5, labeled "Falt") shares its raw note content with a whole tone scale built from the same root, so soloing/melody-writing with that whole tone scale over the altered chord is a direct, low-effort match — demonstrated on Burt Bacharach's "Alfie" (D altered chord → whole tone climb from E to D) and Stevie Wonder's "You Are the Sunshine of My Life" (B major9 → F♯7♯5 altered chord → whole tone climb, **harmonized in parallel thirds**, i.e. two whole tone scales moving together).
- **Historical framing**: associated with early-20th-century Impressionist composers (Debussy's *Voiles* is built almost entirely from it) as a deliberate break from 300 years of tonal-scale convention — Mozart, by contrast, used the same scale a century earlier in "A Musical Joke" specifically to sound like *wrong notes*, i.e. the scale's "correct" modern use started as a satirical device for "incorrect" playing.
- **Guitar examples**: Robert Fripp (King Crimson) built "Fracture"'s opening eighth-note run from a D♭ whole tone scale in 5/8; David Gilmour drops a brief whole-tone fragment into his "Dogs" (Pink Floyd) solo as a single ear-catching moment rather than a sustained passage.
