# Root-Fifth Bass Lines and the 3-Step Style Rhythm Formula

**Root and fifth** — no octave, no chromaticism — are, in BassBuzz's own framing, "the two most awesome notes on bass," and this note is the vault's dedicated home for *why that's true* (frequency-ratio physics, not just convention) plus a reusable **three-step rhythm generator** for making the same two notes sound like country, bossa nova, soul, or rock just by changing what rhythm they're played in. This is the prerequisite half of a two-video BassBuzz pair — [[Jamerson's Root-Fifth-Octave Exercise]] is the note for the fuller root-**fifth-octave** formula (plus chromatic approach walks and syncopation layered on top), and that video literally points back to this one ("check out my root-fifth video in the description"). Fill gap: the vault had root-fifth-*octave* (via Jamerson) and pentatonic riff-writing, but nothing distilling the plain two-note root-fifth interval on its own — its acoustic justification, or a style-adaptation method independent of any specific exercise.

## Why root-fifth works: frequency ratios and consonance

- **Pitch is vibration speed (frequency).** Two simultaneous or sequential notes read as stable ("ahh") or unstable ("AAH") depending on the **ratio between their frequencies**, not on which specific notes they are.
- **Simple ratios = stable, complex ratios = unstable.** 1:1 (unison) and 2:1 (octave) are the simplest possible ratios and sound maximally consonant; as the ratio gets more complex, the interval sounds progressively more dissonant.
- **The perfect fifth is a 3:2 ratio** — one note vibrating exactly 50% faster than the other — which sits at "the perfect sweet spot on the spectrum: enough instability to make interesting basslines, but not so much it distracts from the song." That's the acoustic argument for reaching for the fifth specifically, rather than any other scale degree, as the beginner-safe way to add movement to a root-note-only line.
- **Applies to melodic (one-note-at-a-time) lines, not just simultaneous dyads** — the same ratio logic explains why a bassline built from root-fifth *pairs* reads as stable even played sequentially, exactly how bassists mostly use the interval (as opposed to a guitarist's simultaneous power chord).
- **Same physics as [[Intervals]]'s overtone-series consonance section**, reframed: that note documents octave = 2× the fundamental and perfect 5th = 3× the fundamental (octave-reduced) as consecutive steps down the *same single note's* overtone series; this note's 3:2 framing is the ratio *between two different notes played against each other* — same underlying physics, two complementary lenses (one note's own harmonic content vs. two notes' frequency relationship). Cross-link both whenever consonance/dissonance theory comes up.
- **Worked stable-vs-unstable pairs, confirming the ratio claim on real songs:** stable/simple-ratio — the root-fifth pattern opening "My Girl" (The Temptations) and the root-fifth interval opening "Ninety-Nine and a Half (Won't Do)" (Wilson Pickett); unstable/complex-ratio — the **tritone** opening "YYZ" (Rush) and the **minor second** opening the *Jaws* theme.
- → links [[BASS Slot]] directly: this 3:2 ratio is the acoustic justification for root-fifth as the low end's default harmonic scaffold, the same role [[Jamerson's Root-Fifth-Octave Exercise]] assigns root-fifth-**octave**.

## Finding it on the neck

- **The fifth is the fifth scale degree of the major *or* minor scale built on the root** — walking up either the major scale (C D E F G) or the minor scale (C D E♭ F G) from a given root lands on the same perfect fifth either way, since the natural 5 is diatonic to both qualities. That shared-tone property is *why* root-fifth works without knowing whether the underlying chord is major or minor — see the Strokes example below.
- **Two equally valid shapes, same note name:** up one string + up two frets from the root (the "high" shape), **or** down one string on the **same fret** (the "low" shape) — an octave apart, same note. Mix high and low shapes chord-to-chord for variety rather than defaulting to only one. *(general starting point: try the high shape first — it's the more commonly taught default — and reach for the low shape when the high one would force an awkward jump.)*

## Proof-by-subtraction — why the fifth specifically, not just any nearby note

Worked on the intro of "Sittin' on the Dock of the Bay" (Otis Redding): playing the classic root-fifth intro, then substituting other scale degrees into the same rhythmic slot — the **sixth**, the **third**, and the **fourth** — each one stands out too much and pulls attention away from the song, audibly worse than the fifth in the identical spot. Restoring the fifth makes the bass sit back into the track immediately. This is a direct, ear-testable demonstration of the frequency-ratio argument above: nearby scale degrees that aren't the fifth (or octave) carry more complex ratios to the root and read as more dissonant in exactly the way the theory predicts.

## Momentum from a root-fifth pair, not just harmonic color — "Under Pressure" (Queen/David Bowie)

- The line starts on the **root** (D, 12th fret of the D string) and uses the **low shape** — the fifth **below** the root (drop down a string, same fret) rather than the high shape above it.
- **Momentum test:** the line with the root held the whole time (fifth removed) is "super boring" by direct comparison — the single fifth at the end of the phrase is what creates the pull back toward the root on the next repeat. That pull is *momentum*, a distinct payoff from the "flavor/energy" framing of the primer — the fifth's job here is specifically to generate forward motion into the next downbeat without the complex-ratio dissonance that would compete with the vocal melody sitting on top.
- Confirms the low shape (down-a-string-same-fret) is just as idiomatic as the high shape, not a fallback — chosen here because it's the shape available at that exact fret/string, not because the high shape didn't work.

## Versatility across major and minor — "On the Other Side" (The Strokes)

- The line starts **on the fifth** (not the root) and runs **fifth-root, fifth-root** throughout, over a **G minor → E♭ major** chord change — the **same fretboard shape** (high shape: up a string, up two frets from the root) plays the fifth-root pattern on *both* chords despite one being minor and the other major.
- **Stated beginner payoff:** because the perfect fifth is diatonic to both the major and minor scale of a given root (see "Finding it on the neck" above), root-fifth is a genuinely safe default even for a player who can find roots by ear but can't yet tell major from minor by ear — the two-note vocabulary sidesteps that specific ear-training gap rather than requiring it be closed first.

## The 3-step rhythm generator — adapting root-fifth to any style

The video's headline method, stated directly as usable by a total beginner because the only listening skill it requires is *hearing a rhythm*, not identifying pitches or harmony:

1. **Pick an iconic bassline from the target style.**
2. **Steal the rhythm** — the rhythmic pattern only, not the notes. ("Basslines don't mind" being borrowed this way — cf. [[Bassline Writing]]'s own "steal the rhythm, change the notes" riff-ideation method, method 2 of 7 there, same underlying move applied here specifically to root-fifth vocabulary rather than any note set.)
3. **Plug root and fifth onto that stolen rhythm.**

**Applied across five genres against the same shared sample chord chart (a G–D–E–C progression), so the formula's output is directly comparable style to style:**

| Style | Source bassline | Rhythm stolen | Root-fifth pattern (already present in the source) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Country (also polka, bluegrass, jazz two-beat) | "Walk the Line" (Johnny Cash) | Quarter notes (or half notes, depending on how it's counted) | **Root, fifth, root, fifth** per chord — described as "very typical of this style" |
| Bossa nova — jazz style | "Song for My Father" (Horace Silver) | Dotted quarter, eighth, dotted quarter, eighth (1, 2-and, 3, 4-and) | **Root, fifth-fifth, root-root, fifth-fifth** per chord |
| Bossa nova — traditional Brazilian style | "The Girl from Ipanema" (Getz/Gilberto recording) | Half notes | Mostly **root-fifth**, with occasional **root-root doubling** in place of the fifth and varied octave placement between repeats — worked over D♭ → E♭ → A♭ → D♭ |
| Soul | "Sittin' on the Dock of the Bay" (Otis Redding) | Dotted quarter, eighth, quarter, quarter (1, 2-and, 3, 4) | Bassist **Duck Dunn's** actual line: **root, root, fifth, fifth** with a slide — offered alongside three equally valid alternate orderings over the same rhythm: root-root-root-fifth, root-fifth-fifth-fifth, root-fifth-root-fifth |
| Rock | A clip from "Bonzo Goes to Bitburg" (Ramones) | The Ramones clip's rhythm | **No pre-built pattern to borrow** — unlike the other four genres, rock has no single formula given the genre's internal variety; mostly root notes on the stolen rhythm with fifths dropped in "wherever you want" |

**Practical takeaway on the country/bossa/soul entries:** when the source bassline already alternates root and fifth in a recognizable pattern, step 3 is close to free — copy the pattern directly onto the sample chart's chord changes. The **rock** entry is the exception that proves the rule: without a borrowed pattern, step 3 becomes a genuinely creative choice (root-heavy, fifths as color), which is itself useful information — some styles hand you the vocabulary, others just hand you the rhythm and expect you to invent the note placement.

**Genre-note cross-reference:** the traditional-Brazilian-bossa entry above (half notes, mostly root-fifth mirroring the kick) matches [[Bossa Nova (João Gilberto)]]'s existing § Bass description almost exactly — that note already documents "half notes mirroring the kick (1 and 3, occasional pickup 8th)... never busy." This video adds the **jazz-bossa vs. Brazilian-bossa split** that note didn't previously distinguish (Horace Silver's syncopated dotted-quarter/eighth pattern vs. Getz/Gilberto's plainer half-note pattern with root-doubling) — see that note's § Bass for the fuller genre write-up.
