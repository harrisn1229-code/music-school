# All Processing is Connected - Crucial Knowledge for Pro Mixes & Masters

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XV-0rdUrgHs)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XV-0rdUrgHs)

## Synopsis
A conceptual, no-fluff breakdown from Sage Audio (a professional mastering studio) arguing that every mixing/mastering processor — EQ, reverb, compression, saturation, clipping, limiting — is secretly an EQ, because anything that changes a signal's amplitude unevenly across frequency or across time reshapes its frequency response whether that was the intent or not. The video builds the case in four linked moves: (1) reverb's decay taps concentrate extra amplitude in whatever frequency range decays longest, quietly EQing a signal that was only "given space"; (2) the ADSR framework (attack/decay = transient, sustain/release = tonal) shows that transient and tonal content usually carry different frequency content, so any processor — like Eventide's Split EQ, or an ordinary compressor — that treats the two differently is indirectly re-EQing the signal by shifting their relative balance; (3) waveshaping — the literal reshaping of a waveform's oscillation by compressors, saturators, clippers, and limiters — generates genuinely new, higher-frequency harmonic content, most audible on low frequencies with long oscillation periods, with a compressor's attack time as the single biggest waveshaping variable (lookahead + oversampling largely solve it for compressors, but not for saturators/clippers by design, and only some limiters); and (4) the relationship runs in reverse too — EQing a frequency range after another processor has already added content there (a reverb tail, clipper harmonics) doesn't just change tone, it turns that processor's own effect up or down, changing its perceived character without touching its actual parameters. The practical payoffs threaded through: turn temporal effects (reverb, delay, modulation) down before sending a mix to mastering, since limiting/compression there will lift quiet details — including those tails — more than expected; and a mix-bus clipper triggered mainly by kick/bass will generate harmonics that land in and inflate the low-mids, worth knowing before reaching for a corrective EQ on "muddy" low-mids a clipper itself may be creating. No hard numeric settings beyond one illustrative frequency pair (50 Hz → 150 Hz); this is a mental-model video, not a walkthrough of a specific chain.

## Techniques
- **The core thesis — any processor that changes amplitude unevenly across frequency or time is functioning as an implicit EQ, whether or not that was the intent.** Reverb, ADSR-shaping tools, compression, saturation, clipping, and limiting are all shown to reshape frequency response as a side effect of what they're "supposed" to do — the throughline for every other bullet below. → [[EQ]]
- **Reverb's decay taps concentrate amplitude in whichever frequency range decays longest — a "just add space" move quietly EQs the signal.** A reverb with a longer decay rate in one frequency range (their example: the high mids, roughly **2–5 kHz**) needs more decay taps in that range to represent the longer tail; more taps means more amplification concentrated there, and if the reverb keeps re-triggering off the dry signal, those extra taps overlap and add to one another, amplifying the range further still. Net effect: a signal only intended to get "space" comes back with more energy in whatever band its reverb decays longest in. → [[LEAD Slot]] (2.5–6 kHz), [[Reverb]]
- **ADSR framing: transient (attack/decay) and tonal (sustain/release) content usually carry different frequency content — so any processor that treats them differently is indirectly EQing.** Example: a snare's initial snap (attack/decay) is a different frequency makeup than its ring/resonance/fade (sustain/release, lower in amplitude but longer in duration). Any processor that changes the relative amplitude of transient vs. tonal content — including an ordinary compressor's attack/release settings — shifts the balance between two frequency-distinct parts of the signal, which is, mechanically, an EQ move even when no EQ is involved. → [[TRANSIENT Slot]], [[Compression]]
- **Eventide Split EQ makes ADSR-based EQ deliberate — solo/adjust a sound's transient (attack/decay) content separately from its tonal (sustain/release) content, on the same signal.** Breaks the frequency response into two time zones instead of one, so you can directly boost transients that live in the high mids or tonal content that lives in the lows without conventional band-based EQ. Not owned — no note in `Production/Plugins/`; carries a real, specific technique (ADSR-domain frequency splitting, distinct from anything currently owned), so it's going on the wishlist rather than being skipped. → [[EQ]]
- **A compressor's attack/release settings are, mechanically, an ADSR-domain EQ move — not just a dynamics move.** Worked example: fast attack + fast release + some lookahead + moderate knee on an acoustic guitar clamps the transient snap; since the sustain/release content is lower in amplitude and gets relatively lifted by that same compression, the tonal (sustain/release) frequencies rise relative to the transient (attack/decay) frequencies — an EQ shift nobody dialed in on purpose. → [[Compression Staging]], [[Compression]]
- **Post-compression/limiting amplification hits quiet temporal-effect tails (reverb, modulation) harder than the dry signal — turn them down before mastering.** Reverb reflections and modulation effects (phasing, etc.) sit lower in amplitude than the dry signal almost always (unless the effect is gated), so any compression/limiting that lifts quiet content lifts those tails disproportionately along with everything else — which is why Sage Audio tells clients to pull temporal-effect levels down before sending a mix for mastering; mastering-stage limiting will make reverb/delay/modulation more audible than the mix engineer heard it. → [[Mix Handoff]], [[Reverb]], [[Modulation]]
- **Waveshaping: compressors/saturators/clippers/limiters don't just attenuate a waveform, they generate genuinely new, higher-frequency harmonic content — worst on low frequencies with long oscillations.** Reshaping a waveform (e.g. via gain reduction) isn't pure subtraction; the altered portion of the wave — occupying less time than the original oscillation — reads as new, higher-frequency content layered on top. Worked example: attenuating a 50 Hz wave in a way that reshapes it can generate a 150 Hz component — one compression move produces two simultaneous EQ effects (50 Hz down, 150 Hz up). On complex program material the same mechanism runs across hundreds/thousands of frequencies at once. → [[SUB Slot]], [[BASS Slot]], [[Compression]]
- **A compressor's attack time is the single biggest waveshaping variable — demonstrated directly on a sine wave's oscillation in real time.** Other parameters (ratio, release, knee) contribute, but attack time visibly reshapes the waveform's dips/curvature the most, and the effect is easiest to see when compressing low frequencies hard (their long oscillation period makes the reshaping visually and audibly obvious). → [[Compression Staging]]
- **Lookahead + oversampling largely solve compressor waveshaping — but not saturator/clipper/most-limiter waveshaping, because it's the point of those processors.** Lookahead lets the oscillation pass through before gain reduction is applied, so the compressor retroactively attenuates without cutting into the wave's shape; oversampling helps further. Saturators are designed to gradually saturate and clippers are designed to square the peak — waveshaping is the effect, not a side effect to eliminate — and only some limiters carry enough lookahead/oversampling to avoid it. Practical read: compress with lookahead/oversampling on when the goal is clean gain reduction; expect (and use) waveshaping-driven harmonics from any saturator, clipper, or under-specced limiter. → [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[Limiting]], [[Clipping and Loudness]]
- **A mix-bus clipper triggered mainly by kick/bass generates harmonics that land in and amplify the low-mids — a hidden source of "muddy low-mids," not a fix for it.** High-amplitude kick and bass content crosses a mix-bus clipper's threshold first and most often; the resulting harmonics form in the frequency range directly above the source, so a bass/kick-driven clipper is, as a byproduct, a low-mid booster. Worth checking before reaching for a corrective EQ cut on low-mid mud that the clipper itself may be generating. → [[BASS Slot]], [[BED Slot]], [[Clipping and Loudness]]
- **Asymmetrical saturation generates a 2nd-order harmonic that amplifies the low-mids on bass-register material.** A 2nd-order harmonic sits an octave above the fundamental, so on a bass-heavy source, asymmetrical saturation's 2nd harmonic lands in and lifts the low-mid range; compensate for any overall gain change and A/B specifically for the frequency-response shift, not just perceived loudness. → [[BASS Slot]], [[Saturation and Distortion]]
- **EQ retroactively turns another processor's own effect up or down, without touching that processor's actual parameters — changing perceived character, not just tone.** Since reverb, clipping, saturation, and other processing add or amplify content in specific frequency ranges as a byproduct, EQing that range afterward doesn't just brighten/darken the mix — it amplifies or attenuates the perceived amount of that processor's own effect (boost the range a reverb's reflections occupy and the tail reads as more present; cut it and the tail recedes) even though the reverb's decay time, or the clipper's threshold/ratio, never changed. This is why the same clipper or reverb setting can read completely differently depending on EQ placed after it in the chain. → [[EQ]], [[Masking and Carving]]
