# Songs that use the 4-5-6-1 chord progression

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2BS0saH0SYk)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2BS0saH0SYk)

## Synopsis

Bennett names and traces the IV–V–vi–I "climb back home" progression — a four-chord loop that starts away from the tonic and climbs up to it — through several 2020s pop hits, then digs into why the SAME four chords get relabeled depending on context (major-key IV-V-vi-I vs. relative-minor vi-VII-i-III), and closes with a Lydian thought experiment about why this shape almost never gets heard as I-II-iii-V.

## Techniques

- **The progression: IV–V–vi–I** — in C: F–G–Am–C. Bennett's shorthand "climb back home": it starts away from the tonic and works upward until landing on I, giving it a clear sense of departure-then-arrival distinct from progressions that start on the tonic.
- **Harmonic rhythm as an intensifier**: "Heat Waves" (Glass Animals, bridge) starts at one chord per bar, then doubles the harmonic rhythm partway through — same four chords, twice the pace, read as rising energy.
- **A rushed variant**: "When You Were Young" (The Killers, key of B) keeps the same IV-V-vi-I link but rushes the V and vi chords to half a bar each, buying time to return to IV earlier in the loop — a different pacing trick from Heat Waves' clean doubling.
- **Straight uses**: "Who Knew" (Pink, chorus, key of A), "Beauty and the Beat" (Justin Bieber, key of C), "Apt." (Bruno Mars & Rosé, chorus, key of E♭: A♭–B♭–Cm–E♭).
- **Same chords, different label depending on context**: this exact chord string can also read as **vi–VII–i–III** in the relative minor rather than IV-V-vi-I in the major — which name applies depends on where the song's melody and prior sections put the tonal center, not on the chords in isolation. "Time Is Running Out" (Muse) — the chorus goes F–G–Am–C, but because the verse has already firmly established A minor as the tonal center, the chorus reads as 6-7-1-3 in A minor, not 4-5-6-1 in C, even though the chords are identical to the C-major reading elsewhere in this video.
- **Climbing past the tonic**: Elliott Smith's "Say Yes" bridge deliberately overshoots — most of the song is built on a descending progression, so the bridge inverts that by climbing, and instead of stopping at the IV-V-vi-I arrival point it keeps going past F to a G7 (the major II chord in the key of F — what Bennett elsewhere calls "the Elliott Smith chord," a favorite of his) before resolving.
- **Why this isn't heard as a Lydian I–II–iii–V**: technically the same four chords could be relabeled again as scale degrees 1-2-3-5 of F Lydian, but Lydian is a musically unstable mode that rarely reads as convincingly "home" on its own — a listener needs strong reinforcement (a pedal tone) to actually hear a Lydian tonic. Demonstrated by adding an F pedal note under each chord (F, G/F, Am/F, C/F), which keeps the ear anchored to F as tonic throughout — the closest practical way to make this progression genuinely read as Lydian. "Sarah" (Fleetwood Mac) is cited as the closest real-world example to that Lydian-pedal treatment.
