# Turnarounds

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Jazz Harmony]]. A short progression at the end of a section whose only job is to **get you back to the top convincingly**.

```progression-songs
```

Turnarounds matter far beyond jazz because loop-based music is *all* turnaround — a four-bar loop that repeats is a turnaround by definition. Getting the last bar right is what makes a loop feel circular rather than merely repeated. → [[Loop-Based Harmony]]

For the blues-specific version, see [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *Turnarounds*. This note is the general case.

## The base form

**I – vi – ii – V**, in C: `Cmaj7 – Am7 – Dm7 – G7`

Every other turnaround below is this one modified. It works because the roots walk down in thirds then jump a fourth, and because it ends on V, which wants the I at the top of the next chorus.

## The standard variations

| Name | In C | What changed | Sound |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plain | `Cmaj7 Am7 Dm7 G7` | — | Neutral, endlessly usable |
| **VI7** | `Cmaj7 A7 Dm7 G7` | vi → **secondary dominant** | Brighter, stronger pull to Dm7 |
| **III7–VI7** | `Cmaj7 E7 A7 Dm7 G7` | two secondary dominants | Very strong forward motion |
| **Tritone subs** | `Cmaj7 E♭7 Dm7 D♭7` | subs on the dominants | Chromatic descending bass |
| **♭III7** | `Cmaj7 E♭7 Dm7 G7` | one tritone sub | The cheapest way to sound "jazz" |
| **Backdoor** | `Cmaj7 Am7 Fm7 B♭7` | ii–V → backdoor | Softer, gospel-flavoured return |
| **Minor** | `Cm7 A♭maj7 Dm7♭5 G7♭9` | minor key throughout | Darker; the ii is half-diminished |

All of these are applications of [[Chord Substitution]] to the same four-chord skeleton. Learn the base form and the substitution rules and you can generate the rest rather than memorising them.

## The two-bar and one-bar compressions

Turnarounds compress to fit the space available:

- **Two bars:** `| Cmaj7 A7 | Dm7 G7 |` — two chords per bar
- **One bar:** `| Dm7 G7 |` — just the ii–V, the most common ending in the repertoire
- **Half bar:** `| ... G7 |` — V alone on beats 3–4

**A useful writing habit:** write the four-chord version first, then compress. Compressing a good turnaround gives a good short one; expanding a `Dm7 G7` gives you nothing to work with.

## Tadd Dameron turnaround

A named, dense, heavily chromatic turnaround associated with bebop composer Tadd Dameron — not a fixed four-chord skeleton like [[#The base form|the base form]] above but a longer chain of chords that reads as "overwhelming" on first encounter (per the video below). Found closing out Jerome Kern's "Nobody Else But Me." → [[🎵 JEROME KERN - Nobody Else But Me]]

**Two simplification methods, either usable straight through the whole progression rather than chord-by-chord:**
1. **C minor pentatonic across every chord of the turnaround** — stated to just work with no per-chord scale-matching needed, the cheapest possible read.
2. **The "Coltrane pattern"** — play the **1-2-3-5 chord tones** of each individual chord in sequence, chord by chord, as the more harmonically literal alternative.

Same "there's a cheaper legal read available" logic as this note's own [[#Chained turnarounds: the sharp-4 walkdown|sharp-4 walkdown]] section — treat a dense chromatic turnaround as one collapsible unit (a scale, or a repeating chord-tone shape) rather than as N unrelated chords to individually decode.

## Chained turnarounds: the sharp-4 walkdown

A longer device built entirely from links of the ii–V's already covered above — not a new chord vocabulary, just several short 2–5's chained together. Starts on the **♯IV degree of the key as a minor 7 flat 5 chord** — in C, that's **F♯m7♭5**. From there: a **minor 2–5 down** (F♯m7♭5 → B7) resolving to the **iii chord** (Em7), then a normal [[#The base form|turnaround]] back to I. Every link is typically a **short** 2–5 (both chords sharing one bar).

**Soloing over it — treat every link as its own isolated 2–5, never as one continuous scary progression:**
1. **Copy-paste 5–1 vocabulary over every link, deliberately ignoring each ii chord** — think "5, 1, 5, 1, 5, 1…" straight through the chain.
2. **Mix and match different 5–1 licks link to link** for variety.
3. **Play minor 2–5 language over the first link only** (F♯m7♭5→B7→Em is a genuine minor ii–V–i), then shift to major 5–1/2–5 vocabulary for the turnaround that follows.

This is the same chunking principle documented from a different source on [[Chord Progressions]] ("break a long, non-diatonic progression into small chunks... drill each chunk in isolation, then connect them") — applied here to a specific, named, very common jazz-standard device rather than an arbitrary chart. → [[Secondary Dominants]], [[The ii-V-I]]

## Using them outside jazz

**The last bar of a loop is the whole game.** A four-bar loop that ends on I feels closed and gets boring on repeat. The same loop ending on V, or on a chord a half-step above the downbeat chord, pulls back to the top and can run indefinitely.

Three moves that work in modern production:

- **End on V7 or ♭II7.** The classic pull. `Cmaj7 | Am7 | Fmaj7 | G7 |` or `... | D♭7 |`.
- **End on the ii.** Softer, very common in neo-soul — leaves the V implied and unresolved so the loop never fully closes.
- **Change only the last bar between repeats.** Loop A ends plain, loop B ends with the turnaround. Two loops of material, one bar of writing. This is the cheapest arrangement variation available and it's underused. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
