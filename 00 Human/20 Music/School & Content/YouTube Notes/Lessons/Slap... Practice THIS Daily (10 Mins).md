## What's taught

### Framing
- **One exercise, five levels, 10 minutes a day.** Forum-tested by BassBuzz students; the video explicitly warns against skipping to advanced riffs before nailing levels 1–4 — doing so produces "sketchy"-sounding slap because the fundamentals (clean strike, clean mute) aren't there yet.
- Level 1 is described as deceptively hard — "even intermediate players will make mistakes here" — because it strips slap down to only the essential motion with nothing to hide behind.

### Level 1 — the four ingredients of a clean slap (no pops yet)
- **Ingredient 1, accuracy: hit the string you're aiming for.** The concrete challenge named is moving the thumb between the E and A strings without clipping the wrong one. Whether using thumb-up or thumb-down technique, **maintain the same thumb angle to the string as you move across strings** — consistency of angle across the string set, not just a single correct angle.
- **Ingredient 2, muting: silence every string you aren't playing.** Framed with a vivid analogy — an unmuted, ringing slap is like "a singer who just yelled gibberish in between all their lyrics." Fix: cover the strings you're not playing with whatever spare fingers on the fretting hand make sense. Slapping the A string → cover D and G with ring/pinky, and optionally reach over and cover the low E with index or middle. Slapping the E string → cover A/D/G with whatever's comfortable (e.g. laying the pinky across all three). The instruction is deliberately loose — "just have to get some meat on there," not a single prescribed fingering.
- **Ingredient 3, note length: control how long a slapped note rings before it's muted.** Framed as easy to overlook (a wrong note or wrong rhythm is obvious; a note ringing too long isn't) but just as important to a tight sound — missing it is called "a surefire way to sound like amateur-hour karaoke bass." Mechanically it's an explicit **slap-mute** pattern on every note that isn't meant to ring into the next one: touch the string with any fretting-hand finger right after the slap to cut it off, e.g. slap–mute–slap–slap–mute across a bar.
- **Ingredient 4, rhythm: playing in time, with a concrete trick for it.** Rather than the generic "practice more" answer, the actionable trick given is **listen to the drums, especially the kick drum**, and use it as a timing grid to check against — amateur slappers get so absorbed in the slap-hand mechanics that they forget the rest of the band exists and lose the grid entirely.

### Level 2 — the Teeter-Totter pop technique (full extraction on its own note, [[Teeter-Totter Slap-Pop Technique]])
- Diagnosis of sloppy/slow slap-and-pop: too much **wasted motion** — rotating all the way back up after the slap before coming back down for the pop (a "down-up-down-up" four-part motion) — not a coordination problem so much as a motion-efficiency one, and it's a hard ceiling on speed.
- The fix: **one continuous cycle.** Slap down, then — without twisting the forearm back up — get the thumb off the string (either bouncing off it or striking through it), hook the pop finger under the target string, and twist back up with the forearm to execute the pop. One down/up cycle produces both the slap and the pop.
- Level 2's exercise pattern adds pops to test the Teeter-Totter directly: **slap–mute–slap–slap–pop**, repeated, watching both the motion economy and accuracy — including a harder variant moving the popping finger from the G string to the D string (described as trickier because the finger has to tuck in more to get under the D string). Rhythm discipline (listen to the drums) carries forward from level 1.

### Level 3 — ghost notes (full extraction on its own note, [[Ghost Notes (Slap Bass)]])
- **Definition:** muted slaps and pops — the fretting hand covers the string(s) without fretting an actual note, producing a percussive "thwacky" sound instead of a pitch. Marked as `X` in tab/notation. Mechanically it's the same motion as an un-muted slap "before you got your technique together" — same physical mistake, now deployed on purpose for texture.
- **Rhythmic function, not just texture:** the video's real argument for ghost notes is that a semi-regular thumb pulse (even on notes that don't sound a pitch) acts like a denser metronome click, which is easier to lock a groove against than sparse clicks with big gaps — so ghosting the thumb along between real notes helps *keep* rhythm, not just decorate it. Explicitly not a rule to always ghost every gap ("sometimes you need to leave those gaps for musical reasons"), but the general habit of an active, semi-continuous thumb is called out as something even pros lean on.
- **Reference example: Marcus Miller.** Cited as bouncing his slap thumb on almost every eighth note in a bar — a master who "doesn't need a crutch" but organizes lines so the thumb keeps moving anyway, because (per the host, from 20 years of slap experience) "it just feels good."

### Level 4 — hammer-ons into the slap line (cross-enriches [[Hammer-Ons and Pull-Offs (Bass)]])
- **Framing:** every technique so far (slap, pop, ghost) is executed entirely by the slap hand; the most impressive slap playing brings the fretting hand in as an active contributor too — hammer-ons are named as "the best starting point" among a family of such moves (pull-offs and left-hand slaps are name-dropped as further options not covered here).
- **Mechanic:** a hammer-on is sounding a note purely by striking the string forcefully with a fretting-hand finger — no pluck, no slap. Same basic motion as fretting, "but way more forceful." Can be done cold on any note, but in a slap line it's typically chained right after a slap or pop that sounds a lower note first.
- **The two-fret rule:** to hammer into a target note, **start two frets below it**, slap or pop that starting note, then hammer up to the target. Given as a rule that "almost always works" with **no scale knowledge required**. If the underlying scale is known, the more precise version is to start **one scale-degree below** instead of a flat two frets — described as the theoretically cleaner option for players who know the relevant scale.
- **Worked example:** for a slap-popped octave line ("D octaves"), instead of popping the octave note directly, pop a note two frets below the octave, then hammer on to the octave — same target pitch, extra rhythmic/textural interest along the way.
- **Applied to the exercise:** the octave pops in the exercise get replaced with "pop two frets below, then hammer to the target note" — e.g. bar 1 becomes slap–ghost–slap–slap–pop–hammer, same idea repeated in bar 3, with an additional bonus hammer-on worked into bar 4.
- **The rhythm risk hammer-ons introduce:** normally the plucking/slapping hand is what enforces rhythm; once hammer-ons enter the line, the **fretting hand takes over that job for those notes**, and a fretting hand that isn't yet precise at rhythm tends to **rush** — hammering a note in before its actual rhythmic slot. Named as a very common issue for players new to hammer-ons. The fix given is not a trick, just **practice slow enough to be precise**, since the fretting hand needs to build the same rhythmic reliability the plucking hand already has.

### Level 5 — melodic slap lines vs. "disco octaves" (full extraction on its own note, [[Melodic Slap Lines — Avoiding Disco Octaves]])
- **The problem named:** straight octave-jumping slap lines ("disco octaves") are exciting at first but stop feeling satisfying once the basics are down, because they don't read as melodic.
- **Why, per the video:** the ear's sense of melody is anchored to the human voice, and speech/singing moves in a relatively small pitch range — we don't naturally jump wildly high and low. Music that jumps a lot (like a straight octave line) registers as *less* melodic for the same reason.
- **The fix:** sprinkle in **close-together, non-jumpy notes** alongside the big jumps — even a small amount signals "melody is happening here" to the listener and keeps them engaged, rather than replacing the octaves outright.
- **Where it already shows up in the exercise, pointed out explicitly:** the hammer-ons added in level 4 are close-together by nature; the slap movement at the end of bar 2 stays tight even with the octave pops around it (described as "something you could sing in a stadium"); and the bigger fill added in bar 4 looks jumpy on paper but, followed by ear through the actual slapped notes, still reads as tight and "singable."
