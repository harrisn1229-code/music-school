# Rap Vocal Production

**The strand's most conspicuous gap** — VOX-12 asked what's different about producing a rap vocal versus a sung one and came back blank, in a core lane.

## The one difference everything follows from

**Rap is a rhythmic performance. The thing you're producing is pocket, not pitch.**

Where a sung vocal is judged on intonation, tone and phrasing, a rap vocal is judged on **where the syllables land against the beat**. That single shift changes the workflow, the editing, the processing and what "a good take" even means.

Corollary: the tools you reach for are the rhythm tools. [[Pocket and Microtiming]] is more relevant to a rap vocal than [[Vocal Tuning]] is.

## Pocket and delivery

- **Laying back vs pushing applies to voices too.** A verse delivered slightly behind the beat reads as relaxed and authoritative; pushing ahead reads as urgent and aggressive. Both are choices.
- **Nudge, don't re-record.** A take that's rushing or dragging but otherwise good gets dragged left or right in the timeline until it locks. This is the most basic and most-used rap vocal fix — already noted in `YouTube Notes/You Suck at Producing - Rap Vocals.md`.
- **Edit at the syllable level for timing** — which is the opposite of the comping advice in [[Vocal Comping and Takes]], and deliberately so. Comp *performance* by phrase; fix *timing* by syllable.
- **Consistency of pocket matters more than perfection of any line.** A verse where every bar sits the same distance behind the beat sounds intentional. One where it varies sounds sloppy.

## Workflow differences

| | Sung | Rap |
|---|---|---|
| **Tracking** | Full passes, then punch-ins | **Line by line punch-ins** are normal, not a fallback |
| **Comping** | By phrase, for delivery | By phrase for delivery, by syllable for timing |
| **Doubles** | Often the whole part | **On bar-ends and key phrases**, not the whole verse |
| **Ad-libs** | Optional colour | **A separate pass, structurally essential** |
| **Tuning** | Transparent correction | Absent, or pushed into obvious effect |

**Doubling conventions.** Doubling an entire rap verse flattens it — it removes the dynamic contrast between a plain line and an emphasized one. The convention is to double **the last few words of a bar**, the hook line, and anything you want to hit harder. The double is an accent, not a texture.

**The ad-lib pass is not optional.** In most modern rap the ad-libs carry a large part of the energy and the personality. Track them as a dedicated pass after the main verse, with the artist reacting to their own take. See [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]].

**Breath and space.** Rap fills more of the bar than singing does, so the gaps are load-bearing. Don't remove breaths — they're punctuation, and in a dense verse they're often the only thing separating two ideas.

## Processing emphasis

Different weighting to a sung vocal, not different tools.

- **Compression harder and faster.** Rapid-fire delivery has wide level swings between syllables. Fast attack and fast release, threshold under the loud outlier words, then makeup gain — the result reads as *tighter and more powerful*, not quieter. See [[Compression]].
- **Presence and intelligibility over beauty.** The lyric has to be parseable over a loud beat. That usually means more upper-mid than you'd put on a sung vocal.
- **De-essing matters more**, because the compression that buys intelligibility also raises sibilance. See [[De-essing]] and the chain order in [[Compression Staging]].
- **High-pass around 100 Hz** to kill plosives and mic pops — surgical, not a broad sweep. See [[EQ]] and [[Vocal Mic Technique]].
- **Pitch correction is either absent or an effect.** There's no transparent middle ground in most rap.
