# How To Use Enclosures/Approaches (PART 2)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aWeuuFX48us)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aWeuuFX48us)

## Synopsis

A short (under 8 minutes) Nathan Borton lesson explicitly framed as a sequel — it opens by pointing back to an earlier "enclosures and approaches" video (not currently in this vault) as the prerequisite for the basics, then spends its own runtime on three ways to *expand* that vocabulary. First: the **pivot tone**, a repeated/pedaled note used as the launch point for a chromatic approach to a second note — worked over minor chords (pedal the 5th, chromatically approach the 6th, which becomes the 3rd of the following V7 in a 2-5-1) and over major/dominant chords (pedal the root, chromatically approach the 5th from the 3rd, or the reverse — start on the 5th and walk down). Second: applying half-step approaches to **every tone of an arpeggio** rather than a single target note, a bebop-era staple demonstrated with two real transcribed licks — a Donald Byrd dominant-arpeggio line and a Wes Montgomery altered-dominant line built on an E♭ major triad. Third, and the video's stated capstone idea: applying the *same* approach/enclosure vocabulary to **chord shapes when comping** — treating a voicing like a single note in a line, decorating it with approaches, layering in richer voicing color (minor 9, major 6, major 6/9) and inversions once the basic version is comfortable. All three devices route back to the same core mechanism as [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §2–3 and this vault's other enclosure notes ([[Enclosures Through the Scale]], [[Downbeat and Upbeat Enclosures]]) but none of the three specific devices here (pivot tone, arpeggio-wide approach, chord-shape approach) were previously documented — captured in full on the new [[Pivot Tone Lines]] technique note.

## Techniques

- **Pivot tone on minor chords** — pedal the **5th** of a minor chord, chromatically approach the **6th** from below. Over a 2-5-1, that approached 6th becomes the **3rd of the V7 chord** the instant the harmony changes underneath it — the same repeated note doubling as both a stable anchor and a set-up for the chord change. Multiple 2-5-1 line variations demonstrated using this shape.
- **Pivot tone on major/dominant chords, ascending** — pedal the **root**, chromatically approach the **5th from the 3rd**. Demonstrated in a blues line; described as "quintessential" for the sound of jazz language.
- **Pivot tone on major/dominant chords, descending** — the mirror: start on the **5th**, walk down toward the root through the same territory. Demonstrated over both a blues context and a major 2-5-1.
- **Half-step approach on every arpeggio tone** — instead of approaching one target note, precede **each tone** of an arpeggio with a half-step approach from below, resolving the final approached note into the target chord's 3rd. Worked over a G major arpeggio resolving into a I chord's 3rd; flagged as bebop-era vocabulary, common over dominant chords.
- **Donald Byrd device (solo on "Lex")** — walk up a dominant 9th arpeggio, land a **five-note enclosure** on the dominant chord's 7th, then apply the half-step-per-arpeggio-tone device to the same dominant chord.
- **Wes Montgomery device (solo on "D Natural Blues")** — opens on a classic Wes minor-9 shape (a device he leans on often over minor 9 chords), adds an **enclosure of the 3rd**, then reaches an **altered dominant** (♯5, ♯9 added) outlined largely as an **E♭ major triad over the dominant root** (the source of the altered color), finished with the half-step-per-arpeggio-tone device on each triad note.
- **Approaching chord shapes when comping** — treat a comping voicing as if it were a single note in a melodic line: add approach notes/enclosures *into* the shape the same way you'd approach a single target tone. Worked over a basic 2-5-1 with fundamental guitar voicings first, before adding decoration.
- **Layering in richer voicing color** — once the basic approached-voicing version is comfortable, swap in more colorful shapes for the same targets: a **minor 9**, a **major 6**, and a **major 6/9** shape, each treated as its own approachable "target."
- **Use inversions of the chord shapes** — knowing a chord's inversions, not just its root-position grip, is what makes the approach-into-a-comping-shape device usable across a full progression rather than jumping around the neck; the video specifically reuses Donald Byrd's half-step approach device from the soloing section and applies it to comping shapes and their inversions.
- **Framing/thesis** — once chord shapes are treated as single-note-line "targets" that can be approached or enclosed, comping opens up the same expressive range that melodic soloing gets from the same vocabulary — the video's closing takeaway.
