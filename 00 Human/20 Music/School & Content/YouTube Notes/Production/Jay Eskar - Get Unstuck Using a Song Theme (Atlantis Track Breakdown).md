# Jay Eskar: Get UNSTUCK Using A "Song Theme" (Atlantis Track Breakdown)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HJSK_EIGNP8)

## Synopsis

The full source breakdown behind the "pick a song theme" clip already captured in [[This ONE Question Will Improve Every Song You Make]] — a much deeper, complete walkthrough of building a future house track around a concept (Atlantis as a spiritually/scientifically advanced civilization). Covers chord/harmony writing, melody humanization, layered sound design with explicit frequency goals, a drop-impact mono→wide contrast trick, a shamanic vocal sample process, and his full mix/master routine including a matching-EQ reference technique. Two bits already captured elsewhere from this same raw session — the "use overused sounds if the end result is good" and "don't do surgical EQ mid-flow" quotes — aren't repeated here; see [[How to Be Unique and Find Your Sound (With Less Stress)|the earlier Sol State compilation]] for those.

## Techniques

- **Use an 8-note scale instead of the standard 7-note diatonic scale to unlock harmonies a normal scale can't produce.** A deliberate, uncommon scale choice specifically to access chord colors outside standard diatonic harmony. → [[Chord Progressions]]
- **Extend a loop by adding bars specifically to introduce tension via a chord that sounds "horrible" in isolation but works in context.** Looping a chord progression as-is creates zero tension; adding four extra bars with a deliberately different, dissonant-in-isolation ending chord gives the loop somewhere to go. → [[Chord Progressions]]
- **Write the bass progression FIRST, then build chords on top of it — inverting the usual chords-first order.** Experimented with bass notes to find which one best resolved the progression, then wrote chords over that finished bass line; the resulting chord voicing was neither cleanly major nor minor, adopted deliberately for the "pull and push" ambiguity it creates rather than resolved into a standard triad. → [[Chord Progressions]], [[Bassline Writing]]
- **Give sound design an explicit intention tied to the song's concept, not just a pleasing timbre.** Chose a vocal-like, "growly" bass patch specifically because the concept called for the bass to "talk" — sound design decisions justified by what the song's theme needs to communicate, not by the sound alone. → [[Bass Design]]
- **Write chords before melody — it's easier to find a strong melody once harmony already exists as a foundation.** Reused the same chord progression from the breakdown section but with a different rhythm for the melody's underlying harmony.
- **Treat a melody phrase as a question, answered by the next phrase — a call-and-response structure learned from Hans Zimmer.** Framing a melody as "asking something" and then "replying" gives it a sense of personality/intent almost automatically — described as thinking of the music as "some living thing." → [[Melody Writing]]
- **Add pitch slides mid-note (not just between notes) to make a melody read as more complex and alive.** A/B'd: the same note plain vs. with a slide added partway through it — the slide alone changes the character meaningfully without changing the underlying notes.
- **Make every note in a lead drift slightly in pitch (up or down) rather than holding perfectly static — makes the part read as "speaking" instead of robotic.** Paired with **deliberately varying note LENGTH** (mixing long and short notes rather than uniform rhythmic values) — both moves mirror how human speech actually behaves (never perfectly even in pitch or timing), giving an electronic lead a human character. → [[Melody Writing]]
- **Layer a lead with a specific frequency-coverage GOAL, not by ear alone.** Started with a lead covering mostly mids, then deliberately added layers to cover the missing bands: a pluck layer for extra attack, another layer specifically for added highs — 5-6 layers total, each chosen to fill a gap rather than stacked randomly. Explicit reasoning: don't add layers without a goal in mind. → [[Layering and Stacking]]
- **Every part of a track should represent all three pillars of music — harmony, rhythm, and melody — not just whichever one the part obviously belongs to.**
- **Keep the kick itself simple (mostly just transient shaping) and let background/ambient layers make the drop feel big instead.** Learned from dubstep producers: layer a sustained note, an arp, and "rides" behind the kick/claps specifically for ambience and depth, rather than relying on the kick and clap alone to carry the drop's weight. Short claps plus white noise risers add forward-pulling energy — white noise specifically called out as very effective for this without adding other audible content. → [[Kick Design]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **Drop-impact contrast trick: automate the stereo image to collapse to MONO right before the drop, then "explode" back to full width the instant the drop hits.** Paired with automating master volume as a second simultaneous lever — the combined mono-to-wide-plus-volume-jump reads as a much bigger impact than either move alone. → [[Width and Stereo]], [[Automation]]
- **A melody doesn't need to play constantly to be memorable — let it come in and drop out.** Realized only a few years into producing that a hook can be more memorable precisely because it isn't always present; simplicity and hummability matter more than continuous presence.
- **Layer in a real-world sound tied to the song's concept for a subconscious emotional effect, even at very low, barely-audible levels.** Added a literal fireplace recording for the Atlantis/spiritual-ceremony concept — subtle enough to not be consciously noticed, but contributing a "getting warmed up" feeling underneath the mix.
- **Philosophy: real art aims to be objective, not subjective — don't just make something that makes YOU feel an emotion, make something that reliably makes an audience feel it.** Cites a teacher (Gurudev) on this distinction; used as the standard for judging whether a creative choice is actually working, versus just being personally satisfying.
- **Shamanic vocal sample process:** found an a cappella vocal sample (Cymatics), cut sections, wrote an original melody from the pieces — sounded rough on its own, so ran it through Melodyne/Nectar-style pitch correction, boosted select frequencies with an Oxford Inflator, and finished with a CLA vocal chain to make it sit with the rest of the mix. Matched with shaker and cinematic-drum percussion chosen for a "shamanic" rhythmic feel even though the individual sounds aren't literally shamanic instruments.
- **Mix routine: level everything with NO EQ first, then only add EQ once the raw balance is right.** His stated rule of thumb — if two sounds already sound good level-wise with no EQ at all, adding EQ afterward makes them sound great; skipping straight to EQ before levels are right undermines the whole pass. → [[Gain Staging]]
- **Sub/bass processing recipe:** mono the sub, add distortion/clipping for harmonics (harmonics are "the most important thing in music" by his framing), cut highs and most mids with EQ, apply upward compression plus more distortion on the bass layer, then a parametric EQ pass with a deliberately large (~9 dB) peak boost at a chosen frequency to restore missing harmonic content — explicitly NOT a "just because" boost, done because the rest of the mix was lacking exactly those frequencies. → [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[Bass Design]]
- **Deliberately break the "cut don't boost" EQ rule as a signature move — boosting mids on a bass patch as a recognizable personal touch, "like adding salt to a recipe."** Framed explicitly as intentional rule-breaking used consistently enough to become a personal sonic signature, not a mistake or a one-off. → [[EQ]]
- **Master-bus matching-EQ technique: load a reference track into a matching EQ, capture its curve, then move your own master's EQ curve to align with it.** Used to catch frequency gaps invisible by ear alone (his example: the match revealed a need for more highs he hadn't consciously noticed) — a way to align with "what everyone else is releasing" as a starting reference point, then adjust further by feel. → [[Referencing and Translation]]
- **Master chain specifics:** boost lows after the reference match, then a steep high-pass below ~28 Hz (protects big PA systems from getting muddy on sub-audible rumble), Ozone Imager set mono below 110 Hz, a small (~5%) mid boost, a touch more width/highs (~1%), finishing with a light glue/console-emulation stage (Fruity Balance-style) — described as making a small but real difference by containing everything into one cohesive "box." Comfortable letting reverb tails and similar peaks push into the red on a meter if the result serves the song — "more of a feeling kind of guy" than a strict-numbers one. → [[Master Chain]]
