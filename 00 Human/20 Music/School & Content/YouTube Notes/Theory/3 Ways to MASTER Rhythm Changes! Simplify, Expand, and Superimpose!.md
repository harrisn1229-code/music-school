## What's taught

- **Concept 1 — Simplify: reduce the A section to a 1–5–1 skeleton and solo over B♭ major/blues.** Named trap up front, same framing as the sibling video: unfamiliar players try to outline every fast-moving chord of the A section, producing a "non-stop barrage of eighth-note lines" that gets monotonous for player and listener alike. The fix: most of what's commonly played over the A section boils down to just **three chords — B♭, E♭, F7 (a 1–5–1)** — so solo over **B♭ major** for most of the progression rather than chasing the full changes. **Two Charlie Parker-style licks** demonstrated, both built mostly from **enclosures of B♭'s chord tones** (approach notes wrapping the target from both sides) rather than straight arpeggios — cited as the pattern many jazz greats actually play. Reminder to land on B♭'s **extensions (9th, 13th)**, not just chord tones, for color. Alternative treatment: **treat the A section as a straight B♭ blues** and reuse existing blues vocabulary over it. → [[Blues Form and Harmony]], [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] § *Prolongation* (same "collapse a fast progression to one static sound" idea), [[Extended Chords]] (9ths/13ths as landing tones).
- **Concept 2 — Expand: the Sonny Stitt/Don Byas dominant-cycle substitution over bars 1–4 (taught by guest Jamie Halloran).** The first four bars of rhythm changes are normally **two turnarounds** — a **1-6-2-5** (bars 1–2) followed by a **3-6-2-5** (bars 3–4). The substitution does two things: **(1) convert every chord in both turnarounds to a dominant 7th** — "dominant sevenths are the most flexible harmonic extension you can add to a chord," so they tolerate the reharmonization well; **(2) extend the bar-3–4 cycle of fourths two bars earlier, into bar 1**, so instead of the expected B♭7 in bar 1 you get **F♯7 → B7 → E7 → A7 → D7**, each resolving up a fourth into the next, landing cleanly on the **D7 that already belongs in bar 3**. The line works despite F♯7 having no obvious relationship to B♭ because **both progressions are destination-oriented** — they converge on the same D7 (bar 3) and ultimately the same B♭ (bar 5); the substitute cycle just approaches that destination from a different, chromatically-unrelated starting point. Demonstrated A/B: the original-harmony line and the substitution line played back-to-back over the same A section for direct comparison. **Practical/bandstand note (asked and answered on camera):** the sub is often usable *without* telling the rest of the band in advance, but it works best (a) at a **fast tempo**, where the ear parses melodic shape and destination rather than tracking every reharmonized chord in real time, and (b) with a **strong, confident melodic idea** carrying it — a weak line invites the reharmonization to be heard as a mistake instead of an intentional substitution. **New to the vault** — not the same device as [[Rhythm Changes]]'s existing "Common substitutions" (tritone subs on the bridge, interpolated ii's, diminished passing chords in the A section); closest existing relative is the ii-interpolation entry, but this is a full extended dominant-cycle reharmonization starting two bars ahead of where the harmony calls for one. → [[Turnarounds]], [[Chord Substitution]], [[Circle of Fifths]], [[Secondary Dominants]] — Jamie Halloran (guest), this video.
- **Concept 3 — Superimpose: B♭ diminished over the first four bars, from a transcribed solo (low-confidence attribution).** Same device as [[7 Rhythm Changes Licks Every Jazz Musician Should Know]]'s Concept 6 (B♭ diminished replacing the A section's tonic sound for tension into the IV chord), reached here via a different source: a solo the presenter transcribed, captioned as **"Lewis Smith"** playing on a tune captioned **"Bacon"** *(both renderings unconfirmed — auto-caption garble, low confidence; possibly a misheard player/tune name)*. **Corroborating tune citation, higher confidence:** the same tonic-major-turned-diminished device also shows up in **Benny Golson's "Upper Manhattan Medical Group."** Mechanism stated plainly: take the tonic major chord and make it **diminished**, so it resolves back to itself while adding tension/color — i.e. the tonic-diminished device, not a passing or secondary-dominant use. The transcribed line is described as **mostly triads pulled from the B♭ diminished scale**, connected together rather than run as a straight scale or arpeggio; generalized takeaway matches the sibling video exactly — play either the **B♭ diminished arpeggio** or **triads from the B♭ diminished scale**, connected. → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Rhythm Changes]] § *Common substitutions*, [[7 Rhythm Changes Licks Every Jazz Musician Should Know]].
- **Closing demo, not separately transcribable:** the presenter plays one full chorus over a B♭ rhythm changes, on-screen-labeling which of the three concepts (simplify / substitute / superimpose) is active at each moment — a visual demonstration with no additional verbal content beyond the recap "we can either simplify the changes, substitute the changes, or superimpose other changes."
