> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **Chord = pitch content · voicing = order and spacing · shape = one fingering of a voicing**
> Slide a shape up a fret and the voicing survives while the chord changes, which is the proof they are separate.
>
> **Q2.** **Register and doubling**
> *Also accepted:* octave placement · instrument doubling · decay and reverb
> A doubled course an octave up can be the most audible note in a chord and appears nowhere in its name.
>
> **Q3.** **3rd and 7th, then the extension, then the root, then the 5th · shell = 1-3-7 · rootless = 3-7-extensions**
> Guide tones define quality and function, the root is the bass player's, and the 5th adds nothing to identity unless it is altered.
>
> **Q4.** **A shell or rootless voicing, placed up out of the vocal's register**
> *Rubric (5):* keeps 3rd and 7th and says why (2) · drops root and 5th, naming the bass as why the root is safe to drop (2) · addresses register, comping higher to leave the low mids clear (1)
> The 3rd and 7th are what make it that chord and cannot go. The 5th contributes nothing to identity and leaves first; the root is covered by the bass and its finger is better spent on the extension you want to hear. That leaves a shell or a rootless grip, which is also the fastest thing to find on a tune you do not know, because it transposes without thought. Register is the other half of the answer: the same chord low on the neck is mud under a voice and fine as a solo statement, so when the low mids are occupied you comp higher. That is an arrangement fix for what would otherwise arrive at the mix as a masking problem.
>
> **Q5.** **Perfect 4ths instead of 3rds — a stack of 3rds names a quality and so implies a function**
> Quartal harmony states a set of notes without committing to major, minor or dominant, so nothing in it wants to resolve anywhere.
>
> **Q6.** **Which note the ear treats as the root**
> The fingers never move. The same three notes read as minor 11, sus4, 6/9, major 13 or 7sus4 depending only on the assumed root.
>
> **Q7.** **Move one note an octave; on a collision, move the newly-colliding note to its own nearest octave**
> Cascade that fix until every note has its own string, then repeat from whatever shape you landed on. Unplayable results are expected — discard them.
>
> **Q8.** **Twelve — three inversions on each of the four groups E-A-D, A-D-G, D-G-B, G-B-E**
> Root, 3rd or 5th on the bottom, at three octave locations of the root. The two groups crossing the G–B pair reuse the same shapes with every note above that pair a fret higher — D-G-B the top note, G-B-E the top two.
>
> **Q9.** **You choose each chord by proximity to the previous one instead of defaulting to root position**
> Less hand travel, a higher clean tempo, and a less generic sound from identical notes.
>
> **Q10.** **Play it off the ♭7 of the dominant**
> *Also accepted:* the same family off the 3rd gives a dominant 9
> Every note of the shape built on the dominant's ♭7 is a legitimate altered tone of that dominant, so the grip reads as a rootless altered voicing.
>
> **Q11.** **Hold the common tone, move the rest to the nearest note of the next chord — except the bass**
> The lowest voice stays on each chord's actual root, because the ear reads the chord's identity from the bass.
>
> **Q12.** **Each change keeps its shared note fixed and moves the others by step, over plain roots in the bass**
> *Rubric (5):* applies the hold-the-common-tone rule correctly across the loop (2) · describes at least two changes concretely, naming the held note and where the others went (2) · states the bass stays on the roots and is not voice-led (1)
> A correct answer walks the loop change by change: name the note both chords share, state that it does not move, and account for every other note's destination and distance. The result should be mixed inversions with most voices holding or moving a step, not a row of root-position chords. Underneath, the left hand or bass plays the plain roots throughout — which is why the progression still reads as root position however the top is voiced, and why voice-leading the bass as well would cost the harmonic clarity the root motion supplies.
>
> **Q13.** **A close triad with the middle note up an octave (1-5-3) · chain by thirds, moving one voice per change**
> Because the one-note rule always lands on the closest voicing of the next chord, every quality recurs in a different inversion and full coverage falls out for free.
>
> **Q14.** **The rhythm, not the notes — so drill the rhythm alone on one shell voicing first**
> Metronome on 2 and 4, one static voicing, no changes. Only then does it go through a progression with real voicings.
>
> **Q15.** **Charleston — 1 and the "and" of 2 · push — sustained on the "and" of 3 · downbeat-anchored**
> *Also accepted:* off-the-one, on the "and" of 1 and beat 3 · response, replying to a soloist's phrase
> Charleston is the reference groove and is static; the push supplies forward momentum by arriving before the barline; the downbeat-anchored one avoids syncopation so it never competes with a busy soloist.
>
> **Q16.** **Repetition is what makes a part read as intentional — so lock the motif, then depart, then return**
> Reinventing bar to bar sounds scattered even when every choice is defensible, and a departure only lands because a baseline was established first.
>
> **Q17.** **Only when the soloist has actually stopped — and the first question is where it leads**
> A fill under an active melody is clutter. Its job is to move forward into the next bar or section, which rules out decorating the chord it starts on.
>
> **Q18.** **Assign roles out loud · comp in the holes · track register and move opposite the soloist**
> *Also accepted:* lay out entirely · one player takes a predictable pulse role instead of comping
> Two independent harmony parts read as clutter no matter how good each is, so the division has to be decided rather than discovered.
>
> **Q19.** **Comp lower as the soloist climbs, place hits in the other player's holes, and be willing to lay out**
> *Rubric (5):* moves opposite the soloist's register and says why (2) · places hits in the gaps rather than doubling the other comper or the soloist (1.5) · treats laying out as an available and legitimate choice (1.5)
> With the soloist climbing you drop, because two voices converging on one register cancel each other out and going above a soloist pulls attention off them. Against the other comping instrument you listen for its pattern and land in its gaps rather than alongside it, which is easier when either of you is holding something repetitive. If the roles were never assigned, the honest move is to take the horn-player default and lay out until cued — silence is a real choice, it buys thinking time, and it lets the soloist define what is actually needed before you commit to filling it.
>
> **Q20.** **Closed triads — three notes, no doublings — separated by string group, position and tone**
> They are lean on purpose, which is what lets three stack without turning to mud. The layers use one triad vocabulary relocated, not different material.
>
> **Q21.** **The form — it tells you how much of the chart is genuinely new material**
> AABA or ABAC, and an AABA tune with an 8-bar A is often only 16 bars of distinct harmony. It also licenses the turnaround prediction on the last two bars of each A.
>
> **Q22.** **Substitute something that fits, judged against the melody rather than the page**
> Overriding a stock harmonisation, especially in a printed tag, is normal practice among players who know the tune.
>
> **Q23.** **As a degree of the chord sounding now, not of the key · index voicings by chord tone and string**
> The same pitch is the 5 of C under a C chord and the 4 of D a beat later under Dm. Indexing by which chord tone the melody note is and which string it sits on is the lookup you actually need mid-arrangement.
>
> **Q24.** **Everything larger is the 3rd and ♭7 with something added, and every rule above it is the same economy**
> *Rubric (5):* identifies the 3rd and ♭7 as the minimum that fixes a chord's quality (1.5) · shows the larger voicings as that pair plus additions (1.5) · connects it to register or staying out of another part's way (1) · connects it to one movable shape relocated across a form (1)
> The diad is the drop-priority rule followed all the way down: keep the guide tones, and everything else was optional. Building back up, a shell is the diad plus the root, a rootless 9th or 13th is the diad plus an extension, and a full drop-2 voicing is the diad plus whatever the hand can still reach — every one of them is the same two load-bearing notes with colour stacked on. The register decision is the same argument in a different direction: two notes leave room under a voice or beside another comping instrument where a six-string chord does not, so the reduction that makes the chord legible is also the one that keeps the arrangement clear. And the economy is identical to the string-group and multifunctional material — one movable shape relocated across a whole form, half step down for the IV and half step up for the V, instead of a grip memorised per chord. Knowing which notes are load-bearing is what makes it safe to leave the rest out, and that single judgement is what the whole course teaches.
