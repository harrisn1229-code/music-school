# Sample Selection and Manipulation | Realistic Drum Programming - The Ultimate Guide (Part 1)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tHWlVXzdTFk)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tHWlVXzdTFk)

## Synopsis

Part 1 of Max Konyi's series on realistic (= *believable*, not necessarily acoustic) drum programming, focused entirely on choosing and manipulating individual drum samples before any pattern gets written. Max is a composer/producer working in Studio One, using Battery 4 as his sampler of choice for electronic-leaning kits (and Addictive Drums for fully acoustic ones). His core claim: sample choice alone — same pattern, same mix, just better samples — makes a bigger difference to realism than anything downstream, though pattern/velocity/timing/mixing (covered in later videos) matter too. Most of the video is a hands-on walkthrough building one kit sample-by-sample: kicks, snares (with ghost-note and rimshot articulations), hi-hats (four distinct velocity-tier samples), a stereo clap trick, toms, and layered cymbals — with a running commentary on *why* each choice reads as more believable than the "amateur" version he plays for comparison throughout.

## Techniques

**Sourcing and organizing samples**
- **Where to find samples:** your DAW's bundled one-shot library (Studio One/FL/Ableton/Logic all ship their own); free packs via Google/Reddit searches ("free drum samples"); `Splice`-style subscription services (monthly credits spent on individual hits); or a producer friend's existing collection (how Max started — a friend with years of amassed samples handed them over).
- **Organize by category → subcategory, and cull ruthlessly.** Max's Battery library is organized claps/cymbals/hats/kicks etc. at the top level, then subdivided further (kicks → 808s, electronic, kick-enhancers, soft/urban/hip-hop, acoustic). He spent roughly a week going through his entire library once, deleting everything he knew he'd never use and organizing the keepers this way — a one-time investment that pays off every session afterward, since narrowing "literally a thousand" candidates down to ~50 relevant ones beats sifting the whole pile each time.
- **Genre/stylistic bias is a real, deliberate choice, not neutral.** His personal default is "hyper clean, tight, punchy" over "dirty, gritty, old-school lo-fi" — neither is more correct; the point is to know your own bias and choose it on purpose per track rather than by accident.

**Three pieces of core advice (before touching any sample)**
- **Ear training.** Deliberately listen to drums — in all music you hear, not just your preferred genre — critically enough to form real opinions ("no, no, no, yes, no, maybe") about what you like. Without opinions, there's no compass for picking a good kick vs. a bad one (there's no such thing as an objectively "good" kick — only one that fits the current context).
- **Knowledge of real drum kits.** If you've never studied or played a physical kit, watch drummers on YouTube — how they hit, the different articulations (closed/open hi-hat, stick position, hard vs. soft kick), and how tonal characteristics change with technique. The more you understand the physical instrument, the better you can manipulate a sample to emulate it.
- **Samples get tweaked, never used raw as an exact match.** You'll never find the *exact* sound in your head — treat the best sample you found as a starting point and shape it further with EQ, compression, and pitch-shifting.
- **Build for kit cohesion, not just individually great samples.** A pile of amazing-sounding samples that don't share a frequency balance/character doesn't read as one kit — listen to well-put-together real kits for what "belonging together" sounds like, then pick with that lens.

**Sample cleanliness checklist — issues to watch for while auditioning**
- **Reverb baked into the sample.** Fine if that's the intended aesthetic, but if you want full control over the kit's space later, reject samples with pre-baked reverb (or be aware you're locking that decision in now).
- **Cut-off tails.** Many samples (especially cymbals) have their natural decay abruptly truncated — you'll need to build a custom volume envelope to fake a smoother fade rather than relying on the sample's own tail.
- **Baked-in distortion/grit/off-center panning.** Can be desirable (era-correct hip-hop character) or a problem depending on the goal; off-center panning specifically means downmixing to mono or accepting the imbalance — decide on purpose rather than by accident.

**Kicks**
- **Think of a kick in two (or three) frequency components:** a low/punchy/subby/fat component and a high/click/top component (some producers add a third mid layer). The best single samples nail both; if not, some producers layer a preferred bottom sample with a preferred top sample on two Drum Rack cells assigned to the same key — Max notes this is trickier than it sounds and has had more misfires than wins doing it this way; he generally prefers finding one sample that already works over stitching two together.
- **Watch for unwanted pitch in the kick** — a strong pitched fundamental in the kick can clash/interfere with the bassline; shortening the kick (cutting its tail) tightens it and reduces the audible pitch, at the cost of low-end weight — decide the tradeoff based on how much low end the song's bass is already carrying.
- **Keep multiple kick variants** — e.g. one heavier kick for the downbeat and a tighter secondary kick for other hits — rather than one kick used everywhere.
- **The "duplicate and modify" soft/hard kick pair (his central kick technique):** duplicate the main kick, pitch the copy up ~1 semitone, and pull a low-pass filter down to remove its highs — this softer, darker copy sits at a lower velocity right before the main (unmodified) hit, e.g. "d'Doom." The idea is to emulate a real drummer's physical motion of hitting softer then harder to build into the accented hit — timing, pitch, filtering, and velocity all working together to create forward momentum into the heavier hit. This directly avoids the "machine-gunning" problem: if the *exact same sample* plays repeatedly, the ear can tell it's a loop, not a real instrument being hit multiple times — occasionally desirable as an effect, but the enemy of realism otherwise.

**Snares**
- **Same two-component thinking as kicks:** a high/bright/crack component and a low/thud/body component; the balance between them defines the snare's whole character (poppy-thuddy vs. bright-electronic vs. trashy-metallic vs. tight vs. reggae/dubstep-style, etc.). Can also be layered from two samples (highs from one, lows from another) the same way as kicks.
- **Snares don't have to be actual snare samples** — hand drums, metallic percussion, anything with a contrasting-high character against the kick's low works, "as long as the two things are contrasting to some degree."
- **Multiple snare articulations, each its OWN sample (not the same sample at different volumes):**
  - **Main hit** — the primary, full-velocity snare.
  - **Ghost-note snare** — a completely different sample, pitched down, high-passed, and re-shaped with a custom volume envelope to sit as a soft, syncopated accent distinct from the main hit — not just the main snare played quieter.
  - **Off/secondary snare** — panned slightly to one side, often a different tonal quality (deeper, or a different character entirely) — used as an accent, mirroring how many modern drummers set up a second snare off to the side of their main one.
  - **Rimshot/side-stick** — the wood-on-rim sound, used either as an accent hit or as a full substitute for the snare during a song's softer/quieter sections, exactly as a real drummer would swap to rim clicks.

**Hi-hats**
- **Four distinct samples by velocity tier — small / medium / big (semi-open) / open — not one sample scaled by velocity.** This is the direct fix for the machine-gunning problem on hats specifically: a single hat sample repeated at varying volume still reads as an obviously repeated sample; separate samples per articulation reads as a real player varying their strike.
- **The medium hat is the main/default hit; the small hat covers offbeat/lighter hits; the big (semi-open) hat reads like "taking a breath" — used at natural pause points in a phrase; the fully open hat is reserved for specific moments** (e.g. cueing a drum fill by leaving space for the toms to enter).
- **Match the different hat samples' EQ/pitch/panning to each other** so despite being different samples they read as one instrument being hit differently, not four unrelated sounds glued together — Max does light EQ, volume-envelope, and pitch adjustments across his four hats specifically for this, and pans them all to the same spot.
- **Choke groups.** Chain all of an instrument's articulations together (all hi-hats, both kicks, all snares) into a mute/choke group where a new hit on any member cuts off the others' ringing tails — mimicking the physical fact that a real kit can't have two conflicting hits (e.g. an open and closed hi-hat) ringing simultaneously.

**Percussion — stereo clap trick**
- **A 4-sample randomized "cycle" (round-robin) per key** avoids obvious clap repetition — four similar claps assigned to one note, one triggers at random each time that note fires.
- **Two keys, offset for stereo width without phase issues:** the same 4-sample cycle is duplicated onto a second key; one set is pitched up slightly and has its start times nudged so the waveforms no longer line up with the other set; the two sets are panned hard opposite sides. Both keys get triggered together on every snare hit, so both sides play a randomly-chosen (and now non-identical) clap sample simultaneously — creating stereo width and a bit of crunch under the snare without risking comb-filtering/phase cancellation that would occur if the *exact same* sample happened to play on both sides at once by chance.

**Toms**
- Custom toms don't have to come from your sampler's raw library — Max pulls toms from a fully-detailed acoustic drum plugin (Addictive Drums), processes them the way he likes, bounces them to audio, and loads the results into Battery as his personal go-to tom set going forward.

**Cymbals (crash + ride)**
- **Layer a crash and a ride together** rather than using either alone; pan them differently (ride slightly off-center, crash fuller/more central-stereo) so they read as one cohesive cymbal event with some spatial separation.
- **Cut/trim the very start of a cymbal sample and rebuild the attack with a custom volume envelope (attack pulled up)** instead of using the sample's raw hard transient at note-on — Max prefers a soft swell like "a flourish coming off a kick drum" rather than the harsh stick-crack most raw cymbal samples start with.
- **Heavy high-pass on cymbals** to clear low-mid room for the rest of the kit while the cymbal rings out on top.
- **Build your own custom volume envelope whenever a sample's natural decay is cut off or unusable** — a recurring fix across kicks, snares, and especially cymbals in this kit.
