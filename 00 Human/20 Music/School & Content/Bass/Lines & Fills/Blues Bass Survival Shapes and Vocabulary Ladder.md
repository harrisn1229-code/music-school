# Blues Bass Survival Shapes and Vocabulary Ladder

BassBuzz's five-level answer to "how do I actually play blues on bass," structured as a strict vocabulary ladder rather than a grab-bag of licks: two movable neck **shapes** that locate the I/IV/V of any 12-bar blues in any key without theory, then four layers of melodic vocabulary (root notes → a pentatonic "box" → triad-derived scale runs → chromatic connector patterns) stacked on top of that shape in order of how "spicy" they sound, and finally a **named three-step method for fusing all four layers into real-time improvisation** — the piece the video's host says he's "never heard someone actually break down" elsewhere. This is the bass-fretboard-mechanics companion to [[Blues Form and Harmony]] and [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]] (both built from jazz-guitar reharmonization material, a different register/audience than this beginner-facing video) and a direct sibling of [[Bass Scale Shapes — Major, Minor, Pentatonic & Blues]] and [[Bass Pentatonic Riff-Writing Recipe]] — same channel, same shape-first teaching model, applied specifically to blues. **Not to be confused with** [[The Five-Tier Bass Theory Curriculum]], a *different* BassBuzz "five levels" video (theory-dose-by-goal, not blues-specific) — the two share a numbering gimmick and nothing else.

## Level 1 — Survival basics: the 12-bar form + two fretboard shapes + shuffle

The fastest path to "not embarrassing yourself at a blues jam," in three pieces:

**1. The 12-bar form.** Three chords — I, IV, V — in the standard `I I I I / IV IV I I / V IV I V` layout (12 bars, four beats each); whatever's in those bars repeats for the whole song. Full theory (why every chord is a dominant 7th, the quick-change and minor-blues variants, call-and-response phrasing) lives on [[Blues Form and Harmony]] — this video deliberately skips that theory to get a beginner playing fast, and points its own viewers to a companion PDF for the deeper version.

**2. Blues Survival Shapes — two movable triangle shapes that locate the IV and V from the I, with zero key/interval knowledge required.** Pick any root note anywhere on the neck to be the "1" (the song's key) and the same shape gives you the 4 and 5 relative to it, transposable to any key by just moving the starting fret. Verified against standard EADG bass tuning below — moving to the adjacent higher string at the same fret is a perfect fourth up, which is exactly the interval from I to IV, so both shapes are just physical restatements of real 1-4-5 harmony, not an arbitrary mnemonic.

- **High shape:** root note anywhere → **the 4 is the same fret, one string up (toward the G string)** → **the 5 is two frets further up the neck, same string as the 4.** Worked example straight from the video: root **A♭, 4th fret of the E string** → 4 = **D♭, 4th fret of the A string** (same fret, string up) → 5 = **E♭, 6th fret of the A string** (two frets further, same string). Shape: 1 and 4 share a fret; 5 sits two frets past the 4.
- **Low shape — the mirror image of the high shape.** Root note anywhere → **the 4 is two frets back and one string down (toward the E string)** → **the 5 is two frets further up the neck from the 4, on that same lower string — landing back on the exact same fret the root was on.** (Not given on camera in *this* video — reconstructed here from its "flip the triangle around" description and confirmed against the same interval math as the high shape. **Confirmed with exact frets by the earlier, origin video for this whole method**, [[Beginner Blues Bass "Survival Shapes” (Simple Method for 12 Bar Blues)]]: blues in C, root **C (3rd fret A string)** → 4 = **F (1st fret E string)**, down a string/down two frets → 5 = **G (3rd fret E string)**, up two frets from the 4 — landing back on the root's own fret, one string down, exactly as reconstructed here. A second worked key (F#, 4th fret D string) in that same video confirms the shape transposes.) The useful mnemonic that falls out of it: in the low shape, **the 1 and the 5 share a fret** — the string-down neighbor of wherever the root sits — while in the high shape it's **the 1 and the 4** that share a fret. That's the actual "flip."
- **Both shapes work in any key, anywhere on the neck** — memorize the shape, not a set of frets.

**3. The shuffle feel.** Most (not all) blues is played as a **shuffle** — an uneven, "long-short" triplet subdivision (a triplet with the middle note removed) instead of a straight, evenly-divided 4/4 — described in the video as "lumpy," like marching with a broken foot. Same fact as [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *Feel* and [[RHY-C03 — Swing is a triplet with the middle note gone]]; the beginner-level tell given here is purely aural: **listen for "the limp"** in the guitar, drummer's hi-hat, or ride cymbal, and match it. Not every blues shuffles — a straight-eighths blues reads as rock — so the ear check matters more than assuming.

**Worked reference for all three pieces together:** Tommy Shannon's bass part on "Blues Makes Me Feel So Good" (Albert Cummings) — shuffled root notes only, no embellishment — cited as "super legit" specifically *because* it's Tommy Shannon (Johnny Winter's early rhythm section, then Stevie Ray Vaughan's Double Trouble) playing pure fundamentals, not a beginner simplification. Shannon reappears as the through-line reference for every level up through Level 5 below.

## Level 2 — Common patterns: riff-copying and the Blues Box

**Riff-style blues** — the bassist just doubles a cool guitar riff, relocated to whichever of the I/IV/V chord it's currently sitting under. Reference: "Green Onions" (Booker T & the MGs) — a four-note riff played on I, then transposed and repeated on IV, then V.

**The Blues Box** — a single movable pentatonic/blues-scale shape (the video doesn't give exact fretting on camera — visual-only demonstration, described only as "a simple shape that is physically incapable of producing anything but cool riffs") that, started fresh from each of the three [[#Level 1 — Survival basics: the 12-bar form + two fretboard shapes + shuffle|Level 1 survival-shape]] roots (1, 4, 5), generates idiomatic blues bass ideas without needing new material per chord. This is functionally the same **minor pentatonic / blues-scale box** already fully fretted and fingered on [[Bass Scale Shapes — Major, Minor, Pentatonic & Blues]] § *Minor pentatonic scale* / § *Blues scale* — this video's real addition is the **application pattern**: don't just drill the box once, restart it from I, then IV, then V as the form moves, using the [[#Level 1 — Survival basics: the 12-bar form + two fretboard shapes + shuffle|survival shapes]] to find each new starting fret. Also confirmed genre-general (rock, funk — "the rock rectangle," "the funk fa cube" — same shape, different name depending on who's teaching it) and traceable to a named source: Tommy Shannon himself taught this shape in his own "Hot Licks" instructional DVD. A second worked Blues Box pattern (over Eric Clapton's version of "I'm Tore Down") is referenced as living in the video's companion PDF, not demonstrated in the captions.

## Level 3 — Spicier patterns: triad-derived scale runs

Requires a little scale/triad theory (a triad = the 1st, 3rd, and 5th of the major or minor scale — full formula: [[Scales]]), but the video's own fallback is to just memorize the finger shapes without the theory if needed. Three named patterns, escalating in note count, each pinned to a real recording:

- **1-3-5 (the plain triad).** Reference: Elvis Presley's version of "Hound Dog."
- **1-3-5-6 ("root third fifth sixth").** Credited to **John Deacon** (Queen) on "Crazy Little Thing Called Love" — the same song already logged as a major-pentatonic-flavor reference on [[Bass Scale Shapes — Major, Minor, Pentatonic & Blues]] § *Major pentatonic scale*, now confirmed as specifically a **1-3-5-6** bassline pattern rather than just "major pentatonic in general."
- **1-3-5-6-7-6-5-3 (the fullest of the three — up through the 6 and 7, then walked back down through 6-5-3).** Cited in three separate recordings: "Ice Cream Man" (Van Halen), "Blue Suede Shoes" (Elvis Presley), and what the captions render as **"Boot Mill Blues" by Gary Hoey** — near-certainly a caption mishearing of **"Boot Hill Blues"** (a genuine Gary Hoey blues instrumental); flagged here as a probable transcription error rather than corrected outright, since the title wasn't independently verified against Hoey's catalog.

This same **1-3-5-6-7-6-5-3** shape also doubles as one of the two named "main approach" foundations in the Level 5 method below (paired there with the Blues Box as the two things a bassist alternates between across transition bars).

## Level 4 — Chromaticism: two named walkup patterns

**The general move:** insert a note *outside* the current scale, deliberately, to build tension, then resolve it — "cruising along on the scale, then... a crazy note... then you resolve it." Two specific, named shapes cover most of what shows up in real blues basslines:

**Pattern 1 — the three-fret walkup.** Find a target note (usually the root or the 5th of the *next* chord) and climb chromatically up to it over three frets, arriving exactly as the new chord lands. Framed as a bass-playing-wide device, not blues-exclusive — name-checked in "Chameleon" and "Smoke on the Water" as familiar reference points (not worked as full lessons here) — with two blues-specific worked instances:
- **Walkup to the 5th:** "Killing Floor" (Howlin' Wolf) — a three-fret chromatic climb to the V chord's root. Directly noted as the same line **Led Zeppelin lifted uncredited into "The Lemon Song."**
- **Walkup to the root/octave:** "Tush" (ZZ Top, bass — Dusty Hill) — used specifically to walk back from the IV chord to the I chord.

**Pattern 2 — the two-fret walkup, specifically a turnaround device.** Placed in the **last four bars of the 12-bar form** (the turnaround — see [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *Turnarounds* and [[Turnarounds]] for the general theory) as a quick chromatic lead-in to the V chord that closes the form. Same song, same player as Pattern 1's second example: **Dusty Hill's turnaround in "Tush"** carries both walkup patterns in one tune — the three-fret IV→I walkup earlier in the form, and this two-fret walkup into V at the very end. This is the beginner-fretboard-mechanics counterpart to [[Chromatic Bass Lines]]'s harmony-level line-cliché material — same "walk chromatically toward a target" instinct, taught here as a movable fret-count shape rather than a named chord progression.

## Level 5 — Off-the-cuff mastery: the three-step fusion method

The video's own headline claim: a concrete, sequenced answer to "how do you actually get from knowing all this vocabulary to freely improvising with it," which the host says most teaching just skips (**"throw you a bunch of s**t, and then you have to put it all together yourself"**) — presented as the missing piece between vocabulary and real improvisation, the same underlying problem [[Goal-Tiered Practice and the Self-Taught Bass Commandments]] solves for practice planning generally (pick the right material for where you actually are, rather than being handed everything at once with no sequencing).

**Step 1 — Drill Levels 1–4 individually, in order, over a real 12-bar backing track (the video's own play-along, key of E).** Multiple full passes through the form at each level before advancing: Level 1 (shuffle root notes only, using the survival shapes to track I/IV/V), then Level 2 (Blues Box ideas), then Level 3 (scale/triad patterns — 1-3-5-6, or the fuller 1-3-5-6-7-6-5-3), then Level 4 (chromatic walkups). No fixed time given for this step beyond "multiple runs through the form" per level.

**Step 2 — "Transition on the transitions."** Pick **one** approach as the main bassline for most of the form (e.g., the Blues Box), then on the **transition bar** — the single bar right before a chord change — swap in a *different* approach as a fill that leads into the new chord. Two worked examples given: (a) Blues Box for three bars, then a chromatic three-fret walkup on the transition bar (bar 4, leading into the IV chord); (b) a 1-3-5-3 triad as the main foundation, with the Blues Box swapped in as the transition-bar variation instead. **Explicitly recommended at least a month of practice** at this step before moving on — "don't skimp."

**Step 3 — Freely improvise.** Mix all four vocabulary layers bar-to-bar, spur-of-the-moment, with no planned pattern — "one bar might be a scale thing, and then it's Blues Box, and then it's chromatic." **Start slow and build up tempo**, and explicitly build on top of Steps 1–2 rather than skipping to this step cold, which is named as the mistake most teachers implicitly encourage by not laying out Steps 1–2 at all. Capstone reference: **Tommy Shannon's playing on "Pride and Joy" (Stevie Ray Vaughan)** — cited as the sound this whole ladder is building toward, combining shuffled root notes, chromaticism, and scale patterns "and a couple other cool tricks" in one performance.
