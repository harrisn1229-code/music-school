# Upper structure triads for comping and soloing

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Cbo6tCKEZJY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Cbo6tCKEZJY)

## Synopsis

A short, dense jazz-guitar lesson from Jack Ruch on deriving **upper structure triads** — the triad
hiding inside a seventh chord's 3rd, 5th, and 7th — and using those triads both to simplify comping over
a moving jazz progression and as a target-note map for soloing. The whole lesson is worked over the
seven chords of **Autumn Leaves** (C minor 7, F dominant 7, B♭ major 7, E♭ major 7, A minor 7 flat 5,
D7, G minor 7), which conveniently touches all four common seventh-chord qualities, so it functions as
a mini-reference for "which triad quality sits on the 3-5-7 of each seventh-chord type." The pedagogical
throughline connects directly to Ruch's separate "triads as a fretboard map" lesson (already in the
vault as [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]]): the payoff of learning the upper-structure triad for a chord is
worthless without already knowing that triad's shape in all three fretboard zones, so the two lessons
are meant to be used together — this video supplies the harmonic *what*, the fretboard-map lesson
supplies the physical *where*. Comping and soloing are treated as the same skill at two different
speeds: comping strings the shapes together as chords on one string set, soloing uses the same shapes'
individual notes as a target map for single-note lines.

## What's taught

- **The core derivation — upper structure triad = the top three notes of a seventh chord.** A plain
  triad is root-3rd-5th; add the 7th and the *upper* three notes (3rd-5th-7th) form a new triad in their
  own right, one that's playable on its own as a substitute/simplification for the full seventh chord.
  Worked first on **C minor 7** (C-E♭-G-B♭): the 3rd-5th-7th stack is E♭-G-B♭ — an **E♭ major triad** —
  so anywhere E♭ major is already known on the neck, it can be played straight over Cm7. *(00:00–01:30
  approx.)*
- **The four upper-structure-triad-quality rules, one per seventh-chord type, worked across all seven
  Autumn Leaves chords:**
  - **Minor 7th chord → major triad a minor 3rd above the root.** Cm7 → E♭ major (worked example
    above); Gm7 (last chord of the progression) → B♭ major, by the same rule.
  - **Dominant 7th chord → diminished triad a major 3rd above the root.** F7 → the upper-structure
    triad off F7's 3rd-5th-♭7th is **A diminished**; D7 (later in the progression) → **F♯ diminished**,
    same rule applied a fourth away.
  - **Major 7th chord → minor triad a major 3rd above the root.** B♭maj7 → **D minor** (visible sitting
    right above the B♭ bass note in the full chord voicing); E♭maj7 → **G minor**, same rule.
  - **Minor 7 flat 5 (half-diminished) chord → minor triad a minor 3rd above the root.** Am7♭5 → **C
    minor**, visible directly above the A bass note in the chord voicing.
  - *(general note, not stated as a rule in the video but implied by the four worked examples: every
    upper-structure triad quality is fixed by seventh-chord quality alone, independent of key — once
    the four mappings above are memorized they transfer to any tune.)* → cross-check against
    [[Seventh Chords]] and [[Guide Tones]] (3rd/7th = the two notes that define a 7th chord's quality;
    the upper-structure triad is built from exactly those two notes plus the 5th).
- **Full worked chord-to-triad table for Autumn Leaves** (the progression as taught):

  | Chord | Seventh-chord quality | Upper structure triad (3-5-7) |
  |---|---|---|
  | C minor 7 | minor 7 | E♭ major |
  | F dominant 7 | dominant 7 | A diminished |
  | B♭ major 7 | major 7 | D minor |
  | E♭ major 7 | major 7 | G minor |
  | A minor 7♭5 | half-diminished | C minor |
  | D7 | dominant 7 | F♯ diminished |
  | G minor 7 | minor 7 | B♭ major |

- **Mapping the triad across the fretboard is the prerequisite step.** Because a triad is only three
  notes, it repeats in **three close-position shapes** across the neck, broken down set-by-set across
  string trios — same "three usable zones" system as [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]] (that video works the
  concept in E major for blues soloing; this one applies the identical positional logic to each of the
  seven upper-structure triads above, jazz-standard context instead of blues). The lesson explicitly
  frames "know your triads all over the neck" as the unlock that makes the rest of the lesson usable —
  without it, knowing that Cm7's upper structure is E♭ major is a piece of trivia, not a playable idea.
- **Comping application — string the upper-structure triads together on one string set.** Demonstrated
  restricted to the **D-G-B string set** (the "upper middle" set) for a first pass through all seven
  changes, playing just the relevant upper-structure triad — E♭ major over Cm7, A diminished over F7,
  D minor over B♭maj7, and so on — as a comping voicing in place of the full seventh-chord grip. A
  second demonstrated pass moves around more freely, switching between fretboard zones and string sets
  chord to chord rather than staying pinned to one set, to show what it sounds like once the shapes are
  comfortable enough to relocate on the fly. *(comping section, roughly 02:30–05:30)*
- **Soloing application — same triads as a chord-tone target map for single-note lines.** The
  upper-structure triad shapes double as guide tones for improvised lines: a basic solo demoed on the
  same D-G-B string set outlines each chord almost entirely from its upper-structure triad's three
  notes, chord to chord through the progression — directly the same "use triad tones to outline the
  sound of each chord" idea as [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]'s Step 4 (outline the chord tones of every chord),
  narrowed here specifically to the upper-structure triad rather than the full four-note seventh chord.
  → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]], [[Guide Tones]].
- **Building toward a fuller solo — add connective notes and move across zones/string sets.** The
  video's own intro solo (played cold at the top, before the lesson) is described as built from the same
  upper-structure-triad vocabulary but with more movement across fretboard positions and string sets,
  plus "extra notes added to the triads to glue everything together" — i.e., the triad tones are the
  skeleton, not the whole vocabulary; passing/connecting tones get layered on once the skeleton is
  solid. The full transcription of that intro solo is gated behind the creator's Patreon — not available
  in the free lesson, flagged here rather than fabricated.
- **Practice sequencing implied by the lesson's own structure:** (1) derive the upper-structure triad
  for each chord in the progression using the four quality rules above, (2) map each of those triads
  across the three fretboard zones (lean on [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]] for the positional method),
  (3) comp through the progression on one string set first, then vary string sets/zones, (4) solo using
  the same triads as a target map, single string set first, then with movement and connective tones
  added.
