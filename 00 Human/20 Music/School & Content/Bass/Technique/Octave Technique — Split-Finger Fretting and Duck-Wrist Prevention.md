# Octave Technique — Split-Finger Fretting and Duck-Wrist Prevention

BassBuzz's fix for the specific failure mode octaves expose that flatter fretboard patterns don't: jumping quickly between an octave's low note and high note (adjacent strings, two frets apart) tends to bend the fretting wrist sharply — nicknamed **"duck wrist"** ("quack, quack, quack") — which hurts, and which also throws off the plucking hand, which ends up scrambling to keep pace with a fretting hand that's fighting its own position. Named as a beginner blind spot specifically because it "sneaks up on you" — technique can look and feel solid on simpler patterns right up until an octave line exposes it. Two separate fixes are given: an ergonomic setup fix for the wrist bend itself, and a fretting-hand mechanic (split-finger technique) for the speed/stumbling problem underneath it.

## Duck-wrist fix — ergonomic setup

Explicitly stated to work **even with small hands** — this is a setup adjustment, not a stretch you have to grow into:
- **Raise the strap** so the neck isn't hanging too low — a too-low neck forces the wrist into a sharper bend to reach the fretboard at all.
- **Tilt the neck up further**, beyond just raising the strap, for the same reason.
- **Add a bit more curl/bend in the fingers themselves** so the wrist can stay straighter overall — trading finger curl for wrist angle, rather than eliminating the bend entirely.

**Case study:** a BassBuzz forum student ("Sarah") is shown with visibly reduced wrist bend after a couple of weeks of practicing with this setup, compared to a clearly-bent "before" clip — offered as evidence the fix is trainable, not just a one-time equipment tweak. *(Practice slow motion through the octave shapes first, without worrying about plucking speed, to confirm the wrist itself is no longer bending sharply before adding the split-finger technique below on top.)*

## Split-finger technique — the speed fix

**The default hand behavior that breaks down on octaves:** normally, the index and middle fretting fingers move together as a unit — crossing strings together, wrist and hand traveling as one connected shape. That's fine for patterns where the two fingers land close together, but on a fast low-string/high-string octave jump it means relocating the *entire* hand shape for every single jump, which is what causes the stumbling.

**The fix: split the fingers apart instead of moving them as a unit.** Anchor the **index finger near the low string** and the **middle finger near the high string**, and leave them there rather than sliding the whole hand back and forth between jumps — each finger stays parked at its own string, so an octave jump becomes a matter of *which* anchored finger frets next, not a full hand relocation. This is named directly as "the secret to getting speed and non-stumbles when you're jumping low, high, low, high on octaves."

**When to split vs. when not to — worked against three octave note-patterns:**
- **Low, high, low, low** — split for the first two notes (the actual jump), then **un-split** (fingers return to moving together) for the trailing low-low pair, since there's no jump left to protect against.
- **Low, high, low, high** — split for **all four** notes; every note alternates register, so the anchored-finger setup is needed the whole way through.
- **Low, low, high, high** — **no split needed at all**, since the notes are doubled up in same-register pairs rather than alternating — the hand can move as a unit here without any stumbling risk.

The pattern-by-pattern breakdown matters more than the mechanic alone: split-finger isn't a blanket "always do this on octaves" rule, it's specifically for the jump itself — apply it only where an actual low/high alternation occurs in the line.
