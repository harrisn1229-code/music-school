# Play Faster Without Trying Harder!? Barney Kessel's Secret Method

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7FOUGbs8X-8)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7FOUGbs8X-8)

## Synopsis
Nathan Borton reverse-engineers a fast, fluid line from Barney Kessel's solo on "Mean to Me," from the Poll Winners' debut record, and shows that the speed doesn't come from an exotic picking system — it comes from **sweep picking a small set of arpeggio shapes** (a minor 9 and a minor 7 shape, each playable both ascending and descending, plus two one-direction shapes) and, critically, from treating those shapes as **flexible, movable visual patterns on the neck rather than fixed to the one chord they were first learned over**. The lesson works bottom-up: first get the four shapes and their single-note-per-string sweep motion comfortable in isolation, then apply them — moved chromatically through a ii-V-I, led into from a scale, combined with each other for guide-tone-style top-note motion, and deliberately reharmonized over chords other than the ones Kessel originally played them over (including landing a shape's start note on the altered ♭9 of a dominant chord for an "altered" sound). The channel offers guitar-pro profiles and a bonus etude on Patreon (external, not something to route further into the vault).

## What's taught

### The four sweep-picking shapes (from Kessel's "Mean to Me" solo)
- **Shape 1 — minor 9 arpeggio, bidirectional.** Ascending pick pattern: up–down–down–down–down–up (an added note before/after the core arpeggio shape). Descending (reverse): down–up–up–up–up–down. Played with a single continuous sweeping motion through the strings, "just like if I was to play a chord" — one pick stroke direction carries through multiple strings instead of alternate-picking each note.
- **Shape 2 — minor 7 arpeggio, bidirectional.** Ascending: down–up–up–up. Descending: down–down–down–up. Also usable both ascending and descending, like Shape 1.
- **Shape 3 — one direction only: descending.**
- **Shape 4 — one direction only: ascending.**
- Kessel uses all four shapes within the single "Mean to Me" phrase that sparked this lesson.
- **First practice step:** get comfortable with the shapes and the single-note-per-string sweep motion in isolation before applying them — practice slowly, focusing on a smooth, connected sound through the sweep in either direction *(general starting point — video doesn't give a metronome number)*.

### Applying the shapes for speed without extra effort
- **Move a shape chromatically through a static harmonic pattern.** Shape 1 moved chromatically through a **ii-V-I (251)** produces a fast, smooth line "without a ton of effort" — same treatment repeated with Shape 2 through the same 251. The chromatic sweep-and-slide motion, not picking speed, is what produces the sense of velocity.
- **Lead into a sweep shape from a scale.** Worked in the key of F major over **Gm7 → C7 → Fmaj7** (a major 251): walk up the **G Dorian** scale, then link directly into Shape 1 played over the C7, resolving into Fmaj7 — the scale run and the swept arpeggio connect seamlessly into one line. Ties to this vault's existing scale-into-arpeggio connecting logic on [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]] and the ii-V target on [[The ii-V-I]].
- **Combine shapes within one line for rhythmic variety.** Following an ascending scale-into-Shape-1 line, add in Shape 4 (also ascending) to keep the ascending motion going but vary the rhythmic feel — demonstrates that a line built from "just" a Dorian scale plus swept arpeggio shapes can sound far more complex than its individual parts.

### Guide-tone-style top-note connections between shapes
- **Chain two sweep shapes together by deliberately choosing which chord tone each one starts/lands on, so the TOP notes move by step** — the sweep-picking equivalent of a guide-tone line (see [[Guide Tones]]), but tracking the top note of each arpeggio shape rather than the 3rd/7th of each chord. Worked example: start with Shape 1 descending over a **Gm** chord, then play Shape 2 descending but **deliberately start it on A♭ as the top note** — creating a chromatic half-step connection between the two shapes' top notes — then close the line with one of Kessel's original major sweep phrases. The takeaway: the connecting logic is chosen at the top of the shape, not just wherever the shape happens to start by default.

### Shapes as flexible patterns, not fixed to their original chord (the core secret)
- **The central insight of the lesson:** although each shape is technically derived from a specific arpeggio (minor 9, minor 7), Kessel uses the same visual/fretboard *pattern* over chords the arpeggio doesn't strictly belong to — thinking of them as movable shapes, not chord-locked licks, is what makes them versatile across many harmonic situations.
- **Reharmonizing Shape 3 over an unrelated dominant chord.** Kessel originally played Shape 3 over an **E♭7** in "Mean to Me," but the same shape works effectively played over a **C7** instead — a direct demonstration that the shape isn't tied to the chord it was first learned on.
- **Using a shape's starting degree to target an altered-dominant sound.** Starting Shape 3 on the **♭9 of a C dominant 7 chord (D♭)** rather than its "home" starting note produces a strong **altered-dominant** color — explicit cross-reference to [[Altered Chords]] and the same "borrowed/reharmonized arpeggio over an altered dominant" logic already documented via Jens Larsen on [[Scale Modes Reference]] § *From videos*. Same harmonic-substitution move already applied to Shape 1 earlier in the lesson.
- **Stated generalization:** "just because you first learn to sweep a G minor 9 shape doesn't mean it's the only way to play that shape" — actively experiment for other harmonic contexts where a familiar shape sounds interesting, rather than filing it under one chord quality permanently.

### Combining shapes into longer, fretboard-spanning lines
- **Splice two different sweep shapes together to cover more of the neck in one continuous phrase.** Worked example: Shape 1 combined with a second sweep pattern lifted from the tail end of Kessel's original "Mean to Me" phrase — the combined phrase covers noticeably more fretboard than either shape alone and, played cleanly, "feels great and sounds fantastic."
- **Picking-motion continuity is the hard requirement when splicing shapes:** the sweep-picking direction/motion must line up correctly across the seam between the two shapes, or the combined phrase won't feel smooth.
- **The combined phrase is reversible** — it can be played backward (descending) as well as the original ascending order.
- **Full assembled 251 line (the lesson's closing demonstration):** open with the combined multi-shape phrase from above, use Shape 1 (as taught earlier) to transition smoothly from the V chord into the I chord, and close with the signature major "tag" phrase from Kessel's original line — stitching the whole lesson's vocabulary (combined phrase → single-shape ii-V-I transition → closing tag) into one continuous fast line that "covers a lot of ground on the fretboard" and, with practice, can be played very fast with surprisingly little conscious effort.
