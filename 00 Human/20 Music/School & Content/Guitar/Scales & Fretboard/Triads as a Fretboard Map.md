# Triads as a Fretboard Map

Part of [[Guitar Positions]]. Where [[Chord Voicings]] and [[Chord Inversions]] use close-position triad shapes to **comp** through a progression, this page uses the *same underlying triad shapes* as a **soloing** map — landmarks for finding where an equivalent phrase lives when you move it to a different part of the neck. The core problem it solves: a lick learned by ear off a record usually only lives in the one spot/string-set/key you learned it in — this is a way to generalize it across the whole fretboard on purpose.

## The core idea: three triad shapes = three usable neck positions

Any major triad (root, 3rd, 5th) repeats across the neck in **three close-position shapes**, and each shape anchors a playable 4–5 fret zone. Worked in **E major** (E, G♯, B): on the high E string alone, the three notes fall at **open (root), fret 4 (3rd), fret 7 (5th)**, then the pattern repeats an octave up starting at fret 12 — that ladder is the skeleton. Applied across a full string set instead of one string, the same three notes reshuffle into different **inversions** (3rd-5th-root, 5th-root-3rd, root-3rd-5th) depending on where on the neck you grab them, and those inversion points cluster into three usable zones:

| Zone | Roughly | Maps to |
|---|---|---|
| Open-position triads | frets 0–4 | [[Position 01\|Position 1 (open)]] |
| 5th-fret triads | frets 5–8 | [[Position 05\|Position 5]] |
| 9th-fret triads | frets 9–12 | [[Position 09\|Position 9]] |

Each zone is one physical grip of the E-G♯-B triad in a different inversion/string-set — the same three notes, relocated. Knowing where those three shapes sit turns "the fretboard" into three known landmarks instead of an undifferentiated grid.

## The technique: transpose the *lick*, not just the shape

Take one short phrase — built from ear-vocabulary, not derived from running a scale — and relearn it, note-for-note in **relative** terms (same scale degrees, same rhythm), starting from each of the three triad zones in turn. Worked example: a 12-bar blues shuffle solo in E, one lick per zone —

- **Open position, over the I chord (E, bars 1–4):** top-two-strings lick B→C♯→E→G (5th→6th→root→♭3rd of E), repeated twice; then open E → G → C♯ → open E, sliding up to E on the B string's 5th fret; then on the G string, open G→A hammered to G♯ (the 3rd of E), with a C♯+A double stop.
- **Same zone, over the IV chord (A7, bars 5–8):** stays in the same physical spot but reframes around A (the new root) and G (A7's ♭7), E down to D and back to E; slides up to B (E major's 5th) then the root; more upstroke work on the top two strings; a D-down-to-open-B pull-off, then A, then G hammered to G♯.
- **Same zone, over the V chord (B, bars 9–12) and turnaround:** centers on open B (the root of the V chord) — B up to D with a bend, open E up to F♯, F pulled off to E, back through A/D to open B — then back to the IV chord's 3rd (C♯) — then a classic open-E blues lick (B, D, open E — the 5th, ♭7 and root of E7) walking chromatically up into the root of the V chord on the turnaround.
- **Repeat the exact same relative-degree phrase starting from the 5th-fret triad zone, then again from the 9th-fret zone** — same note-to-note relationships (5th→6th→root→♭3rd, etc.), physically relocated. No new phrase to invent; only the fretting hand's starting point changes.

**Why this is worth doing rather than just playing the lick where it's easy:** each zone forces a different fingering (no open strings once you're above the nut, different pull-offs/hammer-ons, different string for the same interval), and that physical difference genuinely changes the sound — moving off open strings onto the B/G strings in the middle zone reads "sweeter and less aggressive" than the same phrase down low, purely from the change in string and hand position, not a different note choice.

## Vocabulary comes from records, not scale-running

The source lick isn't derived from "playing the scale" — it's assembled from **blues phrasing vocabulary** picked up by ear from listening, the same way the ♭3-friction sound in [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]] is a *feel*, not a formula. Named source listening: early **Freddie King**, **Otis Rush**, and (per the video, phonetically close to "Hu-Suin" in the auto-captions) likely **Hubert Sumlin** for this lesson's own influences, plus **Albert Collins** and **Albert King** cited as artists the technique's author learned isolated one-off licks from as a beginner — each artist's licks were originally locked to one key/string-set/position until deliberately relearned in other zones using this triad-position method.

## Tone follow-through: switch pickups to match the zone

When the lick relocates to a lower-string-heavy zone (the 9th-fret zone here, which sits more on the D/G strings), the tone gets muddier just from being on thicker strings — the fix demonstrated is switching to the **bridge pickup** for that zone specifically, to keep articulation bright rather than compensating with EQ after the fact. A live-performance tone decision made per-position, not a fixed pickup setting for the whole solo.

## Building a full solo: choruses ascend through the zones

Once each zone's version of the phrase is solid on its own, two ways to combine them: (1) **jump between zones mid-phrase**, freely, within a single chorus, connecting fragments from different zones as convenient; or (2) **play one full chorus of the 12-bar form per zone**, ascending — start the first chorus in open position, move to the 5th-fret zone for the second, the 9th-fret zone for the third — adding natural variation each time rather than replaying the seed phrase verbatim. The second approach (start low, end high, one zone per chorus) is the one demonstrated as the lesson's closing full solo.
