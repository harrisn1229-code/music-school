# 10 Songs That Taught Me Punk (Easy to Effin’ Hard)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gRv2RiOWQsM)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gRv2RiOWQsM)

## Synopsis

BassBuzz's Josh runs a personal, chronological "how I learned punk bass" countdown — ten songs ordered easy-to-brutally-hard, each one picked because it taught him a specific real skill, not because it's a generic genre landmark. The throughline is explicitly anti-dogmatic: punk bass is framed repeatedly as "no rules" — weird note choices that ignore scale theory, fingerstyle tone that's "wrong for the style" on purpose, chords played where a bassist "shouldn't," a singer who (per the video) just noodled a riff until it sounded right instead of consulting a mode chart. Difficulty escalates from The Ramones' three-note-but-blazing-fast simplicity up to Matt Freeman's (Rancid) borderline-unplayable "Maxwell Murder" solo, which Josh visibly struggles through live on camera at the end — an unusually candid "I'm not sure I can do this" moment for the channel. Each song contributes one concrete, transferable technique (hammer-ons/pull-offs, pick-vs-fingers tone, bass chords, melodic walking-quarter-note fills, blues-derived fills) rather than a full tab — this is a highlights/commentary video, not a note-for-note tutorial, so no song gets a complete chart. **Confidence flag:** the caption track carries no on-screen text overlays, so several song titles are inferred from context (release year, album, "recorded near where I grew up") rather than stated aloud — flagged individually below wherever inferred rather than confirmed.

## What's taught

- **[~0:00–1:23] Song 1 — The Ramones (1976 debut album; song not stated aloud — almost certainly "Blitzkrieg Bop," the album's fast three-note opener).** Dee Dee Ramone's bassline is only three notes but played blazingly fast — the lesson isn't harmonic, it's pure speed: struggling through "Blitzkrieg Bop" and "I Want to Be Sedated" pushed Josh to work through the discomfort of playing faster than felt natural, and that speed-building paid off across every other style he later played. The Ramones are named as THE tempo template The Clash, Dead Kennedys, Green Day, and Descendents all built on. → [[Hammer-Ons and Pull-Offs (Bass)]] and the Goal-Tiered Practice speed note below both trace back to this "work through the burn" framing.

- **[~1:23–3:01] Song 2 — Dead Kennedys, their first-ever recording, released 1979 (song not stated aloud — the year + "first recording" points to "California Über Alles").** The riff hooked Josh's ear with **deliberately "weird" (non-diatonic) note choices**. The video's stated theory-philosophy point: singer Jello Biafra (credited here as the riff's writer) almost certainly didn't sit down and consciously pick "E-flat locrian" — he noodled on the fretboard until something sounded good, and that anti-rule-bound approach is presented as the real lesson for a beginner intimidated by scale theory. Cross-referenced examples of the same "weird notey riff" vibe: The Melvins' "It's Shoved," Nirvana's "Milk It" (explicitly said to carry the same influence into grunge), and modern touchstone The Bobby Lees (bassist Kendall Wind) as Josh's favorite recent punk find. **Kept on this page only** — a "don't be too rule-bound, experiment on the fretboard" mindset tip rather than a settings-level technique, so it doesn't map cleanly onto an existing technique note.

- **[~3:01–4:34] Song 3 — Sex Pistols; Rolling Stone's "56th greatest song of all time" (song title not stated aloud).** The bassline everyone assumes is Sid Vicious (who "sucked at bass," per the video) is actually **Glen Matlock**, the original Pistols bassist — guitarist Steve Jones played bass on the rest of that album, not Sid. Matlock's fingerstyle playing is called out as carrying real blues/Motown vocabulary into punk, not raw unschooled playing: the fill at the end of a verse is compared directly to Duck Dunn's fill on Otis Redding's "I Can't Turn You Loose" — described as "basically the same thing," a classic blues walk-up. → [[Pick vs. Fingers on Bass — Tone and Technique Tradeoffs]] (the fingers/Motown-vocabulary side of that note).

- **[~4:34–6:19] Song 4 — Green Day; Mike Dirnt, "recorded an hour from where I grew up in the San Francisco Bay Area" (song not stated aloud — the East Bay reference and the technique described point strongly to "Longview," Dirnt's signature hammer-on/pull-off bassline).** Josh names **hammer-ons and pull-offs** directly as the technique this riff taught him: use the fretting hand to sound notes by smacking down (hammer-on) or plucking-as-you-lift (pull-off), without re-striking with the picking hand. Concrete before/after demo: the riff played with **only picked notes** sounds noticeably flatter than the same riff **with the hammer-ons/pull-offs back in** — "it just sounds cooler, and it flows a lot better," because hammered/pulled notes carry a slightly different tonal flavor than picked ones, and mixing the two textures is what creates the variety. Mike Dirnt is credited generally with keeping "melodic punk bass alive in the 90s" despite Green Day catching flak from punk purists for American Idiot-era mainstream success. → [[Hammer-Ons and Pull-Offs (Bass)]] — new technique note, full method.

- **[~6:19–7:56] Song 5 — Goldfinger, "Superman" (song title stated explicitly on-screen per the transcript's inline caption note); bassist Simon Williams.** Melodic, "tasty" playing that Josh says he still hasn't gotten sick of after years. **The core arrangement technique:** the bassline starts with Ramones-style eighth-note chugging ("rim-bim-bim-bim-bim"), then as the song builds, **simplifies down to melodic walking quarter notes** instead of getting busier — counterintuitive but explicitly called "a big part of getting that tasty punk-ska vibe." The same walking-quarter-note device is cross-referenced on Mike Dirnt's "Longview" and on Matt Freeman's playing (song 10) — a recurring punk-ska arrangement move, not a one-off. Video also uses this song as the pivot into a sponsor read for BassBuzz's own paid "Beginner to Badass" course (theory/scales taught through real songs) — noted for completeness, not routed anywhere.

- **[~7:56–9:15] Song 6 — The Clash (song not stated aloud — "big epic slides," reggae-punk hybrid, and the video's own "Clashics" wordplay point strongly to "Guns of Brixton," Paul Simonon's signature bass showcase, though this is inferred rather than confirmed).** Big, beefy slides are the headline sound. This riff is explicitly credited with pushing Josh away from all-fingers playing: **pick playing gives a more cutting tone that makes a line stand out more clearly** than fingers, and some phrases (the riff's "tweedley-dee" run) are simply **easier to execute cleanly with a pick** — it "pops and flows so nicely" that way. Framed as the riff that got him to build some real pick skill despite years of fingers-only habit. → [[Pick vs. Fingers on Bass — Tone and Technique Tradeoffs]] (the pick-tone-and-ease side).

- **[~9:15–10:15] Song 7 — Blink-182, "Carousel" (song title stated explicitly).** Bassist Mark Hoppus uses **bass chords** (double/triple-stops, not single root notes) as the song's signature melodic device, giving the bass a Cure/New Order-adjacent character — Josh compares it directly to Peter Hook's bassline on "Love Will Tear Us Apart." **The technique's actual mechanic:** bass chords played **down low** read as muddy and gross, but the same voicing played **up higher on the neck** sounds clean, "a little more guitar-ly," and genuinely nice — register is what makes or breaks the technique. → [[Bass Chords and Double Stops]] — new technique note.

- **[~10:15–11:11] Song 8 — NOFX, "The Decline" (18 minutes 21 seconds — explicitly stated duration confirms the title).** Called the most epic punk song of all time; Fat Mike's bassist Fat Mike is praised for range-jumping fearlessness — playing chords, dropping into a "fat riff," then leaping up high, described as breaking "all the rules of bass," which the video explicitly frames as fitting punk's whole ethos ("f*ck 'em if there were" rules). Second data point for [[Bass Chords and Double Stops]] — chords used alongside register jumps rather than a fixed low-end role.

- **[~11:11–12:48] Song 9 — Descendents; bassist Karl Alvarez (song not stated aloud).** Named as having the "wrong tone" for punk and it working anyway: a **nasal, warm, punchy fingerstyle tone**, compared explicitly to Jaco Pastorius (who called his own playing "punk jazz"). Concrete before/after demo: the same bassline with **stock pick tone** is "cool, but it loses some of the quirk" that makes Descendents distinctive — the deliberately "wrong" warm fingerstyle tone is treated as a genuine artistic choice, not a limitation. Reinforces the video's recurring "punk has no real rules, even about tone" theme (echoes song 2's note-choice point). → folded into [[Pick vs. Fingers on Bass — Tone and Technique Tradeoffs]] as a third worked example of fingers-for-tone.

- **[~12:48–end] Song 10 — Rancid, "Maxwell Murder" (title inferred from Josh's direct callout — "we want a Maxwell Murder video," addressed to bassist Matt Freeman — from the album *...And Out Come the Wolves*).** Josh's personal "white whale" — a bass solo he calls "too f*cking fast," which haunted him for years since first hearing it on a burned CD-R in 10th-grade biology class. He reports practicing hard specifically to attempt it on camera for this video, and — after visibly hesitating on-screen ("I don't know if I can do this... we'll see") — **learned it faster than expected using the same method taught in BassBuzz's separate "how to learn bass as fast as humanly possible" video** (referenced by description only; that video isn't yet in this vault, so no link). → the Goal-Tiered Practice bullet below captures the reusable "grind through the burn" speed-practice principle this and song 1 both demonstrate.
