# Notes & Pitches

Part of [[Music Theory]]. Foundation for [[Scales]], [[Chords]], and [[Intervals]].

## Pitch names
- 7 letters: **A B C D E F G**, reused up and down the range
- Same letter = higher/lower version of the same pitch (octaves)
- Keyboard landmarks (black-key position rule):
	- **C** = left of the 2 black keys · **D** = between the 2 · **E** = right of the 2
	- **F** = left of the 3 black keys · **A** = right of the middle of the 3 · **B** = right of the 3
	- **G** = left of the middle black key

## Sharps & flats
- ♯ = raise a half-step · ♭ = lower a half-step
- Every black key has **two names** (enharmonic equivalents): C♯ = D♭, D♯ = E♭, F♯ = G♭, G♯ = A♭, A♯ = B♭
- Which name to use depends on the [[Keys|key]] — each [[Scales|scale]] uses each letter exactly once
- **B♯, E♯, C♭, and F♭ are real, not a simplification some teachers skip** — B♯ = C (raise B by a half-step) and E♯ = F (raise E by a half-step); C♭ = B and F♭ = E (lower by a half-step) the other way. They're not optional/wrong spellings — a major scale MUST use every letter A–G exactly once ([[Scales]]), so any key that lands on the "wrong" natural for its 7th or 4th degree has to rename it: e.g. F♯ major's 7th degree is enharmonically F but has to be spelled **E♯** to keep E as the letter used once; G♭ major's 3rd degree is enharmonically B but has to be spelled **C♭** to keep C as the letter used once. Skipping these notes because "they're basically the same as a natural" breaks the one-of-each-letter rule and makes the resulting triads/7th chords misspelled. → [[Scales]] (spelling rules + C♯/C♭ major worked examples) — Daniel Seriff, [BIGGEST Music Theory MYTH That Will RUIN Your Understanding](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ukUxGfmYJlQ)

## Half-steps & whole-steps
- **Half-step** (semitone) = smallest distance; two pitches with nothing between them — *including black keys*
	- E→F and B→C are the natural half-steps (no black key between)
	- A→B is NOT a half-step (A♯/B♭ sits between) — it's a whole-step
- **Whole-step** = 2 half-steps (C→D, A→B, B♭→C)
- All [[Chords|chord]] and [[Scales|scale]] formulas are built from these units — see [[Intervals]] for the full interval names

## From videos

- **"Find one note everywhere" drill — locate every instance of a single chosen note across the ENTIRE fretboard (all strings, up to the 12th fret), broader than the octave-triangle's string-to-string relocation shape.** Worked on E. Recommended to alternate this drill with the natural-notes-per-string approach on different days for variety. Also notes the two structural shortcuts that shrink the neck to memorize: everything repeats exactly one octave higher at the 12th fret (so only frets 0-11 are genuinely new information), and the low E and high E strings share the identical note layout in standard tuning. → BERNTH, [[How to INSTANTLY visualize the FULL fretboard! (Guitar Lesson)]]

- **The low E string and A string carry the identical natural-note sequence (E F G A B C D / A B C D E F G, matching the same shape shifted position), so memorizing the low E string's notes makes the A string "free" — same shape, different starting fret, not a new sequence.** A lightweight companion fact to the octave-triangle entry above (same author, same fretboard-visualization goal, different specific observation). → BERNTH, [[You're Probably Visualizing The Fretboard WRONG!]]

- **Octave triangle — guitar fretboard note-memorization drill using the octave interval's shape to spread known notes across strings.** Learn the 7 natural notes on one string first (low E: E F G A B C D), then use the octave shape (visually forms a triangle across the neighboring strings) to relocate that same note an octave up/down on a different string, filling in sharps/flats between naturals as needed — a guitar-neck-shaped alternative to the linear "walk one string" bass drills below. Recommended 5–10 min daily before practice until note ID is instant; extend the same octave-shape movement to relocate scale fragments and arpeggios once single notes are solid, not just individual notes. → [[Guitar Positions]] — BERNTH, [[The 5 MOST Important Music Theory TRICKS! (Finally Sound Like A PRO)]]
- **The alphabet as a tuner-reading tool — the same "forward = up in pitch, backward = down in pitch" rule turned into a direct beginner action.** If a target note is forward in the alphabet from what a tuner displays, turn toward higher pitch; if it's backward, turn lower — e.g. tuner reads F, target is G (forward, so up, through F♯); tuner reads B♭, target is A (backward, so down). When two paths both work (tuner reads B, target A — short hop down, or the long way forward around the full loop), take whichever is closer. Same piano-keyboard forward/backward framing this note already documents under Pitch names and Half-steps/whole-steps, aimed specifically at the "why does my tuner say the wrong letter" moment. Full tuning-session detail (tuner types, a safety rule for uncertain direction, a clip-on misread fix): [[Bass Playing Position, Setup, and Tuning Basics]]. → BassBuzz, [[How To Tune Your Bass (Beginner Bass Basics)]]
- **Piano-keyboard framing for teaching the 12-note musical alphabet and sharps/flats to a total beginner, before any fretboard content.** 12 notes total — 7 white keys (letter names A–G, looping back to A) and 5 black keys; sharps raise a note one key, flats lower it one key, and the same black key can have two names depending on which direction you approached it from (lowering D → D♭, raising C → the same key, C♯ — "two names, one note"). Gives the no-black-key gaps a beginner-friendly mnemonic name: **"buddied close"** — B→C and E→F "really like each other and wanted to stay extra friendly," same fact this note already states plainly under Half-steps & whole-steps, phrased for someone hearing it for the first time. *(Simplification flag: the video also states flatly "there is no F flat," treating F♭/B♯/C♭/E♯ as simply not existing — this note's own Sharps & flats section documents that those spellings ARE real and required in certain keys to keep the one-letter-per-scale-degree rule intact, so the video's framing is a correct-enough beginner shortcut for money-zone/enharmonic purposes, not the full picture.)* → [[Bass Fretboard Note Memorization System]] (the fretboard system this piano framing sets up) — BassBuzz, [[Learn Your Bass Fretboard Notes (Easy Starter Method)]]
- **Bass-neck version of the same alphabet-as-fretboard-map idea, framed around the four open strings as the starting reference points.** Start from the open strings **E A D G**, then move one fret at a time to walk the 12-note alphabet in order (A, A♯, B, C, C♯, D, D♯, E, F, F♯, G, G♯, A) — a lighter-weight companion to the full [[Bass Fretboard Note Memorization System]] drill logged just below: this is the "here's the map" framing, that note is the "here's how to memorize the whole territory" system. Stated practical payoff: if a bandmate calls out a progression by letter name only ("G to E to C"), the alphabet-as-map lets you find and play those notes immediately with zero other theory needed. → [[Chords]] (triad shapes built on top of this same root-finding step) — BassBuzz, [[Music Theory for Bass]]
- **Say and Play — fretted musical-alphabet walk on bass** — play every fret up a string in turn, saying each note name aloud: sharp names ascending (E F F♯ G G♯ A A♯ B C C♯ D D♯ E, 12th fret = the octave), flat names descending on the way back (E E♭ D D♭ C B B♭ A A♭ G G♭ F E) — the "least clunky" naming convention per the video. Framed explicitly as a navigation drill rather than a memorization drill: it's what lets a player step off a memorized anchor note and reach any nearby fret by walking the alphabet a few steps rather than re-deriving it from scratch. Full system (money-note anchors, octave/unison relocation shapes, dot-marker mnemonics, recall drill) → [[Bass Fretboard Note Memorization System]]. — BassBuzz, [[Memorize the Bass Fretboard in 9 Minutes]]
- **Fret-distance framing for half-steps/whole-steps on guitar** — a half step is simply 1 fret (either direction) on a single string, a whole step is 2 frets; moving toward the guitar body = ascending, toward the headstock = descending. Same B-C/E-F narrow-gap rule as above, taught as a daily drill: play the 7 natural notes up one string saying each name aloud, then deliberately drill the descending direction too (most guitarists are weaker descending) — claimed payoff is noticeably better fretboard fluency within a few weeks. Also calls out that any of the 7 natural notes can take a sharp or flat (including the less-common B♯/E♯/C♭/F♭), and that which enharmonic name is "correct" depends on the [[Keys|key]], not preference — matches this note's own enharmonic-equivalents line. — Daniel Seriff, [Master Basic Music Theory in Just 12 Minutes: A Beginner's Guide](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W8WIEgavHc0)
