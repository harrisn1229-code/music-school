# Blues Phrasing in the Style of B.B. and Albert King

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5p57YVDheyg)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5p57YVDheyg)

## Synopsis

Jack Ruch isolates one specific blues-guitar skill — playing lead guitar fills that **answer** a vocal/melody line rather than **soloing over** it — and demonstrates it twice against the same backing track (a slow 12-bar blues in C, three chords, with an organ standing in for the vocal melody the guitar has to leave room for). First pass is in the style of B.B. King, built around the "B.B. box" (a major/minor pentatonic blend around the root on the B string) with a signature lean on the major 6th and chord-tone targeting into the V chord, drawn partly from B.B.'s live "Three O'Clock Blues." Second pass is in the style of Albert King — a lower, more purely minor-pentatonic approach across the upper and lower boxes, built on pull-off/slide phrase tails and a practical workaround for Albert's famous multi-pitch bends (which depend on his upside-down, tuned-down, open-tuning setup and can't be exactly replicated on a standard guitar). The most valuable part isn't either style's specific licks so much as the discipline underneath both: **fit the phrase to the space in the form, and use note choice to mark the chord changes, especially the move to the V.**

## What's taught

### The core skill — fills that answer, not solo
- **Lead-fill accompaniment ≠ soloing.** A full guitar solo phrases in line with the melody; a lead-fill accompaniment fits *around* an existing melody/vocal line, in the gaps it leaves — the whole lesson is about phrasing lines "where it doesn't step on any toes."
- **Reference players for this exact skill**: B.B. King, Albert King, Eric Clapton, Stevie Ray Vaughan — named as the players who "weave seamlessly in between the vocal phrasing."
- **Demo setup**: a slow 12-bar blues in C, three-chord/typical form, with a simple melody played on organ standing in for the vocal — the same backing track used for both style passes, specifically so the *approach* differs while the target melody doesn't.
- **Both singers, both call-and-response naturally**: because B.B. King and Albert King were singers as well as guitarists, "a lot of this weaving of guitar fills and vocals together" is native call-and-response — the guitar fill is literally answering their own vocal line in performance.

### B.B. King style
- **The B.B. box**: a position centered on the root note on the B string — 13th fret of the B string for the C root used here — combining major pentatonic and minor pentatonic *around that root* rather than one fixed shape.
- **Major/minor 3rd juxtaposition**: bend to the major 3rd, then grab the minor 3rd right after — called out as "that really blue sound" and "a signature thing of B.B.'s."
- **Major 6th accent**: 14th fret of the G string (major 6th in C) — B.B. repeatedly hits the root and falls back onto this note; described as a constant accent in his playing.
- **Down to the lower minor pentatonic box** from that same area, blending major/minor there too, rather than treating it as separate vocabulary from the B.B. box above.
- **"Keep it simple, leave space"** stated directly as the real key to the whole style — simple phrases, plenty of room for the melody, fitting little phrases together rather than filling everything.
- **Chord-tone move into the V (from B.B.'s live "Three O'Clock Blues")**: going from the I chord (C) to the V (G7/G9), outline the change by playing into the **♭7 of the V chord** — a real chord tone — right at the transition; described as having a T-Bone Walker–ish, jazzy quality. The generalized lesson: use note choice to highlight chord changes, and stay aware of where you are in the 12-bar form, not just which scale you're in.
- **Signature bend**: 5th bent up a minor 3rd to the ♭7 (G → B♭ in C).
- **Signature closer**: root note up an octave, let it ring, slide off it.

### Albert King style
- **Same backing track, different territory**: stays mostly in the minor pentatonic realm rather than the major/minor blend of the B.B. box, and sits in a lower fretboard position.
- **Still bends minor 3rd ↔ major 3rd**, the same blue-note vocabulary as the B.B. section, just from a different position — the underlying "grammar" is shared even though the shapes aren't.
- **Why it's only an approximation**: Albert King played a right-handed guitar strung upside down, in an open tuning, tuned down — "impossible to completely accurately emulate" on a standard-tuned, standard-strung guitar. Everything in this section is the closest practical stand-in, not a transcription.
- **Position**: the **upper minor pentatonic box** (the pattern one position up from the "home" box, starting on the ♭3) gets closest to the Albert King sound for the top part of a phrase; drop to the **lower/standard minor pentatonic box** underneath it for the rest.
- **Short-gap call-and-response lick**: bend from the 4th up to the 5th, hit the minor 3rd, fall to root/5th — called a classic Albert move.
- **Long-gap phrase tail**: pick once, then pull-off/slide the rest of the phrase — root → ♭7, pulling off, then sliding down; also played minor 3rd → root → ♭7. This single-attack, pull-off/slide shape is what strings separate pentatonic-box phrases together across longer spaces, and is described as one of Albert's core nuanced inflections/"tails" on phrase endings.
- **Chord-tone move into the V (Albert version)**: minor pentatonic line sliding up into a **double stop pulled directly from the G9 chord** — its ♭7 and 5th together — the same "highlight the chord change" idea as B.B.'s version above, landing on a double stop instead of a single note.
- **Multi-pitch bend emulation (from Albert's "The Sky Is Crying")**: Albert's real technique bends one fretted note up and down through multiple different pitches — possible only because of his upside-down/tuned-down open-tuning setup. On a standard-tuned guitar, approximate the effect by **switching fingers/frets mid-bend**: fret the 13th fret of the high E string, bend up a whole step, then release down to a finger a half-step lower to fake the same rising/falling multi-pitch contour.
- **Closing advice, stated directly**: "the best way to get that stuff happening in your playing is to just listen, listen, and listen" — pointing at Albert's actual recordings ("The Sky Is Crying") over further drilling of the approximation.

### Cross-cutting takeaways
- **The major/minor 3rd blend is the one move common to both styles** — it's the "blue" sound either way, just approached from different fretboard positions/boxes.
- **Chord-tone targeting at the V chord** shows up in both passes (♭7 note for B.B., ♭7+5th double stop for Albert) — a second shared discipline beyond "leave space": mark the harmonic motion, don't just run a scale over the whole form.
- **Recordings named as reference listening**: B.B. King's live "Three O'Clock Blues" (linked in the original video's description) and Albert King's "The Sky Is Crying" (also linked in the description) — both cited as the direct source for a specific lick/technique above, not just general inspiration.
- **Stated real-world use case**: this is explicitly an ensemble skill — "once you get in a situation where you're playing blues with a singer, or if you're singing and playing guitar at the same time" — not a solo-guitar exercise.
