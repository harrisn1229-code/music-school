# How Hozier transformed Arctic Monkey's "Do I Wanna Know"

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vxjYJQ-9e3Q)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vxjYJQ-9e3Q)

## Synopsis

A short, focused breakdown of Hozier's viral 2023 Live Lounge cover of Arctic Monkeys' "Do I Wanna Know" — converted from the original's G minor into G major, but via a more sophisticated technique than the obvious mechanical conversion, producing a tender/heartbreaking major-key feel instead of the "prim and proper" brightness the naive approach would give.

## Techniques

- **The naive parallel-key conversion, demonstrated and rejected**: to convert a minor-key song to its parallel major, raise the 3rd, 6th and 7th scale degrees everywhere they occur (in G: B♭→B, E♭→E, F→F♯). This works and produces a technically correct major-key version — but sounds too bright, "prim and proper," for what Hozier was going for.
- **What Hozier actually did**: raise the 3rd/6th/7th as normal, but ALSO transpose almost every note in the riff down a third (except the tonic G, kept in place both as the anchor note and because it's the riff's first, most recognizable note). Chords shift by the same third to stay consistent with the melody.
- **Why it still sounds like the same riff**: transposing by an interval preserves melodic contour exactly (every note still moves up/down in the same places) — only the actual interval sizes change, so the riff stays recognizable while landing somewhere new emotionally.
- **Framed as easier to think of as "down a third" even though it's technically up a sixth** — Hozier sings the whole thing an octave higher than the reference comparison, so the real relationship is +6 not -3, but -3 is the simpler mental model.
- **Arranging takeaway**: a parallel major/minor conversion doesn't have to shift every degree by the textbook amount — deliberately under/over-shifting relative to the mechanical rule is itself a usable lever for landing between the two modes' default emotional readings (here, avoiding major's usual "bright and happy" without staying in minor's "dark").
- Also credits the softer tempo, mellower instrumentation, and dynamics as contributing to the same tender mood, alongside the harmonic device — but flags the harmonic transposition as the specific thing that caught the presenter's ear.
