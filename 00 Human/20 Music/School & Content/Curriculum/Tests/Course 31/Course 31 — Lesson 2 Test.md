> **Q1.** **The 3rd and the 7th**
>
> **Q2.** **The 3rd says major or minor; the 7th says major, dominant or minor**
> Together they name the chord.
>
> **Q3.** **Because it tells you almost nothing about the chord's quality**
> The root names the chord but says nothing about quality either; the 5th is simply the least informative note present.
>
> **Q4.** **m7♭5 — it needs the ♭5**
> Cm7♭5 and Cm7 share E♭ and B♭, so a voicing that drops the ♭5 states the wrong chord.
>
> **Q5.** **Dm7 F and C · G7 B and F · Cmaj7 E and B**
> *Also accepted:* F/C · B/F · E/B
>
> **Q6.** **C → B → B and F → F → E — one voice holds while the other moves a half step, then they swap**
> Nothing leaps, which is the whole trick.
>
> **Q7.** **The 3rd is held over as the next chord's 7th; the 7th falls a half step to its 3rd**
> That is what root motion by fifths does, and it is the mechanical reason the cell sounds inevitable.
>
> **Q8.** **Root, 3rd and 7th — no 5th; root–7th–3rd is usually the better one**
> Extensions stack on top: shell plus 9, shell plus 13.
>
> **Q9.** **It leaves the register above free and loses no harmony — the 5th and doubled root were the crowding**
> *Rubric (5):* says the shell leaves the register above free for the melody or another player (2) · says the notes dropped were the ones crowding, so no harmony is lost (2) · names a second case — a second chordal instrument, or thinning a crowded mix (1)
> The 3rd and 7th state the chord; the 5th and a doubled root state nothing and occupy the same mids the vocal needs. Playing fewer notes there is not a compromise, it is a more accurate voicing of the same chord. The same reasoning covers a second chordal instrument in the arrangement — two players both voicing five notes fight — and it covers a mix that is crowded in the mids, where cutting the keys to shells frees space without touching the harmony.
>
> **Q10.** **A substitute that keeps the guide-tone lines stepwise is a good one**
> *Rubric (5):* states the stepwise guide-tone test (2) · says the tritone sub swaps the guide tones rather than disturbing them (2) · notes that this solves the horizontal problem before the vertical one (1)
> Write the 3rds and 7ths of the progression as two horizontal lines and see what the candidate chord does to them. A substitute that keeps both lines moving by step or holding is doing the same voice-leading job as the chord it replaced. The tritone sub is the clean case: D♭7's 3rd is G7's 7th and its 7th is G7's 3rd, so the same two pitches are still there and the lines are untouched — the roles have swapped, not the notes. Deciding this first is faster than voicing chord by chord and it produces smoother results.
