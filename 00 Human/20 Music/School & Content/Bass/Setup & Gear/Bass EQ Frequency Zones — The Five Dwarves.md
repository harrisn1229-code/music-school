# Bass EQ Frequency Zones — The Five Dwarves

A named-zone framework for troubleshooting and shaping bass tone with EQ, borrowed from a sound engineer's in-joke T-shirt ("The 7 Bad System Dwarves" of the frequency spectrum) — sound engineers' shorthand for the recurring tonal problems every source can develop. Five of the seven dwarves map onto bass-relevant territory. Each zone works two ways: **cut it to fix a specific symptom**, or **boost it deliberately (while taming the others) to chase a specific bassist's tone** — the same knob, opposite direction, depending on what you're going for. Companion to [[Bass Tone Chain Priority]] (the *order* to check the whole signal chain in) — this note is specifically the EQ-frequency layer of that chain, which that note's Link 5 flags as important without breaking down which frequencies to reach for.

## Why EQ matters this much: it's the LAST link in the chain

Tone is a chain — **fingers → strings → pickups → amp settings → EQ**. EQ's outsized power comes specifically from being last: a genuinely great chain up to that point (great fingers, fresh strings, the right pickups, good amp settings) can still be ruined by bad EQ at the very end — turned into "a muffled blob you'd never be able to hear in the song." The reverse also holds across every example below: dialing in the right zone gets a mediocre setup surprisingly close to an iconic reference tone, EQ alone. Neither direction works without the other — see the Bitey and Edgy entries below, where the artist recipe explicitly requires fresh strings *and* the EQ boost, not EQ alone.

## Beginner starting method — walk to the extremes, then back off

A simpler, pre-dwarves method for anyone still on a basic 3–4-knob amp EQ (bass/mid/treble, no graphic EQ) — a deliberate mistake-first pass that calibrates the ear before ever settling on a value, rather than guessing straight at a target. **Set every knob to noon first.** Work **bass** and **treble** one at a time, and **deliberately skip mid as a beginner** (see below) — this is the fast on-ramp to the zone-by-zone framework above, not a replacement for it once mid-territory (Honky/Growly) is worth tackling.
- **Bass knob:** pluck an open **E** string as the reference note. Roll **all the way counterclockwise** first and listen — thin, "where did the bass go" (this note's own Tubby-adjacent failure territory, from the other direction). Roll **all the way clockwise** and listen — boomy, "earthquake in your house" (straight into Tubby). *Then* roll back to roughly the middle, nudging by ear for the room/gear.
- **Treble knob:** switch to an open **D** string as the reference — treble's effect is easier to hear here than on the low E. Roll **all the way counterclockwise** — muffled, dull, "like a blanket" over the tone. Roll **all the way clockwise** — nasty, overly bright/zingy (Edgy territory). Roll back to roughly the middle.
- **Why skip mid:** mids have too many "right answers," and different amps' mid controls behave differently from one another — a beginner is safer leaving mid at noon and mastering bass/treble first, rather than fighting a control that doesn't have one obvious correct direction. Once bass/treble are solid, the Honky/Growly zone above is the natural next step.

## Tubby — ~80 Hz (rumble / boom)

**Symptom:** flick the amp on, pluck one note, feel a "godawful rumble shaking the stage," drowning out the kick drum.
**Fix:** counterintuitively, **cut the bass knob** on a basic 3–4-knob amp EQ — it removes rumble and opens space for the kick without gutting the low end. On a graphic EQ (Darkglass Microtubes 900 V2 used as the demo unit), pull the **leftmost slider** down.
**Boost for — the Jamerson lane:** James Jamerson's Motown tone (reference: Marvin Gaye's "What's Goin' On," 80–120 Hz dominant) — roll the bass's passive tone knob all the way down, boost the amp's bass knob, reduce everything else to taste. Gets close to that girthy Motown low end with no special gear. **Genre-dependent, not universal** — the same super-Tubby setting on an aggressive rock tune (Tool cited by name) reads as far too much.
→ [[BASS Slot]] (60–250 Hz, bottom edge, bordering [[SUB Slot]])

## Muddy — ~250 Hz (boomy mush, lost note definition)

**Symptom:** low end is already tamed but individual notes still mush together into "a big boomy mess of bassy-somethingness" — you can't tell what's being played.
**Fix:** roll down whatever control sits around 250 Hz (Darkglass: slider at 250). The listed number is only the center — it affects the whole zone, so any nearby control works, and **if there's no dedicated low-mid control, the same bass knob used for Tubby helps with Muddy too, in a pinch.**
**Two-way control:** a tone that reads thin/lacking fullness can take *more* mud added deliberately for a fuller-feeling line — this isn't purely a cut zone.
**Balance point, not elimination — the McVie lane:** John McVie's tone on Fleetwood Mac's "Rhiannon" lives on the knife-edge here — too much 250 Hz muddies the whole song; pull too much out and the bass goes "weirdly hollow," not filling the space it's supposed to fill.
→ boundary of [[BASS Slot]] (top, ~250 Hz) and [[BED Slot]] (bottom, 250–800 Hz)

## Honky — ~500 Hz (nasal)

**Symptom:** low end is fine, no rumble, but the tone has a persistent nasal quality — "the business end of a pissed off goose."
**Fix:** push the ~500 Hz slider/knob down. **No dedicated 500 Hz control?** Don't assume that means new gear is needed — improvise with what's near it: demonstrated live on a bass whose only nearby controls were a 280 Hz low-mid (Muddy's territory) and a 1200 Hz high-mid (Bitey's territory, too high); the actual fix used was the bass's own **contour switch** (cut ~670 Hz + a little low-mid) — "not a perfect fix, but enough to probably de-honk you in whatever room or situation you're dealing with." Myth-bust: claims that sub-$800 basses commonly honk and need replacing are explicitly rejected — "almost never a reason you actually need to buy more bass stuff" once you know how to tame the zone with what you have.
**Boost for — Honky's alter-ego is Growly.** Harnessed deliberately, this zone gives tasty/snarly/growly tone. The Steve Harris lane (Iron Maiden, "Power Slave"): heavy activity right at 500 Hz. Recipe = **boost ~500 Hz** + **switch to the neck pickup** (Harris's original P-Bass only had the neck pickup — on a multi-pickup bass, solo the neck pickup for the same effect; visibly dials up 500 Hz energy on a spectrum view even before the EQ boost is added).
→ [[BED Slot]] (250–800 Hz)

## Bitey — ~1.5 kHz (harsh edge)

**Symptom:** a mellow slow number calls for round/warm tone, but an overly aggressive tone "bites horrifically" through the song when the bass enters.
**Fix:** the **high-mids knob** is the right tool — on a Fender Rumble it's centered at 1.2 kHz, close enough to help; on a Darkglass, a slider sits right at 1.5 kHz. **No dedicated control** → roll the passive tone knob down ~20% as a fallback.
**Judge in context, not solo:** don't overcorrect while soloed — a tone that reads too bitey alone can sit fine once the rest of the band is playing.
**Boost for — the Geddy lane:** Rush's "Closer to the Heart" carries heavy activity around 1.5 kHz vs. the same riff played flat. Recipe = **fresh strings** (dead strings can't supply the high-frequency content this boost needs) + **clacky plucking technique** + **boost Bitey** — EQ alone isn't enough without the upstream chain also being right.
→ [[MIDS Slot]] (800 Hz–2.5 kHz)

## Edgy — ~3 kHz, extending to ~5 kHz+ (unlistenable top end: clank + hiss)

**Symptom:** every other zone is tamed, but the band still cringes — an "unlistenable top end" that's actually **two separate sub-problems** at two different points in the range: clankiness around 3 kHz, and hiss/sizzle at 5 kHz and above.
**Fix:** the amp's stock treble knob is often the wrong tool for this — on the Fender Rumble it's centered at **10 kHz**, "kind of ridiculously high for a bass EQ knob"; it reduces hiss but doesn't touch clank. A graphic EQ handles both as separate moves: **3 kHz slider down** fixes clankiness (attack improves, though hiss can remain), then a **5 kHz+ slider down** separately kills remaining hiss. **No graphic EQ** → the passive tone knob gets a similar, less-precise effect.
**Boost for — two lanes:**
- **Tim Commerford lane** (Rage Against the Machine, "Bombtrack") — heavy 3–5 kHz activity gives his brutal, clean, metallic cut-through-the-mix sound. Recipe = **fresh strings** (max available high end) + **a nudge to the high-mids** (Bitey territory, just below Edgy) + **boost Edgy**.
- **Modern metal / Nolly (Periphery) lane** — goes further: **play with a pick instead of fingers** (a pick puts noticeably more energy in the bitey/edgy range than fingers do) + **distortion** (Darkglass named directly as the go-to unit) on top of the EQ boost.
→ [[LEAD Slot]] (2.5–6 kHz)

## Bonus — thinning the whole tone for lead visibility (Victor Wooten)

A distinct move from every zone above: rather than shaping one named frequency band, this is a **cross-zone shift of the whole tonal balance**, and it directly contradicts the note's own opening framing that a bass needs enough bottom end to fill its role. Standard rule (per a "rules of bass" breakdown): bass tone should carry lots of low end, whether a teacher or a sound engineer is judging it. **The break — Victor Wooten deliberately runs a thinner, more guitar-like tone as a lead bassist**, specifically so intricate, fast lines stay pickable-out even for listeners without a trained bass ear. **The move, concretely: roll off the bass knob, roll up treble and high-mids** — effectively pulling energy out of the Tubby/Muddy territory and pushing it toward Bitey/Edgy. Worked demo on "Me and My Bass Guitar": with a full, solid bass tone, individual notes in a fast passage are hard to pick out; with the bass rolled off and treble/high-mids up, the same passage becomes dramatically easier to follow note-by-note. **Verdict: situational, not a replacement for the default.** For the average bassist supporting a band, bring the properly bassy tone the "Quick reference" table above is built around — but when a specific line or solo needs to **cut through and be heard as distinct notes** rather than blend into the low end, this cross-zone thinning move is the tool, same "cut through the mix" goal as the Bitey/Edgy boost-for lanes above, reached by subtraction (cut lows) rather than addition (boost mids/highs) alone. → [[Bass Tone Chain Priority]] (Link 5, the tone-knob lever this move uses directly) — BassBuzz, [[The 10 Rules of Bass Playing (That Pros Break)]]

## Quick reference

| Dwarf | Zone | Cut fixes | Boost chases |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tubby | ~80 Hz | Stage-shaking rumble, kick masking | Jamerson/Motown girth |
| Muddy | ~250 Hz | Notes mushing together | (rarely boosted — fuller feel if thin) |
| Honky | ~500 Hz | Nasal "blowing your nose" tone | Growly — Steve Harris snarl |
| Bitey | ~1.5 kHz | Too-aggressive tone for a mellow song | Geddy Lee cutting bite |
| Edgy | ~3–5 kHz+ | Clank (3K) / hiss (5K+), audience cringing | Commerford metallic clarity, Nolly modern-metal edge |
