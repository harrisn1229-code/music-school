## Chords
Key: **E minor**, though (as flagged above) the song reads just as naturally as **G major** — verse, pre-chorus, and chorus all draw from the exact same four chords, just entered at a different point each time, which is precisely why the "is it Em or is it G" debate exists.

**Verse (the pedal-point riff, bass holding E under all of it):** Em – C – D – Em → **i – ♭VI – ♭VII – i** (read against G major instead: vi – IV – V – vi)

**Pre-chorus / bridge build:** C – G – D – Em → **IV – I – V – vi** (in G)

**Chorus ("Whoa, we're halfway there"):** D – G – C, folding back to Em on the way out → **V – I – IV (– vi)** (in G)

→ **All four sections share the same four chords — G, Em, C, D (I, vi, IV, V) — just started from a different point in the cycle each time**: verse = vi-♭VI/vi-♭VII-i, pre-chorus = IV-I-V-vi, chorus = V-I-IV. That's the **axis progression** family by definition, not a coincidence — this is one case where the doo-wop-adjacent 4-chord loop really does hold up under scrutiny. → [[The Axis Progression]]

**The key change:** for the final chorus, the whole song transposes up a **minor third**, E minor → **G minor** — Wikipedia's composition notes state this plainly, which resolves the ambiguity flagged above (some fan transcriptions instead claim a whole-step E minor→F♯ minor change). The chart backs up the minor-third reading directly: Gm–E♭–F mirrors Em–C–D exactly, and B♭–E♭–F mirrors D–G–C, both transposed up the same minor third. One of rock's most-cited "truck driver's gear change" moments, landing the last chorus a whole register higher. → [[Modulation (Key Change)]]

## The main riff: pedal point against the changing keys

The iconic main riff (doubled by guitar) uses **pedal point**: the bass holds **E minor** while the keyboards move through **E minor → C major → D major → back to E minor** above it. The held bass note disagrees with the keyboard chords it doesn't match, building tension that resolves when everyone lands back on E minor together. A/B'd directly against a version where the bass instead follows the keyboard's chord changes — that version reads noticeably flatter. **How to apply it:** simply don't move your bass note when the other instruments change chords underneath you. Full device (tonic vs. dominant pedal, why it works, the loop-based-harmony framing) → [[Loop-Based Harmony#Pedal point]], and the spaced-rep flashcard version → [[POP-C03 — Pedal point]].

## Verse → pre-chorus: a scale run instead of a flat chord jump

Chord change: E minor → C major. Two flatter alternatives demoed first (straight E→C jump, or sit on the root until the section changes) before the actual run: climbing the **E minor scale using scale degrees 1, 2, 3, 5, then 6** to melodically connect E to C — creating forward momentum into the pre-chorus rather than a static handoff.

## Pre-chorus bar 4: a run that copies the vocal's rhythm

Rather than the plain D→E motion the guitar chords themselves spell out, this fill instead **mimics the rhythm of the vocal line** at "make it or not" — bass and vocal move together rhythmically at that exact moment, one of BassBuzz's own favorite melodic moments in the song.

## The chorus run: target notes and playing over the bar line

The song's biggest fill connects **three chords (D, G, C)** in a single continuous run rather than resetting at each chord's downbeat — a worked example of **playing over the bar line**: most fills start and end squarely on beat one, but this one runs straight through a bar line instead. The trick to doing this without sounding lost is picking **target notes** first: this run needs to hit **D** on the first beat one, **G** on the next beat one, and **C** on the "and of two" (the run's final target deliberately copies an accent in the vocal line at that same "and of two"). Once the targets are locked, the notes in between are fair game — demonstrated twice with different filler-note choices, both drawn from the E minor/G major scale, both hitting the same three targets. Full general method (not just this song's example) → [[Bass Runs and Target Notes]]; the "spans a bar line" framing itself → [[Syncopation and Metric Displacement#Playing Over the Bar Line]].

## Whole song: bassline density tracks the song's intensity arc

Intro vs. outro is the bluntest data point — a huge jump in busyness. Zoomed in further, first chorus vs. last chorus: the first chorus stays mostly scripted eighth notes with one fill (a 16th-note "hiccup") at the phrase end; by the last chorus — after the song modulates up a key — the same four bars carry a slide at the end of bar 1, an extended version of the scale-run fill, and a bar 4 packed with 16th notes. General lesson: read a song's felt intensity arc first, then decide how much space to fill at the start vs. the end (or verse vs. chorus), saving runs/fills/fast notes for the more intense sections — without going overboard. ## Steal this

- **Hold your bass note through a chord change on purpose** — pedal point trades a moment of tension for a stronger release when the harmony resolves back.
- **A scale run using non-adjacent scale degrees (1-2-3-5-6, not every note) can still read as smooth, forward-moving connective tissue between two chords.**
- **A bass fill doesn't have to invent new rhythm — copying the vocal's own rhythm at a transition point is a legitimate, cohesive fill source.**
- **Pick target notes before improvising a run that spans a bar line** — nail the notes that have to land where the chords actually change, and treat everything else as free choice.
- **Chart a song's intro-to-outro intensity arc and make sure the bassline's busyness (fills, run complexity) actually tracks it** — don't hold a static level of activity across a song that's building.
