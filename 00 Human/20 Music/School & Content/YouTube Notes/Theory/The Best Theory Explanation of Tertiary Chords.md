# The Best Theory Explanation of Tertiary Chords

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JL7lnX3CmDM)

## Synopsis
Jared Borowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared), episode 8 of his ongoing "understand and master chords on the guitar" lesson series, gives a pure theory lecture (no guitar in hand, no fretboard shapes — that's deferred to the next two videos in the series) on how seventh chords are built and, more valuably, *why* each scale degree ends up with the seventh-chord quality it does. He names the underlying structure — **tertiary chords**, chords built by stacking intervals of a third on top of each other, which is how virtually all triads and seventh chords in Western tonal music are constructed — then walks the "skip a note" derivation (take every other scale degree starting from any root) for both triads and their seventh extensions. The real payoff of the video is the second half: rather than just stating that a major scale produces Imaj7–ii7–iii7–IVmaj7–V7–vi7–viiø7 (already documented in [[Chords by Key]]), he derives *why* — checking whether each stacked third interval does or doesn't straddle one of the major scale's two half-steps (3–4 and 7–1) to determine major-third vs. minor-third, then reading the resulting three-interval stack (e.g. major-minor-major = maj7) against a reference spelling table. Worked for all seven scale degrees. He also states a practical "read backward" shortcut for finding any chord's 7th (the 7th of the ii chord is scale-degree 1, because 1 sits directly below 2; the 7th of V is 4; the 7th of vi is 5; the 7th of iii is 2) and a song-analysis tip: since dominant 7 only ever occurs naturally as V of a major key and half-diminished only ever occurs as vii°, spotting either chord type out of context is a strong giveaway for identifying the parent major key/scale — whereas maj7 (I or IV) and m7 (ii, iii, or vi) are ambiguous on their own. This is a genuinely new *derivation method* for content this vault already has the *result* of (the Chords by Key diatonic-7th table) — it belongs primarily on [[Seventh Chords]] as an explanation of the "why," with a shorter cross-link on [[Chords by Key]]. No specific song, voicing, or fretboard shape is taught in this installment — it's positioned as the theory setup for two follow-up videos (not yet in the vault) that cover physical practice exercises for playing these seventh-chord types all over the neck. Also plugs a free "Chords with Color" open-string chord chart (5 keys) as a companion practice resource, referenced generally rather than with concrete content to extract.

## What's taught
- **Tertiary chords, named and defined: chords built by stacking intervals of a third on top of each other — the structural basis for nearly all of Western tonal harmony's triads and seventh chords.** [~1:35] → [[Chords]] (existing "Beyond thirds: non-tertial harmony" section already names "tertial" harmony as the common-practice default; this video is the first source with the term spelled out and derived step by step)
- **"Skip a note" derivation for building any triad off any scale degree:** starting from any root, take every other scale-degree note (1-skip 2-take 3-skip 4-take 5 → root/3rd/5th of the I chord; 2-skip 3-take 4-skip 5-take 6 → root/3rd/5th of the ii chord, i.e. scale degrees 2-4-6). Each adjacent pair in the stack is itself an interval of a third. [~1:52]–[~3:05] → [[Chords by Key]] (matches its existing "stacking every-other degree" description) — general confirmation, not new
- **Two-layer naming confusion named explicitly and worth keeping in mind when teaching/learning theory:** the same three notes can be described from the parent scale's perspective (degrees 2-4-6) or from the local chord's own perspective (root-3rd-5th of "the two chord") — both are correct simultaneously, just different zoom levels. [~3:05]–[~3:40]
- **Building the 7th: add one more interval of a third on top of the triad.** For the I chord (1-3-5) this lands on scale degree 7 (hence "seventh chord"); for the ii chord (2-4-6) it lands back on scale degree 1, because the third above 6 is 1. [~3:40]–[~4:15]
- **Fast mental shortcut for finding any diatonic chord's 7th: think one scale-degree number lower than the chord's own root number** — the 7th of ii is 1 (one below 2), the 7th of V is 4, the 7th of vi is 5, the 7th of iii is 2. [~4:15]–[~4:35] → [[Chords by Key]] — a genuinely useful memory aid not currently on that page's diatonic-7th tables
- **Full spelling + third-stack structure for the four seventh-chord qualities that occur naturally in a major key** (matches [[Seventh Chords]]'s existing formula table exactly, confirmed independently): **major 7** = 1-3-5-7, major 3rd + minor 3rd + major 3rd; **dominant 7** = 1-3-5-♭7, major 3rd + minor 3rd + minor 3rd; **minor 7** = 1-♭3-5-♭7, minor 3rd + major 3rd + minor 3rd; **half-diminished / minor 7♭5** (explicitly called out as two names for the identical chord) = 1-♭3-♭5-♭7, minor 3rd + minor 3rd + major 3rd. [~4:35]–[~6:00]
- **Full diatonic seventh-chord quality map for a major key, stated plainly: I = maj7, ii = m7, iii = m7, IV = maj7, V = dominant 7 (the ONLY place dominant 7 occurs naturally in the key), vi = m7, vii = half-diminished/m7♭5 (the ONLY place that quality occurs naturally in the key).** [~6:00]–[~6:35] — matches [[Chords by Key]]'s existing "7th version of any row" line exactly
- **Song-analysis/ear-training tip: spotting a dominant 7 or half-diminished chord out of context is a strong giveaway for the parent major key**, since dominant 7 only ever occurs naturally as the V chord and half-diminished only ever occurs naturally as vii° — you can back-derive the scale by treating the dominant 7 as "the V of some major key" or the half-diminished as "the vii of some major key." By contrast, spotting a bare maj7 chord is ambiguous (could be I or IV of two different keys) and a bare m7 is even more ambiguous (could be ii, iii, or vi of three different keys). [~6:35]–[~7:15] → [[Chord Naming by Context]], [[Keys]]
- **The core derivation method — determine each stacked third's quality (major vs. minor) by checking whether it straddles one of the major scale's two built-in half-steps (between scale degrees 3–4 and 7–1): if the third-interval gap contains a half-step, it's a minor third; if it doesn't, it's a major third.** This is the mechanism that explains *why* the diatonic seventh-chord qualities land where they do, not just *that* they do. [~7:35]–[~8:35]
- **Full worked derivation, degree by degree, using the half-step-detection method above (the video's central "aha"):**
  - **I chord (1-3-5-7):** 1→3 no half-step = major 3rd; 3→4→5 has a half-step (3–4) = minor 3rd; 5→7 no half-step = major 3rd. Stack = major-minor-major → **maj7**, confirming why I is always maj7 in a major key. [~8:50]–[~9:10]
  - **ii chord (2-4-6-1):** 2→3→4 has a half-step (3–4) = minor 3rd; 4→6 no half-step = major 3rd; 6→7→1 has a half-step (7–1) = minor 3rd. Stack = minor-major-minor → **m7**, why ii is always minor 7. [~9:10]–[~9:35]
  - **iii chord (3-5-7-2):** same minor-major-minor stack as ii → **m7**, why iii is also always minor 7. [~9:35]–[~9:45]
  - **IV chord (4-6-1-3):** 4→6 major 3rd; 6→7→1 minor 3rd; 1→3 major 3rd. Stack = major-minor-major → **maj7**, why IV is always maj7. [~9:45]–[~10:00]
  - **V chord (5-7-2-4) — "worth the whole explanation on its own":** 5→7 major 3rd; 7→1→2 minor 3rd; 2→3→4 minor 3rd. Stack = major-minor-minor → **dominant 7**, and this exact stack (major 3rd with two minor 3rds on top) can ONLY occur starting from scale degree 5 in a major scale — the structural reason dominant 7 exists nowhere else in the diatonic seventh-chord set. [~10:00]–[~10:35]
  - **vi chord (6-1-3-5):** minor-major-minor stack (same shape as ii/iii) → **m7**, why vi is also minor 7 (the third of three places minor 7 occurs diatonically). [~10:35]–[~10:45]
  - **vii chord (7-2-4-6):** 7→1→2 minor 3rd; 2→3→4 minor 3rd (two minor thirds in a row = a diminished triad on its own); 4→6 major 3rd. Stack = minor-minor-major → **half-diminished/m7♭5**, and this two-minor-thirds-in-a-row stack can ONLY occur starting from scale degree 7 — the structural reason half-diminished exists nowhere else in the key. [~10:45]–[~11:20]
