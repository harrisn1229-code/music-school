## Key points
- Early sheet music used a single 11-line staff, with the middle C marked specially, which made reading difficult.
- The introduction of ledger lines split the staff into two five-line staves, improving readability.
- Treble (G-clef) and bass (F-clef) indicate high and low notes, and their symbols mark specific lines on the staff.
- Middle C sometimes causes confusion about hand usage, which was addressed by spacing staves apart.
- The original 11-line staff concept survives in the modern "grand staff," with the treble and bass staves joined by a vertical line.
- A common but inefficient habit among students is reading right and left hand notes separately; instead, one should read both together in a single vertical eye movement.
- Misconceptions about the left hand being harder to read are unfounded.

## Technical terms
- **Clef**: A symbol placed at the beginning of the staff to indicate the pitch of written notes; common types include treble (G-clef) and bass (F-clef).
- **Ledger line**: A short line used to notate pitches above or below the regular lines of the staff.
- **Grand staff**: The combination of the treble and bass staves, traditionally used for piano music, joined by a vertical line and encompassing a wide range of pitches.
- **Treble clef (G-clef)**: A clef that designates the second line as G above middle C.
- **Bass clef (F-clef)**: A clef that designates the fourth line as F below middle C.
