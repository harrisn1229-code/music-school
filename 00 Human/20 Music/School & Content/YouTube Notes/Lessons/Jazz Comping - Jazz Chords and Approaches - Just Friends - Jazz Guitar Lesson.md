## Synopsis

A long-form (~59 min), deliberately less-scripted Jens Larsen lesson that steps back from "here's a drop-2 voicing for a B♭maj7" to address comping as a whole skill — because, as he opens with, a working jazz guitarist spends far more time comping than soloing but almost nobody makes lesson content specifically about it. He picks "Just Friends" as the vehicle: first a full harmonic analysis of the tune (key of F, 32-bar AABA-ish form with nearly-identical A sections, functional breakdown chord by chord, including the ♯IV° diminished and its two derivable "one-note-changed" scale options), then two full comped choruses over a backing track demonstrating his two main voicing families — drop-2 and triads — narrated chord by chord, then a third, more "open" chorus that layers in triad-arpeggiation, sustained-note-plus-moving-inner-melody, and riff-based comping. The back half of the video pulls back further into the philosophy of comping behind a soloist: giving room vs. pushing energy, locking to the rhythm section, style-dependent density (Wes Montgomery/hard-bop busy-but-not-in-the-way vs. Bill Frisell's Paul Motian/Charlie Hayden trio "On Broadway" records, where almost nothing is played and the harmony is just laid down and left open), and named reference recordings to study (Herbie Hancock/Tony Williams behind Miles Davis, Wynton Kelly, John Scofield's "So Near, So Far" with Joe Henderson). Larsen is explicit that this is intentionally *not* a "here's the exact voicing" video — the value is in the reasoning and the listening habits, not a transcript to copy note for note. Most valuable material: the tonic-diminished harmonic-major derivation for the ♯IV° chord (a genuinely new angle vs. the vault's existing harmonic-minor-only diminished-scale coverage), the "reduce a chord run to one word" learning habit applied concretely to Just Friends' structure, the riff-comping practice method as the on-ramp to truly open/free comping, and the sideman-listening material (which reinforces and extends [[Ensemble Listening and Comping Restraint]] with new named reference recordings and a "leaning on energy vs. leaning on groove" framing that note didn't yet have).

## What's taught

### Song analysis — "Just Friends" (key of F, 32-bar form)
- Commonly played in **F or G**. Both halves of the form are nearly identical — the only real difference is the first half needs to lead back into the second half (goes to the dominant) while the second half needs a complete cadence back to the tonic to end the melody. Learn the tune as "mostly one repeating unit," not 32 independently-memorized bars.
- Starts on the **IV chord**, not the I — B♭maj7 for 2 bars, then **iv-minor** (B♭m7–E♭7) for 2 bars, described as "just one thing" (a single iv-minor sound, not two separate chords to think about separately) — same "collapse a run of chords into one word" learning habit Larsen names explicitly: *"you don't want to remember a word by memorizing the letters, you just want to remember the word."*
- Resolves to **tonic** (F, sometimes voiced with the 3rd in the bass), then a **♯IV° diminished** (spelled here as B° with A♭ in the bass, i.e. an inversion) — often alternately written as the "chromatic 2-5" **A♭m7–D♭7** resolving down to **ii** (Gm7–C7). Function-wise it doesn't matter whether the tonic is read as I or iii (Am) — same melody either way.
- **Dominant of the dominant** (D7, "two of two") for 2 bars — Larsen flags this as a spot where he often reaches for a **Lydian dominant**, resolving to the actual **ii-V** (Gm7-C7). Getting back to IV from there: either a **straight cadence to IV**, or the **tritone substitution** — **Fm–B7** resolving to B♭ — both are legitimate, period-appropriate options.
- Second half repeats the same shape, with a real turnaround at the end: **Cm7–F7** (a I-vi-ii-V-shaped ending since the tune starts on IV, the turnaround has to route back to IV: a smooth-sounding option given is **F → Dm7 → D♭m(chromatic passing minor) → Cm7 → F7**) — the added D♭m bar is explicitly framed as a comper's liberty: it can clash a beat against the melody, and that's fine, because it resolves immediately and a soloist never has to reconcile it the way they would if it were "in" the tune's own harmony.

### ♯IV° diminished — two derivable scale options (new vs. existing vault coverage)
- Larsen explicitly avoids the **symmetric (whole-half) diminished scale** as a first choice on diminished chords generally — calls it "not really what we hear in terms of music... a theoretical/mathematical construct," useful as an effect but not what's naturally happening in most standards' actual harmony (he doubts any standard's own written harmony genuinely calls for the diminished scale).
- Instead, for a ♯IV° sitting inside a major-key tune (worked on F major over B°/A♭): take the parent major scale and change **one note** to make the diminished chord's own tones fit —
  - **Option 1 — turn the 4th degree, keep everything else major-scale-derived, then sharp the 5th too:** turning B♭→B (the ♯4) alone just gives F Lydian/C major; to also cover the A♭ (♭6 relative to F) needed for the chord, sharp the **5th degree too** (G→G♯) — the resulting scale is **F harmonic minor**, read from the ♯IV° chord's own perspective as its usable chord-scale.
  - **Option 2 — the harmonic-major derivation (not previously documented in the vault):** instead of raising G to G♯, lower the **6th degree** (A→A♭) — the resulting scale is **C harmonic major** — Larsen's actual working choice, and what he draws extensions from on this chord.
  - Practical rule: **check the ♯IV° chord against the tune's own melody** in whatever standard it appears in — that's what tells you which of these (or neither, if it's a symmetric-diminished-scale situation) actually fits, rather than defaulting to the diminished scale by habit. → routed to [[Diminished Chords]].

### Chorus 1 — Drop-2 voicing choices, chord by chord
- **B♭maj7 as a superimposed D minor 7 shape** — playing a Dm7 grip over the B♭ bass gives B♭maj9 (the same "different chord, same shape" arpeggio logic Larsen applies elsewhere to scales/arpeggios) — flagged caveat: **the 9th (C) isn't available inside a "pure" Dm7 reading**, everything else lines up.
- **E♭7 thought of as E♭ minor 6** — same notes, different mental label; the practical note is to still *hear* it as E♭7 in context, not get stuck on the "minor 6" framing as anything more than a fingering shortcut.
- **F major voiced as an A minor shape** (several interchangeable A-minor-family voicings work equally well here — not a single "correct" one).
- **♯IV° (A♭ diminished) voiced with an added ♭6** — a deliberate color choice, motivated directly by melody-note frequency: the ♭6-over-the-♯IV°-diminished color shows up as a common melody note across multiple standards (cited: "I Remember You").
- **The two-5-one back to IV: Dm7/Dm6-family voicings**, related to the same superimposition logic as the E♭7 entry above.
- Recurring closing-F-chord note: **when the melody itself sits on the root/major-3rd zone that would clash with a major-7 voicing, don't reach for a major-7 chord there "even if you could get away with it"** — pick a plainer voicing (a D-minor-family grip) that supports the melody instead of fighting it; framed with a joke about getting fired if playing behind a singer.

### Chorus 2 — Triad-based comping, chord by chord
- **Core method: take the same drop-2/full-chord voicing from Chorus 1 and drop the bass note**, leaving a bare triad — worked explicitly: B♭maj7 minus the B♭ root = **D minor triad**; B♭m7 minus the root = **D♭ major triad** (which itself extends into a B♭m6/E♭-with-9th color, not a "pure" triad).
- **Discipline rule, stated directly and important:** even though you're physically playing only 3 notes, **keep thinking the full chord's actual name/function**, not the triad's own name — play a D minor shape over an F-major-key ii-V-I context but still *think* "this is the iii chord of F, not a random A minor" — the failure mode is building progressions in your head that don't actually exist in the tune because you started reasoning from the triad's own (wrong) root.
- **Melody-first mindset while triad-comping** — Larsen states this chorus leans more on tracking the melody's contour/rhythm than on chasing exact extensions; extensions get pulled in reactively ("I need to sort of pull something out") based on understanding the chord's role in the key, not from a pre-planned list.
- G7 comped via the same **diminished-triad-with-a-substituted-13th** logic as used on the ♯IV° chord in Chorus 1 — reused device, different chord.
- A named but unbuilt technique note flagged in-video: Larsen mentions "major-flat-5 triads" (has a name in Dutch music theory, no settled English name he knows of) show up constantly in this style of comping and are worth a dedicated future video — **not developed further in this video**, so left as a named-but-undocumented concept rather than routed anywhere concrete.

### Chorus 3 — Opening up: arpeggiation, sustained-note comping, and riff comping
- **Arpeggiate instead of strumming block chords** — mostly with the fingers (not exclusively pick) — pulling individual intervals out of a voicing rather than always striking everything simultaneously; framed as "using the guitar as an instrument a bit better," something Larsen dates to guitarists working roughly from the 1970s on (Bill Frisell, active from the early '80s, cited as a direct influence on this approach).
- **Sustain-a-note + play a moving melody underneath it as a fill** — hold one note ringing while a small stepwise line moves underneath/around it; described as needing to genuinely "combine everything you know... chords, intervals, and melodies... while comping," and something that evolved gradually out of already knowing the drop-2/triad/shell vocabulary rather than being learnable as a standalone trick. Practice method given: play the tune slowly, making small stepwise-moving melodies through the changes, rather than jumping straight to full open playing. → routed to [[Melodic-Harmonic Blending (Comping While Soloing)]].
- **Riff comping — a named, separate approach from open/free comping:** stick with a single rhythmic/melodic idea (a riff) for a stretch of the form — demonstrated as roughly **8 bars per idea**, tracking the tune's own AABA-shaped internal repetition (first 8 static-feeling bars get one riff, the more harmonically-moving 8 bars get another, then the first riff returns with the repeated A section, then a different tag/ending idea closes it out). Two stated reasons to use it: (1) **repetition itself builds energy and sits well in a groove** — especially valuable over a static Latin/bossa/samba feel where the guitar's part usually has to lock to one distinct repeating pattern regardless; (2) **riff comping is explicitly framed as the practice on-ramp to truly open/free comping** — quoting a teacher's point that even "open" playing with almost no audible groove still requires the whole band to have silently agreed on what groove they're *not* playing, so what you do play still has to fit that unplayed groove; riff-based practice through a tune builds the vocabulary and rhythmic habits that make genuinely free playing sound intentional instead of aimless. Also doubles as ear-training for absorbing a new rhythm into your default playing — take a rhythm you don't already reach for, force it through a whole tune's changes a few times, and it "sticks" as usable vocabulary afterward. → routed to [[Jazz Comping — Rhythmic Motif and Walking Bassline]].
- **Form-awareness while comping — deliberately know where the "one" and the top of the form are**, and choose consciously whether to spotlight that landmark or not; flags the real ensemble risk of a soloist who phrases *across* the top of the form (starting a phrase at the end of one chorus, resolving it into the next) — a comper who's too rigidly locked to the downbeat-of-the-form can actively work against that kind of soloist.

### Comping behind a soloist — philosophy and listening (extends [[Ensemble Listening and Comping Restraint]])
- **Give the soloist something to lean on — or, for some soloists, lean on *energy* instead of a fixed groove/vibe**, and push them; which one is right depends entirely on feel, worked out by playing with people repeatedly, not decided in the abstract.
- **Style/density spectrum, two named reference points:**
  - **Busy-but-not-in-the-way (hard bop):** Wes Montgomery with Wynton Kelly's trio, comping behind Cannonball Adderley — genuinely a lot of rhythmic activity, but skillful enough that it never crowds the soloist.
  - **Minimal, harmony-only, groove left open:** Bill Frisell with Paul Motian and Charlie Haden behind Joe Lovano on the "On Broadway" record series — very few notes, long sustained tones (Frisell's compressor pushes this further, at times reading as barely guitar-like), laying down harmony without trying to convey groove at all — deliberately leaves that job entirely to the rhythm section, giving them more freedom. Larsen states he personally identifies more with the Frisell camp, alongside Herbie Hancock and John Scofield as his biggest comping influences.
- **The rhythm-section-as-a-whole matters, not just the soloist** — listen for who's carrying the groove and what role each player has taken; the same comping choice reads differently depending on what the rest of the band is doing.
- **Named reference recordings to study specifically for comping** (not soloing): **Herbie Hancock with Tony Williams behind Miles Davis** — especially high interaction, sometimes reactive to the soloist and sometimes just internal rhythm-section conversation; **Wynton Kelly** — less overtly interactive than Hancock but very solid/tasteful, especially on *Smokin' at the Half Note*; **John Scofield on Joe Henderson's "So Near, So Far"** — called out as an "insane" comping (and soloing) performance in an all-star band context.
- **Behind a bass solo specifically: play less, and play softer/plainer chords** — a bass player is unusually easy to bury sonically, so restraint is even more load-bearing there than behind a horn/vocal soloist; add rhythmic activity only when the time itself needs clarifying, otherwise stay out of the way.
- **General practice advice for internalizing all of this: listen to records, not just written examples** — hearing how comping choices actually connect (or don't) to what a soloist and drummer are doing is, in Larsen's framing, something a written voicing chart can't teach on its own.
