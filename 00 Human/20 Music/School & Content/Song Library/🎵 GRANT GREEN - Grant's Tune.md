# Grant's Tune — Grant Green

> A Grant Green original from *Solid*, picked by audience poll. Nathan Borton's approach: rename the A section's two-chord vamp as a disguised 1-5-1 (D♭7 is G7's tritone sub), lay out four interchangeable soloing systems for it, then treat the B section's slash chords as ordinary E♭-rooted qualities once fingered with E♭ in the bass. → [[How To Solo Over Grant's Tune! (Grant Green)]]

## Chords

**Key:** C minor, modulating to E♭ major for the bridge — confirmed independently by jazzleadsheets.com's own analysis of the tune: chords "pivot between a tonic and a fifth in each measure," C minor in the A section (D♭7 alternating with G7) and E♭ major in the bridge.

**A-section vamp:** Cm7 – D♭7 (repeated) → **i – ♭II7** — a disguised **i–V–i**, since D♭7 is the tritone substitute for G7 (V of C minor). **A-section tag:** E♭ major → **plain ♭III** (relative major), no substitution.

**B section** (slash chords over a held E♭ bass): E♭ | G♭/E♭ | E♭ | D♭/E♭ → finger the upper triads with E♭ underneath and they resolve to ordinary **E♭ major – E♭ minor7 – E♭ major – E♭sus9**. → [[Chord Substitution]]

## Solo concepts by section (Nathan Borton's breakdown)

- **Over the Cm7–D♭7 vamp — four interchangeable systems, pick one and keep its partner sound consistent on the dominant:**
  1. **C blues scale**, hitting **E♭, F, or G** specifically over the dominant (D♭7/G7) — those three notes are chord/color tones of the dominant that also sit inside the C blues scale, so the line's shape doesn't change, it just resolves through those three notes as the harmony turns over.
  2. **C Dorian** (natural 6, ♭7) over the minor, paired with **A♭ melodic minor** over the dominant — A♭ melodic minor is G7's altered scale (melodic minor a half step above the dominant root), so the whole vamp reduces to just two key centers: C minor and A♭ minor.
  3. **C "minor diminished"** (the whole-half diminished scale off C), usable over *both* chords — caveat: the scale's **A natural and D natural sound a little weird against the D♭7/G7**. Fix: **start on E♭ or A and walk straight up** — works over either chord, best used as a transition device between the two or extra "scalier language," not a full-time solution.
  4. **Ignore the Cm7 and play G7♭9 the whole vamp** — the reduced/static "easy mode": treat the loop as one dominant sound rather than two chords.
  - **5-to-1 motifs** to open, close, or connect lines through the turnaround back into Cm7. → [[Turnarounds]], [[Guide Tones]]
- **Over the A-section tag (E♭ major):** plain tonic language — E♭ major vocabulary or enclosed chord tones of E♭maj7, no reharmonization.
- **Over the B section (E♭ major → E♭m7 → E♭ major → E♭sus9):** either **(a) modally** — treat each chord as its own brief key center and match the sound to each; or **(b) blend E♭ blues throughout, swapping only the 3rd** — flat 3rd over the E♭m7, major 3rd everywhere else. Recommended: **combine both**, moving between the modal and blues readings phrase to phrase rather than committing to one.

## Steal this

- **Rename a scary two-chord vamp before soloing over it.** "Minor to an unrelated dominant a half step away" hides a plain 1-5-1 once the tritone sub is spotted — the rename, not new vocabulary, is what unlocks it. → [[Chord Substitution]]
- **When a scale doesn't cleanly fit every chord in a short loop, use it as connective tissue between the chords instead of a full-time solution** — the C minor-diminished walk-up (from E♭ or A) is explicitly a transition device here, not the main system.
- **On slash chords over one held bass note, finger the upper triad with the bass underneath before assuming the progression is exotic** — E♭ | G♭/E♭ | E♭ | D♭/E♭ is just E♭ major/minor7/major/sus9 in ordinary clothes.
- **Practice both halves of the same skill inside one tune** — sitting on a single static sound over a long stretch, and moving one sound fluently across several chord qualities, are different muscles; this tune drills both in eight bars.
