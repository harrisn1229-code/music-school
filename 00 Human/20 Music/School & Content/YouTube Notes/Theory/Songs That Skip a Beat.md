# Songs That Skip a Beat

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N6Wtvs42Ne8)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N6Wtvs42Ne8)

## Synopsis

The presenter's own coined term, "the 3/4 trick": a song that's otherwise in 4/4 drops in a single one-off bar of 3/4, which reads to the ear as a bar that ends one beat early — a "skipped beat" that creates forward momentum/tension. Part of a 3-video arc: this video → [[4 More Songs That Skip a Beat]] (more examples of the same device, plus its beat-displacement cousin) → [[Songs That Add a Beat]] (the inverse trick — a one-off bar of 5/4).

## Techniques

- **The core mechanism**: the ear locks onto a 4-beats-per-bar pulse; when one bar unexpectedly has only 3 beats, the next bar's downbeat arrives a beat "early," reading as skipped/tripped-forward rather than as a deliberate meter change (as long as it's genuinely a one-off — see the "must be exactly one bar" caveat in the companion video).
- **"Heart of Glass" (Blondie)** — a 3/4 bar ends each bridge phrase early; the second half of the bridge then repeats the identical melody WITHOUT the missing beat (full 4/4), which reads as a satisfying resolution of the earlier tension.
- **"I Love Rock 'N' Roll" (Joan Jett)** — a subtler instance, tied closely to the chorus's cadence; the hook is heard once without the missing beat, then immediately again WITH it — a direct contrast that makes the device audible even to a casual listener; the song's final hook restatement resolves back to the full 4/4 version.
- **"All You Need Is Love" (Beatles)** — the most thorough use of the three: the verse constantly alternates 4/4 and 3/4 almost every bar, arguably better described as one repeating 7/4 phrase with occasional 4/4 rather than "4/4 with exceptions" — the presenter notes 7/4 notation would be tidier on paper but risks misleading a performer about how to actually feel the rhythm. Effect: constant forward momentum, vocal-line pauses becoming one beat shorter than they'd be in plain 4/4.
- **"I Say a Little Prayer" (Burt Bacharach/Dionne Warwick)** — layers the trick with a second irregularity: the chorus is built from 3-bar (not the usual 4-bar) phrases, each internally going 4/4-3/4-4/4 — both the missing beat AND the short phrase length push the rhythm forward.
- **"Livin' on a Prayer" (Bon Jovi)** — saves the device for one single high-impact moment: the final-chorus key change (already a classic pop trick on its own) is paired with a skipped beat, landing the listener in the new key one beat earlier than the bar would otherwise have ended — stacking two "surprise" devices at once for maximum impact.
