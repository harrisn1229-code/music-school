# Songs That Add a Beat

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qTdCzot0fqk)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qTdCzot0fqk)

## Synopsis

The requested inverse of [[Songs That Skip a Beat]]/[[4 More Songs That Skip a Beat]] — songs otherwise in 4/4 that drop in a one-off bar of 5/4 instead of 3/4. Third and final video in the arc.

## Techniques

- **"Don't Let Me Down" (Beatles)** — a 5/4 bar leads into each verse line, hidden extremely well: it lands during a break where the drums/other instruments drop out, so the listener isn't feeling a strong 4/4 pulse at that exact moment, letting an extra beat slip in almost unnoticed. Presenter's read on the songwriting cause: not a deliberate "let's add a beat" decision, but the natural result of fitting an already-written lyric/syllable count into the gap — felt right in the moment rather than planned.
- **"Chiquitita" (ABBA)** — an extra beat in the chorus is absorbed cleanly because the DRUM PATTERN itself adjusts: the snare normally falls on beats 2 and 4, and the drummer briefly breaks the repeating pattern on the bar's 5th beat specifically so the snare lands back on 2-and-4 in the next bar rather than drifting onto 1-and-3 (which would sound wrong) — a concrete example of a rhythm section actively compensating for an odd bar rather than just riding through it.
- **"Jóga" (Björk)** — a more OBVIOUS instance: rather than blending the extra beat into the bar, the bar completes a normal 4/4, THEN an audibly separate extra beat is tacked on (conceptually a 4/4 bar followed by its own tiny "1/4 bar," even though no one would actually notate it that way) — reinforced by the kick drum hitting on that extra beat too, making it sound like its own miniature downbeat/new bar rather than a smooth extension.
- **"Mother" (Pink Floyd)** — the most structurally complex example: the verse is in 5/8, meaning a listener tapping along on the expected quarter-note pulse will drift onto the offbeat without realizing why. The unsettled rhythmic feel is argued to directly support the lyric's theme (a surface-level "quaint folk ballad" that's actually about a paranoid, anxious child seeking reassurance from an overprotective mother, touching on bombs/trusting the government) — contrasted directly against the chorus, which relaxes into a comforting, lullaby-like 6/8 as the mother's reassuring voice takes over. Production trivia: the verse's odd meter reportedly proved difficult enough for Pink Floyd's regular drummer Nick Mason that, under recording-schedule pressure, a different drummer was brought in to record the part.
