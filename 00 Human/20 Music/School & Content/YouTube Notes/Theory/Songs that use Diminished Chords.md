# Songs that use Diminished Chords

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2Q_dpxMb328)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2Q_dpxMb328)

## Synopsis

A comprehensive tour of diminished chords in real songs — chromatic passing diminished triads as "harmonic glue" between diatonic chords, the line-cliché connection, why the fully diatonic vii° triad is rare (it's a weak substitute for the dominant 7th) while the natural-minor ii° is genuinely useful, the flexible/ambiguous resolution diminished chords allow, and the three distinct 7th-chord types that can sit on a diminished triad (diminished major 7, half-diminished/m7♭5, and fully diminished 7) — including the quirky fact that inverting a diminished 7 chord produces another, differently-named diminished 7 chord with identical notes.

## Techniques

- **Diminished triad = minor triad with the 5th lowered a semitone** — inherently dissonant in isolation, but functions as a smooth connective device in context.
- **Chromatic passing diminished chord, ♯5 type — "harmonic glue" between two diatonic chords a whole step apart**: "Bennie and the Jets" (Elton John) — G♯dim bridges I (G) to ii (Am), a smoother climb than jumping directly. "Don't Look Back in Anger" (Oasis) — bridges V (G) to vi (Am), simultaneously building tension and smoothing the move; without it, sitting on G for two extra bars would lose the payoff of landing on Am.
- **Chromatic passing diminished chord, ♯4 (raised 4th) type — common in jazz**: "Georgia on My Mind" (Hoagy Carmichael) — lifts IV up to I (in 2nd inversion) for silky voice-leading across three chords. "All Star" (Smash Mouth, chorus) — climbs IV (B) up to a ♯4 diminished (Cdim) then returns DOWN to IV rather than resolving upward — the diminished chord as a brief tension-and-release detour rather than a climbing bridge. "My Iron Lung" (Radiohead, guitar riff) — outlines a similar chromatic diminished shape at the riff's end for an unsettled quality.
- **Brief/passing use still adds cohesion even when structurally unnecessary**: "When the Party's Over" (Billie Eilish, bridge) — a brief A♯dim guides IV up to V; the song would work moving straight IV→V, but the diminished passing chord adds a moment of interest. "Bridge Over Troubled Water" (Simon & Garfunkel, chorus) — a brief F♯dim gives a sweeter resolution to the tonic, paired with stepwise bass motion (A→B).
- **The line-cliché connection**: lowering one voice of a static chord by a semitone step-by-step (e.g. F major's bass note walking F→E→E♭) naturally produces a diminished chord partway through the line, even though the writer may only be thinking "move this one note down." "Life on Mars?" (David Bowie, verse) and "Your Song" (Elton John, end of chorus) both feature a line cliché whose middle step is a diminished chord. → [[Line Cliché]] (existing page on this device).
- **Diatonic vii° (built on the 7th scale degree) is genuinely rare in practice** — theoretically available without leaving the major scale (B° in C major), but the ONLY real examples found are classical (Mozart's Piano Sonata No. 5). Reason: vii° is really an incomplete dominant 7th chord in disguise — the top three notes of G7 (in C major) ARE a B diminished triad, so vii° and V7 do near-identical resolving-to-I work, but vii° is a "lackluster" version since it lacks the root that makes V7's pull to I unambiguous.
- **The natural-minor ii° is a genuinely useful diatonic diminished chord, unlike its major-scale vii° counterpart** — built on the 2nd degree of natural minor without any chromatic alteration. "This Love" (Maroon 5) uses it directly.
- **Diminished chords resolve flexibly, almost anywhere nearby** — being inherently unstable/dissonant, the ear is satisfied hearing a diminished chord collapse into ANY nearby major or minor chord, especially when each note moves by the smallest possible distance (minimal voice-leading). "Because" (The Beatles) demonstrates two different resolutions of the identical D diminished chord across two verses: verse 1 resolves down to the tonic (D minor), verse 2 resolves instead to F♯ major — same chord, two different but equally satisfying landings.
- **Three distinct 7th-chord types built on a diminished triad** (a genuine 3rd option beyond major/minor chords' usual two): (1) **diminished-major 7th** (rare, included for completeness). (2) **half-diminished 7th / m7♭5** — a diminished triad + minor 7th (labeled with a slashed degree symbol, or more commonly "m7♭5" for clarity). (3) **fully diminished 7th** — a diminished triad + diminished 7th (i.e. three stacked minor 3rds); labeled °7 or "dim7."
- **Fully diminished 7th examples**: "Love Is a Losing Game" (Amy Winehouse) opens on a sustained F°7 that resolves to the tonic Cmaj7, setting the song in motion. "God Only Knows" (The Beach Boys) — a C°7 resolves neatly to E major (2nd inversion), giving a mid-verse sway of tension and release. "We Are the Champions" (Queen, chorus) — uses TWO different diminished 7ths: an F°7 lifting to G, then a B♭°7 building tension before resolving to the tonic F. "Michelle" (The Beatles) — uses two diminished 7ths in the guitar/vocal combined (guitar plays plain diminished triads; the vocal melody supplies the 7th) — notable because the two chords (D°7 and B°7) are actually **inversions of each other with identical notes**.
- **A genuine notational quirk, worth remembering when labeling**: because a diminished 7th chord is three stacked minor 3rds, inverting it doesn't produce "[chord] over [bass note]" the way inverting a major/minor chord does — it produces a DIFFERENT diminished 7th chord name entirely, since the notes are symmetrically spaced. Demonstrated directly with the Michelle example above (B°7 inverted = D°7, not "B°7/D").
- **Half-diminished (m7♭5) most commonly appears as part of a ii-V-i progression**: "You Never Give Me Your Money" (The Beatles) uses it this way to return to the A minor tonic after a harmonic detour. Also cited: "My Funny Valentine" (jazz standard) and "Between the Sheets" (The Isley Brothers).
