# [Guitar Soloing Tips] We Don't Talk Anymore - Charlie Puth Style Lesson

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TG7VCfJin5U)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TG7VCfJin5U)

## Synopsis

A three-tier soloing lesson (beginner / intermediate / advanced) from Daniel Seriff, built around a backing track he made for Charlie Puth's "We Don't Talk Anymore." The song sits in the parent scale of E major / C♯ minor (progression: A–B–C♯m–G♯m–A–B–C♯m–C♯m, verse section only — the full track also has a chorus and other sections Seriff skips for the lesson), which puts the whole thing squarely in C♯ minor pentatonic territory. Rather than teaching the song itself (no chords, comping, or melody breakdown — this is a pure improvisation-technique lesson that happens to use the song as a practice vehicle), Seriff layers three tiers of pentatonic-box vocabulary onto the same "box shape one" position: beginners get slide-into-the-9th-fret ornamentation, hammer-ons, pull-offs, and the "don't always start at the bottom of the scale" habit break; intermediates get a colorful added note (the 9/D♯, credited to Jimmy Herring and Derek Trucks) plus a vocalize-a-rhythm constraint-practice exercise; advanced players get two-string-group fragment practice, downward pickslanting, and a "bursting" practice philosophy that explicitly rejects slow-metronome ramp-up in favor of relaxed, away-from-the-track speed bursts. Most valuable part: the tiered structure itself is reusable — each level takes the *same* pentatonic box and adds exactly one new layer of vocabulary on top, which is a clean template for how to re-approach any backing track at increasing skill levels rather than treating "beginner," "intermediate," and "advanced" as separate scales to learn.

## What's taught

### Beginner — ornamenting the plain minor pentatonic box
- **The song's key/progression:** parent scale E major / C♯ minor; verse progression A–B–C♯m–G♯m–A–B–C♯m–C♯m (the lesson's backing track loops just this verse section). C♯ minor pentatonic (C♯ E F♯ G♯ B) is the base scale for the whole lesson, played in "box shape one."
- **Slide into notes from one fret below, on the pointer finger's row of the box (the 9th fret across every string).** Because every note the pointer finger plays in this box sits at the 9th fret, sliding up into each one from the 8th fret is available "at any time" without leaving the scale. **Don't linger on the approach note** — slide through it quickly rather than resting on it, or it reads as a wrong/out-of-tune note instead of an ornament. → [[Blues Lead Phrasing and Touch]]
- **Hammer-ons: fret the lower note on a string, then hammer the pinky onto the note two frets up, repeated string by string through the whole box shape** (worked example: 9th fret high E, hammer to 12th fret). Run across all six strings for a rapid, textured pass through the scale rather than picking every note.
- **Pull-offs (Seriff's preferred term: "pluck-offs") are the hammer-on's mirror — start on the higher fretted note, then pull the fretting finger off to sound the lower note already held down.** Physically it's a small pluck/pull of the string by the left hand, not just lifting off — but pull gently enough that the note doesn't go sharp or get pulled off the fretboard entirely. Fingering note: Seriff uses his pinky on the top fret of the shape where many players use the ring finger — either works.
- **Don't always start a phrase at the bottom of the scale.** Named directly as a common beginner habit (spending too much time down at the root) — starting phrases from the *middle* of the box instead is called out as sounding noticeably better and varies the rhythm/contour of what's played.
- **Vary rhythm and technique deliberately rather than repeating one ornament on a loop** — the explicit framing is "trying to sing with my guitar," using slides/hammer-ons/pull-offs as expressive tools rather than a fixed pattern to run every time.
- **Isolated-technique practice routine: drill one ornament at a time against the backing track** (a full pass of only slides, then only hammer-ons, then only pull-offs) before combining them — and it's fine to not be fast yet; the isolation pass is about getting the technique under the fingers first, speed comes later.

### Intermediate — the added color note + constraint practice
- **Add the D♯ (the 9/2nd scale degree) into the C♯ minor pentatonic box — a note the plain minor pentatonic doesn't have.** C♯ minor pentatonic is 1–♭3–4–5–♭7 (no 2); adding the 9 makes it a hexatonic "blues pentatonic + 9" scale. Fretboard spots given: 11th fret of the low E string, then normal pentatonic notes on the A/D/G/B strings, then 11th fret again on the high E string — the 9 bookends the shape at both outer strings. Credited directly to **Jimmy Herring** and **Derek Trucks**, whose playing Seriff cites as where he originally picked up the sound. → [[Dorian from the Minor Pentatonic Box]] (the same "pentatonic + 9" hexatonic stopping point is already documented there, credited to the same two players, from a different Seriff video)
- **Treat the 9 as a passing tone, not a landing note — don't land directly on it, play through it.** Explicit nuance beyond just "add the note": the 9 sounds best moving through on the way to somewhere else rather than sustained/resolved on, similar in spirit to how a blue note is used.
- **The blue note (♭5) is already implicitly in play alongside the 9** — located at the 10th fret of the A string and the 12th fret of the G string in this box — giving a "blues pentatonic scale with the 2/9 added" as the full working note set for the intermediate tier: minor pentatonic + ♭5 + 9. → [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]]
- **Stop practicing the scale strictly up and down once you know the shape — make music with it immediately instead.** Framed as a mindset shift Seriff made "a few years ago": once the scale's fingering is known, further straight-up-and-down drilling has diminishing returns compared to phrasing musically within it.
- **Constraint exercise: vocalize a short rhythm out loud first, then force that exact rhythm through the scale, varying only the notes/pitches used.** Explicit method: hum/scat a rhythmic cell (Seriff's example: "dr-da-ba-da-da"), then play that same rhythmic shape repeatedly on the guitar while changing which scale notes land on it, cycling to a new vocalized rhythm every few repetitions. **Works down to a single note** — the rhythm alone, played on one pitch, is a valid version of the drill. Framed explicitly as a *constraint* exercise: deliberately restricting freedom (to just a rhythm) produces more usable ideas than open noodling, because the constraint gives the practice session direction. → [[Solo Dynamics and Arc]]

### Advanced — string groups, pickslanting, and "bursting"
- **Practice the scale in two-string groups rather than across the whole box at once — start from the highest strings, not the lowest.** Called out directly against a common bad habit: guitarists default to starting from the low strings; Seriff starts each string-group drill from strings 1–2 and works down (2–3, 3–4, 4–5, 5–6), playing all the scale notes available on just that string pair in sixteenth notes, picking every note.
- **Two-string groups are demonstrated both descending (from the 1–2 pair down to 5–6) and ascending (from 5–6 up to 1–2)** — same fragments, both directions, layered in one at a time (add the 2–3 group to the 1–2 group, then 3–4, etc.) until the full box is covered as a chain of two-string fragments.
- **This whole exercise is explicitly downward pickslanting.** Named directly as the picking-mechanics category the two-string-group exercise falls under — Seriff doesn't explain the mechanic itself in this video (flagged it as a "leave a comment and I'll explain" aside) but states it plainly as the technique underlying why these fragments are picked the way they are.
- **If a string-group fragment isn't clean, break it down further and "burst" it rather than grinding it slowly with a metronome.** Named practice philosophy, stated as a correction to something Seriff used to believe: he used to think slow-metronome ramp-up (a little faster each rep) was the right way to build speed, but now considers "bursting" — playing a short fragment at full/relaxed speed in short attempts, away from the backing track or any click, without demanding perfect accuracy each time — the better method. The goal of a burst rep is to "trigger the nervous system" to learn the physical motion, not to nail the notes cleanly every time. **Caveat stated directly: still practice slowly sometimes too** — bursting is one aspect of practice, not a replacement for all of it, and you should still check in that it doesn't sound like "junk" the whole time.
- **Two-string groups generalize to three-string and four-string groups** — Seriff names this as future-lesson material, not covered here, but flags it as the natural next step once two-string groups are solid.
- **Personal endorsement:** Seriff credits two-string-group practice specifically with improving his own pickslanting technique "like crazy," beyond just being a useful drill.
