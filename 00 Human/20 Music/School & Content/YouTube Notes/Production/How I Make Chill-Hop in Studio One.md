# How I Make Chill-Hop in Studio One

## Synopsis

A live-stream "weekly challenge" entry (week 17, chill-hop, stock instruments, made in **Studio One** — a different DAW than Nate's Ableton Live, techniques below are DAW-agnostic) where Max Konyi builds a chill-hop/lo-fi-hip-hop beat from scratch in real time at 86 BPM. The session is a hybrid of real physical instruments (his own guitar and bass) and stock synths/samplers, built off a beatboxed groove template rather than programmed drums first. He walks a full signal path: beatbox the feel → program drums to match → sketch a jazzy chord progression → lock harmony with a quick guitar take → design a wobbling muted-saw synth lead → smash the bass for sub weight → layer a distorted/filtered piano texture and a stock pad → arrange a drop/fill/return → cut a quick pitch-corrected vocal ad-lib → end on a single sustained note. It's a compact, instructive demonstration of "sketch loose and human first, refine after" composition, plus several concrete chill-hop drum-kit and sound-design choices.

## Techniques

**Workflow — beatbox the groove before programming it**
- **Beatbox the target groove into the track first, then program drums to match** — used specifically for beat-based music because "your voice and body can groove better than your fingers can" (unless you're a very strong player); vocalizing a rhythm generates far more usable groove ideas, much faster, than programming directly. The beatboxed take also reveals the song's swing/feel before a single MIDI note is placed — this pass was clearly swung ("we're swinging, swanging"), which then got dialed in on the actual drum grid.
- **Duplicate a section, strip the melodic parts, and let the drums alone carry a breakdown/build** — for a fill/drop section, duplicate the whole arrangement, delete the melody parts, quantize the bass, and lean on the drums (described as feeling "heavy," with "the slug" on two specific hits) before bringing the rest back in — a simple subtractive arrangement move for a beat-tape-style breakdown.
- **End on a single sustained note ("a Max ending")** — a personal signature: rather than a full outro section, the track just resolves to one held note and stops — noted as a favorite, low-effort but effective way to close a beat.

**Drum kit — a chill-hop kit built from repurposed/found sounds**
- **Main kick tight and pitched high** for a chill-hop-appropriate punch that doesn't dominate.
- **A second "kick" repurposed entirely as a snare** — the sample is the same kick source but pitched way up so it reads as a snare/"dum," not a kick — a cheap way to get a kit-cohesive snare tone that matches the kick's character. → [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **Three different clap layers, panned around and kept at lower volume** — width and texture from clap layering rather than one loud clap.
- **Jazz hi-hats** (softer, less aggressive hat character, fitting the genre's jazzy harmonic vocabulary).
- **Toms built from an unidentified "weird sample" with the filter automated** — an improvised, non-standard tom source shaped into usable toms via filter movement rather than a purpose-made tom sample.
- **Shaker + a couple of cymbals** round out the kit; a cymbal is used specifically to mark the transition into the fill/drop.

**Harmony — a jazzy chill-hop progression**
- **Worked chord progression:** Dm9 → Am7(11) → F♯m7♭5 → Fmaj7 → E♭9(♯11) → back to Dm → Am, then on the repeat: F♯m7♭5 → Am9 → Am6 → an "altered chord" *(qualitative — exact voicing not specified in the transcript)* — a dense extended/altered jazz-harmony vocabulary layered onto a chill-hop groove, consistent with the genre's jazz-rap/lo-fi-hop lineage. → [[Neo-Soul - Lo-fi]]
- **Lock the harmony with a quick, deliberately rough guitar take before finalizing anything** — "provisional guitars," basic amp patch, just to hear the chords in context; the pattern is expected to change later, the point is confirming the harmony reads well over the beat before committing.

**Sound design**
- **Muted saw-wave lead with an LFO-driven pitch wobble, performed live via the mod wheel** — LFO1 routed to both oscillator 1 and oscillator 2 pitch, with the LFO's depth/effect gated by the mod wheel (mod wheel down = no wobble, mod wheel up = the wobble kicks in) — a simple, expressive, performable modulation patch rather than a static LFO. → [[Modulation]], [[Wavetable]]
- **Distort-and-filter a piano sample into a crushed background texture ("doohickey")** — take a clean piano sound, push heavy distortion and filtering on it, and use the wrecked result purely as a low-key textural layer under the main parts, not as a lead instrument. → [[Saturation and Distortion]]
- **EQ shelf boost for extra sub on a real bass take** — after tracking his own bass (self-deprecating about the playing), a low-shelf EQ boost adds the sub weight the raw take was missing. → [[EQ]], [[BASS Slot]]
- **A stock "chill wave" pad layered in as the harmonic bed** — a ready-made stock pad patch reached for directly rather than designed from scratch, used to fill out the chord layer once the lead/bass/guitar sketch was locked. → [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **Quick vocal ad-lib, pitch-corrected with Melodyne** to stay in tune — a fast, low-commitment vocal pass leaned on pitch-correction software rather than multiple vocal takes.

## Sound/tone selection

- **Pitched-up kick sample repurposed as the snare** — reuses the kick's own timbral character for kit cohesion instead of sourcing a separate snare sample. → [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **"Weird sample" toms** — an improvised, non-drum-kit sample shaped into toms via automated filtering; illustrates that tom character can come from anywhere, not just dedicated tom samples.
- **Distorted/filtered piano as a texture layer**, not a melodic part.
- **Stock "chill wave" pad** as the fast path to a genre-appropriate harmonic bed.
- **Real guitar + real bass blended with stock synths/samplers** — a hybrid live-instrument/ITB workflow rather than an all-synthetic or all-live approach.
