# Chord Voicings

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Chords]], guitar side. **Same chord, different arrangement of its notes — and on guitar that choice is most of the sound.** [[Chord Inversions]] covers which note goes in the bass, mostly from a piano angle; this covers the guitar reality, which is that you rarely play all the notes and the interesting decision is *which ones to leave out*.

Built largely from [[Joe Pass Guitar Chords - Enrichment]], which is a 24-page dictionary of exactly this.

---

## Chord vs. voicing vs. shape

Three genuinely separate things, named explicitly by Jared Borkowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared) but implicit in every section of this page: a **chord** is the abstract pitch content — "C major" means C-E-G, any order, any octave, no fretboard involved yet. A **voicing** is the specific order and interval spacing those notes are arranged in — root-3rd-5th stacked as close together as possible is a different voicing from 3rd-5th-root-with-the-root-an-octave-up, even though both are still "C major." A **shape** is the specific physical fretboard fingering used to realize a given voicing — the same voicing can be fingered as an open-position shape, a shape at the 5th fret, or a shape rooted off a different string, all genuinely different shapes playing the identical voicing; a piano player can play that same voicing with zero relationship to any guitar shape at all. Slide a whole shape up or down the neck by a fret and the voicing stays identical while the chord it's voicing changes. — Jared Borkowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared), [[Chord voicings guitar lesson - Unlimited Chord Options!]]

**How to move between them — octave-shift voicing discovery.** Take a known chord shape, move one note up or down an octave (to another instance of the same pitch elsewhere on the neck), and if that lands two notes on the same string, cascade the fix by moving the newly-colliding note to its own nearest octave too. This is the concrete mechanism behind this page's close-position/open-position distinction below — a close voicing becomes an open one exactly by moving an interior note up an octave and leaving a gap. Full method, the collision-resolution mechanic, and four worked chord examples (C major, A major, B7, D minor 7) with three song ties → [[Octave-Shift Voicing Discovery]].

## The core idea: chords are sounds, not spellings

Joe Pass files every chord into **six categories of sound**: **Major · Seventh · Augmented · Minor · Diminished · Minor 7th ♭5**. Any symbol you meet reduces to one of them, and the substitutes available to you come from that category's pool.

| Symbol you see | Reduces to | Category |
|---|---|---|
| C, C6, Cma7, Cma9, C6/9 | C major | **Major** |
| C7, C9, C11, C13, C7♯5, C7♭9 | C7 | **Seventh** |
| Cm, Cm6, Cm7, Cm9, Cm11 | Cm | **Minor** |
| C°, C°7 | C° | **Diminished** |
| C+, C7♯5 (as a colour) | C+ | **Augmented** |
| Cm7♭5, Cø7 | Cm7♭5 | **m7♭5** |

That reduction is the practical skill. Faced with `E♭ma9♯11` at speed, you need "it's a major sound" in under a second, not a spelling.

## Which notes actually matter

For a four-note chord you have six strings and two hands. Something has to go. The priority order:

1. **The 3rd and the 7th — the guide tones.** These define quality and function: 3rd says major or minor, 7th says dominant or major-7th. Lose either and the chord stops being itself. Everything else is optional.
2. **The extension you actually want to hear** — the 9th, 13th, ♯11, ♭9, whatever the colour of the moment is.
3. **The root** — droppable. In a band the bass has it. Dropping it is what makes a **rootless voicing**, and it frees a finger for colour.
4. **The 5th** — the first thing to go, almost always. It adds nothing to identity. (Exception: an **altered** 5th — ♭5 or ♯5 — is a colour tone, not a 5th, and stays.)

**Shell voicing** = root + 3rd + 7th only. Three notes, unambiguous, works anywhere, and it's the fastest way to comp through a tune you don't know. **Rootless** = 3rd + 7th + extensions. **Guide-tone comping** — playing only 3rds and 7ths through the changes — is the standard first exercise, and it's worth doing on its own before adding anything.

**Concrete way to build a rootless voicing without recalculating from a rule table: replace the root with the diatonic neighbor tone a whole step away — the 9th above, or the 7th below.** Both destinations are notes you probably want to hear anyway (the 9th is a colour extension; the 7th turns a triad into a 7th-chord voicing), so the swap does double duty. Worked examples: an **F major triad** with its root replaced by the major 7th (whole step below) → a rootless **F major 7** — and the resulting shape is physically identical to an A minor triad, the same Fmaj7/Am relationship [[Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords]] derives from the rule table; a **G7, first inversion** with its (open-string) root replaced by the 9th (whole step above) → the shape becomes a **half-diminished chord shape in root position**, reread as a rootless **G9**. Stacks with this page's own "the 5th is the first thing to go" rule: take that rootless G9 (3-♭7-9-5) and replace its 5th with the **13th** (another whole step up) for a rootless **G13** (3-♭7-9-13, no root, no 5th) — playable an octave up on the top four strings too. — Jared Borkowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared), [[Rootless Voicings - WOW!]]

Pass's book demonstrates this constantly without naming it: `E B D G` printed under a diagram is a **Cmaj9 with no C in it** — 3rd, 7th, 9th, 5th.

## The named voicing families

| Family | What it is | Sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| **Close** | All notes inside one octave | Dense, cluttered on guitar, mostly impractical |
| **Drop 2** | Take a close voicing, move the **2nd-highest note down an octave** | The workhorse. Sits on strings 5-4-3-2 (warm) or 4-3-2-1 (bright) |
| **Drop 3** | Move the **3rd-highest** down an octave | Wider, bass note + a gap. Strings 6-4-3-2 |
| **Shell** | Root, 3rd, 7th | Lean, unambiguous, leaves room |
| **Rootless** | 3rd, 7th, extensions | The bass has the root; you have the colour |
| **Quartal** | Stacked 4ths | Modal, ambiguous, modern — not in the Pass book (fretboard shapes on [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]) |

Drop 2 is the one worth systematising first: it's the most common jazz-guitar voicing family and its four inversions across two string sets covers most comping situations.

## Voice leading is the actual point

A voicing choice is only good relative to the one before it. Three rules that carry almost everything:

1. **Move the least.** Between two chords, hold every common tone and move the rest by step. `Dm7 → G7`: keep the F and C, move A→B and D→D. Two fingers.
2. **The top note is a melody.** Pass says it flatly in the blues section: *"Try to sustain lead notes from one chord to the next. For example, C9 to F13, sustain the G on top."* Reharmonise **underneath** a held top note. This is the difference between comping that sounds like a progression and comping that sounds like a piece of music.
3. **Approach chromatically.** Any chord can be entered from a half step above or below — slide the whole shape in. Pass builds a 12-bar blues out of one fingering moved in half steps. Whole steps work too, and diminished/augmented shapes connect dominants because they're symmetric.
4. **A rigid shape works too — voice-lead a whole voicing as one unit through an entire cadence.** Take one close-interval, incomplete 3-note voicing and move every note the SAME direction by the SAME interval to land on the next chord — e.g. raise a Dm-family voicing a whole step to get a usable G7♭9, then raise it a whole step again for a usable Cmaj7 — covering a full ii-V-I from one mechanically-transposed shape rather than deriving three unrelated voicings. Pairs naturally with incompleteness: the higher the register or the more altered the dominant, the less a voicing needs to spell every chord tone, since the tensions/alterations already signal the chord's identity. — Gilad Hekselman / Lage Lund / Nelson Veras-style voicings, via Jens Larsen, [[Modern 3 note voicings and voice leading - How to find new jazz chords]]

## Comping, practically

- **Leave space.** The most common failure is playing too much. Chords on beats 2 and 4, or one chord per bar, will nearly always sit better than constant strumming.
- **Play only the strings indicated.** Pass is explicit that several of his voicings sound wrong if you fill in the muted strings. Muted strings are part of the voicing.
- **Register matters more than you think.** The same chord low on the neck is mud under a vocal and fine as a solo statement. When something else occupies the low mids, comp higher. This is an arrangement fix for what would otherwise show up as a mix problem — see [[Masking and Carving]].
- **Hybrid picking** — pick plus 2nd and 3rd fingers — makes the open-voiced shapes (skipped strings) playable. Pass calls it common practice. For a full drill system on the mechanics (planting, finger order, block-chord evenness) see [[Hybrid Picking Exercises]].
- **Two-chord-per-bar reharmonisation** is the highest-value trick: a static bar of `Dm7` becomes `Dm7 | Em7 A7`, `Dm7 | B♭7 E♭9`, `Dm7 | G°`… Full menus in [[Joe Pass Guitar Chords - Enrichment]] §Standard Pattern Chord Substitutions.

## When there's no answer: ambiguous chords

Not every incomplete voicing resolves to one clear chord label, and that's a legitimate stopping point, not a failure. Most often it's a **missing 3rd** that causes this — without it, major/minor can't be determined no matter which note is assumed to be the root. Worked example: a shape where the bottom note as root gives ♭9+5 with no 3rd; a different note as root gives major-7+♭5; the unplayed note as root (rootless) gives ♭9, 5, and ♯11 — three readings, none a complete, unambiguous chord. Context (key, direction of resolution) can sometimes narrow an ambiguous shape down, but in a vacuum it's fine to just note the tensions present and call it "ambiguous" rather than force a label onto it. — Jared Borkowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared), [[Rootless Voicings - WOW!]]

## Getting these under the fingers

The book's own method — and it is a good one — is **ear-first, not name-first**: learn a grip exactly as written, play it in context against the category it belongs to, and only then break it into partials or transpose it. Everything is written in C and meant to be moved to all twelve keys.

A workable order:

1. **Shell voicings** for all six categories, two string sets. Comp a tune with nothing else.
2. **Guide tones only** through a set of changes — 3rds and 7ths, no root, no extensions.
3. **Drop 2**, one string set, all inversions, one chord quality at a time.
4. **Add one colour tone** — the 9th on major and minor, the 13th on dominants.
5. **Altered dominants** last: ♭9, ♯9, ♭5, ♯5. `G7♯5♯9` is the endpoint and it's four notes.
6. **Then the Pass dictionary** as vocabulary to raid, category by category.

**Ear-training add-on for rootless voicings specifically:** hold a rootless voicing and try to sing the unplayed root. There's no reference pitch to latch onto, so it forces hearing the root purely in relationship to the sounding notes — a stated route to actually internalizing a harmonic progression rather than just memorizing shapes. — Jared Borkowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared), [[Rootless Voicings - WOW!]]

- **The "hub-latching approach" — a named practice method for internalizing every inversion of one chord voicing, plus a rooted-vs-rootless comping judgment call.** Pick a familiar root-position voicing as the "hub"/home base, build a path across the chord's other tones (3rd, 5th, ♭7 for a dominant 7), play the inversion rooted on each stop, and jump back to the hub between excursions rather than walking a single inversion up the neck string by string — closer to real comping than a straight-line inversion drill. Worked concretely off D7 on two string-set roots. **Manual-derivation trick** for finding an inversion without memorizing a fixed shape: move a chord tone down to the closest other chord tone on the same string (root → nearest tone lands on the ♭7; 5th → nearest tone on its string lands on the 3rd). **Rooted-vs-rootless judgment call:** return to the rooted hub frequently and treat rootless/upper inversions as short "punches" you venture to and back from — this matters most in **solo guitar** with no bass covering the root; with a bass player present, the constraint relaxes and rootless upper voicings can be used much more freely. Once the path is memorized, drop the fixed order and jump freely hub-to-inversion-and-back — that random-access stage is what actually feeds real comping. - **The complete worked set of 12 E7 dominant-7th comping shapes (4 inversions × 3 adjacent four-string sets), built with a repeatable cycle method, plus a 3-stage practice progression for turning the shapes into real comping.** Each string set (top four D-G-B-e, middle four A-D-G-B, bottom four E-A-D-G) starts from the **same recipe** — root position voiced bottom-to-top **root-5th-♭7th-3rd** — then generates its own three inversions by cycling every note up to the next role in the chord (root→3rd's slot, 3rd→5th's slot, 5th→♭7th's slot, ♭7th→root's slot): 1st inv. **3rd-♭7th-root-5th**, 2nd inv. **5th-root-3rd-♭7th**, 3rd inv. **♭7th-3rd-5th-root** — full derivation method on [[Chord Inversions]]. - **Three physical dim7 shapes on guitar, plus a free-inversion trick from the chord's symmetry.** (1) 6th-string-rooted, A-string skipped — barred (1st finger bar + 3rd finger) or fully fingertip (3rd, 1st, 4th, 2nd fingers); (2) 5th-string-rooted, all fingertips, an easy "box" shape; (3) a higher voicing off the same root, also all fingertips. Because a dim7 is stacked minor 3rds (three consecutive 3-fret jumps, symmetric), sliding ANY of the three shapes up exactly 3 frets (one minor 3rd) produces the exact same four notes reordered — a free inversion with **zero new fingering to learn**, unlike a normal chord (e.g. major 7), where each inversion needs a genuinely new, harder shape. Drill: rotate through all three shapes off the same root, then walk each one up in minor thirds, crossing strings when convenient (same drill pattern as the single-note minor-third arpeggio exercise below). - **The three closed-position triad shapes on the top three strings (G-B-E), all three qualities, both as isolated grips and as a full diatonic run.** Worked in G major: **root position** (root lowest) — G major = frets 12-12-10, G minor = 12-11-10 (lower the 3rd on the B string), G diminished = 12-11-9 (also lower the 5th on the E string); **1st inversion** (3rd lowest) around the 3rd fret; **2nd inversion** (5th lowest, "looks like a D chord moved up") — worked example gives B minor = 7-7-7 (one finger barred across all three strings), C major = 9-8-8, D major = 7-7-5. **Diatonic triad-quality formula for any major key** — I ii iii IV V vi vii° = major minor minor major major minor diminished (G Am Bm C D Em F#dim in G) — the vii° is rare in pop music but worth knowing. - **On a 12-bar blues, cut a full CAGED barre shape to the top 4 strings and let the bass carry the root — it reads as an inversion in theory, not in the ear.** A full E-shape barre chord (root on the low E string) is mostly doubled notes and eats sonic space; dropping the low E and A strings leaves just the top 4 strings (D-G-B-e) with the ♭7 as the lowest note played — technically a first-inversion voicing, but since the band's actual bass player has the real root, it never *sounds* like an inversion (the perceived chord is the sum of everything the band plays, not the guitar part alone).
