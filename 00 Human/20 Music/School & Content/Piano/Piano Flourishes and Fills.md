# Piano Flourishes and Fills

Pianistic devices that read as impressive with relatively low technical cost — glissandi, arpeggiated runs, pedal point, and voice-led chord wandering. The common thread across all of them: a flashy gesture reads as random unless it resolves back to something stable (a root note, the tonic chord) at the end — the resolution is what converts a flourish into something that sounds deliberate rather than showing off.

## Seven core devices

1. **Black-note glissando** — the black keys form a pentatonic scale (E♭ minor pentatonic / F♯ major pentatonic depending on chosen root); pentatonic scales have no semitone intervals, so a fast slide across all the black keys can't produce a harsh clash — the interval floor (a whole tone) rules out the harshest dissonance a run could hit. "Foolproof" in that specific sense. Anchor it by ending on the root note. — "I Don't Like Mondays" (Boomtown Rats)
2. **White-note glissando** — the white keys are a C major scale, so sliding across them WILL clash on the semitone gaps (E-F, B-C), unlike the black-key version. Mitigate by going upward (semitone dissonance reads as less harsh higher on the keyboard) and always resolving onto C. — Louis Armstrong's "La Vie en Rose"
3. **Grace notes (acciaccatura)** sliding into a melody note from a semitone or tone below, rather than striking the target note directly — mimics how a vocalist approaches a pitch. Easiest version: sliding from a black key onto the white key immediately above it.
4. **Arpeggiated octave runs** — one chord shape (e.g. C-E-G-C) repeated across multiple octaves in a fast ascending/descending run. Sounds acrobatic but is mechanically one repeated shape — perceived difficulty is higher than conceptual difficulty. Works over any chord.
5. **Chromatic runs resolving onto a chord tone** — a run through all 12 semitones isn't tied to any key (unlike a diatonic run, which only fits its own key), so the fluid-motion practice transfers to every key. Keep it intentional-sounding by always landing on a note that belongs to the underlying chord.
6. **Pedal point** — hold one bass note fixed in the left hand under ANY chords in the right hand, including chords quite distant from the current key. The sustained bass note glues even a wild chord choice together, reading as sophisticated/cinematic. Resolve back to the tonic chord at the end to keep it sounding intentional.
7. **Chromatic voice-led arpeggio wandering** — start on any chord's broken-arpeggio pattern, then move exactly ONE note of the chord by one semitone; repeat, moving one note by one semitone each time. Because only one voice moves, and only by a semitone, the resulting progression is automatically well-voice-led without needing to plan the harmony in advance.
