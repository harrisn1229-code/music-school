# Bass Scales for Beginners (5 Stupidly Simple Steps)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-GNbG3clxF0)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-GNbG3clxF0)

## Synopsis

A BassBuzz beginner lesson that answers a different question than the channel's sibling video [[7 Days to Learning Bass Scales (Beginner Lesson)]]. That video teaches *five scale shapes* (major, major/minor pentatonic, minor, blues) over a 7-day rotation; this one teaches *one* shape (the major scale, from the same third-fret-of-the-A-string C position) but drills five sequential **skills for turning that one memorized shape into actual music** — the "Lego kit" analogy runs the whole video: a scale shape is a pre-sorted kit of compatible bricks, but a kit is useless without instructions for building something with it. The five steps escalate from pure mechanics to pure musicality: (1) nail the shape itself, with a named beginner failure mode (forcing a full-hand stretch instead of microshifting); (2) transpose the same shape to any starting note/key; (3) extend the shape's range up or down the neck by copying whole per-string "chunks" into an adjacent octave; (4) "steal like a bassist" — a structured four-step process for locating a well-known melody, entirely by ear, inside the memorized shape; and (5) the "Bingo method" — turn the scale's note-order into short numeric patterns, then systematically transpose that pattern through the scale to generate technical workouts or chord-connecting basslines. Jaco Pastorius is invoked three times as the real-world proof each step scales up to professional playing (a scale-shape-based "Chicken" cover, a "Hills Are Alive" quote mid-solo, an "America the Beautiful" solo built on a Bingo-method-style number pattern). A free PDF (fingering charts plus a "mega list" of simple, mostly-memorized melodies with their starting scale-degree marked) backs steps 1 and 4 but isn't itself capturable here — the technique is.

## What's taught

- **[~0:00–1:25] Why scales exist.** The 12-note chromatic alphabet is framed as a spice cabinet — too many notes to use all at once, so a scale is a pre-mixed subset "guaranteed to work together," giving a song a specific, coherent flavor without wrong-sounding notes. Named examples of different scale flavors: Master of Puppets (Metallica) → minor scale → dark; Pride and Joy (Stevie Ray Vaughan) → mixolydian scale → bluesy; Sunshine of Your Love (Cream) → blues scale → also bluesy. States the video's target scale directly: the **major scale** — "the most used scale in music," foundation of the rest of tonal theory, the scale of lullabies.
- **[~1:25–2:25] What a scale shape is, and why five steps.** A scale shape is the fretboard instruction manual for a scale — memorize the shape once, use it forever. Framed as necessary but not sufficient: a shape alone gets you nowhere without a process for actually building basslines, fills, and solos from it, which is what steps 2–5 are for.
- **[Step 1, ~2:25–3:55] The center shape, and the #1 beginner mistake.** C major scale shape from the **3rd fret of the A string**. Fingering up: **middle–pinky–index–middle–pinky–index–ring–pinky**; back down: **pinky–ring–index–pinky–middle–index–pinky–middle**. **The named mistake:** trying to hold the fretting hand stretched across all four frets of the shape at once — almost nobody, including large-handed players, can actually hold that stretch as a beginner, and forcing it is what turns a shape learnable "in like a day" into a weeks-long struggle. **The fix: microshifting** — letting the hand creep/adjust position finger-to-finger for comfort using the exact same fingering, rather than holding one fixed stretch; framed as the single skill that makes the shape playable "no matter how big your hands are." *(Same core concept, applied here to the major scale's own home position, as the microshifting content already on [[Fretting-Hand Strength, Stretch, Stamina, and Finger Control]] Exercise 1/2.)*
- **[Step 2, ~3:55–5:35] Playing the shape from any starting note.** The shape is fully movable — starting it on C names the result "C major scale," starting the *identical* fingering on G names it "G major scale," etc.; C is confirmed as the 3rd fret of the A string via BassBuzz's separate "Money Notes" fretboard-memorization video. **Worked examples:** "Brown Eyed Girl" (Van Morrison) called in G at a jam → relocate the shape to start on G (3rd fret of the E string) rather than staying on C, or the bassline comes out wrong; "Stir It Up" (Bob Marley) called in A → relocate to the 5th fret of the E string. General rule for picking a starting string: anchor the shape's root on the **E or A string**, not higher, so there are enough strings left below to finish the full shape.
- **[Step 2 continued, ~5:35–6:40] The "hidden notes" — open strings inside the shape.** Sliding the same shape down to the **2nd fret** and then the **1st fret** eventually puts the shape's index-finger notes on **open strings** instead of a fretted note — same shape, same note relationships (confirmed by the fretboard dot pattern), but the fingering changes since an open string doesn't need fretting. This unlocks **F major** and **B♭ major** starting from the 1st-fret positions, which beginners often assume the shape "can't reach" simply because the fingering looks different at that end of the neck. Named application: Jaco Pastorius's version of "The Chicken" in B♭, playable using this exact 1st-fret open-string variant of the shape.
- **[Step 3, ~6:40–8:35] The copy-paste technique — extending range by duplicating chunks.** The full scale shape is described as **three "chunks,"** one per string it spans. Because the shape is movable, any chunk can be **copied and pasted** into an adjacent octave to extend the note pool beyond the shape's fixed 8 notes, without learning new fingering. **Two directions demonstrated:**
  - **Down for low notes:** playing in **D major** (root on a middle string) leaves the beefiest low notes on the bass unreachable inside the shape as originally positioned. Taking the shape's **top two chunks and copying them down an octave**, anchored on the low D, recovers those low notes using the exact same shape/fingering — useful for kicking a song "into heavy gear." The very bottom chunk can't be copied this way once the bass runs out of strings, but the notes that *are* recoverable are the same notes, same relationships.
  - **Up for high notes:** playing in **A major**, the same **top two chunks copied upward** from the low A to the high A unlock extra high notes for a bass fill, again capped by running out of strings for the very top chunk.
  - **Cross-reference:** [[Bass Pentatonic Riff-Writing Recipe]] documents a related but narrower octave-shift move (moving one or two *individual notes* up an octave — two strings up, two frets over — to extend a *pentatonic riff's* melodic range mid-phrase); this step is the whole-chunk, whole-*major*-scale version of the same underlying idea, used to extend the shape's usable range rather than to vary one riff.
- **[Step 4, ~8:35–13:00] "Steal like a bassist" — ear-training from known melodies.** Framed as the mistake almost every beginner makes after learning steps 1–3: never connecting the shape to the major-scale melodies already memorized "in your bones" from a lifetime of hearing them (children's songs, holiday music, pub sing-alongs). Full four-step process, worked twice ("When the Saints Go Marching In" and "The Sound of Music"'s "The Hills Are Alive") — captured in full on the new technique note **[[Steal Like a Bassist — Ear-Training from Known Melodies]]**, including the stated stumbling block (not every melody starts on scale degree 1, so the PDF marks each melody's actual starting degree) and the improv-under-pressure payoff (find a known melody inside the current scale shape and "doodle around it" instead of freezing when asked to solo) — demonstrated as literally how Jaco Pastorius solos, via a "Hills Are Alive" quote mid-improvisation.
- **[Step 5, ~13:00–15:15] The Bingo method — numeric pattern generation.** Named and credited broadly ("from Bach to Bob Marley to Beach Boys"). Full method captured on the new technique note **[[The Bingo Method — Number-Pattern Riff Generation]]**: turn the scale's in-order note sequence (1-2-3-4-5-6-7-8) into a short custom number pattern (worked example: 1-2-3-1), then **transpose that same pattern through the scale by adding 1 to every number each repeat** (2-3-4-2, then 3-4-5-3, and so on), chaining the results into a line that's musically far more interesting than playing the scale straight. Two stated uses: **(a) a technique/speed workout** — same fingering skeleton as the plain scale, but different string-crossing/plucking demands, suitable for a week-long metronome-paced drill; **(b) a chord-connector for real basslines** — worked example links the I chord (B) to the IV chord (E) in B major using the descending pattern 8-7-6-5 instead of a plain 1-2-3-4-1 walk-up, explicitly framed as less boring. Confirms the pattern itself can be arbitrary/randomly generated ("you can literally play bingo with a random number generator") — demonstrated live with an improvised pattern, transposed the same +1-per-repeat way. Jaco Pastorius credited a third time: a "very similar 1-2-3-4 pattern" at the end of his solo version of "America the Beautiful."
- **[~15:15–end] Closing takeaway.** The five steps compound — shape, transposition, range-extension, ear-linked melody, and numeric-pattern generation together are what actually turn a memorized scale shape into usable music, versus stopping at shape-memorization alone (the "rookie scale mistake" named going into step 4).
