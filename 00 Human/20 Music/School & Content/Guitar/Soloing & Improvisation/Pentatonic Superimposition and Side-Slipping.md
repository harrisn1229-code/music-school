# Pentatonic Superimposition and Side-Slipping

**Three escalating ways to get more mileage out of one pentatonic scale than "play it over its own tonic": play it wholesale over chords it isn't diatonic to (comparison-by-ear substitution), stack a second pentatonic scale on top of the first (interval-based mixing), and slide the whole scale a half step off and back (side-slipping).** Grounded in Joe Henderson and McCoy Tyner's jazz vocabulary (named source records: *In 'N Out* and *The Real McCoy*). Sits a level up from [[Single-Note Pentatonic Substitutions Over Changes]] (which edits *one note at a time* to track a changing progression) and [[Diagonal Pentatonic Blues Soloing]] (one shape, one edited note, one transposition across a I-IV-V) — the techniques here instead keep the **whole, unedited** pentatonic scale in place and reinterpret it against different harmonic backdrops.

## Foundations: positions and pattern practice

Before the superimposition ideas, the source lesson establishes the raw material:
- **Learn the pentatonic scale in every position across the neck**, not just one box — movable boxes can start on any of the scale's five notes (worked from C major pentatonic: boxes startable on C, D, E, G, or A), the same "cover the whole neck, not one shape" principle as [[Guitar Positions]] and [[Dorian from the Minor Pentatonic Box]]'s fixed-vs-diagonal fingerings.
- **Skip-note practice**: run the scale pattern skipping every other note — builds familiarity with the scale *and* doubles as a source of melodic patterns in its own right, since a skipped-note run already sounds like a constructed line rather than a plain scale.
- **Combine patterns**: mix straight scale-tone runs with the skip-note pattern in the same line; ascend one box shape, then descend the next box shape rather than staying in one position; skip strings while running the scale (not just skipping scale tones) for a third variation.
- **Worked second source for the box-combination idea:** a companion lesson builds five short licks, each deliberately spanning **two adjacent pentatonic box shapes**, moving up the neck lick by lick (lick 1 = boxes 1–2, lick 2 = boxes 2–3, and so on through all five positions) — the same "don't stay in one box" principle above, demonstrated as a set of short, memorable phrases rather than free-form pattern practice. Follow-up drill: once a two-box lick is under the fingers, re-phrase it with a different rhythm rather than treating the note choices as fixed. Fret-level detail isn't recoverable (the video's tab is on-screen only, not spoken) — captured here as the reusable method, not a transcription. — Jesse Glick, [[5 minor pentatonic licks you should know in under 5 minutes (No. 4 is crazy)]]

## 1. Comparison-by-ear substitution — one pentatonic scale over unrelated chords

**Method: take the pentatonic scale's five notes, compare them against a target chord's bass note/root, and judge by ear (or by counting resulting intervals) whether the combination works** — rather than deriving the substitution from a formal mode/relationship rule first. Worked from **A minor pentatonic (A C D E G)** — chosen over its relative C major pentatonic purely because it's easier to relate the notes to a minor tonal center, though the pitch content is identical either way — tried against six different bass notes/chords:

- **A minor** (the scale's own tonic) — the baseline, fully diatonic case.
- **D** (i.e., functions as A minor pentatonic over a D-rooted chord, effectively D Dorian-flavored material) — works.
- **B♭ major 7♯11** — works; stacking the scale's notes against a B♭ bass produces "a lot of pretty extensions" (the ♯11-family colors a Lydian-leaning maj7 chord wants).
- **G♭ altered** — works; against a G♭ bass the scale's notes read as "a lot of altered notes" (the ♭9/♯9/♭5/♯5 family an altered dominant wants).
- **A7** (a common blues-context dominant) — works, with the caveat that the pentatonic's own natural notes over a dominant chord can include the 4th (an "avoid"-type tone against a major 3rd), which is "still okay" in practice because most of the remaining notes are strong. Same avoid-note logic as [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]'s tonic-chord ♭ clash discussion, applied here to a dominant rather than a static tonic.
- **C7** — works.

**The generalizable diagnostic, not just the six worked examples:** lay the pentatonic scale's five notes over any candidate bass note and check what they spell against it — some combinations land on plain chord tones, some land on altered/extension tones (which is a *feature*, not a problem, over an altered or extended chord), and a rare note or two may be a mild clash that's tolerable as long as it isn't a landing/target note. This reframes "which scale fits this chord" from a memorized mode chart into a fast, repeatable comparison anyone can run against an unfamiliar chord on the fly. **Practice application:** take lines already built from the scale/pattern/chord-shape material above and re-run them against each of these different bass notes/chords — the same melodic vocabulary, reharmonized just by changing what's underneath it.

## 2. Mixing two pentatonic scales

Two distinct ways to stack a second pentatonic scale against the first, both over a **static/vamped minor chord**:

- **A 4th/5th-interval pairing (pentatonic from the 5th of the chord).** The A minor pentatonic scale, already established as working over both A minor 7 *and* D minor 7 individually (per §1's "one pentatonic, multiple chords" logic), can be **combined with D minor pentatonic** when the underlying chord is D minor 7 — i.e., play the minor pentatonic built off the chord's own 5th degree alongside the chord-tone pentatonic. Stated as a repeatable rule: **"play a minor pentatonic scale from the 5th of whatever minor chord you're playing"** and mix it with the chord-tone pentatonic. Can be executed either as two genuinely separate scale shapes in different neck positions, or by finding both scales' notes within a single shared position for a more subtle blend (less obviously "two scales," more one enriched sound).
- **A whole-step-up pairing (major 2nd superimposition).** Over a static A minor 7 vamp, alternate between **A minor pentatonic and B minor pentatonic** (a whole step above) — go back and forth between the two rather than blending them into one line. **Named use case: modal-vamp tunes**, with *So What* (Miles Davis) called out directly as the reference context — the same tune [[Quartal Chord Voicings]] uses for its *So What* quartal-voicing case study, so this is a second, scale-based technique (rather than a chord-voicing technique) for the identical modal-vamp situation. → [[Modal Jazz]] (the static-vamp/no-harmonic-motion context both techniques exploit).

## 3. Side-slipping

**Slide the entire pentatonic scale up a half step off its home key, then resolve it back down a half step to land.** Worked example: soloing over A minor 7 with A minor pentatonic as home, deliberately shift to **B♭ pentatonic** (a half step up) for a phrase, then resolve back down a half step to A minor pentatonic. **Harmonic justification given: the B♭ pentatonic functions as a substitute for the V chord** (a tritone-sub-flavored displacement — B♭ sits a half step above A, which is the same "chromatic side-step that implies dominant motion back to the target" logic used in bebop enclosures and tritone substitution more broadly) — the deliberate "wrongness" of the half-step-up scale reads as tension that the half-step-down resolution then releases, rather than as a plain mistake. **The resolution back down is what makes it a "slip" rather than an actual key change** — side-slipping is defined by the round-trip (up a half step, then back), not by staying in the substituted key. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]] (chromatic approach-note logic that side-slipping applies at the scale level rather than the single-note level) — a candidate cross-link once a dedicated chromatic-approach/tritone-substitution note exists on the theory side; flagged here rather than force-fit into an existing page.

## Practical

- These three techniques compose: a single solo can move from home-key pentatonic runs (§ Foundations) → a comparison-substituted pentatonic over a passing altered/extended chord (§1) → a stacked second pentatonic over a modal vamp (§2) → a side-slip for tension-and-release (§3), all using the same five-note scale shapes already under the fingers.
- **Reference listening, named directly in the source:** Joe Henderson, *In 'N Out*; McCoy Tyner, *The Real McCoy* — both cited as the jazz-context source material for how this whole pentatonic toolkit actually gets used in real solos, not just as isolated exercises.

- **Nine pentatonic-superimposition devices over a blues, organized around one meta-principle: move the *pentatonic scale itself* by a clean interval (half/whole step) alongside the chord changes, not just match one scale per chord in isolation.** Selected devices distinct from what's already on this page: **"playing the V on the I"** (a Wes Montgomery device) — use the pentatonic rooted a 5th above the current chord (C minor pentatonic over F7, F minor pentatonic over B♭7) for a sus4-flavored color; **altered-dominant-via-Lydian-dominant borrowing** — read an altered dominant as its Lydian-dominant relative (F7alt ≈ B Lydian dominant) to unlock B major pentatonic (=A♭ minor pentatonic) as a usable superimposition, then slide the same shape down a half step onto the next chord; and a **chromatic ii-V-chain reharmonization** (heard on Chick Corea's "Matrix") — superimpose a descending chain of chromatic ii-Vs over a plain blues turnaround (Bb7→Ebm7→Ab7→Dbm7→Gm7→C7→F), playing each minor chord's pentatonic through the chain for a burst of tension that resolves cleanly back into the form. → [[Chord Substitution]] — Jens Larsen, [[9 Surprising Pentatonic Scale Secrets on a Blues]]
- **Chromatic sliding minor-3rds as a connective device, and holding a deliberately outside note as pure melodic tension rather than an alternate scale.** John McLaughlin's solo on "Take the Coltrane" (F blues) repeatedly connects target notes via sliding chromatic minor-3rds rather than diatonic scale steps, creating ambiguity independent of the underlying chord. Separately, a note that flatly doesn't belong to the current chord/key (e.g. the ♭6 over an F blues) is deliberately picked and **held** rather than passed through quickly, resolving only later — argued as a melodic tension-and-resolution shape, not an attempt at finding a "correct" alternate scale for the moment. → [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]] — Jens Larsen, [[John McLaughlin - This Is Not Like Other Approaches]]

Because the two shapes are pitch-identical, the same lick transfers verbatim between them, useful for call-and-response phrasing; extends cleanly to the blues scale (add the ♭5 blue note to both shapes) and to diagonal pentatonic lines (copy the same diagonal cell into the shifted position). — Brandon D'Eon, [Practice your pentatonics like this to skyrocket your skills](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uEYn-ntPrhU)
