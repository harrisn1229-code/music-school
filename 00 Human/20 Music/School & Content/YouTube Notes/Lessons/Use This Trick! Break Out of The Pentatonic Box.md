# Use This Trick! Break Out of The Pentatonic Box

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=p67aKcImo18)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=p67aKcImo18)

## Synopsis

Jack Ruch's fix for "getting stuck in the minor pentatonic rut" over a minor blues: instead of running one scale over the whole form, insert a real **secondary dominant chord change** at the single transition point where it matters most — going from the i chord to the iv chord (A minor → D minor in his worked example, part of an A minor blues with A minor / D minor / E minor changes). He substitutes an **A7** in the bar before D minor (A7 is V7 of D minor), keeps everything else strictly minor-pentatonic/blues phrasing, and layers three escalating devices onto that one A7-to-Dm transition spot: (1) the plain **A7 arpeggio**, playable from different starting notes and relocatable across the neck/octaves; (2) a **C♯ diminished 7th** substitute (drop the A7's root, add a B♭) that gives a more dissonant, "beautiful" resolution into D minor, built from the exact same arpeggio shapes with one note swapped; (3) **altered voicings and the A altered scale** on the A7, stacking ♭5/♯5/♭9/♯9 color tones over a basic shell voicing (root–♭7–3rd), for chords or lines as simple or as complex as the player wants. The teaching arc is explicit and repeated three times: play straight minor pentatonic through the rest of the form, then deliberately "break out" only at this one transition, then resolve back into pentatonic — building tension against the A minor chord that resolves when D minor arrives. ## What's taught

- **Setup: an A minor blues (A minor / D minor / E minor) with a secondary dominant substituted into the last bar before the iv chord.** Rather than the diatonic Dm, play an **A7** in the bar immediately before the A minor → D minor transition — because A7 is the V7 of D minor (a secondary dominant, V7/iv), it builds tension against the A minor tonality and resolves convincingly into D minor when it arrives. A7 = A major triad (A C♯ E) + ♭7 (G) — i.e. an A major triad with a flat 7th on top, not a plain A minor chord. Works with the band actually voicing the A7 in the rhythm, *or* purely as a soloing/melodic idea over a band that just stays on A minor the whole time — "if it rubs a little bit against that A minor chord that's perfectly okay," since the tension is the point and it resolves at D minor.
- **Device 1 — the A7 arpeggio as the target vocabulary over the transition bar.** Notes: A, C♯, E, G (root–3–5–♭7). Play the arpeggio in one position first, starting from different chord tones for different melodic shapes/sounds, then land on a chord tone of D minor when the change arrives. Once comfortable in one spot, **relocate the same line to different positions and octaves across the neck** — the shape is movable, not fixed to one box. Everything else in the solo (the rest of the 12-bar form) stays strictly minor-pentatonic blues phrasing — the arpeggio is a targeted insert at the one transition bar, not a wholesale scale swap for the whole form.
- **Device 2 — substitute a C♯ diminished 7th for the A7 arpeggio, for extra dissonance.** Derivation: take the A7 arpeggio (A, C♯, E, G), **remove the root (A)** — the remaining three notes (C♯, E, G) are a **C♯ diminished triad**; add a **B♭** on top to complete it into a **full C♯ diminished 7th chord** (C♯, E, G, B♭). Practical result: reuse the *same* A7 arpeggio lines/fingerings, just **swap the A for a B♭** — the C♯ diminished arpeggio line is one-note-different from the A7 arpeggio line already learned, not a new shape to memorize from scratch. Sound: "very dissonant, tense" against the A chord, "resolves really nicely to D minor" — described as more colorful/dissonant than the plain A7 arpeggio version. Same relocate-across-the-neck practice applies once the one-position version is comfortable.
- **Device 3 — altered voicings and the A altered scale over the same A7-to-Dm transition.** Starting point: a **basic shell voicing** of A7 (root, ♭7, major 3rd only — no 5th). Add extensions on top of the shell, one at a time or combined: **♭5**, **♯5**, **♭9**, or **♯9** — "lots of different colorful extensions." Named favorite combination: **♯5 + ♯9**, then dropping down to a **♭9** before resolving to D minor — described as producing "really pretty chord options." Full scale for this sound: **A altered scale** — root A, ♭9 (B♭), ♯9 (C), major 3rd (C♯), ♭5 (E♭), ♯5 (F), ♭7 (G), then root — i.e. the basic dominant shell (root–3rd–♭7) plus four alteration tones (♭5, ♯5, ♭9, ♯9) layered on top, all playable in one neck position. Application scales from very simple (e.g. a short line: ♯9 → ♭9 → root → ♭7 → down to the minor 3rd of the following D minor chord) to fully fleshing out the whole altered scale in longer lines — "it's really up to you to decide what sound you're going for."
- **The repeated teaching pattern, demonstrated three times (once per device) over a full backing-track pass:** stick to strictly minor pentatonic / straight blues phrasing through the bulk of the 12-bar form, then deliberately break out and use the device (A7 arpeggio, then the diminished substitute, then the altered scale/voicings) specifically at the A-to-D-minor transition bar, then resolve back into pentatonic phrasing to close. Each pass plays the full form twice so the transition is heard repeated.
- **Framing/rationale, stated directly:** "a lot of this for me comes down to the underlying chords" — Ruch says he's always thinking about the harmony underneath, and that awareness shapes what he plays melodically; understanding the chord/scale relationship at the transition point is what lets him "break out of that and use it as a jumping off point to create lines," rather than defaulting to one scale over the whole progression.
