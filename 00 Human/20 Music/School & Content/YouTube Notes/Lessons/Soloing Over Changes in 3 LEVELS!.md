# Soloing Over Changes in 3 LEVELS!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TXlDB-pUla4)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TXlDB-pUla4)

## Synopsis

Jack Ruch plays a weekly organ-trio gig in Nashville and uses the band's regular tune — Ray Charles's slow blues **"Hard Times"** — as the vehicle for a layered teaching demo: three complete solos over the *same* progression, each one adding a harder layer of theory on top of the last, so the viewer can hear exactly what changes (and what stays the same) as the harmonic sophistication increases. Level 1 is a pentatonic-blues solo built entirely around phrasing/touch/dynamics rather than theory — mostly E♭ major pentatonic with deliberate dips into E♭ minor pentatonic at specific harmonic pressure points. Level 2 switches to full chord-tone/arpeggio outlining, using CAGED-adjacent arpeggio shapes kept in one small neck position and stitched together with chromatic connecting tones — explicitly presented as "chunk it, then connect the chunks." Level 3 layers in the **altered scale** (melodic minor's 7th mode) over three functioning-dominant spots in the form, taught from first principles (built as a dominant 7 shell — root/3rd/♭7 — plus four altered extensions: ♭9, ♯9, ♯11, ♭13) and always resolved onto a chord tone of the next chord. The most valuable framing is structural rather than any single lick: **the same tune, the same solo skeleton, three levels of vocabulary** — a genuinely reusable practice template for approaching any new tune first simply, then with chord-tone precision, then with outside color. Ruch flags a new tiered-solo-approach course coming with Brett Papa built on exactly this method, and the lesson's tabs/notation/backing track are Patreon-gated.

## What's taught

### The tune and its chords ("Hard Times," Ray Charles slow blues, key of E♭)
Ruch plays the chords down against a backing track on-screen rather than reciting a lead sheet; the transcript captures the changes only as spoken, so the reconstruction below should be checked against the video's on-screen chord names or a real lead sheet before treating it as authoritative — flagged explicitly as **best-effort from audio, not a verified transcription**:
- **I** — E♭ (major/E♭7 as the tonic sound)
- **V7/vi** — G7, moving to...
- **vi** — Cm7 (Ruch also mentions the "minor major 7" variant, i.e. adjust one note of Cm7 to Cm(maj7))
- **ii–V into the IV** — a "251 going to the four chord": B♭m7 (ii of A♭) leading into...
- **I** — back to E♭ briefly, then
- **IV** — A♭7
- **♯IVdim** — a "sharp four diminished passing chord" (E♭dim/A♮dim territory, functioning as a passing chord between IV and I)
- **I** — back to E♭
- **Turnaround** — C7 (the VI7, walked down chromatically in half steps) leading back to the top of the form
- **V (at the very end of the form)** — B♭7, heading back to the top

This is a jazz-inflected 12-bar-family slow blues in E♭ with a 1–6-ish move (G7→Cm7), a ii–V into the IV chord (B♭m7→E♭7→A♭7 — note Ruch also separately names E♭7 as one of the three altered-scale spots, which is the V7 of the IV chord inside that same 251), a chromatic passing diminished chord, and a chromatically-walked VI7 turnaround — structurally close to a "jazz blues" of the [[Blues Form and Harmony]] / [[Turnarounds]] type already documented in this vault, distinct from a plain I-IV-V blues.

### Level 1 — Pentatonic blues foundation (phrasing over theory)
- **Two E♭ major pentatonic patterns** anchor the solo: one built around a **C-shape** fingering for E♭, one around an **A-shape** fingering for E♭ — both rooted on the **6th fret of the A string**, plus a further pattern up the neck from the same root.
- **Two E♭ minor pentatonic patterns** in the same general area, used for a **bluesier**, darker color where the major pentatonic would sound too bright.
- **Ear over theory at this level** — Ruch is explicit that he's not consciously chord-tone-targeting here; some notes land on chord tones by ear, but the actual practice focus is phrasing, dynamics, leaving space, and tone, not harmonic precision. *(Matches the philosophy already on [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]'s Step 1 — play the "home" scale through the changes before chasing every chord.)*
- **Switch to minor pentatonic specifically at the walk-down (C→B→B♭) over the passing/diminished chord** — the one spot in the form Ruch calls out by name as where the bluesy minor sound is deliberately leaned into, rather than staying major-pentatonic-bright throughout.

### Level 2 — Chord-tone / arpeggio soloing (CAGED-position based)
- **Map each chord's arpeggio (root-3rd-5th-♭7) using CAGED-adjacent shapes, staying in one small ~5-fret span rather than jumping position per chord** — the whole E♭-G7-Cm7-B♭m7 stretch of the progression is played from **one C-shape-rooted position at the 6th fret of the A string**, the same physical spot Level 1's pentatonic shapes used.
- **E♭ major / E♭7 arpeggio** (I) in that position, moving directly into **G7's root-3rd-5th-♭7** (the notes found within the G7 chord, nothing extra) — the chord shape itself used as a visual guide for where the arpeggio tones sit.
- **Cm7 arpeggio next**, with a one-note adjustment available to play **Cm(maj7)** instead if the tune calls for it (raise the ♭7 to a natural 7).
- **The 251-into-IV chunk (B♭m7 → E♭7 → A♭7) fingered entirely without changing position** — reinforces the CAGED-adjacency principle already documented on [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]: reach for whichever CAGED grip sits closest to the one just used, not the "textbook" open-position shape for that chord.
- **Chord-tone outlining continues through I → IV (A♭7) → the diminished passing chord → back to I → the C7 turnaround**, which Ruch notes can simply be **walked down chromatically in half steps**.
- **Chunk-then-connect practice method, stated directly**: take a smaller piece of the progression (his example: the first three chords, E♭ → G7 → Cm7), drill the arpeggios in isolation, and use **chromatic passing notes to connect the dots between arpeggios** — the exact "chunk-then-connect" strategy already on [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]] § *Chunk-then-connect*.
- **Blend arpeggio outlining back into Level 1's pentatonic blues phrasing** rather than treating them as separate toolkits — start a phrase with a pentatonic lick, look ahead at the next chord's shape, and flow into that chord's arpeggio from wherever the pentatonic phrase leaves off. A concrete, worked instance of [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]'s "blend chord-tone outlining with pentatonic/blues phrasing" idea from the same channel's other lessons.

### Level 3 — The altered scale over functioning dominants
- **The altered scale is melodic minor's 7th mode** — Ruch's stated reason it works: it contains the ♭9, ♯9, ♯11, and ♭13 all together, the full set of "altered" extensions that can stack on top of a dominant 7 chord.
- **Conceptual model: a plain dominant-7 shell (root–3rd–♭7) with four color notes added on top** — ♭9, ♯9, ♯11 (thought of as the sharp 11 rather than ♭5, because he's building extensions upward from the shell), and ♭13. This is the identical "spot the hidden dominant shell, then everything else is an alteration" habit already on [[Altered Chords]] § *From videos* (Ruch's own earlier video on the same idea), just re-taught here in the context of a specific tune.
- **G altered scale, spelled degree by degree**: **G (root) – A♭ (♭9) – B♭ (♯9) – B (major 3rd) – D♭ (♯11/♭5) – E♭ (♭13) – F (♭7) – G (root)**. Matches this vault's [[Scale Modes Reference]] melodic-minor-mode-VII table (the "Locrian ♭4, ♭♭7" row, enharmonically the same intervals) — the video's degree-by-degree teaching order (start from the shell, add extensions) is a clean concrete worked example worth cross-referencing there.
- **E♭ altered scale spelled the same way for the second application spot**: root, ♭9, ♯9, major 3rd, ♯11, ♭13, ♭7, root.
- **B♭ altered scale for the third spot**, played by sliding the same fingered pattern used for the E♭ altered scale up to the new root — no new shape to learn, just relocated.
- **Fretboard position used in the demo solo**: the altered shape sits on the **top four strings**, chosen because it lines up conveniently with the altered chord voicings in that area of the neck.
- **Three named application spots in this specific tune's form**, all functioning dominants:
  1. **G7 moving to Cm** (the V7/vi in the progression above) — resolve the tension by landing on the **5th of Cm** exactly on the downbeat as the chord changes.
  2. **E♭7 in the 251 heading to the IV chord (A♭)** — played as "a classic 251 line," i.e. the altered color sits on the V of that mini-ii-V-I (B♭m7–E♭7–A♭) rather than on the ii or I.
  3. **B♭7, the primary V of the key, at the very end of the form** heading back to the top — resolved back to E♭.
- **Resolution discipline, stated as the whole point of using the scale at all**: always resolve the altered tension onto a **chord tone of the next chord**, landing it rhythmically on the downbeat of the change — not just running the scale decoratively. Directly matches [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]'s guide-tone-targeting discipline and [[Scale Modes Reference]]'s repeated "the scale choice is framed by where it's going" framing for connective/altered scales elsewhere in this strand.
- **Portable system, stated directly**: learn the altered scale once as "shell + four extensions," then it transposes to any functioning dominant in any tune — no new theory needed per key, only relocating the same shape (demonstrated moving G altered → E♭ altered → B♭ altered, three different keys, one mental model).

### Practice framing (ties the three levels together)
- **Practice against the real backing track with the rhythm section**, experimenting with phrasing from all three levels' vocabulary within the actual time available in the form, rather than drilling scales in isolation.
- **The tiered structure — simple pentatonic phrasing → chord-tone/arpeggio precision → outside/altered color — is presented as a deliberate teaching order**, not an accident of this one video: it's the explicit organizing principle of Ruch's forthcoming course with Brett Papa, which walks through multiple tunes with the same three-level build.
