# The Beatles' "Here Comes the Sun" - SYNTH PARTS YOU'VE NEVER HEARD BEFORE!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nhWaFbcSm-A)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nhWaFbcSm-A)

## Synopsis

A patch recreation of the little-noticed Moog modular synth parts in The Beatles' "Here Comes the Sun," on a period-correct 1969 Moog modular shipped six days after the Beatles' own unit. The synth substitutes for orchestral woodwinds in places and carries a whistle-like top line to the song's outro. The valuable content is modular-specific patching logic — separate envelope generators per stage, a shared "driver" oscillator for synchronized vibrato, and a deliberate anti-distortion gain-staging choice — plus a recurring reminder that extreme volume imbalance between two oscillators is itself a timbral tool.

## Techniques

- **Extreme (not subtle) volume imbalance between two same-type oscillators shapes timbre, not just loudness — two rectangle waves (pulse-width adjusted toward square) mixed at roughly 10:3, the dominant oscillator carrying most of the identity and the quiet one adding only a trace of texture.** A more extreme version of the "voice the harmony quieter" pattern seen elsewhere in this channel's patches — here applied to two oscillators of the same type rather than a harmony interval.
- **Modular-specific envelope patching: route ONE envelope generator to the filter and a SEPARATE one to the VCA, rather than one envelope doing double duty.** With the range of the filter's envelope modulation kept deliberately LOW (not fully open) for subtle "articulation" rather than a big sweep, and a low initial filter frequency plus heavy resonance for the tonal core; resonance again described as thinning the sound out (recurring theme — see [[Filters and Resonance]]).
- **A shared "driver" oscillator, LFO-modulated, sends the SAME vibrato to multiple VCOs simultaneously, keeping them detuning together in sync rather than independently** — the sine-wave LFO feeds an attenuator into the driver, which controls the frequency of both oscillators at once. This is the mechanism behind the song's "whistle" top line and its ending vibrato-out. Worth remembering as a distinct alternative to routing an LFO separately to each oscillator (which can drift oscillators apart rather than together).
- **Deliberately AVOID distortion/gain-stage buzz for a "pure" tone — the opposite choice from this channel's saturation-focused patches.** Pushing the modular's per-channel mixer trim knobs introduces an audible buzz; backing them off (running hotter signal into a lower trim, or vice versa — gain-stage balance) removes it. A reminder that "push the mixer hard for saturation" (seen on other patches from this channel) is a choice, not a default — plenty of parts call for the clean opposite.
- **A synth substituting for orchestral instrumentation IN PARTS of an arrangement, with the real instrument (here, woodwinds/flutes) left untouched in other sections rather than fully replaced** — a hybrid-arrangement choice, not an either/or: some sections get the synth stand-in, others keep the acoustic original, based on which serves that specific passage.
- **Production-adjacent aside, not deeply explored here**: the song shifts out of a straight 4/4 feel into a passage with an odd/mixed-meter grouping (heard, not counted precisely in the video) attributed to George Harrison's Indian-music influence — flagged as a contributing factor to why the song "doesn't sound organic" despite feeling natural; full odd-meter/mixed-meter theory already covered extensively under [[Time Signatures & Note Values]].
