# EQ

General best practice for equalization — corrective and tonal — independent of the plugin. Specific moves live on [[Pro-Q 4]], [[EQ Eight]], [[Pro-MB]] (dynamic/multiband); reusable lessons here. See also [[Masking and Carving]], [[De-essing]].

## What it is
Shaping tone and carving space by boosting/cutting frequency bands — the most-reached-for tool in the mix, and the one most worth doing *subtractively* first.

## Owned (first-call)
[[Pro-Q 4]] (surgical + dynamic) · [[EQ Eight]] (Ableton) · [[Pro-MB]] (dynamic/multiband) · [[Ozone 9 Equalizer]] · [[EQ Three]]

## From books
- **The four-lane drum EQ map: every drum gets a high-pass, and the ascending order of those high-passes IS the lane assignment.** Kick low-cut **40–60 Hz** (rumble) → snare low-cut **100–200 Hz** → toms low-cut **60–200 Hz** (per drum: floor toms low, racks high) → hats low-cut **200–300 Hz** ("or more, use ears"). Then the paired rule that runs through the whole chart: **cut below the fundamental, boost at it** — kick shelf **80–100 Hz** (thump), snare shelf **200–300 Hz** (umph), toms boost **100–300 Hz** (low-end power). Presence side: kick **2–4 kHz** (snap), snare **5–7 kHz** (crisp), toms **5–10 kHz** (high-end punch), hats high-shelf **7–10 kHz+** (sizzle); tom boxiness cut **300–800 Hz**. No Q/gain/slope given anywhere — ranges only, and the card's own footer says "always use your ears." *Read its two "remove harsh freq" high-cuts (snare 15–20 kHz, hats 18–20 kHz) skeptically — real harshness is 2–5 kHz / 5–12 kHz and is better handled dynamically ([[Soothe2]], [[De-essing]]).* → [[Masking and Carving]], [[Kick Design]], [[Snare Design]], [[SUB Slot]], [[BASS Slot]], [[BED Slot]], [[MIDS Slot]], [[LEAD Slot]], [[AIR Slot]], [[TRANSIENT Slot]] — Whipped Cream Sounds, [[Drum Mixing EQ Guide - Enrichment|Drum Mixing EQ Guide]]

## From videos

- **A hand-built analog console designer's case for the midrange specifically, plus subtle harmonic saturation as a distinct "glue" tool from overt distortion.** Rodec's MX modular console (co-designed with Sherman) deliberately gives its channel EQ a fully parametric mid band, on the reasoning that "the mid is where the action happens in music" — modern mixes are described as commonly having plenty of low end and high-end sizzle but a real dip in the middle, and pulling midrange back in (felt physically, "in your chest") is framed as a corrective, alongside the opposite move — deliberately scooping the mid out for a hollow, near-band-pass-filtered character when that's the goal instead. **Subtle saturation as mix glue, distinct from audible distortion**: some of the console's preamp modules include a "+30dB" boost stage explicitly nicknamed as going "past 11" — gently pushing into it, well short of obvious distortion, is said to add harmonics that "blend the mix together" even when a listener wouldn't consciously identify what's happening as saturation. **Architecture behind the console's character**: discrete hand-soldered op-amps of two distinct in-house designs (one deliberately voiced closer to a "70s-style" saturating character for the first gain stage, one clean/high-slew-rate specifically to properly drive the output transformer stage) paired with Lundahl transformers, +26 dBu of headroom, and — notably — designed by ear over 3 years of listening sessions with a guitar-amp builder as the key saturation-focused engineer, rather than a purely spec/measurement-driven process. → [[Saturation and Distortion]] — Anthony Marinelli Music (feat. Tom De Ridder), [[Sherman Filter in a High-End Mixer Should Be ILLEGAL]]
- **Clarity is largely the high/low frequency ratio — a fast go-to move: boost 3-5 kHz (centered ~4 kHz), a few dB on a track, only 1-2 dB on a bus (ears are sensitive enough there that small bus moves read large).** High-pass placement matters too: don't push a lead vocal's own high-pass above its own fundamental (thins it); instead high-pass COMPETING elements (synths, guitars) to just above the vocal's fundamental to open room without touching the vocal. For an "air" boost specifically (distinct from clarity): a parallel linear-phase send, high-Q shelf boosting above ~12 kHz while cutting below, linear phase to avoid phase cancellation on blend, then a low-latency upward compressor to bring the quietest air-range detail forward before blending via the send fader. → [[Masking and Carving]], [[AIR Slot]], [[Compression]] — Sage Audio, [How to Mix for Clarity](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=R-UjkDtleVU)
- **EQ-before-or-after-compression is a false dichotomy on a vocal — use both, for different jobs, in this order.** EQ #1 (pre-compression): attenuate problem content — plosives, aggressive sibilance, nasal tones, unpleasant resonances, unneeded low-mids — so the compressor works on a clean signal. Compress. EQ #2 (post-compression): amplify what you now want more of — a boost around **2–5 kHz** for clarity, air via EQ or an exciter, and (placed later still, after reverb/delay) side-image warmth around **~300 Hz**. → [[Compression]] — Sage Audio, [Vocal Compression: Save Years of Trial & Error](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2RWijLWuW7I)
- **A filter causes amplitude-modulation "ringing" on a signal whenever that signal's amplitude changes — even when the cutoff is set far from that signal's own frequency, on BOTH minimum- and linear-phase filters.** Demoed with a variable-amplitude sine wave through a high-pass set well above the sine's frequency, where theory predicts zero effect: every amplitude change instead triggers a modulating spike/dip that takes measurable time to settle — ~20 ms at 2 kHz, but **close to half a second at 88 Hz**. To fully silence an 88 Hz signal with a high-pass required pushing the cutoff all the way to **~23 kHz**. Linear phase reduces the amplitude artifact but adds audible pre-ringing on top of post-ringing; steeper slopes shift but never remove the tradeoff. A filter near a signal's own frequency is less "surgical" than its dB/octave spec implies — applies to bells/notches too, not just high/low-pass. For genuinely surgical cuts, consider [[RX Spectral Editor]] instead, which selects and removes the spectral data directly rather than filtering it. → [[Phase Alignment]] — Sage Audio, [The Cleanest, Most Accurate EQ Possible (and you've never tried it)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tfq7XkbYk5U)
- **Think of an EQ band as a granular extension of the channel fader — an amplitude tool scoped to a fundamental, overtone, or transient, not a separate "tone" tool.** A channel fader moves an entire instrument's level; an EQ band does the same thing but scoped to just one part of it (a kick's 60 Hz fundamental vs. its 180/360 Hz overtones vs. its ~1 kHz transient click) — Q value is effectively how many "sub-faders" are being controlled at once. → [[RETHINK EQ - It'll Change How You Mix Music]] — Sage Audio, [RETHINK EQ - It'll Change How You Mix Music](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JCjHSd10rGI)
- **EQ's mix-wide loudness impact depends on which frequency you touch, not just the dB amount — ears peak in sensitivity around 3.5 kHz (near the vocal's third formant).** A 6 dB boost doubles perceived loudness of that range, but a boost around 3.5 kHz on a lead/vocal moves the mix's overall perceived loudness (and LUFS reading) far more than an equal-dB boost buried in the low-mids (e.g. 500 Hz on a bass) — a more efficient lever for perceived energy than reaching for the fader. → [[LEAD Slot]] — Sage Audio, [RETHINK EQ - It'll Change How You Mix Music](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JCjHSd10rGI)
- **EQ reshapes dynamic range in ways that compound unpredictably across overlapping bands — recheck peak levels after any EQ pass, not just tone.** Boosting a fundamental raises its peak more than the quiet parts of the same range, widening dynamic range; with multiple bands touching overlapping harmonics, whether a net peak rises or falls after EQ depends on which harmonic actually dominates that peak — don't assume a single-band EQ move leaves levels/peaks untouched, especially right before a compressor or limiter. → [[Compression]] — Sage Audio, [RETHINK EQ - It'll Change How You Mix Music](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JCjHSd10rGI)
- **EQ after a saturator can isolate and reshape the harmonics it generated, for more surgical control than the saturator's own drive/mix knobs.** Send to a parallel track, low-pass to isolate just the fundamental, saturate, then EQ the resulting harmonic stack — boost a shelf above the fundamental to emphasize the added grit, or high-pass out the original fundamental to isolate the distortion-only content and blend it back at a controlled level. → [[Saturator]], [[Saturation and Distortion]] — Sage Audio, [RETHINK EQ - It'll Change How You Mix Music](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JCjHSd10rGI)
- **Extreme EQ boosts can create real psychoacoustic illusions — phantom harmonics and perceived pitch shift — a ceiling to be aware of, not a technique to reach for.** A large boost at a fundamental (e.g. +30 dB at 100 Hz) can shift perceived pitch by up to roughly a semitone even though the actual frequency never moved; a large boost at one frequency (e.g. 2 kHz) can cause the ear to perceive harmonics above it (4 kHz, 8 kHz…) that aren't physically present. Explains why an aggressive EQ move sometimes sounds more drastic than its dB reading suggests. — Sage Audio, [RETHINK EQ - It'll Change How You Mix Music](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JCjHSd10rGI)
- **Vocal formants F1/F2/F3 all live in the mids; F3 (roughly 2–5 kHz) is the single most important range for perceived clarity, and it's also where the ear canal resonates.** Formants are frequency clusters encoding which vowel is being sung; three per voice typically (F1 lowest, F3 highest), varying by singer/vowel. F3 sits in the ear's most sensitive zone — boost somewhere in 2–5 kHz if a vocal reads unclear, attenuate there if it reads harsh/upfront. Isolate a given voice's F3 carefully; once found accurately, very small moves produce a large clarity change. → [[Pitch and Formant]], [[LEAD Slot]] (2.5–6 kHz) — Sage Audio, [Mids - the Most Important Frequency Range](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=23pezFNXW_4)
- **Almost every instrument's overtones occupy the MIDS even when its fundamental sits elsewhere — this is what makes the mid-range the busiest, most information-dense zone in any mix, more than its energy content alone would suggest.** A kick's fundamental/2nd harmonic sit in the lows, but most of its overtones land in the mids; bass guitar's harmonics (carrying most of its actual timbral character) sit in the mids despite a low fundamental; a vocal's articulation content sits above its low-mid fundamental. Not every instrument occupies the lows or highs, but almost every instrument occupies the mids. → [[Masking and Carving]], [[MIDS Slot]] — Sage Audio, [Mids - the Most Important Frequency Range](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=23pezFNXW_4)
- **A seven-part conceptual map of where different kinds of vocal information sit across the frequency range.** Low to high: (1) below the fundamental — unrelated/unmusical (mic rumble, plosives, room noise) — safe to high-pass; (2) the fundamental range itself; (3) 2nd/3rd-order harmonics — fullness/warmth, too much = muddiness; (4)–(5) 4th–5th (up to 7th) order harmonics — vowel articulation, can carry nasal resonance; (6) 8th–11th order harmonics, roughly **2–5 kHz** — the vocal formant carrying vowel + consonant info, the range the ear is most sensitive to (ties to the critical-band/ear-canal-resonance material above) — amplify here for clarity/intelligibility; (7a) **5–12 kHz** — percussive, language-specific consonant content (T, K, S), the first place things turn unpleasant if overdone; (7b) above that — "air," very-high-order harmonics and room reflections. Everything from the fundamental through the high-order harmonics moves together when the note changes; the percussive and air ranges are language/room-dependent content layered on top. → [[De-essing]], [[LEAD Slot]] (2.5–6 kHz), [[Vocal Mic Technique]], [[Pitch and Formant]] — Sage Audio, [This Makes Equalizing Vocals A Lot Easier](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dM8TyAYzpxs)
- **EQ the START of a vocal chain to control what feeds subsequent processors, not to fully balance the vocal — because saturation, compression, reverb, and delay all reshape frequency response too (harmonics, decaying taps), quietly undoing an early EQ move that only chased final tone.** High-pass below the fundamental to remove plosives/rumble before later stages can amplify them; bell-cut a hot low-mid range before it gets compressed harder than intended; small pre-boost on a range you want more of before a compressor, since makeup gain will amplify that pre-boosted range further. → [[Compression Staging]], [[Compression]], [[Reverb]], [[Delay]], [[Saturation and Distortion]] — Sage Audio, [This Makes Equalizing Vocals A Lot Easier](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dM8TyAYzpxs)
- **ADSR framing for EQ decisions: transient (attack/decay) and tonal (sustain/release) content usually carry different frequency information on the same instrument, and arrangement — not just processing — determines whether they collide with something else.** Tonal content = fundamental + overtones, generally declining in amplitude away from the fundamental (a bass on A3/220 Hz is still mostly a low/low-mid signal even though harmonics reach the highs). Transient content sits mostly in the high-mids/highs across almost any instrument (a vocal's consonants, a guitar's pick scrape, a snare's snap) and isn't tied to the fundamental note. Worked arrangement-vs-EQ example: a solo acoustic guitar's low-mid fundamental is fine alone but becomes a masking problem once bass/kick/snare are added — either EQ the fundamental down, or (if you control the arrangement) voice the part an octave higher instead. A second example: a synth with overtones in 2–5 kHz masks the vocal's third formant — dip the synth there, boost the vocal there, or pick a different-overtoned patch at the arrangement stage. → [[Masking and Carving]], [[TRANSIENT Slot]] — Sage Audio, [Arrangement and Music Mixing: Understanding the Relationship](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_--yEbcNjAs)
- **Why EQ is logarithmic: the cochlea encodes pitch logarithmically because each hair cell tops out at ~500 Hz of vibration, so a constant-Q filter's ballooning Hz bandwidth at high frequencies mirrors reduced high-frequency pitch discrimination — not a flaw to fight.** ~10,000 hair cells encode up to 500 Hz each individually; above that, cells work in sequence. Result: pitch discrimination is sharp below 500 Hz, still decent to ~4 kHz, then degrades — a one-octave-Q filter spans ~100–200 Hz in the lows but 1,000+ Hz in the highs, and that wider real-world bandwidth isn't perceived as inaccurate because the ear's own resolution is just as wide up there. Don't second-guess a wide-sounding high-frequency EQ move on this basis. → [[Masking and Carving]] — Sage Audio, [Psychoacoustics Pt.2 - the Logarithmic Ear](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Aha_7purQQo)
- **Center a boost's frequency on its critical band's own center, with a ~1/3-octave filter, to reduce masking of the target note versus surrounding content.** Worked example: boosting A5 (880 Hz) — nearest critical band centers at 840 Hz with a 150 Hz bandwidth; a ~1/3-octave filter on 880 Hz stays mostly inside that band, amplifying the target note more than what could mask it. Don't go narrower to "improve" this — an ultra-narrow filter's steep phase rotation amplifies content right around the center frequency and can itself cause beating (linear-phase avoids this). Occasional tool for dense/masked productions, not a default approach. → [[Masking and Carving]] — Sage Audio, [Psychoacoustics Pt.2 - the Logarithmic Ear](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Aha_7purQQo)
- **Dynamic EQ timing follows the same rule as compression timing — no reliable BPM formula, tune by ear against the gain-reduction/gain-change meter.** The attack/release interaction that defeats formulaic compression timing (see [[Compression]]) applies equally to dynamic EQ; the fix is the same — watch the meter, aim the return to unity right before the next transient. A dynamic EQ built around an envelope-follower/envelope-generator routed to a filter band's gain (create envelope → attach to gain → set positive depth to expand / negative depth to compress; decay controls transient length, curve controls when peak attenuation/amplification lands, delay/attack/hold control onset and duration) CAN hit genuinely BPM-accurate timing, the same way modulated saturation can — see [[Saturn 2]] for the equivalent owned workflow. - **A minimal two-pass mastering EQ structure: corrective before dynamics, tonal after — with a side-image-only HPF placement rule shared by both passes.** Pass 1 (before the RMS comp/upward processor): linear phase at a low-latency setting (minimal pre-ringing, less transient smear than zero-latency), a **side-image-only** high-pass filter placed **between the source's fundamental and its 2nd-order harmonic** (18 or 24 dB/oct is safe at linear-phase low-latency without messing up phase rotation) — this makes everything below the filter identical L/R without touching the fundamental itself — plus a small dip ~250 Hz (masking/mud) and a small dip in a resonant high-mid pocket (vocal nasal resonance, busy instruments). Pass 2 (after dynamics are settled): repeat the side-image HPF cleanup (upstream stages reintroduce side-image lows), dip low mids if still needed, boost lows + high mids, add a side-image bell in the mid-range for stereo impressiveness without unbalancing overall tone, and place the air band on the side (expand), the mid (focus), or both identically. → [[Pro-Q 4]], [[Width and Stereo]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [The Only Music Mastering Video You'll Ever Need](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=M88T8jFL2uU)
- **Every processor that changes amplitude unevenly across frequency or time is functioning as an implicit EQ, whether that was the intent or not — the "everything is EQ" mental model.** Reverb decay taps, ADSR-shaping tools, compression, saturation, clipping, and limiting all reshape frequency response as a side effect of their normal job, not just when an EQ is inserted. Worked example: attenuating a 50 Hz waveform with a compressor can waveshape it enough to generate a 150 Hz harmonic — one compression move produces two simultaneous EQ effects (50 Hz down, 150 Hz up). → [[Compression]], [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[Reverb]], [[SUB Slot]], [[BASS Slot]] — Sage Audio, [All Processing is Connected - Crucial Knowledge for Pro Mixes & Masters](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XV-0rdUrgHs)
- **ADSR framing for EQ: transient (attack/decay) and tonal (sustain/release) content usually carry different frequency content, so anything that treats them differently is indirectly EQing.** A snare's snap (attack/decay) sits in different frequencies than its ring/resonance (sustain/release, lower amplitude, longer duration); Eventide's **Split EQ** (unowned — see [[Plugin Wishlist]]) makes this deliberate by isolating transient vs. tonal content into two separately-adjustable "time zones" rather than conventional frequency bands. → [[TRANSIENT Slot]], [[Compression]] — Sage Audio, [All Processing is Connected - Crucial Knowledge for Pro Mixes & Masters](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XV-0rdUrgHs)
- **EQ retroactively turns another processor's own effect up or down without touching that processor's parameters — it changes perceived character, not just tone.** Since reverb, clipping, saturation etc. all add or amplify content in specific frequency ranges as a byproduct, EQing that range afterward amplifies or attenuates the perceived amount of that processor's effect (boost the band a reverb's reflections occupy and the tail reads as more present) even though the reverb's decay time or the clipper's threshold never changed — the same clipper/reverb setting reads completely differently depending on the EQ placed after it. → [[Masking and Carving]], [[Reverb]], [[Clipping and Loudness]] — Sage Audio, [All Processing is Connected - Crucial Knowledge for Pro Mixes & Masters](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XV-0rdUrgHs)
- **Automate an EQ band directly for a rhythmic notch/wah movement on a bass tone**, rather than reaching for a dedicated filter or envelope-follower device — once a decent base tone is dialed in, automating the EQ itself adds moving notch character on top, before the sound gets bounced and processed further. → [[Automation]], [[EDM Subgenres]] (Dubstep) — Max Konyi, [How to Make Dubstep in Studio One with Stock Instruments | Weekly Challenge #10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sD3mE-siI5M)
- **A low-shelf boost as the fast rescue for a thin real-bass take** — after tracking a real bass part that came out short on weight, a straightforward low-shelf EQ boost adds the missing sub before reaching for anything more elaborate (saturation, layering); a quick first move rather than the final answer when a bass needs more low end. → [[BASS Slot]] — Max Konyi, [How I Make Chill-Hop in Studio One](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C9hcFfEOLhI)
- **Sweep a narrow soloed EQ band up a reference track's whole spectrum to map where every element actually lives** — put EQ Eight on a reference track, expand it, narrow one band's Q (hold Option while dragging), and enable the headphone solo icon so only that narrow slice plays; slowly sweep it up from the bottom (EQ Eight goes down to 10 Hz) while the frequency-to-note readout in the corner names the pitch at each stop. In the worked example (Skrillex & AB), the kick's fundamental sat around 50 Hz (G) with an octave overtone at 100 Hz, while the bass fundamental sat lower at ~40 Hz (E) with its own perfect-fifth overtone above it — kick and bass tuned to *different* notes so they don't fight for the same sub-100 Hz space, which is part of why club music leans on keys like G/F/E: below roughly 30-40 Hz most speakers can't reproduce a fundamental realistically at all, so the overtone is what small speakers actually reproduce. Continuing the sweep revealed toms/percussion and the low end of the vocal in the low-mids, the snare (also tuned to the key) in the mid-range, progressively brighter percussion above that, and finally a high-hat/percussion "airiness" band near the very top — rolling that top band off entirely made the whole track sound noticeably dull, confirming it's doing real work even though it's nearly inaudible in isolation. → [[Referencing and Translation]], [[Masking and Carving]], [[Kick Design]] — You Suck at Producing, [You Suck at Mixing No. 5: Analyzing a Track Using EQ](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N_KIHqHb_jA)

- **Widen a snare's reverb by boosting the SIDE channel and carving the same band out of the middle** — switch EQ Eight to Mid/Side mode: boosting a snare's airy reverb band on the side channel reads as spacious and pleasant, boosting the identical band in the *middle* instead sounds noticeably worse — airiness belongs on the sides.
