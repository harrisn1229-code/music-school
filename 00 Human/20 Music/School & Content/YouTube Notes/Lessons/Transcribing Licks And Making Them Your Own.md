# Transcribing Licks And Making Them Your Own

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zfgjJ6VLNeA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zfgjJ6VLNeA)

## Synopsis

Jack Ruch teaches the *process* of transcribing, not just the vocabulary that comes out of it — this is the methodology companion to [[How Grant Green Played Jazz Blues]] (same channel, same two Grant Green tunes — "Miss Ann's Tempo" and "Number One Green Street," both jazz blues in B♭), and the direct headline case study for [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]]'s thesis that "it's not enough to just simply learn a solo by ear, you've got to get under the hood." Ruch's stated warning up front: transcribing is one of the best ways to improve ear training, phrasing, and vocabulary, but only if you don't stop at learning someone else's lines verbatim — you have to break them down, analyze what's actually happening against the chord underneath, and then rebuild your own line from the same raw material. He works three short phrases from Grant Green solos (over the same B♭ jazz-blues 6-2-5-1 form as the companion video, plus a third tune, "Blues for Willarine," not covered there), explains the harmonic reasoning behind each one, then explicitly plays his own reworked version of each — demonstrating the "make it your own" step live rather than just asserting it. The most concrete, reusable device in the video is one not documented elsewhere in the vault yet: **transposing a fragment by a fixed interval (a half step or a minor third) to fit a related chord**, which Ruch identifies as a genuine recurring habit in Green's own phrasing, not just a teaching trick.

## What's taught

- **The 6-2-5-1 turnaround, explained from first principles (chord-number system).** For a jazz blues in B♭: the vi chord is built on the 6th degree of the B♭ major scale (B♭-C-D-E♭-F-**G**) → G(7); the ii chord on the 2nd degree → C(m7); the V chord on the 5th degree → F(7); the I chord is B♭. Framed as the essential difference between a plain blues (I-IV-V) and a "jazz blues" — the 6-2-5-1 turnaround replaces the simple V-IV-I ending. → [[Blues Form and Harmony]], [[Turnarounds]] § *VI7*
- **Excerpt 1 — "Number One Green Street," I→IV passage.** Over B♭7 moving to E♭ (IV), Green plays notes from the **B♭ altered scale** to color the B♭7 chord, then moves the *exact same passage down a half step* over the E♭ chord — which reads as outlining an **E♭♯11 (Lydian dominant)** sound. Two concepts in one move: (1) altering a dominant I chord with its own altered scale, and (2) sliding that same shape down a half step to land on the ♯11 color of the IV chord it resolves to — a cheap way to get two different, related-sounding "outside" colors from one fingering. → [[Altered Chords]], [[Scale Modes Reference]] (Lydian dominant), [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]
- **Excerpt 1 continued — the VI7 (G7) approach via a diminished-7 arpeggio.** Going into the 6-chord, Green's line is built from a **diminished-7th arpeggio to outline a dominant ♭9 sound**. Ruch states the general rule as "a diminished seventh arpeggio a half step up from a dominant chord," but the worked instance he names is a **G diminished-7 arpeggio played directly over G7** (same root, not a half step above it) — *(transcript flag: these two descriptions don't fully agree; the stated rule — dim7 built a half step above the dominant's root — is the standard "half-whole diminished" device and is probably the intended one; the same-root reading may be an imprecise verbal description or a caption-transcription smoothing. Confirm by ear before treating the same-root version as gospel.)* Either reading nets a genuine, reusable altered-dominant-color device. → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Bebop Vocabulary]] § *Target the guide tones*
- **Excerpt 1 continued — the minor-third transposition device (the video's headline technique).** Over the ii chord (Cm7), Green plays a short phrase, then **immediately moves the exact same phrase up a minor third and repeats it** — explicitly called out as "a really common theme in a lot of Grant's phrasing." This is a distinct move from *reharmonizing* a lick over a different chord (Excerpt 1's altered/Lydian-dominant pivot above) — here the same shape is transposed by a fixed interval and simply replayed, generating new material for free from one idea. → new device added to [[Connecting Licks Into Longer Lines]]
- **Ruch's own reworked version of Excerpt 1 (the "make it your own" step, demonstrated live).** Same B♭-altered lick over B♭7→E♭7, but **resolved differently** — targeting E♭7's own chord tones instead of the original E♭♯11 resolution. Over the 6-chord, a similar diminished run, but this time **staying on chord tones over the ii-minor chord** rather than repeating the altered color. Over the V chord (F7), **another diminished-arpeggio run a half step up from the dominant's root** — the clean, unambiguous instance of the half-whole-diminished device (contrast with the ambiguous G-over-G7 reading above). On the second pass through the form: ♯11 again over IV, then the **same minor-third-transposition idea reapplied** to the 6-2-5-1 material. Stated payoff: "by transcribing and analyzing one little passage we can break it up into multiple different licks and concepts that we can use to create our own unique [lines]." → [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]]
- **Excerpt 2 — "Miss Ann's Tempo," a 2-5-1-of-the-IV line (bars 1–5).** B♭ major triad → G minor triad (with chromatic passing notes) → outlines F minor 7 → B♭7 → resolves to the 3rd of E♭. Named explicitly as a **2-5-1 of the IV chord**: F minor 7 is the ii of E♭, B♭7 is the V of E♭, and E♭ is acting as a local "I." **Stated method, called out directly as important: "you always want to learn the chord progression first — that gives you context for what's happening in the solo"** before transcribing the melodic content over it. → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Chord Substitution]]
- **Excerpt 2 continued — reusing the same line over a different 2-5-1 in the same tune.** Because the tune's 6-2-5-1 turnaround contains its *own* 2-5-1 (C minor 7 → F7 → B♭), Ruch moves the identical F-minor-7/B♭7/E♭ line down to fit **C minor 7 → F7 → B♭**, adjusting the phrasing where needed to make it land correctly on the new chord tones. Concrete demonstration of "a lick learned over one 2-5-1 is reusable over any 2-5-1 in the same key, with adjustment" — ties directly into the recurring 2-5-1 pattern already documented in [[The ii-V-I]]. → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]
- **Excerpt 3 — "Blues for Willarine," a classic 6-2-5-1 line.** A line that "really outlines the chord changes using chord tones and a few altered [notes]" over the full 6-2-5-1 (G7-Cm7-F7-B♭). Ruch reworks it by starting the line identically, then applying the **same minor-third-transposition move from Excerpt 1** to one fragment of it, before tying the result into his own existing vocabulary for the last few chords of the progression. Explicit statement of the underlying goal: "a big part of making these lines your own is combining them with your existing vocabulary, tying them together in unique [ways]." → [[Connecting Licks Into Longer Lines]], [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]]

### The reusable practice method, distilled
1. **Learn the chord progression first**, before or alongside transcribing the melodic line over it — context for what the notes are doing, not just what they are. (Excerpt 2's explicit teaching point, but implicit throughout.)
2. **Identify what harmonic device is happening against each chord** — an altered scale, a Lydian-dominant color, a diminished-7 arpeggio for a ♭9 sound, a 2-5-1-of-the-target-chord shape — name it, don't just replay it by feel.
3. **Rework the resolution.** Take the same lick into the same harmonic situation but resolve it to different chord tones than the original player did.
4. **Transpose by a fixed interval (half step or minor third) to a related chord or a repeat within the same phrase** — the single most concrete, reusable "make it your own" device in this video; not covered elsewhere in the vault before this pass.
5. **Move the whole lick to a different instance of the same harmonic pattern** (any 2-5-1 in the key, not just the one it was originally played over), adjusting the phrasing as needed to land correctly.
6. **Tie the reworked line into your existing vocabulary** rather than leaving it isolated — the actual "own" part of "make it your own" is the splice back into material you already play, not the transcribed fragment in isolation.

No plugin, effect, mixing, or sound/tone-selection content — pure instrument/theory pedagogy (a transcription-methodology lesson built on Grant Green jazz-blues vocabulary, over the exact 12-bar jazz-blues form already documented in [[Blues Form and Harmony]]).
