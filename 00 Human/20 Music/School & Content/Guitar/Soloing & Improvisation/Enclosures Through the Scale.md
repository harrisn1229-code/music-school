# Enclosures Through the Scale

Part of [[Bebop Vocabulary]] (§2 Enclosures) and [[Jazz Harmony]] — a **scale-degree**, chord-tone-independent drill for the enclosure device. Where [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]'s three enclosure shapes are targeted at chord tones and depend on knowing the chord's function (dominant/Dorian/Aeolian) to pick the right note above the target, this drill strips that out entirely: it applies one fixed mechanical rule to **every note of a scale form in turn**, descending from the top all the way to the bottom, without regard to which notes happen to be chord tones. A good on-ramp before or alongside the chord-tone-targeted material — same core device (surround a target note, then land on it), simpler decision tree.

## The core rule
Always **descending**. Starting on the highest note of whatever scale form/box you're working in:
1. Pick your **target** = the next scale tone down from where you are.
2. Descend note by note (through the scale) until you land exactly **one half step above the target**.
3. From there, **skip down PAST the target to the next note that is actually IN THE SCALE** — never skip to a chromatic note, it has to be a diatonic scale tone below the target.
4. **Walk chromatically UP** from that scale tone through however many half steps sit between it and the target, landing on the target.
5. The target you just landed on becomes the new starting point — repeat from step 1 on the next scale tone down.
6. Continue until you reach the bottom of the scale form.

*(general starting point for reading this as note names: over a C major scale form, descending from the top, each "target" is successively B, A, G, F, E, D, C — the descent through the scale itself supplies the "half step above" position for a run of targets naturally, since a fixed rule of "skip to the scale tone below, walk up chromatically" is being reapplied at every stop.)*

## Why the enclosure length varies — not a separate case
How many chromatic notes land between the skipped-to scale tone and the target falls straight out of the rule, it isn't a second rule to memorize: where two adjacent scale tones are already a half step apart (e.g. the 3rd–4th or 7th–root gap in a major scale), step 4's "walk up chromatically" collapses to **zero filler notes** — you land on the target directly off the skip. Where a whole step or more separates them, one or more chromatic passing notes fill the gap. Same mechanism throughout; the shape just gets longer or shorter depending on where you are in the scale.

## Explicitly chord-tone-independent
"Without needing to think of anything as chord tones, we can still do enclosures on any note of a scale that you're in, and it's going to sound fantastic and very jazz." Landing on a scale tone that isn't a strong chord tone still works musically, because the enclosure's *chromatic motion into the target* is what reads as intentional — the ear is carried away from the landing note melodically right after, so it doesn't need to be harmonically "correct" to work. This is the key contrast with [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]'s Shape 3 (up-then-down enclosure), where which note sits *above* the chord tone is function-dependent (Mixolydian vs. Dorian vs. Aeolian) — here there's no such branch, because the target is a scale degree, not a chord tone, and the shape is fixed regardless of harmonic context.

## String-crossing side effect
The skip-down-then-chromatic-walk-up shape forces position/string changes that a straight scalar descent wouldn't — flagged as a practice benefit in passing ("so good for string crossing, too"), not developed as its own separate exercise.

## Prerequisite: know the scale as a map
This only works fluidly once the scale form is known well enough that, mid-descent, the next scale tone down is instantly obvious without hunting for it — "you have to know the scale so well that you just see it as a map." Looking at a scale diagram while first working the drill out is explicitly fine, not a crutch to avoid.

## How to practice it
- Work it out as a **puzzle**, not a lick to copy outright — solve the descent yourself in a chosen scale form before trying to internalize it as muscle memory.
- Demonstrated in C major; the rule is scale-form-agnostic — apply the same descending process to any scale you already know as a shape (pentatonic, modal, melodic/harmonic minor forms, etc.).
- **Don't force full mastery of the whole descending run.** If one particular enclosure spot in the sequence sounds especially good, it's fine to pull just that one shape out and drop it into real playing over a tune rather than drilling the entire scale-length descent every time.
