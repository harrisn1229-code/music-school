## What's taught

- **[~0:00–0:40] Setup.** "Longview" is from Green Day's 1994 *Dookie*, played by bassist Mike Dirnt (born Michael Ryan Pritchard — "Dirnt" is a grade-school nickname from making a "dirnt dirnt" sound while air-bassing). The song is named for Longview, Washington, reportedly Green Day's first-ever show location. This video covers only the main intro/verse riff, not the full arrangement (BassBuzz sells a separate full tab with sheet music, referenced but not linked here).

- **[~0:40–1:50] Level 1 — root notes only, with a specific fretting-hand fingering.** The bare-minimum version: right root notes, no ornamentation, but still musical because of a "consistent walking rhythm that carries the groove on its own." **The actual technique content is the fingering, not the notes:** metal(fret)-pinky, metal-index, shift, middle, pinky, ring, middle — shift back — middle, pinky, middle, index, pinky, middle, index, middle. Framed explicitly as a great beginner drill because it **exercises all four fretting fingers** in one short riff. **The stated payoff of good fingering:** efficient finger choice keeps notes ringing long and lets them flow into each other; sloppy/arbitrary fingering ("crap fingering") introduces extra string noise and cuts notes off early — a direct sound-quality consequence of fingering choice, not just a technicality. → [[Fretting-Hand Finger Independence Drill]] (a second concrete real-song instance of "efficient fretting-hand fingering choice = fewer cut-off notes and less noise," alongside that note's B-minor "Finger Fixer" exercise).
  - **The "caveman method" for arranging your own simplified version of a song above your level:** imagine hearing the song for the first time with a "little eggshell smooth cave brain" and playing only the most standout notes/rhythms you'd naturally latch onto — a generalizable technique for building your own Level-1-style skeleton of any riff that's currently too hard to play in full, kept on this page only (a mindset/practice-methodology tip, not a settings-level technique with an obvious note home).

- **[~1:50–3:50] Level 2 — swing-eighth filler notes, with an explicit definition of the rhythm.** Adds extra notes at the ends of bars 1 and 2 that "bounce off" the drums and make the pattern more memorable. **The video gives an unusually precise definition of swing eighths:** instead of splitting each beat evenly in half ("one-and-two-and"), the swung eighth note lands later, as if the beat is split into three parts with the middle third skipped ("one-two-three, two-two-three" — i.e. the eighth falls on the third partial of a beat-long triplet, not the halfway point). Stated effect: "a lumpy, uneven feel like punk rock meets blues shuffle" — and the video is explicit that the song "would sound totally different" without this specific swing. **Practice cue for feel over formal counting:** think of the main (on-beat) notes as "durance" and the swing-eighth fillers as "bonus dos" — sing/hum that mnemonic pattern to nail the rhythm before trying to count it formally. **A common wrong-version failure mode named directly:** players often play these added notes as generic throwaway open strings, when they're actually specific, deliberately-chosen pitches that are part of the riff's real melody. → [[Syncopation and Metric Displacement]] (this video's worked triplet-based definition of swing eighths, cross-referenced against that page's separate Jamerson-video swing-eighths note).
  - **General takeaway for building your own lines:** a simple root-note bassline gets more character from small filler/passing notes inserted between the main notes — either drawn from the underlying scale (as Dirnt does here) or freestyled by ear — turning something plain into something distinctive.

- **[~3:50–6:00] Level 3 — pick, tone, and a specific downstroke/upstroke rule.** The tab doesn't change from Level 2 — only the plucking-hand tool does. **Why a pick here:** it gives a more aggressive, cutting tone that stands out more than fingerstyle plucking on this riff. **The actual technique (not just "use a pick"):** Dirnt plays every main quarter note as a **downstroke** and every swung eighth as an **upstroke** — deliberately *not* strict alternating down-up-down-up picking. Reason given: strict alternation would put a downstroke on some strong beats and an upstroke on others, producing an uneven strong-weak-strong-weak accent pattern; locking every on-beat quarter note to a downstroke keeps all the strong beats getting the same "oomph." Stated full-bar stroke pattern: down-down-down-down-**up** (the swing eighth), repeated per bar. **Bonus mechanical payoff:** the pick also sets up the riff's big slide at the song's bass entrance more smoothly than fingers do — with fingers, the same passage is described as sounding "kind of dumb and junky." → [[Pick vs. Fingers on Bass — Tone and Technique Tradeoffs]] (new fifth worked example: the downstroke-on-beat/upstroke-on-swing discipline, plus the slide-setup detail).

- **[~6:00–8:00] Level 4 — E-flat standard tuning.** Green Day tuned down a half step (per an anecdote in the video, Billie Joe Armstrong reportedly didn't even own a guitar tuner before 1997's *Nimrod* and tuned to Dirnt's bass by ear instead). **The move:** drop every string a half step — E♭ A♭ D♭ G♭ — then play the whole riff **one fret higher** than the Level 1–3 tab to compensate, which lands the riff in the more fretboard-familiar key of E shape. Sonically this level sounds close to Level 3; the real payoff is authenticity to the record and setting up Level 5. **Why E-flat standard specifically doesn't break existing scale/arpeggio knowledge:** the interval spacing between adjacent strings stays a perfect fourth (five half steps) even after the whole instrument is tuned down uniformly, so every movable shape a player already knows still works identically — unlike a genuinely alternate (non-uniform) tuning, which would break those shapes. **Named risk:** reading a Longview tab written for E-flat standard but playing it on a bass still in standard E tuning puts everything a half step sharp — flagged explicitly as a common mistake. *(Kept page-only — a single concrete tuning data point; no dedicated alternate-tunings note exists yet in the vault, and one real-world example isn't enough to justify creating one per the depth standard's ≥3-tips bar. Revisit if a future video adds more tuning content.)*

- **[~8:00–end] Level 5 — open-string pedal tone / bass-chord finish.** "Maximum dirt mode": on the last few strums, let the **open D string ring** while playing higher melodic notes on another string — a chord-like, harmonically fuller sound without losing low end, versus a single melodic line that would leave "the track feel empty on the bottom." Named sibling examples of songs using the same open-string-pedal device: "All Right Now" (Free), "Lopsy Lue," "Love Will Tear Us Apart" (Joy Division/Peter Hook). **Mechanical notes:** the fretting fingering is unchanged from earlier levels — only the D string needs to be left open/unmuted on the final bar; **all strokes on the last measure should be downstrokes**, specifically so every note in that closing phrase lands with even strength (echoes the Level 3 downstroke-for-consistency logic, applied to a full bar instead of per-beat). **The generalizable rule stated directly:** this technique only works when the chord/passage's root note is one of the bass's open strings (E, A, D, or G in standard tuning) — you let that root ring open, then layer higher melodic notes from the related scale on top. → [[Bass Chords and Double Stops]] (new open-string-pedal-tone variant of the existing register-based bass-chord technique — a third mechanism for getting a chord-like bass sound, alongside the existing "play the shape higher up the neck" rule).
