# Writing a Complete Song in a Minor Key (Part 1) - Chord Progression

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZNbr82VZOu8)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZNbr82VZOu8)

## Synopsis
Jared Borowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared) walks his full personal process for composing an original song's chord progressions in a minor key, using G minor as the worked example, in a deliberate four-step build: (1) systematically map out **every** diatonic chord option in the key — triads and then seventh chords, run both along single strings (root to root, high to low and back) and across strings via the circle of fourths — so the harmony is fully "seen" before any songwriting decision gets made; (2) sketch a plain, generic song-form skeleton (intro–verse–pre-chorus–chorus–verse–pre-chorus–chorus–bridge–chorus–ending) with nothing written yet, purely to have a template to fill in; (3) fill each section in one at a time — verse first, not the intro — reaching for the harmonic-minor-borrowed major V for a "true minor" pull, a secondary dominant (V7/iv) to add push into the pre-chorus, and a ♭13 extension on that dominant chord (derived from the parallel harmonic minor scale, not the chord's own major scale) for melodic color right before the minor resolution; and (4) write the intro last, built from a fragment of the chorus ("song signature") so the listener recognizes the song from its very first notes. The strongest material for Nate: the **systematic string-by-string + circle-of-fourths chord-mapping drill** (a genuinely reusable practice routine, not song-specific), the **explicit derivation of the minor-key seventh-chord ladder** (i7–iiø7–♭IIImaj7–iv7–v7/V7–♭VImaj7–♭VII7) matched note-for-note against [[Chords by Key]]'s existing table, the **worked secondary-dominant + ♭13-extension move** (G7♭13 → Cm, i.e. V7♭13/iv → iv) as a concrete, transcript-grounded example of exactly the kind of chord [[Secondary Dominants]] and [[Extended Chords]] describe only abstractly, and the **write-the-intro-last "song signature" principle**, a genuinely new songwriting-workflow idea not yet on [[Arrangement and Song Form]]. Explicitly framed as Part 1 of a series — Part 2 (not yet released/queued) covers writing melody over this same progression.

## What's taught
- **Choose the key first, and prefer movable (non-open-string) shapes when the key doesn't offer easy open chords** — worked in G minor throughout; plugs his free "Chords with Color" chart (5 keys × open-string-friendly minor-key voicings) as a companion resource for keys that DO offer open shapes. [~0:55] → [[Chords by Key]]
- **Step 1, Exercise 1 — map every triad along the 6th string, tonic to tonic, using a simple 1-5-3 (root-5th-3rd, three-note) voicing.** Start on the tonic, climb as high as the guitar allows, then go below the tonic and back up — deliberately mirrors his own root-to-root approach to scale practice (start on the main sound, go above it, go below it, land back on it, so the ear locks onto a tonal center). Full ladder mapped and named in order: **i (minor)** → **ii° (diminished, a whole step up)** → **♭III (major, a half step above ii)** → **iv (minor, a whole step up)** → **v/V (played BOTH ways — minor as the natural-minor v, then major as the borrowed V)** → **♭VI (major, a half step up)** → **♭VII (major, a whole step up)** → back to **i**. [~1:32]–[~7:08] → [[Chords by Key]], [[Chord Voicings]]
- **Why the V gets played both minor and major: the borrowed major V IS the harmonic minor scale, and it's what makes a progression read as "functionally minor" rather than modal/natural-minor.** Natural minor's v (minor) is the diatonic default; raising its 3rd to make it a major V borrows from harmonic minor and creates the strong pull back to the tonic minor that "classical/traditional minor" progressions lean on — leaving it as v (minor) instead reads as more modal. Referenced (not covered in this video) his own separate videos on the harmonic minor scale and on chords through the minor key for the deeper theory. [~3:19] → [[Chords by Key]] (already documents this exact borrowed-V fact), [[Scale Modes Reference]]
- **Naming quirk explained: "flat six" and "flat seven" describe the CHORD ROOT's position in the scale (1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7), not a chord quality label** — the ♭VI chord is called "flat six major" (root sits on ♭6 of the scale, chord itself is major quality) and ♭VII likewise. [~4:33]–[~5:15]
- **Step 1, Exercise 2 — repeat the exact same triad-mapping ladder along the 5th string** instead of the 6th, same 1-5-3 voicing, same tonic-to-tonic climb-and-descend shape, played fingerstyle (plucked) but stated as equally valid strummed with correct muting. Purpose stated directly: seeing the same chord options in a second physical location on the neck. [~7:08]–[~7:42] → [[Chord Voicings]]
- **Step 1, Exercise 3 — the full seventh-chord ladder, along the 6th string, using a 1-5-7-3 (bottom-four-string) voicing.** Named degree by degree: **i7 (minor 7)** → **iiø7 (half-diminished)** → **♭IIImaj7 (major 7)** → **iv7 (minor 7)** → **v7, then turned into dominant 7 (V7)** — same "play it both ways" logic as the triad V, and called out again as the source of the "true functional minor key sound" — → **♭VImaj7** → **♭VII7 (dominant 7 — a dominant 7 chord occurs naturally off the ♭7 degree of a minor scale)** → back to **i7**. This ladder is the exact 7th-chord row Nate's own [[Chords by Key]] table already documents (i7 · iiø7 · ♭IIImaj7 · iv7 · v7/V7 · ♭VImaj7 · ♭VII7) — direct transcript confirmation of that table, not new theory. [~7:42]–[~10:53] → [[Chords by Key]], [[Seventh Chords]]
- **Step 1, Exercise 4 — the same seventh-chord ladder repeated off the 5th string root** (Gm7 off the 5th string this time), same degree order, same purpose (a second physical map of the identical harmony). [~10:53 region, folded into the seventh-chord section]
- **Step 1, Exercise 5/6 — circle of fourths (descending fifths / ascending fourths) run through the SAME diatonic chords, both as triads and as 7ths, specifically to see the "crossing strings" options** the straight up/down-one-string drills above don't reveal. Start on the highest playable position and move through the progression via 5–1 (dominant→tonic) root motion: worked triad example **iv → ♭VII → ♭III → ♭VI → ii° → i** (Cm → F → B♭ → E♭ → Adim → Gm in G minor) — explicitly named as the same chord sequence as the jazz standard **"Autumn Leaves"** (and "a million other songs"), because functional harmony (especially jazz) leans heavily on circle-of-fourths root motion; then repeated switching strings at each chord instead of walking a straight line, and once more as full 7th chords (**Cm7 → F7 → B♭maj7 → E♭maj7 → Aø7(half-dim) → D7/Dm7 → Gm7**, run twice through). Root motion by a 4th up / 5th down = a "5-1 relationship" / dominant-tonic relationship — creates a felt "gravity"/arrival sense independent of the actual chord qualities involved. [~10:53]–[~15:52] → [[Circle of Fifths]], [[The ii-V-I]], [[Chords by Key]]
- **Step 1, Exercise 7 ("exercise 17" in his own numbering slip) — free improvisation over the mapped-out chord vocabulary**, explicitly as the fluency step: don't play random chords, chase what genuinely feels good, repeat moments that land ("oh, that sounded good, I'm going to use that") — framed as already being real music-making, not just review, and the direct bridge into the actual songwriting that follows. [~15:59]–[~18:07]
- **Step 2 — sketch a generic song-form skeleton BEFORE writing any chords**, purely a fill-in-the-blank template: **Intro → Verse → Pre-chorus → Chorus → Verse → Pre-chorus → Chorus → Bridge → Chorus → Ending.** States plainly the form itself is completely unoriginal and that's the point — "paint by numbers" removes the open-ended "what happens next" problem so creative energy goes into the content of each section instead of inventing structure from scratch. Pre-chorus is called out as optional/a choice, not mandatory. [~18:07]–[~18:38] → [[Arrangement and Song Form]], [[Musical Form]]
- **Step 3 — fill sections in starting with the VERSE, deliberately saving the intro for last** (see the "song signature" principle below for why). Verse progression built live: **i → ♭VII → V7 → i** (Gm → F → D7 → Gm), i.e. the borrowed-major-V-as-dominant-7 resolving hard back to tonic minor — explicitly called out again as "that thing that makes it sound true, kind of traditional minor," played twice through for the full verse. Side technique mentioned in passing: alternates strumming pattern between low strings and high strings for arrangement variety/orchestration within a single part (points to his own separate strumming-technique videos, not covered here). [~18:38]–[~20:11]/[~20:11]–[~21:39]
- **Pre-chorus built to deliberately introduce chords NOT yet used** — explicit rule of thumb for writing a new section: ask "what's a fresh area we haven't gone to yet." First pass: ♭VImaj7 → iv (Cm) → V7 (D7) → i (Gm) — i.e. reaching for the previously-unused ♭VI and iv chords. [~21:39]–[~23:19]
- **Worked secondary dominant, named and explained live: V7/iv.** Starting from ♭VImaj7 (E♭maj7), turning what would be the iv chord's own root (C) into a dominant 7th makes it "not in the key of G minor" — a **secondary dominant** (his term: "a chromatic chord") that tonicizes/leads specifically into the iv chord (Cm) rather than the tonic. Final pre-chorus progression at this stage: **♭VImaj7 → C7(V7/iv) → Cm(iv) → V7(D7) → i**, i.e. the C7 slots in as an approach chord immediately before the Cm it's borrowed to point at. Directly names [[Secondary Dominants]]'s core mechanic (V7 of a non-tonic diatonic chord) with a concrete worked minor-key example this vault's existing Secondary Dominants note doesn't yet have. [~23:19]–[~24:46] → [[Secondary Dominants]], [[Chord Progressions]]
- **Worked ♭13 extension on that same secondary dominant, explained via its harmonic-minor-scale origin: G7♭13.** With the pre-chorus already using C7 (V7/iv) into Cm, adds one more layer of color: the ♭3 of the Cm chord (E♭) is reused as a **♭13 extension riding on top of the G7** that leads into it (a shell voicing of G7 with a ♭13 added) — described explicitly as "specifically something that exists when you're about to go to a minor chord." Mechanically: the ♭13 sits a half step above the chord's own 5th (5 of G → 13 of G, raised... i.e. lowered a half step from the natural 13/6th). Explains WHY it has to be ♭13 and not a plain 13 by scale-membership: on a parallel worked example over D7 leading back to Gm, the natural (major) 13th of D7 is the major 3rd of G — outside the G minor scale — while the ♭13 IS inside G's minor scale (1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7), so extensions built off any chord in the key still have to relate back to the tonal center's actual scale, not get derived generically from each individual chord's own major scale. Final pre-chorus, full form: **♭VImaj7 (Ebmaj7) → G7♭13 (V7♭13/iv) → Cm (iv) → V7 (D7)** → back to i, looped twice, with a ♭VII dominant 7 (B♭7) used to lead back into the top. [~24:46]–[~27:37] → [[Extended Chords]], [[Chords by Key]]
- **Chorus built as a simple two-chord back-and-forth that deliberately steps OUTSIDE the minor coloring** — ♭VImaj7 ↔ ♭VIImaj7 (E♭maj7 / B♭maj7), consciously avoiding "the minorness of the whole key" for a brighter, more major-leaning lift, while still conceptually staying "in G minor" overall. Transition detail: since a half step below E♭ is D7 (the verse's own V7 chord), the chorus loops back into the verse cleanly for free — no extra connecting chord needed, the existing harmony already sets up the return. [~27:37]–[~29:20]
- **Bridge built deliberately simple, on purpose, as a reaction to how "dense" the pre-chorus got** — explicit stated principle: don't over-innovate every single section; after a harmonically busy section (secondary dominant + extension), the next section can (and often should) be a simple palette cleanser. Reached again for "what haven't we used yet" (half-diminished, i.e. ii°/iiø7, not yet used anywhere in the song) as the one new color, kept everything else plain: **i (Gm) → ♭VI triad (Eb) → ♭VII triad (F)**, leading back into the chorus. [~29:20]–[~31:00]
- **The "song signature" principle — write the intro LAST, and build it from a fragment of a section the listener will hear again (the chorus).** Deliberately deferred the intro until every other section existed, reasoning that a good intro should contain "a piece of what we used later in the song" so the very beginning of the track is recognizable — cites the live-show phenomenon of a crowd cheering the instant they recognize a song from its opening notes as the target feeling. Explicitly warns against an intro that "could be anything" and is only identifiable once vocals/melody arrive. Concrete move here: intro = a short riff/vibe lifted directly from the chorus material, to be dressed with a melodic line over it (deferred to the Part 2 melody-writing video). → [[Arrangement and Song Form]] [~31:00]–[~33:00]
- **Whole-process framing, stated explicitly as the actual takeaway:** map the key's chord options first (Step 1) so you're never "guessing and checking," sketch a generic song-form skeleton (Step 2) so structure isn't invented from scratch every time, then fill sections one at a time (Step 3, verse before intro) reaching for one or two deliberate non-diatonic/color moves per section (a secondary dominant, an extension, a fresh unused diatonic chord) rather than over-innovating everywhere at once, and write the intro last as a callback fragment. States this is one method among many (melody-first, lyrics-first, beat-first are all equally valid) — not a universal rule, just his own repeatable "paint by numbers" system. [~33:00]–end
- **Final assembled progression for the whole song (Part 1's deliverable), pieced together and played with a simple drum/bass backing track** at the very end of the video, previewing the finished Part 1 chord structure before Part 2 covers writing melody/vocal lines over it. [~36:40]–[~38:00]
