# Songs that use Polyrhythms & Polymeters

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GVDGxWL9oOo)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GVDGxWL9oOo)

## Synopsis

Defines and clearly distinguishes two commonly-confused concepts — polyrhythm (two evenly-spaced pulses superimposed within one shared pulse/time signature) and polymeter (two genuinely different time signatures played simultaneously at a shared tempo) — with a wide range of worked song examples for each, from foundational (built into the whole song) to occasional (a brief, deliberately disorienting feature).

## Techniques

- **Polyrhythm, precisely defined, plus the naming convention**: more than one evenly-spaced pulse occupying the same bar at once — e.g. 3 evenly-spaced beats and 4 evenly-spaced beats superimposed. Named as "[non-main] over [main pulse]" — the SECOND number is the prevailing time-signature pulse. "Fake Empire" (The National, 3/4) — left hand plays 3 evenly-spaced beats/bar (the main pulse), right hand plays 4 on top = **4-over-3**. "Chariots of Fire" (Vangelis) — synth pulses 4 eighth notes/bar (the main pulse), piano enters with 3 evenly-spaced notes on top = **3-over-4** — explicitly flagged as a DIFFERENT poly­rhythm from 4-over-3 despite using the same two numbers, because which number is the prevailing pulse flips.
- **3-over-2 ("vertical hemiola") as the most common, easiest-to-play polyrhythm**: "Daydreaming" (Radiohead) — right hand plays 3 evenly-spaced beats in the time the left hand plays 2. General mechanism: any quarter-note triplet layered over a straight 4/4 pulse is a 3-over-2 polyrhythm — "Light My Fire" (The Doors, organ solo — drums shift to marking triplets), "Gloria" (Them, guitar solo plays triplets over the band's straight 4/4), "Is This Love" (Bob Marley, more extensive/structural use in the chorus melody).
- **Foundational vs. occasional polyrhythm use — a real distinction with different musical effects**: a polyrhythm used as the entire song's rhythmic foundation throughout is sometimes called a **cross-rhythm** (the Fake Empire/Daydreaming examples). A polyrhythm can instead appear only briefly (a solo, a bridge, 1–2 bars) as a deliberate disorienting effect. **The disorientation-preservation mechanism, named explicitly**: Jacob Collier's "Hajanga" (128 compound time, 4 prevailing beats/bar) drops a 3-over-4 polyrhythm drum part into the second verse for only 2 bars at a time before it disappears — kept intentionally too short for the ear to "accommodate it into the groove" and normalize it, so it reads as equally disorienting every time it reappears rather than becoming a settled part of the pulse. General rule extractable from this: to keep a polyrhythm feeling disorienting rather than groove-absorbed, don't let it run long enough for the ear to adapt.
- **Higher-order polyrhythms exist and are named the same way**: TOOL's "Lateralus" — drums in 6/4 (main pulse), vocals in an evenly-spaced 8-beats/bar pulse on top = **8-over-6**.
- **Polymeter, precisely defined and distinguished from polyrhythm**: polyrhythm = multiple pulses within ONE shared time signature/pulse; polymeter = multiple DIFFERENT time signatures played at the same tempo (one shared tempo, not one shared meter). **Practical notation note**: even though a true polymeter has two real time signatures sounding at once, only one gets written on the page (it's impractical to notate two simultaneously) — the other part gets forcibly notated inside the chosen signature instead, which is a transcription convenience, not evidence the part isn't "really" in a different meter.
- **Worked polymeter examples**: "Kashmir" (Led Zeppelin) — drums in a solid 4/4, string melody in 3/4, same tempo, same downbeat, genuinely a 4-against-3 polymeter (not to be confused with a 4-over-3 polyrhythm — different mechanism, same ratio). "Let Down" (Radiohead) — guitar part in groups of 5/8 against drums/bass in a clear 4/4, drifting out of phase almost immediately for a "chaotic, cascading" effect. "5/4" (Gorillaz, aptly named) — guitar riff establishes 5/4, drums enter in 4/4, producing a 5-against-4 polymeter.
