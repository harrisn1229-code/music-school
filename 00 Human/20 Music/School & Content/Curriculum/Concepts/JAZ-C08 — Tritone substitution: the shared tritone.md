# JAZ-C08 — Tritone substitution: the shared tritone

**Q.** Why can D♭7 stand in for G7, which chords qualify for this treatment, and what does the swap buy you?

> [!question]- Answer
> Both chords contain **the same tritone**. G7's is B and F; D♭7 contains F and C♭, and C♭ is B. The 3rd and 7th simply swap roles, so the tension and the resolution are unchanged.
>
> **Only dominant chords qualify** — the tritone is what is being preserved, and only a dominant 7th has one. The substitute always sits **a semitone above the target of resolution**. What it buys is chromatic bass motion: `Dm7 G7 Cmaj7` becomes `Dm7 D♭7 Cmaj7`, roots walking D – D♭ – C.

## Why it matters

That descending chromatic bass is one of the most recognisable sounds in jazz and neo-soul, and it is available for the price of one chord. Substituting the V of a ii–V–I gives the very common `IIm7 – ♭II7 – Imaj7`.

It is already in pop, found by ear rather than by theory — the chromatic iii → ii → ♭II descent in "Toxic" reads as tritone subs of a 6-2-5, and "One Note Samba" stacks two subs to turn a whole turnaround into a chromatic descending bassline. Worth remembering that the theory is a lens applied after the fact, not necessarily the writer's own model.

## Drill

Take a 3-6-2-5 turnaround and tritone-sub every dominant in it. Play the resulting bassline on its own and confirm every root drops a single semitone.
