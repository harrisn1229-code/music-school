# Birthday — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

One of very few Lennon/McCartney originals built on a **strict twelve-bar frame** ([[BLU-C01 — The 12-bar form]]) — for all the blues they covered early on, they used the actual form sparingly, as a colour rather than a home idiom. But the best lesson in the essay is not the blues: it's the pair of **connector sections that do the same structural job by opposite means**.

**Form.** Effectively the standard two-bridge model, disguised by a long instrumental opening (the entire verse played through as an anticipation) and by the connectors introducing each bridge. Excluding the long connector, the sung sections form a near-palindrome. Throughout, the lead vocal enters after the downbeat; the only vocals landing on a downbeat are the female backing singers in the bridges.

**Harmony.** Every verse chord is a dominant seventh, with one deviation from the textbook: bar 10 sustains V instead of dropping to IV. The long connector's payoff is a **deceptive resolution** ([[HAR-C04 — Deceptive cadence]]) — eight hammered bars of V going not to I but to **♭III**, which is less remote than it sounds, being the relative major of the parallel minor ([[POP-C08 — Relative-key switching]]). The bridge bassline is the detail worth copying: a chromatic climb that simply keeps going past its target so the chord lands in second inversion, momentarily keyless, until the bass drops to the root and reveals it as V again.

**Arrangement.** **Antiphony is the unifying device and it's in every section** ([[BLU-C05 — Call and response is the form]]) — only the responder changes: lead guitar, then the vocal, then a processed tack piano, then the backing singers. The two connectors are the teaching point: the long one prolongs V for sixteen bars and resolves deceptively; the short one is four bars of guitars in plain octaves sliding over in the parallel natural minor, and it's the only place in the song the drums stop. The second time round the listener needs relief, not another build.
