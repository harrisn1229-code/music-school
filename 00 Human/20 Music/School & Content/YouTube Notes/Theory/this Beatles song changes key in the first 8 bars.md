# this Beatles song changes key in the first 8 bars

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ik5Bq0jgcFc)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ik5Bq0jgcFc)

## Synopsis

A bar-by-bar harmonic analysis of the first 8 bars of "If I Fell" (Beatles), which crams a modulation and two tritone substitutions into its opening 18 seconds — the inverse of the usual pop key change (which lands in the final third for an energy lift). Doubles as a worked lesson in how the exact same chord can read completely differently depending on what harmonic context has been established around it.

## Techniques

- **Tritone substitution used as a direct 1-chord resolution device, not the classical Neapolitan function**: "If I Fell" opens ♭II → I (D major resolving down to D♭ major, in the intro's key of D♭ major) — a flat-2 chord resolving straight to the tonic. The video is careful to distinguish this from a true **Neapolitan chord**, which conventionally resolves TO the dominant (♭II → V), not to the tonic directly. A ♭II chord resolving straight down to I is instead a **tritone substitution** of the standard V→I perfect cadence — substitute the dominant for the chord a tritone away from it, and you land on the ♭II; described as "a jazzy, spicier alternative to a traditional perfect cadence," common in jazz but usable in pop too. → [[Tritone Substitution]], [[Neapolitan Sixth]] (the two concepts share a chord — ♭II — but resolve in opposite directions, worth keeping distinct).
- **The exact same chord (D major) functions completely differently depending on which 4-bar block it appears in**: in bars 1–4, D major (the tritone-sub ♭II) sounds chromatic and tense, actively wanting to resolve down to D♭. In bars 5–8, after D major has been repeatedly established as part of the song's harmonic fabric, the exact same chord instead becomes a genuine ii–V–I pivot into a **new key** (D major) — it stops resolving down and instead resolves UP into its own ii chord (E minor 7), installing D major as the new tonic. Same three notes, opposite harmonic behavior, purely from context. → [[Modulation (Key Change)]].
- **Hiding a real key change by repetition-then-betrayal**: bars 1–4 and 5–8 start identically (same E♭m7, same melody, same tritone-sub D chord) — priming the ear to expect the same resolution both times. The "trick" is that bar 8 betrays that expectation: instead of resolving down to D♭ again, it pivots up via a ii-V-I into D major. Because the D chord had already been "trusted" from the first pass, the actual modulation is, in the video's words, "hidden in plain sight" — most listeners (including the analyst himself, on repeated listens) never consciously register that a key change happened at all.
- **A key change placed at the very START of a song, rather than the pop-standard final-third placement**: explicitly framed as the opposite of the usual energy-lift modulation — used here purely for harmonic interest in the opening, not for a late-song lift.
