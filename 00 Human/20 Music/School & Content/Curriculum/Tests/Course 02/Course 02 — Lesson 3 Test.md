> **Q1.** **Treat any diatonic chord as a temporary tonic and put its own dominant in front of it**
> The borrowed tension points at a chord that isn't the real tonic.
>
> **Q2.** **A7, made by raising the 3rd of Am to C♯ — labelled V/ii**
> *Also accepted:* A7 · five of two · V of ii
> The chord on A in C major is Am; sharpening its third turns it into a dominant 7th aimed at Dm.
>
> **Q3.** **A borrowed leading tone**
> C♯ is a semitone below D, so it pulls into Dm the way B pulls into C in the home key.
>
> **Q4.** **Borrowing chords from the parallel minor while keeping the tonic**
> *Also accepted:* from the parallel key · same root, other mode
> The key centre stays; the collection of available notes changes.
>
> **Q5.** **Fm (iv) · A♭ (♭VI) · B♭ (♭VII) · E♭ (♭III)**
> *Also accepted:* any four of the parallel-minor triads with correct numerals
>
> **Q6.** **IV → iv → I — the major subdominant darkening before it resolves home**
> The same chord heard twice, once bright and once shaded, which is why the move is so recognisable.
>
> **Q7.** **Secondary dominants add pull; borrowed chords add colour**
> One creates motion, the other creates mood.
>
> **Q8.** **Because the tonic never moves — only the notes available around it change**
> A key change relocates home. Modal interchange keeps home exactly where it was and repaints the room.
>
> **Q9.** **The secondary dominant is a non-diatonic dominant 7th resolving down a fifth. Borrowed chords aren't.**
> *Rubric (5):* names the dominant-7th quality as the secondary-dominant tell (2) · names resolution down a fifth to its target (1) · says a borrowed chord typically isn't a dominant and resolves to the tonic or just sits (2)
> Quality does most of the work. A dominant 7th that isn't in the key is almost always pointing at something, and where it points is a fifth below its root. A borrowed chord is usually a plain triad or minor chord, and it either goes home or stays put rather than aiming anywhere. The tell is reliable because the two devices have different jobs — pull needs the tritone that only a dominant 7th supplies, and colour doesn't.
>
> **Q10.** **The Fm changes the mood of the bar; the E7 changes where the bar is going**
> *Rubric (5):* says the borrowed Fm adds colour without redirecting the progression (2) · says E7 creates pull toward Am, adding motion (2) · notes that one is a vertical/mood change and the other a horizontal/directional one (1)
> Swapping F for Fm leaves the progression's shape untouched — the chord occupies the same slot, does the same subdominant job, and just arrives darker. Putting E7 in front of Am doesn't recolour anything; it inserts tension aimed at the next chord, so the loop gains a direction it didn't have. One is a change to how a moment feels, the other to how the moments connect. That's why "both leave the key" is the least informative thing you can say about them.
