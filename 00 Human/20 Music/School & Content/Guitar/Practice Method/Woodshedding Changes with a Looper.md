# Woodshedding Changes with a Looper

A practice-order method for working out lines over a chord progression using a hardware looper pedal with a built-in drum machine (or a DAW backing track doing the same job) as the rhythmic/harmonic anchor, rather than practicing free of any groove. Sibling to [[Backward Chaining Practice]] (a different practice-order methodology, same channel) and the physical-practice half of [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]'s chunk-then-connect strategy.

## Why loop the chords instead of playing free

Playing ideas with no backing reference lets you invent nice-sounding lines, but you can't tell whether they'll actually **fit the pocket of the groove** — timing and phrasing only prove out against a real rhythmic/harmonic bed. Looping the chord progression with a drum groove underneath keeps you "rhythmically and harmonically accountable" while you work out phrasing, so what you land on is something you know will sit correctly, not just something that sounds fine in isolation. — Jack Ruch, [[How using the TELEPORT can IMPROVE your PRACTICE routine!]]

## The loop-and-work routine

1. **Record 2–4 bars of the chord chunk you're targeting into the looper**, with the built-in drum machine running underneath for rhythmic support — a **one-measure count-in before recording** makes it easy to lock the chunk in cleanly on the first take. A **dedicated record button with no secondary functions and no latency** (as opposed to loopers where record shares a footswitch with other functions) matters here — any lag or mis-hit while trying to nail the chunk's start breaks the whole loop and forces a redo.
2. **Stop the loop and work out ideas against silence first** — look at the chord shapes and their corresponding arpeggios in position, without the groove running, to plan targets and connecting tones before trying to execute them in time (mirrors the out-of-time stage of [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]'s two-stage drill).
3. **Start the loop and test the idea against the groove** — play the planned line over the looped chunk to hear whether the phrasing actually locks with the drum machine's rhythm; expect flubs and ugly note choices early on, and use the loop's low-pressure repetition to iterate rather than aiming for a clean take immediately.
4. **Chunk long progressions into 2–4-chord units, work each in isolation, then expand.** A long, twisty chord progression gets broken into small pieces (e.g. the first 3 chords, then the next 2–5–1 unit, then the turnaround) — each chunk is looped and worked separately until it's comfortable, then adjacent chunks are combined, and finally the whole progression is looped so the connected phrasing can be run start to finish. Same "chunk-then-connect" logic as [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]] § *Chunk-then-connect*, here driven by the looper's per-section record/clear workflow instead of just mental sectioning.
5. **Swap drum grooves/tempos to match the tune's feel** before looping — e.g. an eighth-note rock groove at 86 BPM for one progression, a slower 128-ballad-feel groove for a gospel-style progression — so the chunk being drilled is practiced against a rhythmic context that actually matches the target song, not a generic click.

## Worked example: a Ray Charles–style gospel progression

Looped in chunks over a slowed 128-ballad drum groove: **E♭maj → G7 → Cm** (targeted first, chord tones addressed individually per chord since the move isn't a simple diatonic run), then **B♭m7 → E♭9 → A♭7** (a secondary [[The ii-V-I|ii–V]] resolving to the ♭IV rather than the I — see [[The ii-V-I]] for the general "251 as a landing-spot device" framing), each chunk looped and soloed over individually before being tied into the full progression. — Jack Ruch, [[How using the TELEPORT can IMPROVE your PRACTICE routine!]]

## The gear behind it: what makes a looper good for this

Reviewing the **Teleport** (a new looper pedal with an app for sharing recorded loops/performances with other players): the features that actually matter for this practice use are a **built-in drum machine** (locks parts in rhythmically without a separate backing track), a **one-measure count-in on record**, and a **dedicated, single-function record button with virtually no latency** — avoiding the sync problems that come from loopers where record shares a button with other functions, especially when layering multiple rhythm parts. Nate's own looper, the [[Line 6 DL4]], is a comparable class of tool for this exact routine — pair the drum-groove-and-count-in workflow above with it, or with a DAW backing track (Jack Ruch notes he mostly uses his own DAW and pre-made backing tracks nowadays rather than a pedal looper day to day). — Jack Ruch, [[How using the TELEPORT can IMPROVE your PRACTICE routine!]]
