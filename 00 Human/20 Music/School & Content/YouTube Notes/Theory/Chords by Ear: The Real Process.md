# Chords by Ear: The Real Process

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9aM3JbcPNAk)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9aM3JbcPNAk)

## Synopsis
Max Konyi (Mind of Max, creator of the Sonop ear-training app) breaks down the actual mental process of working out a chord by ear — not "here's the answer" but the escalating decision tree that gets there when recognition isn't instant. He frames every chord as having three separable aspects (root/degree, quality, bass note) that eventually fuse into a single holistic "feeling" the way [[Scale Degree Ear Training]] describes for single notes, and lays out three modes of attack — immediate recognition, theory-assisted deduction, and brute-force note-finding — escalating only as far as needed. This is the theory-companion piece to [[Transcribing and Playing by Ear]]'s real-time transcription episodes: where those videos show the process running live over real songs, this one slows down and narrates the decision logic in isolation.

## Techniques
- **Every chord has three separable aspects** — the root/scale-degree (which number the root sits on), the quality (major, minor, sus2, dominant 9, diminished 7, etc.), and the bass note (usually the root, but not when the chord is inverted) — each with its own trainable feeling; over time they stop being three separate lookups and fuse into one instant gestalt recognition, mirroring how [[Scale Degree Ear Training]] describes chords as "meta feeling states."
- **Mode 1 — Immediate recognition.** The target state: a chord's full identity (root, quality, bass) is obvious the instant it's heard, no conscious steps at all. This is what years of drilling chord-type-plus-position combinations against a drone eventually produces.
- **Mode 2 — Deduction.** When recognition doesn't fire instantly, narrow the technically-infinite possibility space with perceptual clues layered together: a recognized quality ("this sounds major"), a recognized bass scale-degree, a recognized bass-to-bass *motion* between the current and previous chord, and genre-specific harmonic conventions (jazz, blues, classical, and R&B all imply different chord vocabularies, so genre context itself is a clue). Combine the clues with music theory to shrink the field to a handful of candidates, then test each — either by playing it on an instrument and comparing directly, or by imagining/singing it (the imagined version doesn't need to be perfectly clear, just close enough to judge a match against the source).
  - **Worked example:** an unfamiliar major-sounding chord inside an established major key narrows immediately to I, IV, or V (the three major diatonic triads). The tonic (I) is already known from context, so it's ruled out. Hearing that the chord's bass note sits a half-step *above* the previous chord's bass rules out V (nothing in the diatonic scale approaches V by a half-step from below) and confirms IV — which does have exactly that half-step approach, from iii (3→4 is the only diatonic half-step landing on 4). Two clues (quality + bass motion) plus one piece of theory (which diatonic degrees sit a half-step apart) collapse "could be anything" into one answer.
- **Mode 3 — Brute-force note-finding.** The last resort when a chord gives no usable clues at all (an unfamiliar voicing, an extension nobody's drilled yet, a genuinely ambiguous inner texture): listen to it repeatedly and pick out individual pitches one at a time rather than trying to hear the whole thing at once. **Grab the outer notes first** — the lowest and highest pitches — since they immediately constrain the possibility space (the bass tells you likely root/inversion, the top note tells you likely melodic function), then work inward, guessing at inner tones. A solid theory background makes brute force converge much faster than blind pitch-matching — recognizing early that "this must be a flat-9 over a flat-7" from partial evidence narrows the guessing dramatically compared to starting from zero. Even a rough sense of a "cluster" of close inner tones (without resolving each pitch individually) counts as useful evidence to carry into the next pass.
- **Close the loop: once solved, label it and drill it.** After working out a chord's full identity through deduction or brute force, don't just move on — listen to it again, play it, and name it out loud so the discovery moves permanently into the Mode-1 immediate-recognition category. Skipping this step means re-deducing the same chord type from scratch next time it shows up.
- **The whole system is genre-aware.** Recognizing which harmonic vocabulary a piece of music is drawing from (jazz vs. blues vs. classical vs. R&B) is itself a perceptual clue that narrows candidates in Mode 2 — genre convention isn't separate from ear training, it's one more input to the deduction process.
