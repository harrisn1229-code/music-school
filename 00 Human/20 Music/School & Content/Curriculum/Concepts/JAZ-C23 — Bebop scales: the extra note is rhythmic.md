# JAZ-C23 — Bebop scales: the extra note is rhythmic

**Q.** What makes a scale a bebop scale, and what problem does the added note solve?

> [!question]- Answer
> A normal seven-note scale with **one chromatic passing tone added**, making eight notes.
>
> The reason is rhythmic, not harmonic. Play a seven-note scale in straight eighths from a downbeat and by the next bar the chord tones have shifted onto offbeats. **The eighth note puts them back on the beat, every bar.** Bebop dominant on G is G A B C D E F F♯ G — the F♯ is the addition, and running it down from the root lands G, F, D and B on downbeats — the four chord tones of G7.
>
> The rule: the extra note goes wherever it makes chord tones fall on strong beats. If yours already do, you do not need one.

## Why it matters

It reframes a scale as a rhythmic object rather than a pitch collection, which is the useful half.

The transfer is to basslines: the added eighth is exactly what makes a walking-ish line land its chord tones on the beat instead of drifting off it. What does *not* transfer is the note rate — bebop's density is a genre convention, not a principle, and importing it wholesale into a trap beat just sounds busy.

## Drill

Write a one-bar eighth-note run using the seven-note scale and mark where the chord tones land. Add the passing tone and mark them again.
