> **Q1.** Sharps **F C G D A E B** · Flats **B E A D G C F**
>
> **Q2.** **F♯ major** — F♯ C♯ G♯ D♯ A♯ E♯
>
> **Q3.** **D♭ major** — B♭ E♭ A♭ D♭ G♭
>
> **Q4.** **C♭ = B.** Correct in **G♭ major**, whose fourth degree is C♭ (the scale already used B for B♭).
>
> **Q5.** P4 → P5 · M6 → m3 · m3 → M6 · A4 → d5
>
> **Q6.** **An augmented 3rd.**
> D E F is three letters, so the number is 3 no matter how it sounds. F♯♯ is five semitones above D — the same pitch distance as the perfect 4th D→G — but the letters say 3rd, and letter distance fixes the number.
>
> **Q7.** Perfect: **unison, 4th, 5th, octave.** Major/minor: **2nd, 3rd, 6th, 7th.**
>
> **Q8.** ♯11 → **augmented 4th** · ♭13 → **minor 6th**
>
> **Q9.** **Tonic** (C, Am, Em) · **Subdominant** (F, Dm) · **Dominant** (G, B°).
>
> **Q10.** **Authentic (perfect) cadence.** Maximum resolution — the default ending of most phrases in Western music.
>
> **Q11.** **Plagal cadence.**
> Gentler because IV contains **no leading tone** — there's no semitone pulling up to the tonic, so it settles rather than resolves. Hence its use in gospel and rock.
>
> **Q12.** **Deceptive cadence.**
> Believable because **vi shares two notes with I** — in the key of C, the vi chord is A C E and the I chord is C E G, so C and E are common to both, so the ear accepts it as a near-miss substitute for the expected tonic.
>
> **Q13.** **Half cadence.**
> It ends open — a question that the next phrase answers. It's what creates antecedent/consequent phrasing and therefore 8- and 16-bar sections.
>
> **Q14.** **B and F.** B pulls up to C; F pulls down to E.
>
> **Q15.** **D7 (D F♯ A C)**, resolving to **G**.
>
> **Q16.** Borrowed from **C minor** (the parallel minor) — a **borrowed chord**, or modal interchange.
> It's the iv, and it works because the ear accepts the parallel key's colour over the same tonic.
>
> **Q17.** **A passing tone.**
>
> **Q18.** **Duration and confirmation.**
> Tonicization points briefly at a new tonic and leaves; modulation stays and confirms it with a cadence. It's a spectrum, set by how long you remain and whether you cadence there.
>
> **Q19.** **A B C D E F G.** Parallel formula: major with **♭3, ♭6, ♭7**.
>
> **Q20.** **A B C D E F G♯.**
> Solves: the raised 7th gives minor a real leading tone and therefore a functioning V chord. Creates: an **augmented 2nd** between F and G♯.
>
> **Q21.** **A B C D E F♯ G♯.** Raising the 6th as well removes the augmented 2nd that harmonic minor leaves.
>
> **Q22.** **C D E G A**
> — the **4th and 7th** are removed. Those two create the half-step tensions against the major triad, so without them nothing in the scale can clash.
>
> **Q23.** **D E F G A B C.** Characteristic note: the **natural 6th (B)** — what separates it from natural minor.
>
> **Q24.** **E♭ F G A B♭ C D.** Characteristic note: the **♯4 (A natural)**.
>
> **Q25.** **Its tonic triad is diminished**
> — there's no perfect 5th, so nothing stable to resolve to. The "home" chord never sounds like home.
>
> **Q26.** **Derivative:**
> same seven notes as a parent scale, started on a different degree. **Parallel:** same root as a major/minor scale, with specific degrees altered.
>
> **Q27.** **C Lydian.** The chord is asking for a ♯11, and Lydian's raised 4th *is* that note.
>
> **Q28.** Borrowed chords are just the **triads built on a parallel mode's scale**.
> Borrowing Fm into C major is the same as taking the iv of C minor — the chord view and the scale view describe one operation.
>
> **Q29.** **Stack thirds from the root — two thirds a triad, three a seventh chord, six a 13th**
> The system is **tertian** harmony. Six applications exhaust the diatonic notes, which is why 13 is the largest ordinary extension.
>
> **Q30.** **M3+m3 (4+3) major · m3+M3 (3+4) minor · m3+m3 (3+3) dim · M3+M3 (4+4) aug — the middle note**
> Quality is two intervals in an order, not a fact memorised per chord. Only the 3rd moves between major and minor; the 5th is what decides diminished against augmented.
>
> **Q31.** **Secundal (2nds) · quartal (4ths) · quintal (5ths) — it has no honest name for a stack of 4ths**
> Quintal respells as the same notes as quartal, so it isn't a separate system. The naming convention describes harmony built in thirds specifically, which is why standard symbols keep mislabelling quartal voicings as sus4.
>
> **Q32.** **maj7 · dominant 7 · m7 · mMaj7 — plus m7♭5 and dim7 on a diminished triad**
> The grid is {major or minor triad} × {natural 7 or ♭7}. **Dominant 7 occurs on exactly one degree of a major key (V) and half-diminished on exactly one (vii)**, so either one spotted out of context names its parent key.
>
> **Q33.** **A complete lower structure — a full triad for the 9 and 11, a full seventh chord for the 13**
> It is **not** about octave placement: an extension voiced next to the root is still a 9. Pull the 3rd and the added 2 stays a 2; pull the 7th and the added 6 stays a 6.
>
> **Q34.** **A chord over a bass note that isn't its root — if the bass is already one of its tones it's an inversion**
> If it isn't, you get a new composite chord with its own name, function and resolution. The reliable home position is the upper triad's root on the ♭6 of a major key.
>
> **Q35.** **Sus replaces the 3rd, add adds to an intact triad with no 7th — the test is whether the 3rd is present**
> Present → the added note is the 9 (Cadd9 = C E G D). Absent → it is the 2 (Csus2). Neither is an alteration: nothing is sharpened or flattened.
>
> **Q36.** **A chord tone shifted a half step — the movable ones are the 5th and the 9th, sometimes the 11th**
> So ♭5, ♯5, ♭9, ♯9, ♯11, and it is a dominant-family device. The one rule about the melody: **do not alter a note the melody is sitting on.** Sus removes, add adds, altered *moves* — that is what separates the three.
>
> **Q37.** **B D F♯ G♯ — the added 6th is always the major 6th, even on a minor triad. C6 is note-for-note Am7**
> The "minor" in Bm6 names only the triad. A minor triad with the actual flat 6 is a different chord, written m♭6.
>
> **Q38.** **Four — and the test is whether any note of it is the leading note of the chord that follows**
> The pattern is three stacked minor 3rds, four notes in all, so any of the four can be read as the root. No → a passing chord, driving nothing. Yes → a dominant substitute, and it resolves.
>
> **Q39.** **Only which chord tone is in the bass · three · voice leading, bass-line control, hand economy**
> The notes themselves are unchanged. A triad has two inversions, a seventh chord three. On guitar the live decision is usually which tones to drop instead, and the bass note follows from that.
>
> **Q40.** **Bass as root · relate every upper note to it · name the chord from those degrees — B♭/C is C7sus4(9)**
> B♭ D F over C reads as ♭7, 9 and 11. It stops short in two cases: if the bass is already a tone of the upper chord there is no new chord, and if the composite has no 3rd and no 7th the reading is genuinely ambiguous.
>
> **Q41.** **The name exposes a relationship rather than stating a fact — pick the one that predicts where you go**
> Not the spelling with the fewest accidentals and not the one a chord chart would print: the one that reflects the sound you hear and the voice leading as it resolves. C-E-G-A is C6 or Am7, and each name implies a different resolution.
>
> **Q42.** **Chord = pitch content · voicing = order, spacing and register · shape = one fingering that realises it**
> The choice is harmonic because register, spacing and doubling change what the ear hears the chord doing — the same pitch classes arranged differently can stop functioning the same way.
>
> **Q43.** **3rd and 7th, then the extension you want, then the root, then the 5th**
> **Shell** is root + 3rd + 7th; **rootless** is 3rd + 7th + extensions. The 5th goes first unless it is altered, and the root goes because the bass has it. Drop 2 moves the 2nd-highest note of a close voicing down an octave; drop 3 the 3rd-highest.
>
> **Q44.** **1-4-7 off a scale degree — a stack of 3rds names a quality, a stack of 4ths commits to none**
> Nothing in a quartal voicing wants to resolve anywhere, which is the point rather than a side effect. One grip reads as minor 11, sus4, 6/9 or major 13 depending only on which note the ear takes as the root.
>
> **Q45.** **Move one note an octave; if two notes land on one string, move the colliding one to its nearest octave**
> Cascade that fix until every note has its own string, then repeat from the shape you land on. Discard the unplayable results. It is the mechanism behind close and open position, not just a name for them.
>
> **Q46.** **Twelve — four adjacent 3-string groups, three inversions each; you pick by proximity, not root position**
> E-A-D, A-D-G, D-G-B, G-B-E. The two groups crossing the G–B pair reuse the same shapes with every note above that pair a fret higher — D-G-B the top note, G-B-E the top two — to compensate for the B string's tuning. Less hand travel, higher playable tempo, less generic.
>
> **Q47.** **One shape re-pointed off a different chord tone — m7♭5 off a dominant's ♭7 reads as that dominant, altered**
> The fingering never changes, only which note you treat as the root. The same family played off the 3rd of a dominant instead reads as a dominant 9.
>
> **Q48.** **Hold the common tone where it is and move every other note to the nearest note of the next chord**
> The exception is the lowest voice — the bass or a piano left hand — which stays on each chord's actual root, because that is what the ear reads the chord's identity from.
>
> **Q49.** **A close triad with its middle note up an octave (1-5-3) — chain by thirds, moving one voice per change**
> Moving exactly one voice by a half or whole step always lands you on the closest voicing of the next chord, so each quality comes back in a different inversion and the key gets covered as a side effect.
>
> **Q50.** **The rhythm — drill it alone on one static shell voicing first; the failure is playing too much**
> Metronome on 2 and 4, until it is automatic, and only then through real changes with real voicings. Chords on 2 and 4, or one per bar, nearly always sit better than constant strumming.
>
> **Q51.** **Repetition is what reads as intentional — hold one motif for a full pass, then depart from it and return**
> Reinventing the rhythm bar to bar reads as scattered even when every individual choice is right. The return only lands because the motif was established as the baseline first.
>
> **Q52.** **Only when the soloist or singer has actually stopped — and the first question is where the fill leads**
> A fill layered under an active melody is clutter, not a fill. Its job is to connect forward to the next bar or section rather than decorate the chord it starts on.
>
> **Q53.** **Assign roles out loud · comp in the other's holes · move opposite the soloist — or lay out**
> Not playing is a legitimate choice. The unrehearsed default is that piano is primary and the guitarist behaves like a horn player until cued.
>
> **Q54.** **Closed triads, no doublings, no six-string chords — separated by string group, position and tone**
> One triad vocabulary relocated, not different material per part: a bed part low and arpeggiated, a backbeat part on the top three strings, a double-stop line above that.
>
> **Q55.** **The form, before a single chord — then reduce the chords to recurring named devices**
> AABA or ABAC tells you how much of the chart is actually new. "A secondary ii-V into the IV chord" is one device, not four decisions. The printed harmonisation is a starting point, so substitute where a chord clashes with the melody.
>
> **Q56.** **As a scale degree of the chord sounding now, not of the key — index voicings by chord tone and string**
> The same pitch — G — is "5 of C" under a C chord and "4 of D" a beat later under Dm. And do not harmonise every note: single melody notes between chord grabs sound better and read as phrasing.
>
> **Q57.** **The 3rd and the ♭7 — and the identical shape moves down a half step for the IV, up a half step for the V**
> Those two notes alone fix the quality, and with a bass player on the root that is all the guitar owes the chord. Everything larger is this pair with colour added on top.
>
> **Q58.** The letters are fixed first — one per degree — and accidentals applied after
> A heptatonic scale uses each letter exactly once, in order, so E♭ Lydian's letters are E F G A B C D before any accidental is decided. Then the formula (major with ♯4) says which get one: E♭ F G A B♭ C D. Without the letter rule you might write G𝄪 (G double-sharp) for the ♯4 — the same pitch as A, but it repeats the letter G and skips A altogether, and the scale stops being spellable as a scale.
> *Rubric (5):* letters fixed first, one per degree (2) · accidentals applied after (1) · concrete right/wrong spelling (2)
>
> **Q59.** V–I resolves because the tritone inside V resolves
> The tritone (augmented 4th / diminished 5th) splits the octave evenly and is maximally unstable. In a dominant 7th it sits between the 3rd and the 7th — B and F in G7. Each is a semitone from a tonic chord tone, and they resolve by step: B up to C, F down to E. The interval's instability *is* the cadence's engine.
> *Rubric (5):* names the interval and its instability (2) · locates it as 3rd-and-7th of the dominant (2) · describes the stepwise resolution (1)
>
> **Q60.** **Because natural minor has no functioning dominant.**
> In A natural minor the chord on the 5th degree is Em — minor, with G natural and no leading tone — so V–i has no tritone and doesn't resolve. Raising the 7th to G♯ turns Em into E7, restores the tritone (G♯ and D), and gives the key a real authentic cadence. Harmonic minor is a *harmonic* fix that happens to be described as a scale.
> *Rubric (5):* identifies the v-is-minor problem (2) · names the leading tone / tritone restoration (2) · concludes it's a harmonic requirement (1)
>
> **Q61.** **A♭ is ♭VI and Fm is iv**, both borrowed from **C minor**, the parallel minor.
> The mechanism is **modal interchange** — the tonic stays C while chords are taken from a parallel mode. G is diatonic and unchanged.
> *Rubric (5):* identifies both chords with numerals (2) · names the parallel minor as the source (2) · names modal interchange (1)
>
> **Q62.** C up to A = major 6th · A up to the next C = minor 3rd
> 6 + 3 = 9 and the quality flips major↔minor, so they're inversions of each other. Note that *direction* is not what inverts an interval — A played *down* to the C below it is still a major 6th, because the bottom note is whichever pitch is lower, not whichever is played first (see [[FND-C06 — Interval quality names]]). The inversion needs the lower note moved up an octave, which is what makes the second leap a 3rd.
>
> **Q63.** **D Dorian**
> — the natural 6th (B) is the bright, hopeful colour, the standard modal-jazz and neo-soul choice. **D natural minor (Aeolian)** — the ♭6 (B♭) is darker and more conventional. **D minor pentatonic** is a third option: removes the 2nd and ♭6 so nothing can clash, at the cost of colour. The choice is really about whether you sound the 6th, because that's the only degree that differs.
> *Rubric (5):* two valid scales named (2) · the distinguishing degree identified (2) · a characterisation of each colour (1)

> **Q64.** **Tonicization.**
> D7 is V/V pointing at G, and there's a V–I into G — but it lasts four bars and then leaves without a confirming cadence *in* G or any sustained establishment of G as home. Four bars is borderline, which is the honest answer: the distinction is a spectrum set by duration and confirmation, not a binary. If the section stayed in G and cadenced there again, it would be a modulation.
> *Rubric (5):* answers tonicization (1) · identifies D7 as V/V (2) · argues from duration/confirmation and acknowledges the spectrum (2)

> **Q65.** **The stacking rule fixes the letters — C E G every time — and the accidentals then set the quality**
> Stacking thirds from C skips a letter each time, so all four triads are spelled on C, E and G whatever they sound like. Course 01's arithmetic then supplies the quality: M3 then m3 gives C E G, m3 then M3 gives C E♭ G, m3 then m3 gives C E♭ G♭, M3 then M3 gives C E G♯. Nothing is memorised — the letters come from the chord rule and the accidentals from the interval rule, which is also why an augmented triad is C E G♯ and never C E A♭.
> *Rubric (5):* the stacking rule fixes the letters and they don't change across the four qualities (2) · the accidentals supply M3/m3 and therefore the quality (2) · at least three of the four spelled correctly (1)
>
> **Q66.** **The bass keeps the roots, so the function is never in doubt; only the upper voices move minimally**
> C as E-G-C, then Am holds C and E and moves G to A (E-A-C), then F holds A and C and moves E to F (F-A-C), then G has no common tone so each voice steps to the nearest: F-A-C becomes G-B-D. Function is a property of the chord and its root, not of which inversion the upper voices happen to land in — and the bass, the one voice the common-tone rule exempts, is exactly the voice that carries the root. So T–T–SD–D is unchanged while the top three voices barely move.
> *Rubric (5):* a worked voice leading with common tones actually held (2) · the bass exempted and left on the roots (1) · argues function comes from the chord and its root rather than the inversion (2)
>
> **Q67.** **Rootless Am7 is a C triad and rootless Dm7 is an F triad — the loop is now I–I–IV–V, not I–vi–ii–V**
> Am7 is A C E G, so dropping the root leaves C E G, a plain C major triad. Dm7 is D F A C, so dropping the root leaves F A C, a plain F. The ii survives the treatment because IV and ii are both subdominant — the function is intact and only the colour changed. The vi does not: with no bass player to supply the A, the tonic substitute collapses into the literal tonic and the loop loses a chord change. That is the condition hiding inside the drop-the-root rule — the root goes *because the bass has it*, and here nobody does.
> *Rubric (5):* identifies both rootless chords as C and F triads (2) · gives the new numerals (1) · distinguishes the ii→IV swap keeping subdominant function from the vi→I collapse (2)
