# Songs that use the 4-3-2-1 Chord Progression

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vaavaj9DOGY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vaavaj9DOGY)

## Synopsis

The companion piece to Bennett's "1-2-3-4" video (already documented on [[Chord Progressions]]): the mirror-image descending walk down the harmonized major scale, IV-iii-ii-I, almost always dressed in 7th-chord extensions for a lush, jazzy color. Traces the same shape through soul, video-game, Beatles, and George Harrison catalogs, plus a common voicing variant that swaps the iii chord for a first-inversion I.

## Techniques

- **The progression: IV–iii–ii–I**, literally reading down the harmonized major scale one step at a time — the descending mirror of the "1-2-3-4 climb" progression documented on [[Chord Progressions]].
- **Almost always voiced with 7ths** for a smooth, jazzy quality: "Loving You" (Minnie Riperton, key of A) puts the major 7th right in the melody, foregrounding the maj7/min7 color. "If I Ain't Got You" (Alicia Keys, key of G, both intro and chorus) uses the identical maj7/min7 dressing — Bennett suspects direct inspiration from Riperton's song.
- **A reusable voicing trick, named explicitly**: Keys voices the IVmaj7 chord as a minor triad in her right hand (e.g. E minor) with the root's own IV bass note underneath (C) — an Em chord over a C bass reads as Cmaj7, giving a "lush slash chord" sound. Applicable anywhere a maj7 chord is wanted with an easy triad-over-bass shape.
- **Dominant pedal variant**: "Blinded by the Light" (Manfred Mann's Earth Band cover, key of F) plays the same B♭-Am-Gm-F progression in the chorus, but in the verse holds F's dominant (C, the 5th degree) constant in the bass under all four chords — B♭/C, Am/C, Gm/C, F/C — creating tension via a **dominant pedal point**, then resolving when the chorus drops the pedal. A clean example of reusing one chord progression twice in a song but changing its character with a bass treatment alone. (Side note: Bruce Springsteen's original "Blinded by the Light" does NOT use this progression — Manfred Mann's cover re-harmonized the melody onto different chords entirely.)
- **Turnaround variant**: "Zelda's Lullaby" (Koji Kondo, *The Legend of Zelda*, key of C) plays Fmaj7-Em7-Dm7-Cmaj7 straight the first time, then on the repeat detours F-Em-E♭-Dm7-G — landing on the dominant (G) as a turnaround to set up the next section, rather than resolving back to I.
- **Adding secondary dominants on a repeat**: "Oh My Love" (John Lennon) plays the progression plain the first time through, then on the second pass inserts secondary dominants — an F7 resolving to Bm (the "five of two"), and a similar V-of-I insertion later — to add harmonic interest to a repeated section without changing the underlying shape. → [[Secondary Dominants]].
- **iii-for-I-first-inversion swap**: a common variant replaces the iii chord with the I chord in first inversion (root moved to the bass's 3rd) — in C: F–C/E–Dm–C instead of F-Em-Dm-C. "Life's Been Good" (Joe Walsh, verse) and "Let It Be" (The Beatles, end of verse/chorus, key of C) both use this exact F-C/E-Dm-C shape; McCartney reuses it later, stretched out, before the guitar solo, then transposes the whole shape down a 5th (B♭-Am-G-F-C) for an elongated variation.
- **George Harrison's usage**: "Long, Long, Long" (*The White Album*) uses the straight 4-3-2-1 shape but inserts a quick V chord (C) just before returning to I — the same secondary-dominant-insertion device John Lennon used in "Oh My Love." "Awaiting on You All" (*All Things Must Pass*) descends the progression quickly, then climbs back up as a turnaround between repetitions — a fast-descent/turnaround-climb pairing.
- **Songs that use BOTH the 1-2-3-4 climb and the 4-3-2-1 descent** (ties directly to the companion video): "Lean on Me" (Bill Withers, key of C) climbs C-Dm-Em-F, hovers on IV, then climbs back DOWN F-Em-Dm-C — both shapes in one phrase. "Like a Rolling Stone" (Bob Dylan) similarly climbs up and later climbs back down within the same song, generally "exploring" the C major scale up and down rather than committing to one direction.
