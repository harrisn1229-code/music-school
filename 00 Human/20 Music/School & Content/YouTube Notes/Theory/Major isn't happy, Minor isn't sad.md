# Major isn't happy, Minor isn't sad

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OMWszWYX2OQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OMWszWYX2OQ)

## Synopsis

A correction to the "major = happy, minor = sad" shorthand — worked examples of happy-sounding minor songs and sad-sounding major songs, arguing "brightness" (not "happiness") is the accurate descriptive term for the major/minor contrast, with tempo and rhythmic feel identified as the actual bigger driver of perceived mood.

## Techniques

- **Happy minor-key songs, disproving the "minor = sad" rule**: "Move On Up" (Curtis Mayfield) is built entirely from three minor 7th chords (Em7–Fm7–Bm7) yet reads as upbeat, not sad. The Tetris theme (also minor) reads as upbeat/positive rather than sad, largely credited to its brisk staccato tempo/rhythm rather than its tonality.
- **Sad major-key songs, disproving the "major = happy" rule**: "Everybody Hurts" (R.E.M., largely D major) and "When Somebody Loved Me" (Toy Story 2, F major) both read as genuinely sad despite major tonality — described as a distinct *tender, heartbreaking* sadness (warmth + brightness) rather than the more *mournful, hopeless* sadness typical of minor-key sad music (cited comparison: Moonlight Sonata, Radiohead's "Street Spirit").
- **The correct term is "brightness," not "happiness."** Major is relatively brighter, minor relatively darker — a purely tonal-color description with no built-in emotional claim, which frees up four independent combinations: bright-happy, bright-sad, dark-happy, dark-sad.
- **Tempo/rhythmic feel as the dominant mood axis, demonstrated directly**: the same notes, same tonality, played fast + staccato reads as upbeat/positive; played slower + legato the identical material reads as reflective/mournful — a controlled demonstration that mood is often driven more by rhythm/tempo than by major-vs-minor tonality itself.
- **Parallel-key modulation as the clearest A/B brightness comparison a song can offer**: "I'm Still Standing" (Elton John) shifts from B♭ minor (verse) to the parallel B♭ major (chorus) — hearing the same tonic directly contrasted in both modes back-to-back is the cleanest way to hear "brightness" as a relative, not absolute, quality.
