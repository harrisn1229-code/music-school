# JAZ-C12 — A turnaround is a controlled reset

**Q.** Give the base turnaround, say what job it does, and give the writing habit that goes with it.

> [!question]- Answer
> **I – vi – ii – V** — in C, `Cmaj7 Am7 Dm7 G7`. Its only job is to end a section by pointing back at the top: the root falls a third, then rises a fourth twice, and it ends on V, which wants the I of the next chorus.
>
> The habit: **write the four-chord version first, then compress.** Two bars (`| Cmaj7 A7 | Dm7 G7 |`), one bar (`| Dm7 G7 |`, the commonest ending in the repertoire), or V alone on beats 3–4. Compressing a good turnaround gives a good short one; expanding a bare `Dm7 G7` gives you nothing to work with.

## Why it matters

Loop-based music is *all* turnaround — a four-bar loop that repeats is a turnaround by definition, and getting the last bar right is what makes a loop feel circular rather than merely repeated.

Three moves that work directly in production: end on **V7 or ♭II7** for the classic pull; end on **the ii**, softer and very common in neo-soul, leaving the V implied and the loop never fully closed; or **change only the last bar between repeats** — two loops of material for one bar of writing, which is the cheapest arrangement variation available.

Every named variation — VI7, III7–VI7, tritone subs, ♭III7, backdoor, minor — is Lesson 3's substitution rules applied to this one four-chord skeleton, so they can be generated rather than memorised.

## Drill

Write the four-chord turnaround, then compress it to two bars, one bar and half a bar. Then take a loop of yours that ends on I and re-end it on V, on ii, and on ♭II7.
