> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **Showcase lines work over static held chords · busy harmony above means roots only**
> The bass gets the floor when nothing else is using it. When the chords are moving, they're already supplying the interest.
>
> **Q2.** **It supports — it doesn't compete**
> A hook and a showcase bassline are two things asking for the same attention, and the hook wins by default.
>
> **Q3.** **The riffs *are* the line**
> Static harmony moves all the interest into the fills between roots, and one perfectly placed fill can be the entire event.
>
> **Q4.** **A question about what else is moving, not about taste**
> *Rubric (5):* says busyness is decided by how much the harmony and the top line are already doing (2) · says most overwritten basslines were written against harmony that was already carrying the section (2) · notes that reframing it makes the decision checkable rather than a matter of preference (1)
> Treating it as taste means every bassline is an argument you can't settle. Treating it as a question about the rest of the arrangement makes it answerable in ten seconds: mute the bass, listen to what's left, and count what's moving. If the chords change twice a bar and there's a vocal on top, the answer is roots and the discussion is over. If the harmony is two chords held for eight bars each and nothing is singing, the bass is the only voice in the room and it should behave like one. The reason this matters more than it sounds is that overwritten basslines are almost never overwritten in isolation — they're written against a busy arrangement by someone listening to the bass in solo.
>
> **Q5.** **What story is the bass telling · what story are the keys telling · do they talk to each other**
> Three instruments arranged as a call and response rather than as stacked parts.
>
> **Q6.** **Keep the harmony dead simple — the arrangement carries it**
> Two chords is enough. What makes the section is the exchange between the parts, not the number of chords they're exchanging over.
>
> **Q7.** **The bass patch plays its own upper voice a 3rd or 5th above the root, in contrary motion**
> That's genuine two-voice writing rather than a melodic line with a thicker patch — the two voices move against each other rather than in parallel.
>
> **Q8.** **Switch to a 16th grid and move some notes off the kick's strong beats onto the weak spots between**
> The strong beats are 1, 2, 3 and 4; the funk is in what happens between them.
>
> **Q9.** **Octave uppers and downers for bounce · a chromatic walk-up or walk-down between phrases**
> The walk steps through out-of-scale passing tones toward the next section's starting note, which is why it works independently of whatever scale the line is in.
>
> **Q10.** **New rhythm, register and contour per section — every line returns to the same root as home**
> Rising section, peak, B section, wind-down: genuinely different lines. The shared root is what keeps four of them reading as one song rather than as four sketches.
>
> **Q11.** **Rhythmic, harmonic and melodic — syncopation loosens the rhythmic one**
> *Rubric (5):* names all three jobs — locking with the kick, playing the root, carrying hooks and fills (2) · says syncopation deliberately loosens the rhythmic lock (1) · says the lock is loosened rather than abandoned, because the line still has to return to the kick's strong beats often enough to hold the groove together (2)
> The three jobs run at the same time and they pull against each other, which is why bass writing feels harder than it looks. Rhythmically the line is locking with the kick; harmonically it's stating the root so the chord above has a floor; melodically it's carrying whatever hooks and fills the arrangement gave it. Syncopation is a deliberate loosening of the first job — moving notes off the strong beats and onto the 16ths in between — and it works precisely because the other two are still holding. The root is still under the chord, so nothing gets harmonically confusing, and the kick is still marking the strong beats, so there's a grid for the syncopation to be heard against. Loosen it too far and you lose the reference: the notes stop reading as displaced and start reading as unrelated. The check is whether the line still lands with the kick often enough that the offbeats sound like a choice.
