## Synopsis

Another octave-triangle walkthrough (same core drill as [[The 5 MOST Important Music Theory TRICKS! (Finally Sound Like A PRO)]]), but this one pushes the idea further into a genuinely new application: using the three-octave E-D-high-E triangle as a compositional device for wide-interval, "piano-like" licks, plus the practical muting technique needed to execute the required string-skipping cleanly.

## What's taught

- **Octave triangle, restated (see [[Notes & Pitches]] for the already-logged core drill).** Learn the low E string's natural notes, connect to the D string and high E string via the octave shape (which visually forms a triangle across the three strings), fill sharps/flats after naturals are solid.
- **5th-fret shortcut for learning an adjacent string, restated (see [[You're Probably Visualizing The Fretboard WRONG!]]'s parallel-layout entry).** The note at the 5th fret of a lower string matches the next string's open note (standard tuning), so a new string's notes can be thought of as "the previous string starting from its 5th fret" rather than memorized fresh.
- **New: composing wide-interval "piano-like" licks from the octave triangle, across 3 octaves.** Instead of using the octave triangle only to double-check an unsure note, build actual lines from it: take one small scale fragment/fingering and repeat it in each of the three octave-triangle positions (low E, D, high E strings) rather than staying in one neck position — worked over the A minor scale, and again as a fast lick built from just two small repeated fingerings (one hammer-on-based, one pull-off-based) moved through three octaves. The intended effect is a "piano player moving a phrase between both hands" quality — wide register jumps that don't sound like a guitarist sliding up the neck.
- **Muting/damping for clean octave-triangle string-skipping.** The named risk: string-skipping across the triangle's wide jumps leaves the skipped strings ringing (open-string noise) or produces an accidental unintended pull-off sound when landing on the next octave. Fix: mute the strings not currently in use (a string dampener like a GruvGear FretWrap is named as his personal tool, but explicitly frames the exercise as something that should also be practiced clean-handed without any gadget) and land each octave jump precisely on the intended string, not an adjacent one. Accuracy, not speed, is called out as what actually makes these licks sound clean.
