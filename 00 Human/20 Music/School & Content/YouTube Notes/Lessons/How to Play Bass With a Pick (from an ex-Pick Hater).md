# How to Play Bass With a Pick (from an ex-Pick Hater)

## Synopsis

BassBuzz's Josh frames this as his own "ex-pick-hater" conversion story — for years he dismissed pick playing as a fingerstyle crutch and disliked its clicky tone, until studying famous pick players (Paul McCartney, Carol Kaye, Bobby Vega, Jason Newsted, David Ellefson, and a long roll call of others) forced him to confront that the "real bassists use fingers" myth doesn't survive contact with who's actually played bass with a pick. The video is a complete, practical on-ramp to pick playing rather than just a tone argument: it opens by naming and debunking the myth (three social causes, none of them technical), explains the actual acoustic mechanism behind why picks sound different (transients, brightness) with genre-spanning proof that pick tone isn't limited to rock/metal, walks through the three physical pick variables (size, thickness, material) as a troubleshooting menu, gives a five-point checklist for how to physically hold a pick, reverse-engineers five components of picking-attack mechanics by comparing how three famous players actually move (body-part source, strap length, string placement, attack direction, angle), and closes with the three basic picking-stroke patterns (down, alternate, economy) each with a concrete practice drill. Despite being a full pro-pick video, it explicitly still recommends beginners build fingerstyle first — the pick is framed as an additional tool to add on top of a fingerstyle foundation, not a replacement for building one.

## What's taught

**The myth, and why it doesn't hold up.** "Real bassists use fingers, not picks" is stated up front as a persistent, repeated claim in bass culture, then immediately undercut by a long list of bass legends who play/played with a pick at least some of the time: Paul McCartney, Carol Kaye, Anthony Jackson, Bobby Vega, Sting, Peter Hook, Chris Squire, Steve Swallow, John Deacon, Noel Redding, Jason Newsted, David Ellefson, Matt Freeman, Justin Chancellor, Tim Lefebvre. Three likely social (not technical) causes are named for why the myth persists anyway: (1) fingerstyle as an in-group signal distinguishing "real" bassists from guitarists who default to a pick when they pick up a bass; (2) picks being genuinely easier on day one (though both are equally hard to master past that point), which breeds snobbery from players who started on fingers; (3) players adopting "I am a pick player"/"I am a fingerstyle player" as a fixed identity rather than treating technique as situational — "I'm a bass player who uses whatever technique the song needs."

**Why a pick actually sounds different — the mechanism.** A pick gives a generally brighter tone than fingers, and bigger **transients** — the volume spike at the very start of a note. A finger-plucked note's waveform shows a fairly mellow drop-off after the initial pluck; the same note picked shows a sharper, bigger spike right on the attack that then drops more dramatically. That sharper transient reads as percussive "snap," which is specifically why a pick helps a bass line cut through a mix of drums and guitars rather than get buried in the low end.

**Pick tone isn't limited to rock/metal.** Genre-spanning proof, named directly: Paul McCartney got plenty of great **non-aggressive** tones with a pick; Tim Lefebvre's tone on "Lazarus" (David Bowie's *Blackstar* album) is dark and clicky; Steve Swallow gets an **upright-bassy** tone in a jazz setting specifically using a **copper pick**. Strings, pickup, EQ, and palm muting all shift pick tone further, the same way they'd shift fingerstyle tone.

**The speed argument is weaker than it sounds.** Picks can make fast playing easier, but most players don't actually need that level of speed — plenty of very fast players (Alex Webster, Adrian Farrow) play entirely fingerstyle.

**Recommended sequencing: fingerstyle first, even in a pro-pick video.** Starting on a pick can undermine a beginner's commitment to learning alternating index/middle plucking, and long-time pick-only players often struggle badly picking up fingerstyle later — stated as the reason BassBuzz's own Beginner to Badass course puts strong emphasis on alternating plucking before introducing other techniques.

**Pick anatomy — three variables, and a troubleshooting map:**
- **Size:** standard (same as most guitar picks) is the default; tiny jazz picks are more of a guitar-player thing; big triangle picks give more surface to hold, useful for a beginner who keeps dropping the pick.
- **Thickness/gauge:** thin picks flex more, glide off the string more easily (harder to get stuck), and add a clicky sound from the pick bending — good for beginners. Thick picks are less flexible, respond to the string differently, and give a darker, bassier tone — the presenter's own preference for most playing.
- **Material:** Tortex, nylon, felt, wood, metal, even a credit card — each hits the string differently and changes tone; no prescribed "correct" material, buy a variety pack and compare.
- **Troubleshooting:** dropping the pick a lot → try a big triangle. Getting stuck on the string → try a thinner pick. Don't like the tone → try a different material.

**How to hold a pick.** Between thumb and index finger — all correct grips are variations on this. Thumb out in front, pressed into the side of the index finger at roughly a 45° angle (or tilted further down so the index tip runs parallel to the thumb); slide the pick in tip-first, pointing away from the pick hand. **Five-point checklist:** (1) thumb below the center of the pick, only a little sticking out; (2) the tip of the thumb goes past the pick; (3) the *side* of the index finger presses the pick, not the pad; (4) wrist straight or slightly bent forward; (5) grip firm enough not to drop it, loose enough not to over-grip.

**Five components of picking-attack mechanics, reverse-engineered from famous pickers:**
1. **Body-part source of motion:** McCartney — mostly wrist, side-to-side. Bobby Vega — elbow movement + forearm rotation, a distinctly different style. Jason Newsted — a mix of wrist, elbow, and whole-arm-at-the-shoulder movement. Conclusion: there's no single correct source — shift between shoulder/elbow/forearm/wrist/fingers (or blend them) depending on the part, and to avoid fatigue.
2. **Strap length:** Carol Kaye plays with the bass above the waist, David Ellefson (Megadeth) mid-waist, Kim Gordon (Sonic Youth) just below the waist — three working pros, three heights, so strap length doesn't meaningfully constrain pick technique.
3. **Where along the string to pick:** closer to the neck = rounder, more tubular. Closer to the bridge = sharper, scrapier. Middle is the general default unless chasing a specific tone.
4. **Attack direction:** straight across-and-back, good for fast chains of same-direction notes; or strike down into the strings and back up on the upstroke, more natural when the motion comes from forearm rotation and better suited to funkier lines.
5. **Pick angle/slice:** dead-on (flat against the string) is the default. More angle adds pick-scraping noise — a deliberate choice for a nastier, grittier sound, not a flaw.

**Three picking-stroke patterns, each with a drill:**
- **Down picking** — down strokes only. Simplest to start (no per-note decision), gives consistent driving attack. Watch-out: when crossing strings, move the *whole arm* rather than twisting the hand's angle to reach the new string.
- **Alternate picking** — down + up strokes, same speed logic as two-finger alternate plucking beating one-finger. Trade-off: a natural uneven lilt (downstrokes land stronger than upstrokes). Drill: run any known scale down-up per note — the video demos a G minor scale this way.
- **Economy picking** — identical to alternate picking on one string, but chains same-direction strokes across a string crossing whenever the pick is already positioned to continue that way (e.g. ending a downstroke on the E string flows straight into a downstroke on the A string, rather than flipping to up just to alternate) — the pick equivalent of fingerstyle raking. Drill (3 notes per string): ascending, each string starts on a down (`down-up-down` per string, E→A→D→G); descending, each string instead starts on an up (`up-down-up` per string). Flagged as genuinely hard even for an experienced pick player — the payoff of the simple repeating pattern is building down/up-continuity coordination in isolation before applying it to real, less-regular lines.
