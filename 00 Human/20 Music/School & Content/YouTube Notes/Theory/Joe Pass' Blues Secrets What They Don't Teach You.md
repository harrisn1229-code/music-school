## Synopsis

Nathan Borton distills a specific stretch of Joe Pass's own *Blue Side of Jazz* masterclass (explicitly credited and linked in the description) into a compact, spot-by-spot guide to reharmonizing a plain 1-4-5 twelve-bar blues, comping-first with the equivalent single-note-line application shown for every device. He opens by playing a full etude — chords in chorus one, the same chord motions translated into single-note lines in chorus two — that packs in everything the lesson covers, then works backward through the 12-bar form (in G: G7 to C7 and back to G7, i.e. the vault's already-documented **quick-change** blues skeleton) explaining each embellishment at the bar-position where it lives: motion into the IV chord, the IV-back-to-I connector, the I-to-IV approach treated as a miniature key change, the I-to-V stretch dressed up with borrowed dominants, and a reharmonized turnaround closing out the final four bars. The connective thread running through nearly every device is **common-tone voicing** — holding one note (usually the top voice) across a chord change so the ear tracks a held pitch through a moving harmony, the same voice-leading principle already documented on [[Chord Voicings]] from Joe Pass's own *Guitar Chords* book (*"sustain lead notes from one chord to the next"*). A second recurring option offered at almost every spot is a **half-step (chromatic) substitute** for whatever V–I or ii–V motion was just shown — approach the target chord from a half step away instead of via a functional dominant — which the lesson treats as an interchangeable alternative color, not a replacement device. Every harmonic idea is given both a comping voicing and a soloing application (arpeggio outline or "jazz language" over the same motion), consistent with the source material's stated comping-then-lines structure. This is a narration-only teaching video reconstructed from Joe Pass's visual/audio masterclass — Borton names chord qualities and functions clearly and consistently, but exact voicing shapes and precise chord spellings aren't independently visible in the transcript (no on-screen chart, audio-only captions), so specifics below are captured as clearly as the narration supports and flagged where genuinely ambiguous. ## What's taught

- **Baseline progression — the video's working example, in G:** `G7 (I) → C7 (IV) → G7 (I)` across the first four bars, i.e. the **quick-change** blues variant already documented on [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *The variants worth knowing* (bar 2 moves to IV instead of staying on I) — stated as the unmodified skeleton every device below decorates, exactly the same "state the plain form, then reharmonize it" structure as [[Reharmonizing The Blues - 3 Levels]] already on the vault. → [[Blues Form and Harmony]]

- **Device 1 — dominant-chain approach into the IV chord (first four bars):** instead of moving straight `G7 → C7`, Joe adds "a quick five to one before the C7" — described as adding motion *ahead of* the existing G7-to-C7 resolution rather than replacing it, which reads as chaining another dominant a 4th above G7 (i.e. **D7**) in front, giving **D7 → G7 → C7** — three dominants walking down in fourths into the IV chord, the same "chain V7s a fourth apart" logic as [[The ii-V-I]] § *Chains* and [[Secondary Dominants]]. *(Exact voicing not visible in the transcript — captured as the harmonic motion described: extra dominant motion added ahead of an existing V→I relationship, "creates a great sense of motion over these static dominant chords.")* The same device is stated to repeat **moving back out of C7 to G7** — i.e. the mirror version, **A7 → D7 → G7**, approaching the return to I the same chained-dominant way. **Soloing application:** outline these 5-to-1 motions with arpeggios, or with "jazz language" (bebop-style enclosures/passing tones) over the same chord shapes. → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Secondary Dominants]]

- **Device 2 — common-tone connector from IV back to I (bars 5–6), the lesson's most concretely-spelled device:** **F♯13♭9 → G7**, explained directly as a **common-tone harmonization** — the top note of F♯13♭9 is **G**, and that same G is retained as the top note of the following **G13** voicing, so the ear tracks one held pitch while the chord underneath resolves a half step up. **Stated to generalize:** this works *any time* the IV7 chord moves back to the I7 chord in a blues, and is explicitly said to also work over the **first two measures** (i.e. the same connector reused at the quick-change spot in bars 1–2, not just bars 5–6). **Soloing application:** outline the motion with arpeggios/chord shapes while focusing on the common tone G between the two chords; over the F♯13♭9 itself, just play the guitar voicing as an arpeggio (voicing-as-arpeggio, a direct, low-overhead soloing shortcut). This is the same top-note-sustain principle already on [[Chord Voicings]] § *Voice leading is the actual point* ("the top note is a melody... sustain lead notes from one chord to the next"), now with a concrete IV→I blues example attached to it. → [[Chord Voicings]], [[Guide Tones]]

- **Device 3 — the half-step (chromatic) substitute, offered as a standing alternative to every V–I/ii–V shown above:** "we can always move a half step into other chords" — replace any of the 5-to-1 motions already covered with a chromatic approach from a half step away instead of a functional dominant resolution. Framed as a rule to carry through the whole form (*"keep this half-step rule in mind as we move through the blues form"*), applicable both to comping voicings and to single-note lines. This is the same half-step-entry idea already generalized on [[Chord Voicings]] § *Voice leading is the actual point* (*"approach chromatically... slide the whole shape in"*) and is functionally adjacent to (though not identical to) the tritone-substitution family on [[Chord Substitution]] § 2 — a chromatic-neighbor approach chord rather than a tritone-related dominant substitute. → [[Chord Voicings]], [[Chord Substitution]]

- **Device 4 — treating the IV chord as a temporary new key center (I→IV approach, bars 4→5):** rather than moving straight into C7, two options are given: **(a)** approach it by half step (Device 3, reapplied here specifically), or **(b)** treat C7 as its own momentary tonic and play a full **ii–V into it**: **Dm7 → G7 → C7** (Dm7 is C7's own ii, matching [[The ii-V-I]] § *The major version* exactly — same shape, same "landing chord becomes a candidate for a ii–V dropped in front of it" logic already documented on that page's `## From videos` from [[Reharmonizing The Blues - 3 Levels]]). **A further chromatic layer on top of (b):** insert **D♭7** between the two, giving **Dm7 → D♭7 → C7** — the ii chord followed by a half-step-below chromatic approach dominant in place of the "real" G7, i.e. Device 3's half-step-substitute rule applied specifically to the V slot of this ii–V. **Soloing application:** arpeggios over the ii–V shape (as in Device 1), or jazz language outlining the 2–5 moving into the IV chord. → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Chord Substitution]] § 4 *Interpolating a ii in front of any dominant*

- **Device 5 — IV back to I again (bars 6→7 return), two interchangeable connector options:** **(a)** reuse Device 2's common-tone voicing — F♯13♭9 → G7 — right before the return to I, or **(b)** substitute a **C♯° (C-sharp diminished) → G7** move instead — a diminished passing chord a half step below the target resolving into the dominant, the same family of device as [[Diminished Chords]]'s dim7-as-chromatic-connector material and [[Chord Substitution]]'s half-step-substitute framing (Device 3) applied with a diminished sonority specifically. Stated plainly that "either way will work" — presented as two equally valid connector choices for the same structural spot, not a preferred/fallback pair. → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Chord Voicings]]

- **Device 6 — dressing up the I-to-V stretch (moving toward bar 9) with borrowed dominant chords, two variants:** **(a)** add the **iii and vi chords of the key, both made dominant** rather than left diatonic — in G, that's **B7 (iii7) and E7 (vi7)** — described directly as fitting "the blues aesthetic" precisely because making normally-minor diatonic chords into dominants matches [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *Why every chord is a dominant 7th*'s point that the dominant 7th is blues's home sound, not a tension needing resolution. The move into B7 (iii) uses the half-step motion from Device 3; the following **E7 (vi) is explicitly named as functioning as the V7-of-the-ii** ("acts as the 5 [chord] of the two in our key"), i.e. a secondary dominant resolving toward Am7 (ii), **replacing the spot where the plain V would normally sit**. This whole shape — iii7 → vi7 heading toward ii — matches [[Turnarounds]]'s documented **III7–VI7** turnaround row (`Cmaj7 E7 A7 Dm7 G7`, here transposed to G as `B7 E7 (Am7 D7...)`) applied mid-form rather than only at the final turnaround. **(b)** the same iii7–vi7 pair, but reached with half-step motion leading into *each* chord instead of the diatonic approach in (a) — the same Device-3 substitute layered on top of Device 6's own borrowed-dominant idea. **Connecting device across both variants:** common-tone voicings link the chords together — **two separate worked common-tone-voicing progressions given for this exact stretch** (both distinct from Device 2's F♯13♭9→G7 shape; specific voicings not independently spelled out in the transcript beyond "connect them with common tones on top," captured qualitatively per the depth-standard caution against guessing ungiven numbers). **Soloing application:** two single-note-line examples outlining these chord progressions are demonstrated (again, shapes not independently verifiable from narration alone). → [[Turnarounds]] § *III7–VI7*, [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *Why every chord is a dominant 7th*, [[Chord Voicings]]

- **Device 7 — the final-four-bars turnaround, the lesson's most structurally interesting reharmonization:** at the spot where the plain form would land on **C7** (the IV-chord-adjacent bar near the end of the form), Joe instead adds **"the two chord of the key in front of the 5 [chord]"** — i.e. substitutes that C7 slot with a **ii–V into the coming V chord**, matching [[The ii-V-I]] § *Chains*' "make the I of one unit into the ii of the next" device and [[Chord Substitution]] § 4's ii-interpolation rule, here applied to displace a IV-family chord with a ii–V unit rather than adding a ii–V in front of an unchanged chord. **Then, instead of resolving straight back to the I7 chord**, the turnaround itself is upgraded to what the video names directly as a **"three dominant six-two-five"** — i.e. a **III7–VI7–ii–V** turnaround chain (in G: **B7 → E7 → Am7 → D7**) — which is *exactly* the vault's already-documented [[Turnarounds]] **III7–VI7** row (`Cmaj7 E7 A7 Dm7 G7`), extended out to its full four-link "3-6-2-5" form and confirmed here independently, by name, in a different source. **Harmonization instruction repeated one final time:** try to harmonize this whole turnaround with common-tone voicings (Device 2/6's connective principle, now applied across the full turnaround chain). **Soloing application:** single-note lines outlining the turnaround's chord tones, the same chord-tone-outlining approach used throughout the rest of the lesson. → [[Turnarounds]] § *III7–VI7*, [[The ii-V-I]] § *Chains*, [[Chord Substitution]] § 4

- **Pedagogical structure worth naming on its own:** every device in the lesson is given in pairs — a comping/voicing version *and* a single-note-line version outlining the identical chord motion — reinforcing the same "comp it, then solo the same changes" structure [[Reharmonizing The Blues - 3 Levels]] already demonstrated on the vault (chords first, then lines over the identical reharmonization). The whole lesson is itself a condensed reading of Joe Pass's own *Blue Side of Jazz* masterclass, explicitly credited as the source rather than original material — worth treating as a compact index/preview of that longer source rather than a fully independent lesson; Borton states Pass goes considerably deeper on voicing/harmonization specifics around the masterclass's own 28-minute mark, not reproduced here.
