# Your Mother Should Know — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

**Form:** Intro (two vamping measures in the relative minor) · Verse (an unusual **11 measures**, three phrases, **the middle one a measure short**) · Verse · Bridge (a single long **instrumental** phrase of 6 measures, sitting where a sung refrain would normally go) · Verse · Bridge · Verse · Outro. The second verse's final measure is stretched by a **limping half measure** as it feeds the bridge — a trick whose only Beatles precedents are Lennon's ([[ARR-C06 — Section-length conventions, and breaking them on purpose]]).

More music-hall nostalgia, and against the surface impression **less four-square than "When I'm Sixty-Four"**. Verse and bridge share one plan: **start in A minor, converge slowly and steadily on the relative major, then flip straight back to the minor at the last moment** ([[POP-C08 — Relative-key switching]]). Pollack's comparison is "And I Love Her", where the same gambit produces anxious bittersweetness; here the minor is a foil whose only job is to make the major sound cheerier.

The engine is a **run of three dominants back to back in measures 7 through 9** ([[HAR-C07 — Secondary dominants]]) spelling a descending chromatic fragment, answered by an upward chromatic push in the backing vocals. The first verse's bass walks down the scale, putting one chord into second inversion ([[POP-C04 — Four chords with a moving bass]]).

The sing-it-again tag appears **only after the first verse and the second bridge**, though it would have fit everywhere. Using it everywhere would have cost it everything.
