# How To Outline Blues Changes

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aNbT1ClvI7A)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aNbT1ClvI7A)

## Synopsis

A short, tightly-scoped Jack Ruch lesson (same channel/teacher as [[Jack Ruch - Beyond Pentatonics - Add Dominant 7 Arpeggios to Your Blues]] and [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]]'s source video) that isolates just **one small chunk of a 1-4-5 blues** — the V chord walking down to the IV chord and into the turnaround (bars 9–11 of a 12-bar form) — and drills a single short lick over that chunk, then relocates the exact same lick to four different fretboard positions. Worked over a 12-bar blues shuffle in **G** (I=G, IV=C7, V=D7). The teaching point is deliberately minimal: you don't need a full arpeggio or a new scale to make a solo "outline the changes" — landing briefly on **one chord tone per chord** (the root of the V chord, then a chromatic step up to the major 3rd of the IV chord) is enough to signal the harmony, and everything else can stay in ordinary G blues/pentatonic vocabulary. The video opens by naming an explicit practice philosophy: break a long form into small 2-chord chunks (I→IV, V→IV→turnaround, etc.) and drill each chunk in isolation rather than trying to solo over the whole 12 bars at once — the same chunk-then-connect instinct already documented on [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]] and [[Woodshedding Changes with a Looper]]. Tabs/notation for the exact lick are paywalled on the creator's Patreon (not extracted here — only the transcript-described note choices and positions are captured).

## What's taught

- **Practice philosophy stated up front: chunk the form, don't solo the whole 12 bars at once.** "When I'm practicing playing over a chord progression like a blues, I like to break things down into smaller sections" — e.g. drilling I→IV ideas separately from V→IV→turnaround ideas. This lesson works only the second chunk. *(0:00–0:30, paraphrased — the transcript doesn't carry exact timestamps.)*
- **The core lick: anchor on the root of V, then chromatic-step up to the 3rd of IV.** Over **D7 (V)**, lean heavily on the note **D on the 3rd fret of the B string** — repeating/circling that root tone is what "drives home the sound of that chord" rather than continuing generic G pentatonic. Variations offered on the same idea: play **D–F–G–A**, or a small chromatic pull-off **between A and G**. Then, moving into **C7 (IV)**, take that same D note and **walk it up chromatically to E** — E is the **major 3rd of C7** — landing on that one chord tone is enough to "hint" the new chord before dropping straight back into ordinary G minor pentatonic blues phrasing. Directly echoes [[Dominant 7 Arpeggios Over Blues Pentatonics]]'s claim that a chord's own 3rd is the strongest single target note, but here it's reached by a **chromatic half-step walk-up from the previous chord's root**, not a full arpeggio.
- **Turnaround lick: G blues, but land on D.** At the very end of the chunk (the turnaround), the phrase stays in ordinary G blues vocabulary but is aimed to **land on a D note**, outlining the **V7 (D7)** chord one more time right before the 12-bar form repeats from the top.
- **No full arpeggio needed — one hinted chord tone per chord is enough.** Stated explicitly: "we don't have to... get too deep into outlining the full arpeggio, we're just kind of using these basic chord shapes as a visual guide to see these chord tones" — the goal is a brief target-note hit, then straight back to pentatonic-blues language, not a sustained arpeggio run.
- **Relocate the identical lick to four fretboard positions, visualizing a chord shape at each stop as the landmark for where the chord tones live:**
  1. **D on the 3rd fret of the B string** (the original position the lick was introduced in).
  2. **Open D string** — same chromatic-pulloff idea, played from the open string; tonal color note: pick **down near the bridge** for this open-string version, for a different attack/brightness than the fretted positions.
  3. **D on the 5th fret of the A string** — same idea, a new part of the neck that "lends itself to stretching out" differently, with access to different surrounding notes/directions.
  4. **D on the 7th fret of the G string** — visualized specifically as **a D chord shape connected to an adjacent C bar-chord shape**, so the chord tones for both D7 and C7 are seen as two adjacent CAGED-family grips rather than found by ear alone.
  - Same relative-degree relocation instinct as [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]] (same channel/teacher) — take one short phrase, keep it musically identical, and relearn its starting point at several places on the neck — but here the "landmark" being relocated is a **single chord-tone anchor note visualized against a chord shape**, not a full 3-note triad shape.
- **Embellishment is left open-ended.** "There's a million ways you can embellish these phrases with hammer-ons and pull-offs and chromatic passing tones, little bends and slides" — framed as the player's own voice, not a fixed formula; exact fingerings/embellishments are reserved for the Patreon tab download.
- **Big-picture payoff stated directly:** tying licks together across different neck positions, so a player has "access to these different ideas all over the fretboard" and isn't locked into one spot depending on where a solo is coming from/going to — matches [[One-Position Fretboard Overview Drill]]'s and [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]]'s framing of position fluency as the actual goal, not the licks themselves.
- **Sponsor content flagged, not extracted as technique:** a closing plug for tabs/notation/exact fingerings on the creator's Patreon ($5/mo tier referenced elsewhere on this channel) — noted for context only, no additional technique content from the paid material is in the free video.
