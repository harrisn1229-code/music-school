# Do You Want To Know A Secret — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

A song built almost entirely from **repetition and chromatic three- and four-note fragments**, which sounds like lazy craft and is the point — Pollack reads the mantra-like restatement as supplying the emotional realism the lyric can't. Formally lopsided on purpose: slow ad-lib intro, long verse, unusually short bridge.

**Form.** The compact single-bridge model. The lyric is identical across all three verses; intro and bridge carry the only new words. Entry rhythms are deliberately differentiated — the intro sings after the downbeat, the verses are led in by a long guitar pickup ahead of the beat, the bridge attacks on the beat. Verse **14 bars** as a couplet of 6 + 8; bridge **6 bars** as 4 + 2.

**Harmony.** Home key is major, but the intro is in the **parallel minor**, and the switch at the first verse is the song's best cheap trick — smooth because the keys are parallel, still startling. A **♭II Neapolitan** appears in both intro and verse, acting there as a dominant substitute back to I ([[HAR-C08 — Borrowed chords and modal interchange]]). The recurring chromatic chord stream in the odd bars is coloristic rather than functional; the ear tracks only the endpoints. The verse's second phrase broadens its harmonic rhythm and lands a **deceptive cadence on vi** before cycling back to V ([[HAR-C04 — Deceptive cadence]]). The bridge is a real modulation, not a tonicisation ([[HAR-C10 — Tonicization vs modulation]]), and coming out of it is textbook: the ear retroactively rehears the bridge's tonic as ii of the home key.

Pollack's added-sixth argument is worth the essay: an outtake ends cold on an added-sixth chord where the release fades, and much of the song already implies it — every deceptive cadence where you expect I and get vi. **I with an added sixth is I and vi superimposed**, so that chord summarises the song's ambiguity, and the fade masks it.

**Arrangement.** George takes the lead — his first on a Lennon–McCartney song — with doo-wop backing that pointedly **enters at verse 2**; an outtake has it from the top, which breaks the layering habit.
