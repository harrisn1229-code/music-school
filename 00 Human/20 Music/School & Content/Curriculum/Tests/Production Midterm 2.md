> **Q1.** **Oscillator generates · filter removes · amplifier shapes**
>
> **Q2.** **The amp envelope makes it audible · the filter envelope carries the character**
> Without an envelope on the amplifier there's no level over time and therefore no sound at all.
>
> **Q3.** **A filter can only remove harmonics the oscillator already generated**
> It can't add anything, so nothing downstream can put content into a signal that never had it.
>
> **Q4.** **The oscillator — swap the waveform for a richer one**
> Opening the filter further can't help when there's nothing above the fundamental to let through.
>
> **Q5.** **It self-oscillates into a sine — the filter is a sound source in its own right**
> With enough resonance it rings at its cutoff frequency with no input at all.
>
> **Q6.** **Sustain is the only level — attack, decay and release are all times**
>
> **Q7.** **From the attack peak down to the sustain level**
> It sets how long that fall takes, not how far it falls. How far is sustain's job.
>
> **Q8.** **Nothing — the peak and the sustain level are the same height, so there's no journey to time**
>
> **Q9.** **Attack near zero, short decay, sustain at or near zero, short release — sustain decides**
> Raise sustain and the note holds for as long as the key is held, which makes it a bass rather than a pluck.
>
> **Q10.** **A filter envelope with a healthy envelope amount — snap is the sound changing while it plays**
> Bright at the attack and darkening immediately is what makes a volume shape read as an instrument.
>
> **Q11.** **An oscillator below hearing range wired to a parameter — you hear what it moves, never the LFO**
>
> **Q12.** **You've built FM — the only difference between modulation and audio is speed**
> Past roughly 20 Hz the movement stops reading as movement and starts reading as tone.
>
> **Q13.** **Modulate timbre, not level — level reads as volume automation, timbre reads as alive**
> Cutoff and wavetable position are the two targets that matter.
>
> **Q14.** **Two modulators at different rates — an 8-bar LFO on cutoff, a faster one on position**
> Mix in an envelope too: an LFO is periodic and will eventually reveal its loop, where an envelope fires once per note.
>
> **Q15.** **Unison stacks copies · detune spreads them in pitch · unison alone just sums louder**
> The thickening comes from the copies beating against each other, so unison supplies the mechanism and detune only makes the copies non-identical enough to interact.
>
> **Q16.** **They can partially cancel in mono · detune below about 150 Hz smears the pitch**
> So keep subs and 808s mono with no detune, and layer rather than detune if you want width down there.
>
> **Q17.** **Subtractive removes harmonics · FM creates sidebands that weren't there**
> Subtractive's oscillators are harmonic by construction, so it can't produce inharmonic content at all.
>
> **Q18.** **At the source — a scannable table of waveforms with the position modulatable**
> Subtractive imposes its movement downstream with a filter; wavetable generates it at the oscillator.
>
> **Q19.** **The carrier-to-modulator ratio — integer gives harmonic sidebands, non-integer gives inharmonic ones**
> Integer ratios give pitched results such as basses and electric pianos. Non-integer ratios put sidebands between the harmonics, so the ear can't fuse them into one pitch and it reads as metallic.
>
> **Q20.** **Audio chopped into 1 to 100 ms grains and replayed — position and pitch are decoupled**
> The four controls are position, size, density and pitch. The decoupling lets you freeze a moment or stretch enormously without transposing.
>
> **Q21.** **A limiter is a compressor with a very high ratio acting as a ceiling — around 20:1, fast attack**
> Same device, extreme setting. Not a different category of tool.
>
> **Q22.** **The dynamic range at the top, which is what they need to work with**
> A limited mix arrives with its loudest moments already decided and no way to undo them.
>
> **Q23.** **How abruptly the ratio engages — it isn't ratio, which is how extreme the compression is**
> Knee is most of why two compressors at identical settings sound different.
>
> **Q24.** **Soft on the vocal for transparent levelling · hard on the drum bus so you hear it work**
> A soft knee starts compressing gently before the threshold is properly reached, so nothing announces itself. A hard knee slams the full ratio on at the crossing.
>
> **Q25.** **A crushed copy blended under the dry signal — the dry path keeps its transients**
> Nothing was taken away from the untouched copy, so peaks arrive at full height while the compressed copy fills in underneath.
>
> **Q26.** **Because the quiet material rises while the peaks don't move**
> The crushed copy is reduced most during peaks and barely at all in quiet passages, so what it adds is largest exactly where the signal was quietest.
>
> **Q27.** **Reach for multiband when only part of the spectrum has the dynamic problem**
> The failure mode is that broadband sees one band's peaks and pulls the whole signal down with them.
>
> **Q28.** **The whole vocal dips on exactly the notes where the low-mids bloom**
> The top end that was fine gets ducked too, and because the ducking is locked to specific notes it reads as a performance problem rather than as processing.
>
> **Q29.** **It generates new harmonics and rounds transient peaks — even-order for warm, odd-order for aggressive**
> Even-order harmonics sit at octaves and read full and warm; odd-order add edge and grit.
>
> **Q30.** **Because rounding the peaks lets the average level rise without the meter moving**
> Loudness is perceived from the average rather than from the peak.
>
> **Q31.** **Subtractive EQ → compression → de-ess → tone-shaping EQ**
>
> **Q32.** **De-ess after, because compression raises the esses · boosts last, so nothing squashes them**
> Reducing the body brings sibilance up relative to it, so the right amount to remove is only knowable after the compressor. A presence lift placed before the compressor is a lift the compressor immediately pulls back down.
>
> **Q33.** **One replaces patch recipes, the other replaces presets — a mechanism transfers and a setting doesn't**
> *Rubric (5):* says Course 13 replaces following a patch recipe with knowing why it works (2) · says Course 14 replaces reaching for a compressor preset with deciding what the device should do (1) · says a mechanism transfers to instruments and sources the recipe's author never used, and turns troubleshooting into a lookup (2)
> A recipe tells you which knobs to turn on one specific instrument to get one specific sound. It works exactly once and it fails silently: when the result is wrong you have no way to know which step went astray, because you never knew what any step was for. The mechanism is portable in a way the recipe isn't. Once the chain is oscillator, filter, amplifier, a new synth is a five-minute problem of finding where those three are drawn on this panel. Once a limiter is a compressor at an extreme setting, the master-chain question answers itself rather than needing a rule to memorise. And both convert troubleshooting from guesswork into a lookup: the symptom names the block or the stage, and the block names the handful of controls worth touching. That's the difference the two courses exist for, and it's why they pair — both sit under tools that are already in daily use and neither adds a tool.
>
> **Q34.** **Envelopes fire per note · LFOs run on a clock · a compressor is driven by the signal itself**
> *Rubric (5):* sorts them correctly — amp and filter envelopes triggered by each note, LFO periodic and independent of playing, compressor reacting to incoming level (2) · says the filter envelope and the cutoff LFO change timbre where the amp envelope and the compressor change level (2) · says the timbre changes are what read as the sound being alive (1)
> Two axes separate them and both matter. On what drives them: an envelope is triggered by the note and does the same thing every time, an LFO runs on its own clock regardless of what's played, and a compressor is driven by the audio itself, which makes it the only one of the four that's reactive rather than scheduled. On what they change: the amp envelope and the compressor both act on level, and the filter envelope and the cutoff LFO both act on timbre. That second split is the one the listener hears as aliveness. Level changes read as somebody moving a fader — expected, and quickly ignored. Timbre changes read as the sound itself doing something, because that's what physical instruments do when they're played harder or softer. Which is why "modulate timbre, not level" is the rule, and why a patch with only an amp envelope and a compressor on it can be perfectly shaped and still sound dead.
>
> **Q35.** **Fix brightness at the oscillator if you can — saturation is how you add content a filter never could**
> *Rubric (5):* says a filter can only remove, so brightness has to exist in the source or be generated (2) · says saturation generates new harmonic content, which makes it the one downstream device that can add rather than subtract (2) · gives a case — a sine or a sub that needs to be audible on small speakers, where there is nothing to uncover (1)
> The order follows from what each stage can do. Inside the patch, brightness is an oscillator decision: start on a saw and the filter has something to carve, start on a sine and no amount of filtering finds anything. So the first move for a dull patch is always upstream, at the waveform. Saturation is the exception that proves the rule, because it's the one thing in the chain that generates content rather than removing it — which makes it the only route to harmonics the oscillator never produced. The clean case is a sub or an 808 that vanishes on phones. Its fundamental is below what a small speaker can reproduce and there are no upper harmonics to boost, so EQ has nothing to work with. Saturate it and you create harmonics at multiples of the fundamental, which are reproducible up there and which the ear uses to infer the missing pitch. That's a sound you cannot get subtractively at any setting.
>
> **Q36.** **Non-integer ratios put sidebands between the harmonics — a filter can't relocate a partial**
> *Rubric (5):* says the carrier-to-modulator ratio sets where the sidebands land (1) · says integer ratios give harmonic results and non-integer ones give inharmonic partials the ear can't fuse into a pitch (2) · says a bell is physically inharmonic, and that a filter can only attenuate existing partials rather than move one to a non-integer position (2)
> Start with what a bell is. Struck metal vibrates in modes that aren't whole-number multiples of a fundamental, so its overtones sit between the harmonics and never fuse into one clear pitch — that unfused quality is what "metallic" means. FM reaches it because the ratio decides where sidebands fall: an integer ratio puts them on harmonics for pitched sounds, and something like 1:1.41 puts them between, which is the bell's arrangement. Subtractive can't follow because both of its operations are the wrong shape. Its oscillators produce harmonic partials by construction — that's what makes them periodic and pitched — and its filter can only make an existing partial louder or quieter. Nothing in the chain can put energy at a non-integer position, so a bell is outside the method rather than merely difficult within it.
>
> **Q37.** **That a filter can brighten anything · that a limiter is a special mastering object**
> One stops you hunting for the right filter setting on a source that has no harmonics to find. The other stops you treating the 2-bus as a place where different rules apply, and makes the no-limiter rule obvious rather than something to remember.
>
> **Q38.** **Both control how abruptly something engages · attack is scheduled per note, knee is level-dependent**
> The analogy holds on the gesture — gradual versus immediate — and breaks on what triggers it. An attack time fires identically on every note regardless of how hard it's played, where a knee's behaviour depends on how far the incoming signal is from the threshold at that instant.
>
> **Q39.** **Detuned voices differ in pitch so they can cancel · the parallel copies stay in phase**
> *Rubric (5):* says wide detuned voices are near-identical waveforms at slightly different pitches, so their phase relationship drifts and summing to mono can cancel (2) · says parallel compression sums a signal with a level-shaped version of itself, which stays in phase (2) · notes that the parallel case is therefore safe to sum where the detuned case has to be checked (1)
> Summing two related signals is only a hazard when their phase relationship varies. Detuned unison voices are the same waveform at slightly different frequencies, which means their relative phase is continuously sliding — that sliding is precisely what produces the beating you hear as thickness. Stereo playback keeps them partly separated, but summing to mono adds them directly, and at moments when they're out of phase they subtract instead. A patch that sounded huge can arrive hollow. Parallel compression looks similar and isn't. The crushed copy is the same signal with its gain reshaped over time; it hasn't been pitch-shifted or delayed, so it stays aligned with the dry path and the two always add. Nothing about it changes when the mix is summed. The general test is whether the two signals differ in pitch or in time, or only in level — the first two can cancel, the third can't.
>
> **Q40.** **Fix dullness and lifelessness in the patch, then subtractive EQ → compression → de-ess → tone-shaping EQ**
> *Rubric (5):* names at least two things to fix in the patch rather than the chain — a dull waveform, no modulation on the timbre, unison with no detune (2) · gives the chain order correctly (2) · says the chain refines a patch that already works and can't rescue one that doesn't (1)
> Two categories of problem look identical from the chain and only one of them is a chain problem. If the pad is dull, that's the oscillator — no filter or EQ finds harmonics that were never generated, so the fix is a richer waveform. If it's static, that's modulation — the sound is identical in bar eight to bar one, and the answer is a slow LFO or a long envelope on cutoff or wavetable position, not a chorus. If it's thin, check whether unison is running without detune, in which case the copies are just summing louder. Only once the patch itself is right does the chain earn its place, and then the order is the standard one: subtractive cuts first so the compressor isn't reacting to rumble it was never meant to hear, compression next, de-essing after that if the source needs it, and tone-shaping boosts last where nothing downstream can squash them.
