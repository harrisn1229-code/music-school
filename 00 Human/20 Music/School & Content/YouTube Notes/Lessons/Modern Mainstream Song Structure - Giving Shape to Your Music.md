# Modern / Mainstream Song Structure - Giving Shape to Your Music

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2CMUdPcF9lA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2CMUdPcF9lA)

## Synopsis

A fundamentals-level lesson on the standard pop/rock song structure (Intro–Verse–Chorus–Verse 2–Chorus–Bridge–Chorus–Outro), aimed at a student who has just built one loop and needs to turn it into a full song. Max Konyi walks through what each section is *for* (not just its order), then makes the case for giving each section — verse, chorus, bridge — its own distinct-but-complementary chord progression rather than reusing one progression for the whole song. He closes by showing how to decide whether a loop-in-progress feels like a verse, a chorus, or a bridge, and previews using an arranger track (Studio One's version of section chunks) to reorder the song once it's built. Most of the ground here — proportional section lengths, complementary B-section writing, chorus-growing-longer, breaking convention deliberately — is already covered in much more depth by the "You Suck at Producing" material already in the vault (see [[Arrangement and Song Form]]); this video is the 101-level version of the same territory, with one genuinely new concrete trick (choosing an unused chord to open the bridge) and a useful plain-language framework for classifying a loop's structural role.

## Techniques

- **The standard pop/rock section order and what each section is for** — Intro → Verse → Chorus → Verse 2 → Chorus → Bridge → Chorus → Outro. **Intro**: often instrumental (no vocal/lyric yet), can be short, exists purely to introduce the song and set the feel. **Verse**: the storytelling section — lyrics/content change every time it recurs, even though the melody usually stays close to identical between Verse 1 and Verse 2 (small differences give the second verse its own feel without losing recognizability). **Chorus**: the "main event"/climax — this is where the song's title usually lives, it repeats near-verbatim every time it returns, and it carries "the hook," the part designed to get stuck in the listener's head. **Bridge**: a deliberate contrast section that appears exactly once — a different vibe from everything heard so far, functioning as a brief departure before returning to the chorus one last time.
- **Give each recurring section (verse, chorus, bridge) its own distinct chord progression, rather than one progression for the whole song** — a single reused progression for the entire track is common and totally acceptable in pop/folk, but a more interesting, less repetitive song comes from writing complementary-but-different progressions per section: the verse's progression happens every time a verse plays, the chorus's every time the chorus plays, and so on, so each section reads as its own distinct part of the song rather than a re-skin of the same four bars. *(general starting point — the "complementary" requirement is that the sections still flow into one another, not that they're harmonically unrelated)*
- **Pick a chord you deliberately avoided in the verse/chorus to open the bridge, for instant contrast** — once the verse and chorus progressions are locked, audit which chords from the song's key you used across them (worked example: verse+chorus used C, D, E, G, and A, but never F) — then start the bridge's progression on the chord you left out (F). Because that chord hasn't been heard yet anywhere in the song, its arrival immediately signals "somewhere new" even before any instrumentation/energy change kicks in — pair it with a genuinely different drum loop/instrumentation for the section to land as intended. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **Decide whether a loop-in-progress reads as a verse, a chorus, or a bridge by its felt function, not by write order** — ask what the loop's role feels like: does it feel like the **main event**/highlight of the song (→ chorus), does it feel **toned-down or secondary**, with room for melodic/lyrical elements that change over time (→ verse), or does it feel like **its own separate entity**, a vacation from everything else in the song (→ bridge)? In practice the verse or chorus usually gets written first since they're the load-bearing sections, and the bridge decision comes later, informed by what's already been used (see the unused-chord trick above) so it can deliberately contrast rather than being written blind.
- **Pre-chorus exists as an optional linking section** — the structure isn't always a hard cut from verse straight into chorus; a pre-chorus can sit between them as a short bridge-like link that smooths the transition. Named as a variation to be aware of, not elaborated on in this video (already documented in the vault elsewhere — see [[Sampling and Sound Selection]], [[Sound Selection Guide]], and the Dua Lipa/Dance Pop chain material).
- **Keep the fundamental structure simple while learning it: Intro–Verse–Chorus–Verse 2 (same as Verse 1 but slightly different)–Chorus–Bridge–Chorus–Outro** — internalize this skeleton before layering in variations (pre-choruses, broken conventions, uneven section lengths); the goal at this stage is understanding what each slot is for, not maximizing structural cleverness.
- **An arranger track (Studio One) lets you reorder a song by dragging whole labeled section-chunks, moving every instrument inside that section along with it** — build the song as discrete section blocks (verse, chorus, bridge, etc.) on a dedicated arranger lane, then drag a block to a new position in the timeline and every track's content within that section's time range moves with it, rather than manually re-cutting every individual clip. *(DAW-specific chrome — Studio One's Arranger Track; Ableton's closest equivalent is Arrangement View section markers/Locators plus multi-track range-select-and-drag, which accomplishes the same reordering without a dedicated arranger lane.)*
- **Understanding "key" (as in "the key of C major") is presented as the prerequisite for writing complementary chord progressions** — flagged as the next thing to learn before attempting the per-section progression-writing above; not covered in this video itself (points to a follow-up video on keys).
