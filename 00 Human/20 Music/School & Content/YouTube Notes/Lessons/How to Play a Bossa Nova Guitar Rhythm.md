# How to Play a Bossa Nova Guitar Rhythm

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vtaNw5PCkWE)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vtaNw5PCkWE)

## Synopsis

An 18:57 guitar lesson from Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared teaching **"the classic bossa nova accompaniment pattern"** — the standard two-bar right-hand comping figure that every bossa nova player leans on, taught fingerstyle (thumb + i/m/a fingers, no pick, no pinky) with a chord-tone-controlled bass note rather than an open string. Jared's pedagogy here is deliberately incremental: rather than handing over a written-out rhythm, he builds the two-bar pattern **one note at a time**, always re-anchored to a steady quarter-note bass, so the offbeats get internalized by feel rather than memorized off a chart — the same "internalize, don't read tabs" philosophy that runs through his other technique videos. He demos it on a specific D major 7 fingering chosen for bass-note control, then applies the finished pattern to the A section of **"Wave" (Jobim)**, including how he loosens the strict pattern when comping behind a melody or soloist. Direct, practical, and squarely a technique video rather than a theory lecture — this is the first video in the vault's Bossa Nova coverage to actually teach the **right-hand rhythm pattern itself** (the existing Rick Beato note covers "Girl from Ipanema"'s chord shapes + a pattern description in passing; this one is the pattern as its own subject, worked from zero).

## What's taught

**Chord voicing used to teach it — D major 7, bass-controlled:**
- Index finger: 5th fret, A string (root, D)
- Ring (3rd) finger: 7th fret, D string (5th, A)
- Middle finger: 6th fret, G string (7th, C♯)
- Pinky: 7th fret, B string (3rd, F♯)
- Low-to-high spelling: **1–5–7–3** (D–A–C♯–F♯) — a compact 4-string voicing using only the middle four strings.
- Explicitly the *same* chord as barring the top 3 strings at the 2nd fret over an open D bass — he uses the fretted-bass version instead specifically **to control the bass note** rather than leave it as a ringing open string, since the whole point of the exercise is separating "bass player" and "chordal player" as two independent voices.
- Right-hand assignment: **thumb (p)** = bass note only; **index/middle/ring (i/m/a)** = the upper 3-string chord; **no pinky used** — only the middle four strings of the guitar are involved for this voicing.

**The two-bar pattern, built incrementally (his own words, cleanest summary quoted directly):**
> "beat one and beat two of two measures have the chord... the bass note is one three, one three each measure... the chord is on beat one and beat two, and then it's on beat four of the last measure — everything in between are just these up beats, these off beats."

Reconstructed from that summary plus the step-by-step build order he narrates:
- **Bass (thumb):** half notes on **beats 1 and 3** of every bar — "boom, boom" — steady and unchanging, the anchor everything else is measured against.
- **Bar 1:** chord lands **together with the bass on beat 1** (the *only* point in the whole 2-bar pattern where bass and chord attack simultaneously), then alone on **beat 2**, then on the **offbeat after beat 3** (the "&" of 3), then on the **offbeat right before the next bar's downbeat** (the "&" of 4 — an anticipation, arriving early into bar 2).
- **Bar 2:** chord hits continue landing on offbeats through the bar (an "&" partway through, then the offbeat just before the last beat — the "&" of 3), with one final **on-the-beat** chord hit on **beat 4**, the last beat of the two-bar cycle, before the whole thing loops.
- **Net feel: only 2 of the ~6 chord attacks in the whole two-bar cycle land on the beat** (bar 1 beat 1 — with the bass — and bar 2 beat 4); everything else is syncopated onto the "&"s. Jared calls this out directly as *"a lot of offbeats — that's what makes this so syncopated, it makes it so groovy."*
- ⚠️ *Confidence note:* the individual offbeat slots in bar 2 are the least precisely stated part of the transcript (he demonstrates them musically rather than counting every one aloud) — the beats-1/2/4-on-the-beat-only rule above is a direct quote and solid; the exact 8th-note position of each bar-2 upbeat is a best reconstruction from the build sequence, not a verbatim count.

**Right-hand touch:**
- **"Planting"** — pre-place/prepare the plucking fingers on the strings just before the attack, which is what produces the characteristic rhythmic "bounce" rather than a flat pluck.
- **Optional damping:** he sometimes lets a stroke "calm down" (mutes the ringing notes) before plucking again, for a more clipped, percussive rhythmic color — explicitly optional, a stylistic choice layered on top of the core pattern, not part of the pattern itself.

**Practice progression (how he has you build it):**
1. Play the bass alone (half notes, beats 1 & 3) until it's rock steady — this is the fixed reference everything else locks to.
2. Add exactly **one** chord attack at a time, in the order the pattern actually occurs, always re-establishing the bass first — never jump to the full pattern before each individual addition feels secure.
3. Once the full 2-bar pattern is solid, **isolate the offbeats** if they're the sticking point: tap your foot on the quarter-note beat and pluck *only* the offbeat 8th notes (skip the downbeats entirely) — he compares the resulting feel directly to **ska or reggae accenting**. Add the bass back in afterward once the offbeat feel alone is comfortable.
4. Optional: practice with a **metronome that has a distinct offbeat click** (different tone for the "&"s) so the ear locks onto the offbeat grid directly rather than counting up to it.

**Next level — root/5th alternating bass:** once the core pattern (static root under every bass hit) is solid, upgrade the thumb part to **alternate root and 5th** on the two bass hits per bar (beat 1 = root, beat 3 = 5th, or similar) instead of repeating the same bass note — his explicit "next level" add-on. He notes the static-root version already sounds complete and the alternating-bass version is really only worth the extra coordination cost when sustaining one chord for a longer stretch (e.g. holding a chord through several bars) rather than moving through a fast-changing progression, where the plain version reads cleaner.

**Applied to a real tune — "Wave" (Jobim):**
- Plays the pattern through the **A section of "Wave"** starting on the same D major 7 voicing taught above — states this is the actual opening chord of the tune's main melody section, and that these are "the exact voicings" used on famous recordings of the song (name-checks Frank Sinatra's popular version and "plenty of Jobim versions").
- The progression moves through **Dm7 → G7** as part of the A section (not the full harmonic path, and the transcript doesn't give every chord in order — the tune is asserted to use the same 1-5-7-3-type compact voicings throughout).
- **Comping adaptation when accompanying (not soloing):** when another player is on the melody, singing, or improvising, he **loosens the strict pattern** — specifically, on a chord change he'll sometimes play just the root under the new chord instead of the full root+upper-voicing hit, purely for convenience/timing, while keeping the underlying groove/pulse going rather than breaking it to nail every note of the strict pattern. The strict full pattern is presented as the thing to *practice* until it's second nature; live comping is described as an "adaptation" of it, not a note-for-note performance of the drilled version.
