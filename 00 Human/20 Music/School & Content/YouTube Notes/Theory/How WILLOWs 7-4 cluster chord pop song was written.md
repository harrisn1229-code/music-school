# How WILLOW's 7/4 cluster chord pop song was written

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Q9pGvRRPnUc)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Q9pGvRRPnUc)

## Synopsis

A songwriter interview (with Brad Oberhofer, one of four co-writers and the pianist) about how Willow Smith's "Symptom of Life" — previously analyzed by this channel for its 7/4 meter and dense cluster chords — was actually built, correcting a published transcription in the process.

## Techniques

- **Songwriting order, straight from the writer**: drums came first (Asher's 7/4 groove — one of three 7/4 tracks on the same album), then the cluster-chord piano part improvised over it intuitively ("whatever mood I'm in"), then Willow's vocal melody written over the finished instrumental — praised by the pianist himself as unexpectedly strong given how dense/dissonant the underlying harmony was.
- **Cluster-voicing construction, exact**: 4 notes in each hand, occasionally adding a barely-audible extra note in the right hand — kept deliberately light-touch so the melody note on top can still poke through the dense harmony rather than getting buried; piano choice matters too (some pianos render the close-together notes more "piercing" than others).
- **Verification-by-source beats a published transcription.** The channel's own earlier video (and a musicnotes.com transcription) disagreed on whether one voicing contains an F; going straight to the songwriter confirmed the F IS there — the published transcription is simply wrong, apparently from approximating rather than accurately hearing the cluster.
- **Deliberate density contrast between verse and chorus as the arrangement's core device.** The verse stacks busy drums + dense cluster-chord piano + rhythmically "acrobatic" vocals; the chorus strips the piano out entirely, drops to just bass + heavily stacked/layered vocals — engineered specifically as a release valve after the verse's accumulated density, described by the co-writer as his favorite moment in the song for exactly that reason.
