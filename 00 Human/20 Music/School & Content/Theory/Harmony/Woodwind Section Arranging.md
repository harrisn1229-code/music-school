# Woodwind Section Arranging

What actually defines a woodwind, the four families that make up an orchestral section, how doubling and auxiliary instruments work, and the transposition convention that makes woodwind writing different from strings. Third installment of the same crash-course series as [[String Section Arranging]] and [[Brass Section Arranging]] — and the one where the family is genuinely *diverse*: strings and brass each share one sound-production mechanism, woodwinds do not.

## What makes an instrument a woodwind

Every woodwind makes its sound by **splitting or vibrating blown air against some sort of edge**. That air travels down a pipe, and **the length of the pipe sets the pitch** — keys and holes work by effectively changing that length. The mechanism is shared with brass only in the "air down a pipe" half; the edge is what makes it a woodwind (see the brass comparison below).

**Four different edge types, and they're what split the family up:**

- **Single reed** — one short, sharp piece of cane vibrating as you blow onto it: clarinet, saxophone.
- **Double reed** — two pieces of cane vibrating against each other: oboe, cor anglais, bassoon.
- **Open hole blown across** — no reed at all, the air is split on the rim: flute, piccolo.
- **Fipple** — the open hole at the end of the instrument does the splitting: recorder, ocarina, tin whistle.

**"Woodwind" is a historical name, not a material one.** They were once made almost exclusively of wood; metal became the preferable choice for several of them (the flute being the obvious case), and the label stuck.

## The four orchestral families, plus doubling

The section is built from **four types of player: flutes, oboes, clarinets, bassoons.** A smaller orchestra carries two of each; up to three of each is normal at full size.

**More than four instruments show up anyway, because of doubling.** A player in one of the four categories also plays an **auxiliary instrument** — a variation on their main one — and may switch between them *inside the same piece*, which is a real added technicality of the job.

| Main | Auxiliary | What it adds |
|---|---|---|
| Flute | **Piccolo** | Smaller flute, an extra octave at the **top** — the highest range in the orchestral woodwinds |
| Oboe | **Cor anglais** | Effectively an alto oboe: lower range, richer tone |
| Bassoon | **Contrabassoon** | An extra octave at the **bottom** |

**Worked example of doubling in practice:** in Bartók's *Dance Suite*, all the bassoonists play regular bassoon through the first movement; in the second, one of them switches to the contrabassoon sitting ready beside them.

**A naming trap worth memorizing:** the **cor anglais** is French for "English horn" and is a **woodwind**; the **French horn** is named in English and is a **brass** instrument. The languages are swapped relative to the names.

## Range: the bottom is fixed, the top is not

The **bottom note of a woodwind's range is non-negotiable** — it's set by the full length of the pipework. The **top of the range is flexible**, and the trade is quality: the higher you push, the less pleasant and less usable the note becomes. So each instrument has a practical ceiling you don't want to venture far past, rather than a hard one. (Compare [[Brass Section Arranging]], where the ceiling is flexible for the different reason of trained embouchure control.)

## Woodwind vs. brass — the one distinct difference

Both are blown, so the confusion is fair. The dividing line is **what vibrates**:

- **Brass** — the *player's lips* vibrate (effectively blowing a raspberry into the mouthpiece); that's what resonates the air column. See [[Brass Section Arranging]].
- **Woodwind** — the *breath is blown over a sharp edge* (reed, hole rim, or fipple), and that's what creates the resonant frequency in the pipe.

This is also why the **saxophone is a woodwind despite being made of brass** — the family is defined by sound production, not material. It's the same definitional rule that puts the alphorn and didgeridoo in the brass/labrophone family.

## Transposing instruments — the big one for composing

**Many woodwinds are transposing instruments: the note that sounds is not the note on the page.** An alto sax player reads a C, thinks a C, and an **E♭** comes out. The tenor sax is in B♭ — the same player reading the same written C on tenor now sounds a **B♭**.

**Why this exists: it's built for doubling.** Since a player switches between instruments within their family, the goal is for the **same fingering to produce the same written note on every instrument in that family**, regardless of size. The keys and holes sit in the same places, but the differing sizes and shapes mean those positions produce different sounding pitches — so the player is handed sheet music "in the wrong key" to cancel that out. Bennett's analogy: imagine keyboards with identical layouts that each gave you different notes depending on the instrument's size — unworkable without exactly this convention.

**How to reason about it:** a non-transposing instrument is simply *in C* — the piano is "the C piano," the violin is in C. An **E♭ clarinet** is in E♭, three semitones above C, so **anything played on it sounds three semitones higher than written**, relative to the piano.

> **Worked example.** To have the E♭ clarinet sound in **D major** alongside the rest of the ensemble, you write its part in **B major** — three semitones *lower* than the intended key, cancelling the instrument's three-semitone-*sharp* offset.

**Some transpose by a plain octave**, which is far easier to hold in your head — the contrabassoon sounds one octave below what the bassoonist reads.

## Telling them apart by ear

The video's cheat sheet before its guess-the-instrument quiz — useful as timbre ear-training and as a "which voice do I actually want here" reference when writing or picking a patch (see [[Sound Selection Guide]]).

| Instrument | Character |
|---|---|
| **Flute** | Light and breathy |
| **Piccolo** | The flute but much higher — a whistling, shrill tone; if a woodwind sounds *particularly high*, it's this |
| **Oboe** | Richer than the flute, but still quite pure |
| **Cor anglais** | The alto oboe — similar to the oboe but deeper and richer, with a melancholic, emotive quality |
| **Clarinet** | More rounded than the oboe, less high-frequency information, warmer |
| **Bassoon** | Rounded but deep, and can sound honky; in its higher range it's easy to confuse with the clarinet, so lean on register |
| **Contrabassoon** | Lower still than the bassoon; if a woodwind sounds *particularly low*, it's this |
| **Saxophone** | The odd one out — not in the standard orchestral lineup, so it reads as jazzy/poppy rather than orchestral; reedy, similar to the clarinet |

**The two practical shortcuts in that table** are the extremes: unusually high → piccolo, unusually low → contrabassoon. The genuine confusions are oboe/cor anglais (same family, different depth) and clarinet/bassoon-in-its-upper-register.
