# Reharmonizing The Blues - 3 Levels

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=09SK9oP8JVI)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=09SK9oP8JVI)

## Synopsis

A tight, three-level guitar lesson from Jack Ruch that takes a single plain 1-4-5 12-bar blues in C and reharmonizes it with progressively denser jazz devices, comping and soloing over each version so the harmonic difference is heard, not just described. Level 1 inserts **major ii–V–I's** at four specific spots in the form (into bar 4's IV chord, back to I at bar 6, replacing the V-to-IV move at bars 9–10, and in the final turnaround) — the same device documented generally on [[The ii-V-I]] and [[Reharmonization]] § 2, applied here as a repeatable "insert a ii–V wherever you're about to land" checklist rather than a single worked example. Level 2 stacks **minor ii–V–i's** on top of that (targeting the relative minor at bars 2–3, à la Charlie Parker's *Blues for Alice* changes, and D minor at bar 8), plus turns the turnaround into a full **VI–ii–V** by using A7 as a secondary dominant into D minor. Between levels 2 and 3 there's a short but clear statement of the soloing philosophy underneath all of it: think in the reharmonized changes (even when the band is only playing the plain progression) and outline their chord tones, rather than running the blues/pentatonic scale — new chords means new target tones, which is what actually produces more melodic, less scale-bound lines. Level 3 tritone-substitutes every functioning dominant in the whole progression (both the plain V's and the V's inside the inserted ii–V's), turning diatonic root motion into a chromatically descending bass line. The video is short (10:12) and dense — nearly every sentence carries a concrete chord-by-chord instruction rather than general theory talk, and it plays as a natural "next lesson" after the vault's existing Jack Ruch jazz-blues material ([[The Key To Understanding A Jazz Blues]], [[Think Different with Chord Substitutions]]). ## What's taught

- **The plain baseline: a basic 1-4-5 12-bar blues in C**, stated explicitly before any reharmonization starts — `C7 | F7 | C7 | C7 / F7 | F7 | C7 | C7 / G7 | F7 | C7 | C7` with the final two bars as the turnaround. This is the unmodified skeleton every level below modifies in place. → [[Blues Form and Harmony]]

- **Level 1 — major ii–V–I insertions, four specific slots in the form:**
  - **Bar 4, heading to the IV chord (F7):** insert its own major ii–V — **Gm7 → C7 → F7** — in place of just landing on F7 directly. *(Gm7 is F7's own ii chord: a minor 7th a fourth below F7's root.)*
  - **Bar 6, heading back to the I chord (C):** insert **Dm7 → G7 → C** — the textbook C-major ii–V–I, dropped in ahead of the tonic bar. → [[The ii-V-I]] § *The major version*
  - **Bars 9–10, where the plain form has V (G7) moving to IV (F7):** replace that stretch with a ii–V of its own — **Dm7 (bar 9) → G7 (bar 10) → C7 (bar 11)** — turning what was a single V-to-IV move into a full two-five landing on the I7 that follows.
  - **The turnaround (final bar):** close with a **2-5-1 back to C** rather than a plain V or I.
  - **General device stated directly in the lesson and worth lifting out on its own:** *any spot in a progression where you're about to land on a chord* is a candidate for dropping a ii–V in front of it — this is the same "interpolate a ii" reharmonization tool as [[Reharmonization]] § 2 and [[Chord Substitution]] § 4, here demonstrated as a repeatable checklist run across an entire 12-bar form rather than a single textbook example. → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Reharmonization]], [[Chord Substitution]]

- **Level 2 — minor ii–V–i's, layered on top of Level 1's changes:**
  - **Bars 2–3, targeting A minor (the relative minor of C):** **B half-diminished (Bm7♭5) → E7 (or E7 altered) → Am** — the minor-key ii°–V–i shape. Explicitly named as **"very similar to the changes Charlie Parker used in Blues for Alice"** — i.e. this isn't an arbitrary insertion, it's referencing a real, recognized jazz-blues reharmonization lineage. Stated to transition especially smoothly into the Level-1 ii–V that follows it at bar 4.
  - **Bar 8, targeting D minor:** **E half-diminished (Em7♭5) → A7 altered → Dm** — same minor ii–V–i shape, different target.
  - **The turnaround upgraded to a full 6–2–5:** use **A7 as a secondary dominant resolving to D minor**, turning the plain turnaround into a VI–ii–V shape — matches [[Turnarounds]]'s documented **VI7 turnaround** variant (`Cmaj7 A7 Dm7 G7`), here used specifically to set up the *minor* ii–V–i that leads back into the top of the form rather than resolving straight to C. → [[Turnarounds]] § *VI7*, [[Secondary Dominants]]
  - **Minor ii–V–i, general shape as stated:** half-diminished ii° → dominant/altered-dominant V → minor i. Matches the minor version already documented on [[The ii-V-I]] § *The minor version* (`Dm7♭5 → G7♭9 → Cm7` in C minor) — this lesson is the same shape applied twice inside a still-major-key blues, targeting the relative minor and the iv chord's own minor color rather than a whole piece being in a minor key.

- **Soloing philosophy tying the reharmonizations to improvisation — the lesson's real payoff, not just a chord-substitution exercise:**
  - **"All I'm really doing in these solos is outlining the chords by playing chord tones"** — using only the notes found within each (reharmonized) chord rather than running a scale over the whole form. The stated reason the reharmonizations matter for soloing specifically: *more chords means more target tones to play off of*, which is what lets the line move more melodically and with more harmonic interest than blues-scale/pentatonic noodling. → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] (the vault's general chord-tone-targeting practice ladder — this video's soloing philosophy is the same core idea, "target chord tones, not a blanket scale," applied as the reason *to reharmonize* rather than as a standalone drill)
  - **"The band doesn't even have to play the chords you're outlining"** — a soloist can privately think in a denser reharmonized version of the changes and use it to inform note choice even while the rhythm section plays the plain progression underneath. Demonstrated directly: soloing over a bare I–IV–I stretch of a basic 12-bar while mentally hearing the Level-2 (minor ii–V–i) reharmonization of that same stretch, producing lines "that just would not be possible if I was just thinking about a scale." This is a distinct, useful variant of prolongation-style thinking — reharmonizing *only in the soloist's head*, independent of what's actually being comped — worth flagging next to [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]'s prolongation section (which collapses a busy chart to fewer functions; this is the inverse move, imagining a denser chart than what's actually being played).

- **Level 3 — tritone substitutions on every functioning dominant in the whole progression:**
  - **The rule stated plainly:** substitute a dominant chord with the dominant a tritone away — C7 ↔ G♭7, E7 ↔ B♭7 — and it applies to *every* chord functioning as a V, including the V's buried inside the ii–V's added in Levels 1 and 2, not just the form's original V7 (bar 9's G7).
  - **Worked substitutions from the transcript:** the Level-2 minor ii–V into A minor, **Bm7♭5 → E7 → Am**, becomes **Bm7♭5 → B♭7 → Am** (E7 replaced by its tritone sub, B♭7). The Level-1 ii–V into the IV chord, **Gm7 → C7 → F7**, becomes **Gm7 → G♭7 → F7** (C7 replaced by G♭7). → [[Chord Substitution]] § 2 *Tritone substitution*
  - **Explicit usage caveat carried over from the transcript, not a general-knowledge add:** *"this is something you might want to just pepper in in certain spots"* — the full-tritone-sub-everywhere version demonstrated is a maximal teaching example, not necessarily the intended default; use it selectively in practice.
  - **Effect named:** turns the form's diatonic root motion into **chromatically descending bass motion** — same mechanism documented generally on [[Chord Substitution]] § 2 and [[Reharmonization]] § 3. → [[Chromatic Bass Lines]]

- **Pedagogical structure worth naming on its own — reharmonization as stackable, additive levels, not competing alternatives.** Level 2 is built directly on top of Level 1's changes (not a separate reharmonization of the plain form), and Level 3 is applied on top of *both*, substituting the V chords that Levels 1–2 introduced along the way. The lesson comps and solos over each cumulative level before moving to the next, so the ear hears the progression get progressively denser rather than three disconnected demos. This is a clean teaching template for practicing [[Reharmonization]]'s techniques in combination rather than in isolation.
