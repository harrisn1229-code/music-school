## What's taught

- **[~0:00–0:53] Setup.** "Smells Like Teen Spirit" is from Nirvana's 1991 *Nevermind*, bass by Krist Novoselic. This video covers only the main intro/chorus riff — a full song tab with sheet music is sold separately (link in the original video's description, not captured in this transcript pull).

- **[~0:53–2:34] Level 1 — root notes only, simplified rhythm, "the caveman method."** The bare minimum to "play this with your friends in a garage": right root notes, but with a heavily simplified plucking rhythm (the real rhythm doesn't arrive until Level 3). Fingering: **1st-fret notes with the index finger, 4th-fret notes with the pinky finger.** Demoed with a guest, "Noob Josh." **The generalizable takeaway — the "caveman method":** when a song is above your current level, listen for its most important notes and rhythms rather than trying to nail the whole part — imagine hearing it as a "caveman" with a totally blank musical brain, and build a Level-1-style skeleton out of whatever stands out on that first, dumbest possible pass. **This is the identical named technique already documented from [[The 5 Levels of Longview (For Bass)]]'s Level 1** ("a little eggshell smooth cave brain") — two separate BassBuzz "5 Levels" videos now use the exact same device to open the beginner tier, confirming it's a repeatable teaching pattern for this channel rather than a one-off gag. → [[Goal-Tiered Practice and the Self-Taught Bass Commandments]]

- **[~2:34–4:27] Level 2 — adding "spicy" slides.** Still the Level 1 rhythm, but with small slides worked into the line, which BassBuzz ties to the way Kurt Cobain "sloppily hits open strings in between chords" on guitar. **Fingering changes to accommodate the slides:** start on the index finger for the 1st-fret notes, hold the index down while sliding *up* to the 4th fret of the E string, catch that note with the **middle finger**, then play the 4th fret of the A string with the same middle finger, **slide it back down**, and return to the index finger on the 1st fret — summarized in the video as "index, slide, middle, middle, slide." **General takeaway stated directly:** a plain root-note rock bass line gets a lot more interesting with a few slides dropped in between the roots — "you can never really have too many bass slides." → [[Bass Slide Technique]]

- **[~4:27–7:09] Level 3 — the real rhythm: following the drums, not the guitar.** The first level that's genuinely performance-ready. **The common beginner mistake named directly:** copying the guitar's rhythm (the "chika-chicka" strum pattern) instead of playing what the bass actually does. **What the bass really plays:** guitar, bass, and drums all line up on the first two beats of the bar; then the guitar continues alone with its scratchy 16th-note strum while the **bass instead follows the kick drum**, landing 16th-note groupings on beats 1 and 3 specifically — this is what glues the rhythm section together and creates contrast against the guitar part rather than doubling it. **Counting method taught for the 16ths:** "one-e-and-a, two-e-and-a, three-e-and-a, four-e-and-a" per bar, with **beats 1 and 3 as the tricky 16th-note spots and beats 2 and 4 as plain, easy quarter notes.** *(The exact syllable-by-syllable count in the auto-captions is a little garbled around the "and a" vs. "and uh" transcription — the structural claim, 16ths only on beats 1 and 3, quarters on 2 and 4, is stated clearly and repeated, so that part is high confidence even where the exact count text isn't.)* A fill covers the transition into the verse; missing it and just repeating the main riff instead is called "no biggie." → [[Locking In With the Drummer]]

- **[~7:09–7:47] Level 4 — switch to a pick for tone.** Framed as "controversial" for some players, but stated plainly as what Novoselic actually did. **The payoff:** a brighter, more aggressive, "trebley" tone that cuts through the mix better than fingers and gives the song a more aggressive vibe — demonstrated back-to-back, fingers then pick, on the same riff. → [[Pick vs. Fingers on Bass — Tone and Technique Tradeoffs]]

- **[~7:47–9:26] Level 4.5 (secret) — a ghost note that makes the picking easier, not just texture.** A ghost note (marked `X`) is a pluck/pick with the strings muted instead of fretted. Novoselic plays one **on beat 3**. The video's real point isn't the percussive sound — it's that the ghost note **solves a picking-mechanics problem**: down-strokes feel more natural on the beat and up-strokes off the beat, so the ghost note on beat 3 supplies a "free" down-stroke that isn't tied to a real pitch, which then lets the next (off-beat, real) note fall naturally on an up-stroke, landing back on a down-stroke for beat 4. **Full picking pattern given, down/up marked:** `down up down down up down up down down down down up down down`. → [[Ghost Notes (Slap Bass)]], [[Pick Grip, Attack Mechanics, and Picking Patterns]]

- **[~9:26–end] Level 5 — the monster slide, and two deliberately-quirky timing details.** **The big slide:** start on the index finger at the 1st fret, keep pressing, slide up to the **6th fret**, then back down to the 1st fret — described as the "biggest slide in this line," distinct from Level 2's small connective slides. **Timing quirk #1 — the slide's late, triplet-feel entry:** most cover versions start the slide squarely on a normal beat subdivision, but the actual studio bass track starts it *later* than that — as if the beat were divided into a triplet and the slide begins on the triplet's last third rather than an even subdivision. BassBuzz notes Novoselic doesn't play it this way live (the slide comes in earlier in live footage), so the quirk may be a one-take studio artifact rather than a repeatable technique — either way, it makes the studio version "quirkier and a little less rhythmically predictable." **Timing quirk #2 — uneven 16th notes on beat 3:** the same four 16th notes counted in Level 3 ("three-e-and-a") aren't played perfectly evenly on the record — the off-beat 16ths land noticeably late, and neither the guitar nor the drums match that lateness, so it's isolated to the bass. **Framing for both quirks:** could be an intentional rhythmic-instability choice or just sloppy alternating-picking technique — either way, BassBuzz calls it "part of the picture" if you're chasing the studio take exactly, and compares it directly to "the ripped jeans and the greasy hair and the oversized flannel that makes it all grunge." → [[Bass Slide Technique]], [[Pocket and Microtiming]]
