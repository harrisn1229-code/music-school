# Chromatic Mediants and Pop Modulation

**Two ways popular music leaves the key**, and neither is the classical pivot-chord modulation [[Modulation (Key Change)]] describes.

## Chromatic mediants

**A chord whose root is a 3rd away from the tonic, with a quality the key doesn't provide.**

From C major, the diatonic chords a 3rd away are **Em** (iii) and **Am** (vi). Change the quality or chromaticize the root and you get a chromatic mediant:

| Chord | Relationship to C |
|---|---|
| **E major** | iii with a major quality |
| **A major** | vi with a major quality |
| **E♭ major** | ♭III |
| **A♭ major** | ♭VI |

**They share a common tone with the tonic**, which is why they don't sound random — C major and E major share E; C and A♭ share C. One note holds while everything else shifts.

**What they sound like:** a lift, a widening, a scene change. **No dominant pull** — a chromatic mediant doesn't resolve anywhere, it just *arrives*. That's exactly why film and game scoring lean on them so heavily, and why they read as cinematic rather than as a progression.

**Using them:** drop one in where you'd otherwise put a diatonic chord and let it sit. They work best with slow harmonic rhythm and a sparse arrangement — there needs to be room to hear the shift. See [[Loop-Based Harmony]].

Distinguish from [[Borrowed Chords]]: ♭VI and ♭III are *both* borrowed chords and chromatic mediants. The difference is framing — as borrowed chords they're colour from the parallel minor; as chromatic mediants they're a root-motion relationship. Same chord, two lenses.

## The truck driver's modulation

**A direct step up — usually a semitone or a whole tone — for the final chorus, with no preparation.**

Also called the "gear change" or "pump-up" modulation. Everything shifts up, nothing sets it up.

**Why it's a cliché:** it's completely unprepared and it announces itself. There's no pivot, no dominant, no reinterpretation — the song just lurches upward. It reliably raises energy, which is why it was used constantly through the 80s and 90s, and it's now so recognizable that it mostly reads as a gimmick.

**It still works.** "Cliché" means overused, not broken. If the song wants an unsubtle final lift, this is the tool that does it.

## Subtler ways to change key

Three alternatives, in rough order of how much preparation they need:

**1. Shift the mode instead of the key.** Keep the tonic, change the collection — C major to C minor. The key centre never moves, so it's not really a modulation at all, but the colour change is dramatic. See [[Borrowed Chords]] and [[Parallel Minor]].

**2. Approach the new tonic through its own dominant.** Want to get to E♭? Put a B♭7 in front of it. The dominant does the work of announcing the new key, and it takes one chord. This is a secondary dominant used as a hinge — see [[Secondary Dominants]].

**3. Pivot on a shared chord.** The classical method, and the smoothest. Find a chord diatonic to both keys, approach it in the old key, reinterpret it, cadence in the new one. **Am is vi in C and ii in G** — follow it with D7–G and the modulation lands without a seam. See [[Modulation (Key Change)]].

**The relative-key switch** is the fourth option and it's barely a modulation: C major to A minor uses the same seven notes and only changes which one is home. See [[The Axis Progression]] → *Relative major and minor*.

## Choosing

| Want | Use |
|---|---|
| Blunt energy lift, final chorus | Truck driver |
| Colour change, same tonic | Modal shift / borrowed chords |
| Quick, clean move to a new key | Approach through its V |
| Smooth, unnoticed key change | Pivot chord |
| Darkening without a key change | Relative minor switch |
| A lift with no resolution — cinematic | Chromatic mediant |

## From videos

- **A full progression built ENTIRELY from chromatic mediants, plus how a 4th-motion pivot can make an out-of-key chord sound resolved anyway.** "Wuthering Heights" (Kate Bush) verse loop: A→F (major 3rd apart), E→C♯ (minor 3rd apart), and the loop-closing C♯→A is also a third apart — every chord change in the progression is a chromatic mediant, giving it an "uncanny, spooky" film-score quality rather than a pop-normal one. Later the progression adds an out-of-key A♭ chord that still sounds resolved: the preceding chord moves to it by a **perfect 4th**, and 4th/5th root motion is strong/familiar enough to make a non-diatonic chord land as resolved regardless of key membership — the interval relationship overrides key context. → [[Cadences]] (this song's tonic-avoiding pre-chorus) — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Kate Bush's unique chord progression]]

- **Chromatic mediants used to DISSOLVE a key rather than modulate to a new one.** Radiohead's "Sail to the Moon" spends its whole runtime in A major/minor mixture, then on the refrain's final repeat swaps in an E major 7 where an E minor chord had always been, followed by an A♭ major 7 — with the C major chord just before them, three chords related by thirds but not functioning as a progression. Rather than landing in a clear new key (the usual chromatic-mediant use case elsewhere on this page), the song's closing seconds string together more of these unrelated chords as instrumentation peels away, so the tonality floats away rather than resolving anywhere — a "dissolve, don't modulate" use of the same device. → [[Time Signatures &amp; Note Values]] and [[Modal Interchange]] for this song's other complexity layers — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Radiohead's most complex song]]
- **Why a minor-3rd key change reads as SMOOTHER than a semitone modulation despite moving further — the shared-stable-note mechanism, stated precisely.** A key's two most stable notes (tonic + 5th degree) stay CONSONANT in a key a minor 3rd away — C major → E♭ major: C and G both still sound harmonious in E♭ major. Contrast a semitone jump, C major → D♭ major: C and G become dissonant, forcing a full reorientation with essentially zero overlap between the keys — the actual source of a semitone modulation's "stark/brash" character vs. the minor-3rd's smoother lift. Large worked catalog, both directions: up — "Sk8er Boi" (D→F, each chorus), "Here, There and Everywhere" (G→B♭, chorus), "Stacy's Mom" (E→G, from the solo onward), "Duel of the Fates" (Em→Gm), "Summer of '69" (D→F, bridge), "Bad Day" (E♭→G♭, bridge), "Living on a Prayer" (Em→Gm, final chorus, made extra startling by a coinciding one-off 3/4 bar that lands the new key one beat early), "A Whole New World" (D→F); down — "Something" (C→A), "You Are the Sunshine of My Life" (B→G♯, bridge). **A minor-3rd modulation can imply a relative-minor resolution and then swerve past it for extra lift**: "Savior Complex" (Phoebe Bridgers) modulates up a minor 3rd then back down, landing on a chord that sets up a relative-minor resolution the ear expects, then resolves to the brighter parallel major instead. — David Bennett Music Theory, [The 3 Semitone Key Change](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mjFACmJ5ON8)
- **A precise three-part definition — a 3rd from the tonic, chromatic to the key, AND the same quality as the tonic (major/major or minor/minor), which is what guarantees the shared common tone that makes it feel connected rather than random.** Film score: "The Imperial March" (G minor→E♭ minor, common tone B♭; a later switch to E♭ MAJOR instead is the diatonic ♭III, not a chromatic mediant, read as a hint of good left in Vader). Howard Shore's LOTR score uses them extensively (Aragorn/Arwen theme, I→♭VI). Pop with the identical I-♭VI relationship but a lighter emotional read: "It Won't Be Long" (Beatles). "Enjoy the Silence" (Depeche Mode, C minor→E♭ minor). **7th chords "supercharge" the bond** by adding a second common tone: "Light My Fire" (The Doors, Am7→F♯m7). **Version-dependent status**: "Morning Bell" is a true chromatic mediant on *Amnesiac* but NOT on *Kid A*, where a different bass note reframes the same chord as a plain maj7. **Explicit, stated overlap with other labels — not a competition, a set of valid lenses**: "Head Over Heels" (Tears for Fears, A→C) reads equally as chromatic mediant or modal interchange. "Happier Than Ever" (Eilish, C→E) reads as both chromatic mediant AND secondary dominant (E = V/vi, resolving to Am) — Bennett's own tie-break: prefer "secondary dominant" when the chord actually resolves via textbook V/x motion; prefer "chromatic mediant" when it doesn't resolve and just alternates. → [[Secondary Dominants]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use Chromatic Mediant chords]]
- **A chromatic-mediant pair closing out an otherwise one-off progression: B♭ → D♭ in Blur's "Coffee & TV"** — a minor 3rd apart, both major-quality (the textbook chromatic-mediant shape), sharing the common tone F that keeps the ear anchored across the otherwise-foreign root motion; B♭ itself is reached from the preceding chord via a circle-of-fifths fifth-step, not key membership — see [[Circle of Fifths]]. - **Build the voicings first, choose the root/bass second — then move only in 3rd-related roots so no chord in the loop ever reads as V or IV of the next, keeping every chord a self-contained "island."** An original vamp (E♭maj7 – Bmaj7 – Dmaj7 – Cmaj7/E, all major-7, no dominants anywhere) is composed in the reverse of the usual order: interesting-sounding voicings (wide intervals mixed with close ones) are chosen first, and the bass note/root is decided **afterward**, once the shapes already exist — offered as a reusable compositional order, not a one-off description. **Every root relationship in the progression is a 3rd, never a 4th or 5th, and this is stated as the deliberate reason it avoids sounding functional:** "we don't have like a 5-1 or a 1-4... moving in thirds like this is a little bit more vague." Chromatic-mediant relationships specifically: E♭→B is a minor-3rd-down move sourced as the ♭6 borrowed from E♭ minor (the same relationship behind the opening chord of bossa nova standards like "Night and Day" and "Tristeza" — compare this page's own ♭VI entry above); B major substitutes for the "expected" B minor before moving to D major, another 3rd relationship; Cmaj7/E (E in the bass, not the root) closes a third relationship back to E♭. **A concrete non-functional melody-writing device, given as the practical payoff of "island" chords with no dominant pull to lean on:** identify which notes literally *change* between one chord voicing and the next and target those specifically in the melody — worked example, G (of E♭maj7) moving to G♭/F♯ (of Bmaj7). Landing on a note that's genuinely new (absent from the previous chord's scale) is what makes the harmonic change audible without any functional cue to signal it. → [[Modal Jazz]] § *Modal islands*, [[Loop-Based Harmony]], [[Chord Voicings]] — Jens Larsen, [[Jens Larsen - Modern Jazz Harmony - Chord Progressions and Analysis]]
- **Jump a planing progression up a minor 3rd, but move the pedal bass by only a half-step — the mismatch is what makes it bizarre** — from John Williams' "Rebel Fanfare" (Star Wars): a set of major triads planes in parallel over a static bass pedal ([[20th-Century Harmony|planing]]), spelling out a clear tonal progression against that pedal. When the whole triad pattern transposes up a minor 3rd (into a new implied key, still internally tonal-sounding on its own), the bass does NOT follow the same minor-3rd jump — it moves up only a half-step. That mismatch between "chords say minor-3rd key change" and "bass says half-step" is what generates the unsettling color; a matched transposition (moving the bass up the same minor 3rd) would sound conventional and lose the effect. Execution note: pick the exact transposition amount and bass note **by ear/feel case by case**, not by a fixed rule applied mechanically each time. → [[Modulation (Key Change)]] — Max Konyi, [[Star Wars Harmony: 3 Hidden Techniques in John Williams' Music]]
