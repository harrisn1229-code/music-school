# Writing songs using simple sounds with Alex Kapranos

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OUlivFBjOBg)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OUlivFBjOBg)

## Synopsis

A short Mix with the Masters screen-capture piece with Alex Kapranos (Franz Ferdinand) walking through his Ableton Session View as he sketches melodic ideas. It's compact — five and a half minutes — but dense with a genuinely reusable songwriting method: deliberately writing melodies on "wrong"-sounding or unfamiliar instrument voices (his go-to example is horrible-sounding brass samples, though this particular demo uses a Wurlitzer) specifically because it breaks the idiomatic muscle-memory of an instrument you already play, producing lines you'd never otherwise write. Around that headline idea sits a full miniature workflow: start from a simple swung drum beat, jot down several melodic sketches as color-coded clips grouped by song section, delete the rejects and keep the best one or two, launch parts live from a controller (Push or, here, a Novation keyboard doing the same job), then — critically — walk away for anywhere from half an hour to a couple of days before judging any of it, because distance is what actually reveals whether a part works. The clip's closing beat is the best illustration of that last point: a bridge section he'd spent 45 minutes agonizing over gets cut on the spot once he listens back with fresh ears and hears that it's "too long, too involved, really boring" — despite the time already sunk into it.

## Techniques

- **Write melodies on deliberately "wrong"-sounding or unfamiliar instrument voices to escape your own idiomatic playing habits.** His stated favorite trick: really bad/awful-sounding brass samples, especially for vocal-line writing — "when you write melodies with these kind of samples and sounds, you end up coming up with lines that you would never sing, and when you actually sing those melodies they sound usually kind of quite surprising and kind of fun." The mechanism he names directly: a melody played on an instrument you're fluent on (his example is guitar) tends to fall into shapes that "come naturally" under your hands — the instrument's own idiom quietly limits what you write. Switching to a sound you don't have physical fluency on (or one that's simply unpleasant, like the bad brass) removes that constraint, and "that's kind of what's cool about just jumping between different sounds, you end up writing different melodies." → [[Melody Writing]], [[Sound Selection Guide]] *(general starting point on which instruments qualify as "wrong" — his named example is bad brass samples specifically; any voice you don't play fluently should work by the same logic)*
- **This particular demo used a Wurlitzer instead of the brass trick, and still got a melody he wouldn't play on guitar** — "I just went for a Wurlitzer, I've always loved Wurlitzers... I had a Wurlitzer for years." *(Transcript confidence note: the caption track garbles this passage — "wireless"/"whirl" in the raw captions — reconstructed here as "Wurlitzer" based on context; treat as medium confidence, not a verbatim quote.)* → [[Vintage Gear Worklist]]
- **Start from a simple, swung drum beat before writing any melody** — programs "a very very simple beat" first (visible as small blocky clips in Session View), probably with a light swing, as the rhythmic bed the melodic sketches get written against. Referenced Booker T & the M.G.'s "Green Onions" (swung a bit) as a groove touchstone while sketching. → [[Melody Writing]], [[Rhythm and Groove]]
- **Sketch multiple melodic ideas per song section as color-coded Session View clips, then delete the rejects and keep only the best one or two.** Workflow: jot several quick melodic sketches for a section, color-code every clip belonging to that section the same color, then move on to the next section/file once it's settled. Normally deletes rejected sketches as he goes rather than letting them accumulate — in this specific demo two sketches from an otherwise-discarded batch happened to survive because he liked the melody in them. → [[Session View Workflow]]
- **Launch written parts live from a controller (Push, or here a Novation keyboard doing the same job) rather than clicking clips with the mouse** — "I guess I'd quite often use Push to do this, I'm using this keyboard which does exactly the same sort of thing... same principle, you can launch the parts that you've written from that." Treated as interchangeable hardware for the same Session View launch function. → [[Session View Workflow]], [[Live Performance Setup]]
- **Write melodic sketches for every section first, then deliberately walk away before judging any of them — anywhere from half an hour to a couple of days.** States this as his standard process, not a one-off: write melodies section by section, then step away and come back later to listen with fresh ears rather than evaluating in the moment while still close to the material. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **A part that took 45 minutes to write got cut on the spot, once distance revealed it wasn't working — regardless of the time already invested in it.** Listening back to a bridge section he remembers spending "ages on it... 45 minutes or something coming up just that part," written specifically to bridge into what would become the chorus: on relisten it "doesn't work" — "too long," "too involved," "really boring." His own framing of why this matters: "that's kind of why it's important to take time and distance from things, because you realize that that doesn't work" — the point isn't that the part was bad when written, it's that only the returning-with-distance step actually surfaces that. Sunk time spent on a part is not a reason to keep it. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
