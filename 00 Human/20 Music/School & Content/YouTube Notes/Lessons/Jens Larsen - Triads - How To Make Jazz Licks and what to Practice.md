# Triads - How To Make Jazz Licks and what to Practice

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AxVF86KmLAk)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AxVF86KmLAk)

## Synopsis

Jazz guitarist Jens Larsen makes the case that triads are one of the strongest melodic building blocks in jazz vocabulary — he name-checks Wes Montgomery, Kurt Rosenwinkel, and Lage Lund as players who all lean on them — and spends the video demonstrating four distinct ways to vary a triad melodically rather than just running it straight: **superimposing diatonic triads onto a chord's extensions**, **playing triads from a different inversion** (starting on the 3rd or 5th instead of the root), **reordering the notes into a pattern** (3-1-5 instead of 1-3-5), and **spreading a close-position triad into a wide open voicing**. Every device is worked over the same **Dm7-G7(alt)-Cmaj7** ii-V-I in C, so the four techniques stack cleanly for comparison, and each one gets a "practice it through the scale" close so it becomes a reflex rather than a memorized lick. The most valuable through-line is the framing itself: a triad isn't just a static arpeggio shape, it's raw melodic material with at least four independent knobs (which triad, which inversion, what note order, how spread) that can all be turned on the same three notes — and a nice bonus technique tucked into the 2nd-inversion example is **borrowing a passing triad from the parallel harmonic minor on a dominant chord** (an F minor triad from C harmonic minor, en route to G7 in a C major context) for extra color. This is a pure instrument/theory lesson — no gear, plugins, or production content.

## What's taught

**Framing (~0:00-1:30):** Every scale/arpeggio exercise should be treated as a melodic building block you actually intend to use while soloing, not an abstract drill — it's not enough to hit the right notes over each chord, the notes also need to form a strong melody. Triads specifically are singled out as one of the strongest such building blocks, used by a wide range of jazz guitarists (Wes Montgomery, Kurt Rosenwinkel, Lage Lund cited by name). The video covers triad ideas, inversions, patterns, and spread voicings, delivered via a worked ii-V-I lick plus scale-exercise versions of each device.

**Why practice arpeggios diatonically (~1:30-2:30):** An arpeggio played over a chord is really one layer of a two-layer system — the arpeggio (chord tones) plus the rest of the parent scale (the notes available around it). Practicing arpeggios *inside their scale* builds the connection between the local chord-tone layer and the larger scalar context, which matters because the rest of an improvised line, once you leave the arpeggio, is usually built from scale notes.

**1. Diatonic triad superimposition (~2:30-5:30):** Worked over Dm7-G7-Cmaj7 — a chromatic enclosure targets F on beat 3 of the Dm7 bar, then an **F major triad** (the 3rd-5th-7th of Dm7, "top three notes" of the chord) is played as an open-structured melodic unit; on G7, a **G major triad** (root position) plus a short D minor pentatonic fragment; on Cmaj7, three back-to-back diatonic triads — **E minor** (3rd-5th-7th), **A minor** (6th/13th-root-3rd), **G major** (5th-7th-9th) — each one a clean, nameable chord-tone read. Two practice methods given: (a) run every diatonic triad of a major scale, root position, straight up the scale; (b) work them along the neck by string set instead, starting from whichever diatonic triad shape sits physically first on that string set. Either way, the practice payoff is knowing **which triad sits where** relative to any given chord (all seven diatonic triads of a major scale: I major, ii minor, iii minor, IV major, v minor, vi minor, vii diminished) and what scale degrees each one covers against a target chord.

**2. Triad inversions (~5:30-9:30):** An inversion moves the triad's lowest note up an octave — root position C-E-G → 1st inversion E-G-C → 2nd inversion G-C-E. **1st-inversion worked lick:** D minor triad 1st inv (F-A-D) on Dm7 → A minor triad (C-E-A, reading as the 7th/9th/5th against Dm7) → on G7alt, a B augmented triad first (noted as *not* a true inversion since augmented triads are symmetrical — inverting one just produces another augmented triad) → a D♭ major triad 1st inv (F-A♭-D♭, reading as the 7th/♭9th/♭5th of G7alt) → resolves to Cmaj7 via a C major triad 1st inv (E-G-C) then an A minor triad 1st inv (A-E-C). The hardest part, named directly: you have to start thinking of the triad **from the 3rd**, not the root — the fix is drilling a full scale of 1st-inversion triads until naming the starting note (not the root) becomes automatic; it's explicitly fine to learn the melodic shape by ear first and backfill the note names after. **2nd-inversion worked lick:** starts on the 5th, up to root, up to 3rd. Uses diatonic passing chords — D minor 2nd inv → E minor 2nd inv → **F minor 2nd inv** (deliberately **borrowed from C harmonic minor**, treating the D7 in that spot as if it were built on C harmonic minor rather than plain C major) → G major → resolves with a short scale melody to the 3rd of Cmaj7. Explicitly called "a nice trick" — using material from the parallel minor key on the dominant to get more variation into lines, and the rhythm is deliberately shifted so the F minor arrives early, inside the D minor bar, blurring the bar line a little.

**3. Triad patterns — reordering the notes (~9:30-12:00):** Beyond which note is lowest (inversion), the *order* the notes are played in is its own variable. Straight is 1-3-5; the pattern demonstrated is **3-1-5** — start on the 3rd, drop to the root, leap up to the 5th — which introduces a wide 5th-interval leap mid-phrase that breaks up the line. Worked example: on Dm7, an **F major triad played 3-1-5** (A down to F up to C), then a D sus4 triad (mentioned, not elaborated), then a small diatonic third move (E to G); on a D7 altered (borrowed dominant context), an **F diminished triad 3-1-5**, then a **G diminished triad 3-1-5**, up to E♭ down to B, resolving to the 9th of C; then an **A minor triad 3-1-5** closing the phrase. Same practice close as the other devices: run the 3-1-5 pattern through a full scale of diatonic triads, ascending, until it's a reflex.

**4. Spread (open-voiced) triads (~12:00-16:00):** Construction: take a close-position triad (C-E-G) and move the *middle* note (E) up an octave, spreading the three notes across a much wider interval span — works as both a chord voicing and a strong arpeggio shape because the wide leaps are still fully consonant. Worked example: **D minor 1st inversion spread** (F, D, up to A) on Dm7, followed by a descending scale run — explicitly resolving a phrase that opened with big ascending leaps by moving stepwise in the *opposite* direction, a general shape-balancing principle. On G7, an **E♭ major 1st-inversion spread triad** (reading as ♭13-root-♯9, altered-dominant color tones) — E♭ down to G, back up to E♭, then B♭ — followed by an unelaborated **"A♭ minor Coltrane pattern"** reference, resolving to D on Cmaj7. Closes with a **G major 1st-inversion open-voiced spread triad** on Cmaj7 — down to B, up to G, then D, ending on the 3rd (E). Practical notes: spread triads are harder to play than close-position ones but make genuinely good **right-hand technique exercises** because of the repeated non-adjacent string skips; practice them in isolation through inversions first, then through a full scale, same pattern as the other three devices — and treat the string-skipping itself as a separate right-hand coordination drill, independent of the harmonic content.
