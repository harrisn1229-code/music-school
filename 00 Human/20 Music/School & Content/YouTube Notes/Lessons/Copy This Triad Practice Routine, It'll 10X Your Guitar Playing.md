# Copy This Triad Practice Routine, It'll 10X Your Guitar Playing

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rtKZBns_IMY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rtKZBns_IMY)

## Synopsis
Daniel Seriff's guitar lesson on connecting diatonic triads with minimal hand movement, using close-position triad shapes on the three thinnest strings (G, B, E). The core idea: any major key's seven diatonic triads (I–vii°) can each be found, in a *single* consistent inversion, within one five-fret span — so instead of jumping around the neck chord to chord, the whole key lives under the hand in one spot, and switching inversions just relocates that spot. He walks through the three basic triad shapes (root position, 1st inversion, 2nd inversion) built off major/minor/diminished in the key of G, shows the diatonic triad-quality formula (I ii iii IV V vi vii° = major minor minor major major minor diminished), then plays the full G-major diatonic set through three separate 5-fret positions — one per inversion type — framing consistent-inversion movement through a scale as the practical form of **voice leading** on guitar. Closes with a short section on making the bare triads sound more musical: adding a scale tone above or below the shape and toggling to it (with hammer-ons/slides), and "hearing ahead" to the next chord's notes while sitting on the current one. Video is a promo funnel for his paid course (Solo on Guitar: Fretboard Freedom) but the lesson content itself is self-contained and concrete.

## What's taught
- **Three essential closed-voicing triad shapes on strings G-B-E, root position** — demonstrated in G major at the top of the neck: **G major** = frets 12-12-10 (G string-B string-E string) = G, B, D, root lowest. **G minor** = 12-11-10 (lower the 3rd, on the B string) = G, B♭, D. **G diminished** = 12-11-9 (lower both the 3rd and the 5th, the 5th on the E string) = G, B♭, D♭. Root position always has the root as the lowest-pitched note of the three.
- **1st inversion shape** — third is the lowest note; demonstrated down around the 3rd fret. Exact frets weren't clearly captured in the captions (the spoken numbers land in a "[music]" gap where he's playing rather than narrating) — qualitatively it's the same major→minor→diminished move as root position (lower the 3rd for minor, then also the 5th for diminished), just with a different note on the bottom.
- **2nd inversion shape** — fifth is the lowest note; visually described as "looks like a D chord just moved up." Relative degree pattern 5-1-3 (major) → 5-1-♭3 (minor, "the saddest of all chords... like Spinal Tap said") → lower again for diminished.
- **Diatonic triad-quality formula (any major key)** — I major, ii minor, iii minor, IV major, V major, vi minor, vii° diminished, back to I major. Stated as a fixed pattern that holds in every major key, demonstrated in G: G, Am, Bm, C, D, Em, F#dim, G. The vii° (F#dim in G) is flagged as rare in popular music but worth knowing.
- **The 5-fret voice-leading trick — play the whole diatonic set in ONE inversion, in ONE five-fret position, instead of shifting shapes chord to chord.** This is the video's central technique: normal triad practice shifts *which shape* you use as you move up the neck, which breaks the sound up; picking a single inversion and staying in its 5-fret zone for the whole diatonic run keeps the hand still and the voicing consistent, which is what actually produces smooth voice leading on guitar (see [[Chord Inversions]]'s existing "pick the inversion for the smoothest bass/voice motion" principle — this is the guitar-position version of that idea, applied across a full scale rather than one chord change).
  - **1st-inversion position, frets 1-5 (lowest zone):** G, Am, [B — substituted with a nearby "wonderful shape" rather than the literal B-minor 1st-inversion grip, per Daniel, to stay in the 5-fret window], C, D, Em, F#dim, back to G. Exact frets not clearly captured in captions.
  - **2nd-inversion position, frets ~5-9 (middle zone):** G major (same familiar shape moved up) → A minor → **B minor = 7-7-7, one finger barred straight across** → **C major = frets 9-8-8** (strings G-B-E) → **D major = frets 7-7-5** → E minor → F#dim → back to G. Called out as staying "so far out of the other instruments' way" — useful specifically when a bass player, keys player, or another guitarist is already covering the low/mid register; these high, thin triads sit as "icing on the cake" rather than competing for the same space.
  - **Root-position, frets ~8-12 (highest zone):** back to the 12-12-10-family shape used in the opening demo, walked through the same G-Am-Bm-C-D-Em-F#dim-G sequence up near the top of the neck. Description: "this nice little stretchy F#dim" — the biggest stretch of the three positions.
- **Practice routine as demonstrated:** each chord gets a full 4 beats before transitioning to the next (i.e. one bar per chord in 4/4), played with a loose "funky" strumming pattern over a backing track (download link in his description), working through the diatonic sequence chord-to-chord until the changes are smooth back-to-back — get each shape solid individually first, then link them at tempo.
- **Arpeggiating instead of strumming** — plays the same 5-fret diatonic sets as broken arpeggios rather than strummed chords, notes it works especially well with a little delay on the signal.
- **Embellishment 1 — add a scale tone above/below the triad and toggle to it.** From any of the three triad shapes, grab one extra note that's diatonic to the key just above or below the shape and switch back and forth between the plain triad and triad+note. Worked on the G major shape: an A on the high E string, an E on the B string, or a C on the G string — all valid "toggle" targets, described as each having a different character worth trying by ear rather than one correct answer.
- **Embellishment 2 — hammer-ons and slides into the added tone**, rather than picking it separately, to make the toggle feel like an ornament instead of a second chord.
- **Embellishment 3 — "hear ahead" by visualizing the next chord's notes while still on the current one** (a melodic-anticipation/lead-in move): on the G chord, think ahead to A minor's notes; on B minor, think ahead to C; on D, think ahead to E minor. On the trickier F#dim he says he mostly just "hangs on it" rather than anticipating forward, calling the shape "a little wacky."
