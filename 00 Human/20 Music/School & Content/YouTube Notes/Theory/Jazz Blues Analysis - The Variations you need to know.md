# Jazz Blues Analysis - The Variations you need to know

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=txuIdbHe8XU)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=txuIdbHe8XU)

## Synopsis

Jens Larsen's analytical walkthrough of the standard jazz-blues 12-bar form in F, aimed at giving a soloist/comper a map of where the harmonically "safe/generic" spots are versus where the real decisions happen. Rather than teaching one fixed set of changes, Larsen breaks the form into three four-bar phrases (tonic / subdominant / turnaround-dominant), points out that the cadences at bar 4 and bars 9–10 are just ordinary dominant cadences that take the same substitution/alteration options as any V7→I anywhere else (so not worth over-thinking), and then spends most of the video on the one spot that genuinely varies between tunes — bar 5–6, the return from the IV chord back to the tonic — walking through the three real options (sharp-4 diminished, iv-minor, and a chromatic parallel-ii-V chain) with the scale choice and named tune examples for each. He explicitly frames why every chord in a blues gets treated as a dominant 7th (a historical shift from major/6th-chord tonics in earlier blues to all-dominant harmony since swing/bebop), which is the conceptual key to why blues harmony tolerates otherwise-"wrong" ♭7s everywhere. He deliberately excludes "power blues" (Bird blues backcycling) as a separate topic for another video. Pure harmonic-analysis content — no plugin, mixing, or sound-selection material.

## What's taught

- **The three-four-bar-phrase reading of a 12-bar (jazz) blues** — instead of memorizing 12 individual bars, split the form into three four-bar chunks and name each chunk's function: **bars 1–4 = tonic**, **bars 5–8 = subdominant then back to tonic**, **bars 9–12 = the ii–V–I (functioning more like a dominant section) plus the turnaround**. This is the organizing frame the rest of the video hangs off. → [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *The 12-bar form*
- **Why every chord in a blues is treated as a dominant 7th, stated as a deliberate analytical choice, not an accident.** Larsen analyzes the whole form "as if F7 is actually a tonic chord," which "seems to make no sense" on paper but is exactly the blues convention — treating I, IV, and V all as dominant 7ths makes the harmony *feel* a little atonal, since none of the ♭7s are pulling toward resolution the way they would in functional major-key harmony. *(general framing, no timestamp in transcript)* → [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *Why every chord is a dominant 7th*
- **Historical framing for the all-dominant convention:** earlier blues (cites early Charlie Parker solos as an example) didn't clearly outline a dominant chord until deep into the form — early blues tended to be more tonal, with the I chord voiced as a plain major or major-6th chord rather than a dominant 7th for the whole progression. The shift to treating every chord as dominant is dated to **the swing/bebop era** onward. This is a piece of harmonic history the vault's existing blues page doesn't carry yet. → [[Blues Form and Harmony]]
- **Bar 4's cadence to the IV chord takes the same options as any generic dominant cadence, and Larsen explicitly says not to over-think it:** play it plain (F7 straight to B♭7); use a **complete secondary ii–V into the IV** (Cm7–F7–B♭7, i.e. interpolating the IV's own ii in front of it — a direct application of [[Chord Substitution]] § 4); alter the dominant (F7alt); **tritone-substitute** it; or use a **full tritone ii–V**. All of these are named as interchangeable — "not too much happening" that differs from any other dominant cadence — and equally usable either comped as written or inserted freely into a solo line regardless of what the rhythm section is actually playing. → [[Chord Substitution]] § *Tritone substitution*, § 4 *Interpolating a ii*
- **The cadence back to I at bars 9–10 (Gm7–C7–F) takes the identical set of options** — the V chord (C7) can be altered or tritone-subbed the same way as the bar-4 cadence; explicitly stated as not carrying much special weight of its own, since Larsen already has other videos dedicated to dominant substitution in general. → [[Chord Substitution]], [[Secondary Dominants]]
- **"The place where the most things are happening" is bar 5–6 — the return from the IV chord (B♭7, bar 5) back to the tonic (bar 6)** — Larsen frames this as the one spot in the form that genuinely distinguishes one jazz-blues tune's changes from another's, and works through three named options for it:

  1. **Sharp-4 diminished (the most common of the three).** Bar 6 becomes a diminished chord built on the raised 4th degree (**B°7** in the key of F), resolving into F7 — "strictly speaking" the target is often voiced as F7 with C in the bass (F7/C), though Larsen notes soloists/compers don't need to think that precisely; treating it simply as "B diminished going back to F7" is enough. **Scale choice:** over the B♭7 in bar 5, play straight **mixolydian** (spelled here as the **E♭ major scale over B♭7**); for the B°7 in bar 6, take that same E♭-major-scale material and **raise B♭ to B natural**, which produces **C harmonic minor** — the "easier" way in is to think of the B° resolving to a **C minor triad**, since F7's own upper structure contains C-Eb-G-adjacent tones that the C-minor-triad notes tag cleanly against. Named as an especially good spot to get comfortable with the sharp-4-diminished sound generally, because the form gives you more time on it than a typical mid-standard occurrence. **Tunes that use this exact device:** *Tenor Madness*, *Blue Monk*, *Straight, No Chaser* (auto-caption spelled "tena Madness," corrected here — high confidence given the context). → [[Diminished Chords]] § *sharp-4 diminished*, [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *The variants worth knowing*
  2. **iv-minor.** Instead of the sharp-4 diminished, bar 6 goes to a **minor iv** (in F: B♭m7, sometimes voiced B♭m6, occasionally with a following E♭7 making it a small ii–V into the tonic) before resolving to F7 — functionally still just "a iv-minor chord in a major key" even though the tonic itself is dominant. **Scale choice:** **A♭ major** over B♭m7–E♭7; if isolating just the E♭7, an alternative is **Lydian dominant off A♭ melodic minor** — noted as "only one note apart" from the plain A♭-major reading, so not a huge practical difference. Named as a common device in **Coltrane's blues themes** and **a lot of Charlie Parker blues themes** — described directly as "part of the Parker blues progression." Two specific tune references are given but the auto-captions garble both tune titles beyond confident recovery (rendered as "all enrolments turnaround" and "chichi from Parker... has a four minor" in the raw captions) — **not reproduced here as fact; flagged rather than guessed**, per the workflow's transcript-grounded-only rule. → [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *Minor blues*, [[Chord Substitution]]
  3. **Chromatic parallel ii–V chain.** Once bar 6 is already a minor iv (B♭m7), it can be extended into a full **descending chain of minor ii–Vs a half step apart**: B♭m7–E♭7 → Am7–D7 → A♭m7–D♭7 → (resolving into the bars-9-10 Gm7–C7) — Larsen plays a full 12-bar minor blues built this way. Explicitly framed as **shared vocabulary with "Blues for [Alice]" or "Freight [Trane]"-style pocket-blues themes** (both tune names are also caption-garbled — "blues for Ellis" / "freight train" in the raw transcript; "Blues for Alice" already has a documented home on [[Reharmonization]] as the Level-2 minor-ii-V blues reharm, which this video's chain closely matches, so that identification carries reasonable confidence even though the caption text itself is unreliable) — useful as **soloing vocabulary you can lay over a plain blues backing** even when the band isn't actually playing the chromatic chain, without necessarily needing to treat every step as a literal complete ii–V (which can read as melodically clunky) — sometimes it's more natural to just think "minor chord" and let the V implication stay loose. Also name-checks **Chick Corea** as a soloist who leans on this same parallel-minor movement in (an unidentified, caption-garbled tune — not reproduced as fact). → [[Reharmonization]] § *A worked, stackable-levels teaching template* (the closest existing worked match), [[Chord Substitution]], [[Secondary Dominants]]

- **Bars 9–10's plain ii–V (Gm7–C7) sometimes gets a passing chord inserted between the ii and V** — "every now and again" a variation plays Gm7–B♭7–C7 instead of the plain Gm7–C7, though Larsen notes most versions leave it as the plain two-chord cadence. → [[Blues Form and Harmony]], [[Passing Chords]]
- **The turnaround (bars 11–12) is most likely some flavor of 1-6-2-5 or 3-6-2-5** — stated as the default expectation rather than demonstrated chord-by-chord in this video; Larsen points to his own separate turnarounds video for the fuller catalog of options (matches the vault's existing [[Turnarounds]] page, which already documents the VI7 / 1-6-2-5 family in depth). → [[Turnarounds]]
- **What's deliberately left out, and why:** "power blues" (Bird-blues-style backcycling) is explicitly excluded as its own separate topic, described as functioning differently enough from the rest of this form's variations that it "stands alone" and isn't really used as a reharmonization of a plain blues — Larsen flags it as something he'd cover in a dedicated video on request, and name-drops **Coltrane's** more radical blues reharmonizations, plus **West Coast Blues** and **Blue Set** as tunes that partially draw on power-blues backcycling, as further-viewing pointers rather than worked examples. No new note created for this — the vault already covers Bird-blues-family reharmonization on [[Reharmonization]] and [[Blues Form and Harmony]], and this video doesn't add worked chord-by-chord content beyond the name-drop. → [[Reharmonization]]
