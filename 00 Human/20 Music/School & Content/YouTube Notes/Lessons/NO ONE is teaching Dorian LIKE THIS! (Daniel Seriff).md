# NO ONE is teaching Dorian LIKE THIS! (Daniel Seriff)

## Synopsis
A guitar-specific angle on the Dorian mode from Daniel Seriff: rather than teaching Dorian as a new seven-note scale shape to memorize from scratch, the whole lesson is built on the premise that most players already own the hard part — the minor pentatonic box — and Dorian is just that box plus two notes. Worked entirely in the key of D minor (the classic 10th-fret box), the lesson first adds the notes one at a time onto the familiar pentatonic shape, explicitly naming the optional "hexatonic" stopping point along the way (pentatonic + the 2, without going all the way to the full mode), then re-teaches the same two added notes inside a second, movable "diagonal pentatonic" fingering that Seriff frames as his preferred practical system because it copy-pastes to any root/string-set on the neck. The most useful theory nugget is a direct, ear-first A/B: Dorian and Aeolian (natural minor) are separated by exactly one note (the 6th, natural vs. flat), demonstrated back-to-back so the character difference is heard, not just read off a formula table — this matches [[Diatonic Modes]]'s "one distinguishing degree" framing exactly but earns its own citation here for the concrete before/after audio demo. A sponsor read for Seriff's own paid course sits at the midpoint (11:49 total, ~1:20 of it ad); everything either side is genuinely actionable fretboard content, not just a theory table restated on guitar strings.

## What's taught
- **Start from the minor pentatonic box you already know, in the key of D (10th-fret box)** — D minor pentatonic notes are D (root/1), F (♭3), G (4), A (5), C (♭7), repeating D-F-G-A-C-D-F up the neck; only five notes, no 2 and no 6 — this is the base shape everything else gets added onto. (~1:30–2:30)
- **Don't dodge the pentatonic scale — it's not the problem.** Explicit reassurance/reframe before adding anything: if pentatonic soloing isn't sounding good, that's a decoration/groove-lock problem, not a scale-choice problem — spend real time making the plain pentatonic sound melodic first, since that foundation carries into every mode built on top of it. (~2:45–3:15)
- **Add the 2 (E natural) at three specific spots around the D minor pentatonic box: 12th fret low E string, 12th fret high E string, and 9th fret G string.** The 2 is called out as an easy note to feature — "you can land on it almost any time and it's going to sound great." → **[[LEAD Slot]]** soloing vocabulary. (~4:50–5:40)
- **Pentatonic + the 2 alone is a usable stopping point — the "hexatonic" scale** — named players who lean on exactly this six-note version rather than pushing to the full seven-note mode: **Derek Trucks** and **Jimmy Herring**. Explicitly framed as permission to use partial extensions, not just five-note-or-seven-note. (~5:40–6:00)
- **Add the note that actually defines Dorian: the major 6 (B natural), at the 12th fret of the B string and the 9th fret of the D string.** Framed as "the sound of Dorian" and "what separates Dorian from other minor scales" — the single highest-priority note to target once you're past plain pentatonic soloing. Demoed live over a D minor 7 → G7 backing track at ~80 BPM (funky feel). (~6:00–7:00)
- **Practice/phrasing habit while soloing: don't reach for the modal color notes (the 2, the 6) right away — center on the plain D minor pentatonic first and save the added notes for later in the improvisation**, so they land as deliberate color choices rather than getting buried in the first few bars. (~7:00–7:20)
- **Dorian vs. Aeolian (natural minor) is a one-note difference, demonstrated by direct A/B**: Dorian has the natural 6 (B), Aeolian has the flat 6 (B♭) — everything else identical — played back to back so the "much sadder" character of the flat 6 is heard directly rather than just described. Exact match for [[Diatonic Modes]]'s "one distinguishing degree" framing (Dorian = minor + raised 6th) but worth citing here for the concrete audio A/B. (~7:20–8:10)
- **A movable "diagonal pentatonic" fingering, framed as the practical alternative to box shapes because it copy-pastes anywhere on the neck** — worked a second time in D minor, this time starting high on the two thinnest strings (B and high E) rather than the low-string box: root D on the B string, index finger one whole step below for a "two-note set" (♭7 then root), then a three-note set on the high E string (♭3, 4, 5) completing the 5-note diagonal minor pentatonic across just two strings. Full reusable fingering + the same two Dorian notes added onto it → written up separately as its own technique note since it's a distinct, reusable fretboard system, not just a restatement of the box shape. → [[Dorian from the Minor Pentatonic Box]] (~9:00–11:20)
- **Same "add the 6, then the 2" sequence applied to the diagonal shape, with exact fret relationships stated relative to the existing notes rather than absolute positions** — the 6 sits one fret below the ♭7 on the same string (so it's found *relationally*, not memorized as a new absolute location), and the 2 stacks onto the high E string alongside the existing three-note pentatonic set. Same "copy-paste to any root" claim applies to the completed Dorian shape, not just the pentatonic base. → [[Dorian from the Minor Pentatonic Box]] (~9:45–11:00)
- **Song references for where Dorian shows up in real records** (framed as "you already know this sound" ear-training context, not analyzed further): Mary Jane's Last Dance (Tom Petty), Oye Como Va (Santana — called out as "the King of Dorian"), Breathe (Pink Floyd), Wicked Game (Chris Isaak), Whipping Post (Allman Brothers), Chameleon (Herbie Hancock), Lucky (Daft Punk) — plus the general rule that **any one-chord minor vamp is a Dorian candidate by default**. (~0:20–0:50)
