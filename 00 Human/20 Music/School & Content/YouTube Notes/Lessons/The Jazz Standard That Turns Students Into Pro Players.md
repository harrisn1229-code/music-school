# The Jazz Standard That Turns Students Into Pro Players

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rWY3y4TChUc)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rWY3y4TChUc)

## Synopsis

Nathan Borton frames Jerome Kern's "Nobody Else But Me" as a single, unusually dense practice vehicle — one standard that happens to contain seven distinct, commonly-recurring jazz harmonic devices, so drilling this one tune functions as a compressed survey of most of what a student will run into across the wider standards repertoire. He comps through the tune once for ear reference, then walks each device in turn with a guitar voicing/lick example: 5–1 motion into multiple key centers, a "dominant two chord" (the ii chord made dominant instead of minor, treated as a static lydian-dominant/tonic-dominant color rather than a functioning V), passing diminished chords (two solving methods — reading as a rootless dominant, or as triads pulled from the diminished scale when the chord resolves downward), a backdoor dominant (♭VII7→I, treated with the same lydian-dominant/tonic-dominant sound as the dominant-two chord), a chord walkup progression at the tune's end (offered three ways: play the literal changes, substitute a long ii–V across the whole passage, or ignore the chords entirely and outline the key center), the Tadd Dameron turnaround (a dense chromatic-root turnaround simplified two ways — C minor pentatonic over the whole thing, or the "Coltrane pattern" of 1-2-3-5 chord tones per chord), and non-functional changes (a passage that exists purely because Kern is reharmonizing the melody, not moving through real function — solved either by targeting the melody note directly or by treating each chord as its own local sound/scale). Most valuable parts: the two-method passing-diminished-chord split (dominant read vs. triad-from-diminished-scale read, chosen by which way the chord resolves), and the three-tier "how much do you actually need to track" approach to the walkup and Tadd Dameron sections, which generalizes well beyond this one tune.

## What's taught

**1. Five-to-ones in multiple keys** *(~1:15)*
- Every key's V chord wants to pull to that key's I — the device that "solidifies the sound of a key." A G7 makes you want to hear Cmaj7 land right after it.
- "Nobody Else But Me" signals a key change with a 5–1 progression — measures 6–9 carry **two different 5–1's, one in B and one in E**.
- **How to play it:** reuse your existing 5–1 vocabulary/licks, just transposed into whatever the new relative key is — recognizing the 5–1 shape is what makes tracking multiple key centers "not a problem." → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Modulation (Key Change)]]

**2. Dominant two chords** *(~2:10)*
- A common jazz device: the **ii chord of the key is dominant instead of the expected minor quality**. In this tune the key center is C, so the "dominant two chord" is **D7**.
- This chord **does not function like a normal V–I** — it doesn't want to resolve to I, it just sits there as its own stable color. Borton flags that intermediate players commonly mis-hear/alter it toward a 5–1 sound, which is a mistake — "it's really this sound."
- Named directly: **lydian dominant / tonic dominant sound** — carries the **9, 13, and ♯11**.
- **Scale for lines:** the **melodic minor scale built off the 5th of the dominant chord** (for D7, that's A melodic minor).
- Voicings: several extra grip options given on-screen for this chord quality (not individually transcribable from audio-only captions — see the video's own linked Guitar Pro PDF for exact grips). → [[Modal Jazz]] § *Tonic dominant*, [[Altered Chords]]

**3. Passing diminished chords** *(~4:00)*
- Occurs at the end of "Nobody Else But Me": a run of diminished chords bridging two stronger chords.
- **Two solving methods, chosen by the diminished chord's own root motion:**
  1. **Read it as a rootless dominant a half step below its target.** Any diminished chord sitting a half step below a major or minor chord functions as the **dominant of the chord it's resolving to**. Worked example: **C°7 → D7** — because C° sits a half step below D, treat it as A7 (C°7 shares three notes with A7♭9) and play straight 5–1 vocabulary over "A7 → Dm."
  2. **Read it as triads out of the diminished scale, when the diminished chord resolves DOWNWARD.** Worked example: **E♭° → D** — because this diminished chord moves down rather than up a half step, the dominant-substitution read doesn't apply; instead play the triads embedded in the diminished scale. A single guitar voicing conveniently stacks an **F triad over the E♭ diminished chord**; that F-triad shape **transposes in minor 3rds** to generate every usable triad over the same diminished chord (symmetrical structure — matches [[Diminished Chords]]'s "repeats every 3 frets/keys" property).
- → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Passing Chords]]

**4. Backdoor dominants (backdoor 5–1's)** *(~6:30)*
- Measure 26: **B♭7 → Cmaj7** — a backdoor dominant resolving to the major I, "another way to get back to the one... in a less predictable fashion than just going 5 to 1."
- **Definition given directly:** a backdoor dominant is a dominant chord **built off the flat-7 of whatever key you're in**.
- **How to treat it:** exactly like a dominant-two chord (see § 2) — the same **lydian dominant / tonic dominant sound**, not a functioning V. In this tune's key, that means reusing the **same grips/lines already built for D7, now moved over B♭7** (and its related minor-side target treated as F minor).
- → [[Turnarounds]] § *Backdoor* (matches this page's `Cmaj7 Am7 Fm7 B♭7` row transposed), [[Modal Jazz]] § *Tonic dominant*

**5. Chord walkup progressions** *(~7:40)*
- The tune's **last three bars** walk from the ii chord up to the V chord instead of the usual static ii–V — described as overwhelming to players who are "so used to playing over 251 progressions" that anything else throws them.
- **Three explicit levels of how much to actually track, cheapest to most literal:**
  1. **Ignore the walkup chords entirely and outline the key center** — play jazz language that spells out the tune's tonic (Cmaj7) straight through the passage.
  2. **Substitute one long ii–V across the whole walkup** — collapse the moving chords into a single 2–5 shape and play that instead of chasing each chord.
  3. **Play the literal walkup chords as written**, chord by chord, for players who want the full harmonic detail.
- → [[Chord Progressions]] (matches this page's "break a long progression into small chunks, or collapse it" chunking principle)

**6. Tadd Dameron turnaround** *(~9:30)*
- The second-to-last device: at the end of the tune, instead of a plain turnaround, "Nobody Else But Me" uses the **Tadd Dameron turnaround** — a denser, more chromatic chain of chords that "can be a bit overwhelming" on first encounter.
- **Two simplification methods given, either usable over the whole progression:**
  1. **C minor pentatonic straight through every chord of the turnaround** — stated directly to just work, with no per-chord scale-matching needed.
  2. **The "Coltrane pattern"** (Borton's own term for it, credited as common usage — "what is called the Coltrane pattern by many people"): play the **1-2-3-5 chord tones** of each individual chord in the turnaround, in sequence, chord by chord.
- No existing home for this specific named turnaround in the vault — added as a new subsection on [[Turnarounds]] rather than a standalone note (small, single-device topic that belongs with the rest of the turnaround catalog). → [[Turnarounds]] § *Tadd Dameron turnaround*

**7. Non-functional changes** *(~11:30)*
- Measures 9–12: a passage that isn't moving through real harmonic function at all — it exists purely because **Jerome Kern is reharmonizing the melody**, not because the chords are pulling toward a target.
- Concrete mechanism given: the melody note itself becomes **the top of each new chord** — e.g. a D♭ chord appears because the melody note is now voiced as the top of D♭, then an E♭ chord appears right after because the *same held/repeating melody idea* is now voiced as the top of E♭.
- **Two solving approaches for soloing over it, explicitly offered as alternatives:**
  1. **Play the melody itself** — the simplest option, since the chords only exist to harmonize it.
  2. **Treat each chord as its own local sound.** Over the D♭ chord, reuse the same major-key language already built for C major (§ this video's opening comping example), just transposed to D♭ (since D♭ is also functioning as a plain major sound in this spot). Over the E♭7, treat it as a **tonic dominant** and play either the **E♭ lydian dominant sound** or **B♭ melodic minor** (E♭7's own 5th, matching the § 2 melodic-minor-off-the-5th method applied here again).
- → [[Reharmonization]]
