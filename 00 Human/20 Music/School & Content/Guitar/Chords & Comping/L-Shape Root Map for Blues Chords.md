# L-Shape Root Map for Blues Chords

Part of [[Guitar Positions]]. A movable-shape system for **finding the IV and V (and, for the minor blues, ♭VI) chord roots of a 12-bar blues purely by counting strings and frets from the I chord** — no separate memorized chord shape per key, no scanning the neck for the next root. Where [[Blues Form and Harmony]] documents the *harmony* of the 12-bar form and [[Chord Voicings]] documents *shell-voicing* shapes in general, this page is the missing piece between them: **where your hand physically goes** once you've grabbed the I chord. The root layout it produces looks like a small "L" on the fretboard — that's the whole mnemonic.

## The core idea

Once you know where the I chord's root sits, the IV and V roots are a fixed number of strings and frets away — **the same offset in every key**, because it's a relative (movable) shape, not a fixed-position chord. Two versions exist depending on which string the I chord's root falls on.

## Dominant blues (I7-IV7-V7) — root on the low E string

Worked in A (A7-D7-E7), but the offsets are key-independent.

1. **I chord (A7):** root on the **low E string**. Play any dominant-7 shape off that root — a full **E-shaped bar chord**, or a smaller **shell voicing** (root-3rd-♭7 only) for a band context or to dodge a full bar-chord grip. Matches the E-string-root shell already documented on [[Chord Voicings]] § *From videos* (Seriff's own maj7→dominant7→minor7→dim7 shell-shifting shape).
2. **IV chord (D7):** move **down one string** (E string → A string), **same fret**. Play the same shape family there (bar chord = classic A-shaped bar chord; shell = the shell shape relocated).
3. **V chord (E7):** from the IV chord, move **up two frets**, same string.

**Root layout:** I → (down a string, same fret) → IV → (same string, up two frets) → V. Traces a small **L**.

## Dominant blues (I7-IV7-V7) — root on the A string

Same three chords, mirrored, for when the tonic falls on the A string instead (worked from A at the 12th fret of the A string).

1. **I chord:** shell voicing (root-3rd-♭7) or full bar chord, root on the **A string**.
2. **IV chord:** move to the **low E string**, **down two frets** (toward the headstock).
3. **V chord:** from the IV chord, **up two frets** (back toward the body), same string.

Same L shape, mirrored — confirms the mnemonic generalizes to "one string over, ± a fret count," not to one specific starting string.

## Minor blues (i7-iv7 + ♭VI7-V7 turnaround) — root on the low E string

Worked in A (Am7-Dm7, turnaround F7/F9-E7/E7♯9-Am7-A7), root at the **5th fret of the low E string**.

1. **i chord (Am7):** small minor-7 voicing (drops two strings vs. a full bar chord) — playable 4-finger, 2-finger-plus-partial-bar, or full bar fingerstyle (mute the open low string if fingerstyle).
2. **iv chord (Dm7):** move **down one string** (E → A), **same fret**. Same minor-7 shape, relocated — matches the classic D minor-7 shell shape.
3. **V chord (E7, or E7♯9 — the "Hendrix chord," see [[Altered Chords]]):** from the iv chord's shape, **up two frets**, same string.
4. **♭VI chord (F7, or F9):** from the V chord's shape, **up one more fret**, same string.

**Root layout:** i → (down a string, same fret) → iv → (same string, up two frets) → V → (same string, up one fret) → ♭VI. Four roots, not a clean L, but the same "count strings and frets from the last shape" logic carries through.

## The turnaround itself

**Common mistake this page exists partly to correct:** guitarists often assume the minor-blues turnaround is **5-4-1** (Em7-Dm7-Am7) — you hear that wrong version in **Ain't No Sunshine** (Bill Withers). That's rare. The actual standard is **♭6 → 5 → 1** (♭VI7 → V7 → i7 → I7) — in A: **F7/F9 → E7/E7♯9 → Am7 → A7**. This is the third independently-sourced worked instance of this exact turnaround in the vault (see [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *Minor blues*, [[Turnarounds]]) — new here is the specific **F9** and **E7♯9 (Hendrix chord)** voicing options for the two turnaround chords, and the fret-counting root map above for finding them without re-deriving the shapes from scratch. The F9 voicing also has its own nickname, the **"James Brown chord"** — same naming pattern as the "Hendrix chord" for E7♯9.

## Dominant blues vs. minor blues — the naming trap

The plain I7-IV7-V7 blues (A7-D7-E7) gets casually called "minor blues" because the **minor pentatonic** solos so well over it — but every chord in it is a **dominant 7th**, a major-quality chord (major 3rd). The correct name is **dominant blues**; "minor blues" belongs to the separate i7-iv7 progression above, where the chords themselves are minor. See [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *Why every chord is a dominant 7th* for the harmonic reason this works at all.

## Drill — run both L-shapes through all 12 keys

The point of a movable shape is that it costs nothing to transpose. Concrete practice routine:

1. **Pick a starting root on the low E string** (start at fret 5 = A, since that's what the source video uses) and play the full dominant-blues L-shape (I-IV-V, shell voicings) through one 12-bar cycle, quick-change version: `I IV I I / IV IV I I / V IV I V`.
2. **Move the whole L-shape up one fret** (now rooted at B♭) and repeat the same 12-bar cycle. Continue chromatically up the neck, one fret at a time, through all 12 keys, until you run out of usable neck (roughly frets 1–12 on the low E string covers a full octave of keys).
3. **Repeat the same 12-key chromatic walk with the A-string-root version** of the L-shape, starting from a comfortable A-string fret (12th fret = A, per the source video, or start lower and work up).
4. **Switch to the minor-blues 4-root chain** and repeat the same chromatic walk-up, one key per pass, using the i7-iv7-V7(♭9/♯9 optional)-♭VI7 shapes and the ♭6-5-1 turnaround.
5. **Once all three shapes (dominant L, alt-string L, minor chain) are solid moving chromatically, randomize the starting key** (roll a die, pick a random fret) and find the full form cold — this is the real test: can you land the IV, V (and ♭VI) roots instantly from an arbitrary I chord without hunting.
6. **Play along with a real backing track** in a random key (Sweet Home Chicago-style for the dominant form, Thrill Is Gone-style for the minor form) rather than just counting frets in silence — the shape only earns its keep once it survives tempo and a band.

*(General starting-point drill structure — the video itself only demonstrates the shapes in A; the chromatic-walk/randomize/backing-track progression above is a standard technique-internalization drill applied to this specific shape, not something stated in the source.)*
