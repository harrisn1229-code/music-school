# Treading Water Drill

Part of [[Bebop Vocabulary]] and [[Guide Tones]] — a graduated practice system for turning "chord tones land on the beat" from a rule you know into a reflex your hands have. Jared's own name for it: **treading water** — deliberately looping back and forth between a chord tone and its neighbor tone(s) instead of drowning (stopping, or defaulting to running the scale straight up and down) while your ear/brain catches up. The whole system runs over a single static chord, in one fretboard position, so nothing but the targeting itself is being practiced.

## Setup and prerequisites

Before starting the drill you need, over **one chord in one position** (worked example: B♭7, 5th position shifting to 6th):
- **Chord tones cold** — root, 3rd, 5th, ♭7 of the target chord, known well enough to improvise with *only* those four notes and have it sound intentional.
- **The matching scale cold** — B♭ Mixolydian (= E♭ major, same notes, different tonic — a [[Diatonic Modes]] relative-mode relationship), known well enough to improvise with just the scale.
- **The bebop dominant scale, up and down, in time** — Mixolydian plus the chromatic passing tone between ♭7 and the root (1‑2‑3‑4‑5‑6‑♭7‑[passing tone]‑1 ascending, mirrored descending) — see [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §1 for why the extra note exists. This is the prerequisite from [[Bebop Scale Guitar Lesson (Level 1) - Master the Art of Intentional Note Placement|"Level 1"]] of the same series and it's not a nice-to-have — the whole drill is built on top of it.
- A **backing loop of a simple shell voicing** of the target chord (root‑3rd‑7th or root‑7th‑3rd, see [[Guide Tones]] §"Shell voicings") to play against, so the ear has a harmonic reference the whole time.

**If any prerequisite isn't solid, that's the actual practice target** — Jared's explicit framing: finding you can't yet improvise confidently with just the chord tones, or just the scale, is "hitting a gold mine," not a reason to feel behind. Go drill that first rather than forcing this on shaky footing.

## The core move: chord tone on the beat, neighbor tone on the offbeat

In straight eighth notes, alternate a chord tone (on the downbeat) with an adjacent scale tone — its **upper or lower neighbor tone**, the official [[Non-Harmonic Tones|non-harmonic-tone]] term — on the offbeat. Both directions are practiced separately off every chord tone: e.g. off the root, root↔♭7-below and root↔2nd-above are two *different* cells, not one. **Play straight, not swung**, while the pattern is still a drill rather than music — swinging an exercise that isn't musical yet reads as forced/cheesy; save swing feel for when you're actually playing.

## The 4-step progression (run this at every note-count level below)

1. **Tread water as long as you need.** Loop chord-tone↔neighbor-tone freely, as slow as necessary, with or without the backing loop, until it feels stable — don't move on until you're genuinely ready, not on a fixed rep count.
2. **Exactly two reps**, then move to the next cell. Forces the transition itself to be deliberate rather than open-ended noodling.
3. **Exactly one rep**, then move on. This is the hardest step and the one where the drill stops sounding like a drill and starts sounding like "an awesome scale pattern" / an actual lick — because by definition, playing it once each just *is* a chord-tone-on-every-beat melodic line.
4. **Let go and just improvise**, watching for the drilled cell to surface on its own. Don't force it every time — the point is noticing it show up naturally and feeling how much more controlled the line feels versus playing the scale straight.

Work steps 1–4 at each note-count level (below) before moving to the next; the whole thing is meant to be worked as a checklist, one chord tone/direction at a time, then repeated ascending and descending.

## Note-count levels — build the cell size up

- **2 notes** — chord tone + one neighbor (one side at a time: e.g. root↔♭7 below, root↔2nd above; 3rd↔2nd and 3rd↔4th; 5th↔4th and 5th↔6th; ♭7↔6th and ♭7↔major-7th-as-passing-tone). Eighth notes, chord tone always on the beat.
- **3 notes** — chord tone with a neighbor on *each* side, three notes total, still landing the chord tone on the beat each pass.
- **4 notes** — chord tone plus two more notes extending one direction. **Deliberately includes the major 7th over a dominant chord** — a note that's in neither the chord nor the Mixolydian scale, normally a "bad note" over a dominant 7 — and it only works here because it's placed strictly as a chromatic passing tone on an offbeat; Jared calls out the resulting feel as "almost polymeter." This is a direct, applied instance of [[Bebop Vocabulary]]'s core rule (the added chromatic tone exists purely to keep chord tones on strong beats, not for its own color) and doubles as a real-world [[Non-Harmonic Tones|chromatic passing tone]] example.
- **5 notes** — same idea extended further; Jared notes it actually lines up *more* naturally than the 4-note version because it divides evenly against the beat.

## Why it works / what it's for

The stated goal isn't making these cells sound good on their own — it's building "vocabulary": your own hands generate original melodic material because the chord-tone-targeting reflex is now automatic, rather than the alternative of running the scale straight up/down (which is accurate but reads as "sounding like a scale, not music") or leaning on memorized licks from other players (fine, but stifling if you can't manipulate them). Jared frames the identical targeting ability as the same skill needed to hit a **chord change** accurately when the harmony moves — this whole system is deliberately practiced over one static chord first so the targeting itself, not the chord-change timing, is what's isolated.

**Expect it to be tedious.** The video's own title calls this out directly — it's monotonous, repetitive, and that's the honest cost of the payoff; give it real practice time (Jared: hours, across many sessions, over weeks/months), not a single pass.
