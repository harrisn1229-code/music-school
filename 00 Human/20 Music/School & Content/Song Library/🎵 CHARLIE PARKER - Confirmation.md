# Confirmation — Charlie Parker

> A 32-bar AABA bebop standard in F major. Nathan Borton pulls the melody apart phrase by phrase into named, reusable devices — framed the same way as his other Parker-melody breakdowns in this vault — rather than teaching the tune as a fixed thing to memorize. See [[Learn Charlie Parker's Phrasing Secrets... with CONFIRMATION!]] for the source video, full routing, and confidence notes. A follow-up in Borton's Parker-melody series (an earlier video on the same topic is referenced but not yet in the vault).

## Chords
**Key:** F major · **Form:** 32-bar AABA.

**A section:** opens on **Fmaj7 (I)**, then drops straight into a minor ii–V starting a half step below the tonic — **Em7♭5 → A7♭9** (ii–V of D minor, the tune's signature "Bird changes" move) resolving toward **Dm7**. The line keeps cascading down in secondary ii–V pairs — **Cm7 → F7** (ii–V of B♭) landing on **B♭7 (IV7)** — continuing the cycle back toward **G7 → C7** (ii–V home). Each A section closes on a turnaround built from secondary dominants — **Am7 → D7 → Gm7 → C7** — back to **Fmaj7**.

**Bridge:** breaks from the A material — the A sections are a partial contrafact of "Twilight Time," but Parker wrote the bridge's harmony himself — modulating briefly to **♭VI (D♭maj7)** via its own ii–V (E♭m7–A♭7) before the CESH device (chromatic root motion under held harmony, see the melody breakdown below) walks it back to a plain **ii7–V7–I** cadence home to F.

Real book editions vary slightly bar to bar (Confirmation is notorious for that); this is the commonly cited skeleton. Note the source transcript itself isn't spelled out chord-by-chord — Borton narrates by function (`our ii chord`, `our dominant 7 chord`, `the B♭7`, `our A7`) rather than a full lead sheet, so the skeleton above reconstructs concrete chords from his functional labels rather than inventing bar numbers he didn't give. See [[The ii-V-I]], [[Turnarounds]], [[Bebop Vocabulary]].

## Melody breakdown (Nathan Borton's phrase-by-phrase breakdown)

**A section:**
- **Opening phrase: a 6th-interval leap right off the top of the melody** — the interval Borton calls out as a Parker signature worth training in isolation. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]], [[Intervals]]
- **A minor ii–V lick (E half-diminished → A7, resolving toward D minor) built from a triad plus the 6th.** Walks the root and 3rd of Em7♭5, descends a G minor triad (the chord's own ♭3-♭5-♭7 spelled as a triad), then jumps a 6th from A7's root to its 3rd. → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Guide Tones]]
- **Blues mixed into the line over B♭7 — the F blues scale (rooted on B♭7's own 5th), not B♭'s.** → [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]]
- **A dominant line resolvable two ways** — a plain 5–1, or the same idea stretched into a full ii–V–I by prepending a short line. → [[The ii-V-I]]
- **A 5–1 phrase layering the 6th with two guide-tone connective lines** — ♭9 of the dominant down to the root, and the 4th of the dominant resolving to the 3rd. → [[Guide Tones]]
- **The 4th-to-3rd tension-and-release device, extended into a jump to an altered tone.** Start on the 4th (an "avoid" tone against the chord), resolve to the 3rd, then leap to an altered extension — worked here as the ♭5 of A7; other options named (♯9, ♭9, ♯5). → [[Altered Chords]], [[Bebop Vocabulary]]
- **More blues-over-dominant variation, then the A section closes on a strong 5–1 phrase** Borton recommends as a general line-ending device.

**B section:**
- **CESH (chromatic embellishment of static harmony) opens the B section** — the root of the ii chord moved chromatically up or down through the changes while the harmony sits still. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §6
- **A closing phrase Borton says he "uses all the time"** to end a line — demonstrated in isolation, then in context.
- **The B section closes on another 5–1 line**, "super cool" — the same closer logic as the A section.

## Steal this

- **Leap a 6th to open or close a line, or drop a random 6th into a straight scale run to see what falls out** — the cheapest way to get the interval into your ear and your fingers before trying to place it deliberately.
- **Over any dominant chord, try the blues scale rooted on its 5th, not its own root** — the F-blues-over-B♭7 device, transposes to any dominant.
- **When you want a tension-and-release move on a major or dominant chord: start on the 4th, resolve down to the 3rd, then jump to an altered extension (♭9/♯9/♭5/♯5).** Works even though the 4th "shouldn't" be there — the resolution is what earns it.
- **CESH: pick one note (often a chord root) and walk it chromatically underneath otherwise-static harmony** — a way to generate motion without changing the underlying chord.
- **Keep a "lick journal" categorized by device type (5–1s, CESH, altered, etc.), not by tune** — makes vocabulary reusable across changes instead of stuck to this one melody. → [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]] §*A collection-first variant*
