> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **A passing chord connects two chords already in the progression; a substitution replaces one**
> Which is why a passing chord is usually optional and a substitution is not.
>
> **Q2.** **Passing chord on the weak beat, the real chord on the heavy beat**
> The ear then tracks the actual harmony and hears the insert as colour.
>
> **Q3.** **Dominant diminished · a chord shape slid a fret · diatonic · dominant, plain or tritone-subbed**
> C♯dim7 → Dm7 is the first family, and it works because C♯dim7 is a rootless A7♭9.
>
> **Q4.** **The melody note must be a usable tone of the new chord**
>
> **Q5.** **A chord tone or a functioning extension — not a note the new chord contradicts**
> Chord tones are 1, 3, 5, 7; extensions are 9, 11, 13 or an altered version. Excluded: a natural 9 over a ♭9 chord, or a natural 4 sitting on the 3rd.
>
> **Q6.** **Melody-first — choosing chords first and hoping the melody survives is how you get the clashes**
> Write out the melody, decide what each note could be relative to a candidate chord, and choose from there.
>
> **Q7.** **Cmaj7 root · Am7 ♭3 · Fmaj7 5th · Dm7 ♭7**
> *Also accepted:* any four of Cmaj7 (root) · Am7 (♭3) · Fmaj7 (5th) · Dm7 (♭7) · A♭maj7 (3rd) · F♯m7♭5 (♭5)
>
> **Q8.** **Delayed resolution — harmonise the expected tonic note with anything except the tonic chord**
> The tonic arrives late, and the substitutes chain: A♭maj7 → B♭9 → C, or A♭maj7 → G7sus4 → C.
>
> **Q9.** **The melody does not change, so nothing has to be re-sung or re-recorded**
> *Rubric (5):* says the topline is untouched, so the change costs only the chord choice (2) · says the same note takes a different meaning under each chord (1) · names the payoff — a second section, or the sustained-vocal neo-soul sound (2)
> Every other reharmonization technique asks something of the arrangement — new bass motion, more chords, a different harmonic rhythm. This one asks nothing: one note is already sitting there and you change what is underneath it. The note becomes a root, then a ♭3, then a 5th, then a ♭7, and each reading feels different while the vocal take stays exactly as recorded. That is most of the neo-soul trick, and it is also the cheapest route to a second section, since running the same held note through a different chord set on the repeat reads as development rather than a loop going round again.
>
> **Q10.** **When the tune is the point — it competes with the melody for attention and pulls focus**
> A strong vocal hook or lead line is the case to leave alone.
>
> **Q11.** **Reharmonize the repeats, not the first statement**
>
> **Q12.** **The listener has already learned the plain version, so the change is heard against a reference**
> *Rubric (5):* says the plain first statement teaches the listener the tune (2) · says the changed repeat is then heard as a departure from something known (1) · says it yields a second section out of existing material (2)
> A reharmonization only reads as a choice if the listener knows what it is a choice against. Play the altered version first and there is nothing to compare it to — it is just a set of chords, and a dense one, competing with a melody nobody has learned yet. Play the plain version first and the second pass registers as the same tune wearing something else, which is what development is. The free part is structural: verse one and verse two are now different sections and only one of them had to be written.
