# Pop music has a new favourite chord progression

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tj0tkv2tNSo)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tj0tkv2tNSo)

## Synopsis

The classic "1-5-6-4" pop loop (I-V-vi-IV, [[The Axis Progression]]'s canonical rotation) has reportedly become so overused it reads as cliché — Bennett argues a different ordering of the SAME four chords, IV-I-vi-V ("4-1-6-5"), has quietly become the new go-to in the last 10-20 years, then explains via cadence theory why this particular reordering keeps a song feeling perpetually unresolved and loop-friendly.

## Techniques

- **The new order: IV–I–vi–V.** Same four chords as the classic I-V-vi-IV Axis loop, reordered so the progression never lands on a clean V→I perfect cadence.
- **Not a plain cyclic rotation of the classic loop** — cyclically rotating I-V-vi-IV only ever produces V-vi-IV-I, vi-IV-I-V, or IV-I-V-vi (all three already documented on [[The Axis Progression]]); IV-I-vi-V swaps the position of vi and V relative to that third rotation, making it a genuinely distinct fifth member of the family rather than a relabeling of an existing one.
- **Songs**: "Learning to Fly" (Tom Petty) and "Fast Car" (Tracy Chapman, both the 1988 original and its 2023 cover/2015 hit cover) predate the "recent" framing, establishing the shape isn't brand new, just newly dominant. "High Hopes" (Panic! at the Disco, key of F: B♭-F-Dm-C) is the modern example, with a variation on the repeat.
- **iii-for-V substitution on a repeat**: "High Hopes"'s second pass through the loop swaps the closing V chord (C) for iii (Am) instead — justified because V and iii share two of three notes (C major and A minor differ by only one note, G vs. A), so iii can stand in for V while giving "a slightly different flavor" and approaching the next IV chord "from the other direction" (3 up to 4, instead of 5 down to 4).
- **Also found in rock**: "Mercy" (Muse, 2015) is built on this progression as a loop from beginning to end of the song, not just a section.
- **Why it works — the harmonic-function read**: I, IV, and V are the "primary colors" of a key (tonic/subdominant/dominant); vi is the secondary "alternative darker home" a song can resolve to instead of I. IV (subdominant) provides mild tension wanting eventual resolution; V (dominant) provides stronger tension wanting resolution even more.
- **The mechanism that makes the loop feel perpetual**: the progression reaches V — maximum tension, primed for a V→I perfect cadence — but instead of resolving to I it goes to IV, a **deceptive/interrupted cadence** baked into the loop's own structure. Every repetition sets up the expectation of resolution and then denies it, which is why these progressions read as endless loops rather than complete phrases. Even landing on I doesn't feel "done," because I sits in a structurally weak position in the loop (not preceded by a real perfect cadence), so the ear keeps expecting more.
