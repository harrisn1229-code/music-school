# Memorizing Jazz Standards by Harmonic Chunking

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Jazz Harmony]]. An **analysis-first** method for internalizing a jazz standard's chord changes — memorize by grouping raw chord symbols into functional units (a form section, a cadence, a secondary ii–V) rather than memorizing a flat row of individual chord names. Complements, rather than replaces, [[Internalizing a Tune Through Repetition]]'s **repetition-first** process — that note front-loads raw play-through-and-listen repetition before any harmonic analysis; this note is the analytical scaffold that makes each repetition land faster once the chart itself is legible as a small number of chunks instead of 20+ separate chords.

## Why memorize at all
Reading a chart costs attention that would otherwise go toward playing well, and — just as importantly in a jazz context — reading means you can't watch the other musicians on the stand. Internalizing the tune frees that attention for actually interacting/reacting with the band in real time, not just executing your own part.

## The core method: the phone-number analogy
A phone number isn't memorized digit by digit — it's memorized as country code + area code + the rest, because that structure means there's genuinely less to hold in memory. Apply the identical idea to a chord chart:

1. **Identify the form first.** How many bars, how many sections, where sections repeat. AABA, ABAC, a 2×16-bar form, whatever it is — see [[32-Bar Song Form]], [[Musical Form]]. Once the form is known, material that repeats (a whole A section, or even just the first few bars of two different sections) only has to be memorized **once**.
2. **Within each section, name the cadences and small functional units** rather than the individual chords — a secondary ii–V, a IV-minor-to-I move, a turnaround, "the dominant of the dominant." Chunk each of those into a single mental "thing" ("a 2-5 to six," not "half-diminished, then dominant, then minor").
3. **Group chords by function on the strong beats, treat the rest as embellishment.** When a stretch of the chart looks dense (several chord symbols per bar), check which chords actually land on the strong beats and carry the real harmonic motion — the others are frequently passing/auxiliary color that doesn't need to be remembered as its own event, just as a decoration on the chunk it sits inside.
4. **Cross-reference against tunes you already know.** The real payoff isn't chunking one chart — it's recognizing that the *same* chunk (secondary ii–V to the vi, IV-minor-to-I, V7/V at the end of a first half or a bridge) shows up in the same structural slot across dozens of unrelated standards. Once a chunk is chunked once, it's recognized instantly everywhere else it appears — "when you've learned one, you've almost learned ten already."

## Worked example: "There'll Never Be Another You" (E♭ major, ABAC, 32 bars)
Full chart and chunk map on the song's own note: [[🎵 HARRY WARREN - There Will Never Be Another You]]. Summary of the chunking, top to bottom:
- **A (8 bars):** I (2 bars) → **2-5 to vi** (Dø7–G7→Cm) → **2-5 to IV** (B♭m7–E♭7→A♭) — 6 raw chords reduced to 3 chunks.
- **B (8 bars):** opens on IV (a very common ABAC-form slot-device) → **IV-minor-to-I** → **V7/V for two bars** ("5 of 5") → a plain ii chord that opens back to the top of A, rather than cadencing home (an ABAC form's first half never really ends — see [[32-Bar Song Form]]).
- **C (8 bars):** first 3 bars identical to B (reuse, no new memorization) → **♯4-diminished reharmonized as a minor ii–V, resolving to G minor** → the closing stretch reduces to **4 large 4-bar chunks**: cadence-to-C-minor, 2-5-1-to-the-sixth, "4-minor back to 1," and the "5-of-5-to-2-5" chunk (independently common at the end of an AABA bridge too, not just this ABAC form's ending).

## Practicing the transfer
Chunking is a cognitive/visual shortcut — it still needs to connect to what you actually *hear*, or it's just trivia. Two concrete ways to build that connection: **play already-known standards in different keys** (forces you to re-derive the chunk by ear/function rather than by fixed fingerings), and **comp for vocalists** (forces real-time recognition of the chunk under pressure, without a chart). → [[Transcribing and Playing by Ear]]

## Theory as language, not a dictionary
Don't try to memorize every possible chunk shape in the abstract — that's the equivalent of memorizing a dictionary instead of learning a language. The chunk vocabulary is built the same way vocabulary in a spoken language is: by encountering it in context, over and over, inside real tunes, until it's recognized by ear before it's ever consciously labeled.
