# CAGED Minor Chords by Flatting the Third

Part of [[Guitar Positions]]. [[CAGE System Scale Shapes]] and [[CAGED Chord Shapes by String-Shift]] both cover the five **major** CAGED shapes (C-A-G-E-D). This page is the minor-chord counterpart Jesse Glick says his students keep asking for: there is no separate "minor CAGED system" to learn — **every one of the five major CAGED shapes becomes its minor equivalent by finding the note that's the chord's major 3rd and flatting it one fret (one half step)**, leaving every other fretted note in the shape untouched. The whole lesson is one mechanical rule applied five times, plus the reminder that it's really "you know the chord, you can find the third in the chord" — a general chord-tone skill, not five things to memorize.

## The core mechanism

1. **Identify the third inside the chord shape you already know.** Find it by counting up the major scale from the root — root, 2nd, **3rd** (e.g. for a C chord: C-D-**E**, so E is the note that needs to move).
2. **Flat that one note by a half step (one fret down)** — nothing else in the shape changes. That single fretted-note edit is the entire major→minor conversion.
3. This is the physical, on-the-fretboard version of "a minor chord needs a flat third" — the theory statement most players can recite without knowing what it means in their hands. See [[Intervals]] for the major 3rd / minor 3rd interval-quality rule this mechanically executes.

## Applied to each of the five CAGED shapes

Video order: A, G, E, D, then C last (skipped at the start because the open-position C shape needed extra explanation).

- **A shape** — find the 3rd by counting up the major scale (root-2nd-3rd); the chord's fretted 3rd sits an octave up from the open/unplayed scale-degree 3 the counting lands on. Flat that fretted note one fret → A-shape minor.
- **G shape** — **the trickiest of the five**, because the full 6-string G shape doubles the 3rd (it appears twice in the voicing, once lower and once higher). *(Transcript is a captions pull and the exact fret numbers/finger assignments for this shape are spoken vaguely — flagged qualitatively rather than guessed.)* Lower instance: root, 3rd (on the A string), 5th — flat the 3rd on the A string and that alone is enough to hear as G-shape minor. Higher instance: the second 3rd isn't reachable at its own fret to simply "move down," so instead of sliding an existing finger, **add a new fretted note** a half step below where the natural 3rd would be — Glick names the target pitch directly as an **E♭** — to cover the doubled 3rd properly. Net effect: same G shape, both instances of the 3rd resolved to the flat 3rd, one by moving a finger and one by fretting an added note.
- **E shape (standard barre chord)** — "super easy": if the full barre shape is a C chord, C minor is just **lifting the middle finger** — physically the same move as flatting the 3rd a fret, since removing that fretted note exposes the string's fretted-one-lower note underneath the barre.
- **D shape** — the one 3rd in this shape sits on the highest string; bring it down one fret and play the rest of the shape unchanged → D-shape minor.
- **C shape** (covered last) — root note plus the 3rd on the D string; drop that D-string note one fret → C minor triad.

## Extends past full chord shapes — to bare triads too

Glick calls this out directly as "so valuable moving forward" beyond full 5–6-string CAGED grips: the identical rule applies to any bare triad. Two worked examples, both D triads in different shapes/positions — in each, find which fretted note is the 3rd (in one, it's the note under the index finger) and bring it down one fret to get the minor triad. Ties directly into [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]]'s triad-relocation approach and [[Chord-Tone Labeling by Scale-Degree Counting]]'s "find the 3rd by counting" skill — the flat-a-fret rule is the payoff those chord-tone-identification skills are for.

## Soloing/riffing application

Once the 3rd inside a shape is identified, the same knowledge tells you what to avoid when soloing over that chord in its minor form: riffing on a chord shape and treating it as minor means **skipping the major-3rd fret** entirely and targeting the minor 3rd (the flatted note) instead — the chord-shape skill and the note-choice skill are the same piece of information used two different ways (comping vs. lead).

## From videos

- **A dissenting critique, worth holding alongside the flat-the-3rd method above: the "highest fret of one shape = lowest fret of the next" CAGED continuity rule that works for major shapes breaks for minor shapes.** Worked on E minor: E-shape → D-shape follows the rule cleanly, but the expected C-shape E minor voicing that continuity predicts is awkward to physically play and doesn't actually preserve the rule; the G-shape E minor voicing is similarly impractical and gets substituted for something closer to the E shape again in practice. Conclusion: CAGED is unreliable specifically for generating *different voicings of the same chord* once you're past the major shapes — useful as a starting map, but "don't let it become a crutch." **Recommended alternative for finding a chord anywhere on the neck: know the chord's actual notes** (e.g. F = F-A-C) and locate those three notes directly wherever they fall, rather than routing through a CAGED shape at all — the same chord-tone-first philosophy behind [[Chord-Tone Labeling by Scale-Degree Counting]]. — Brandon D'Eon, [The CAGED System Actually Sucks...](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QT44Vye-uSY)
