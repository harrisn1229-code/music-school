# Directional Alternate Picking System

Jared Borkowski's (Sound Guitar Lessons) core picking-hand default: a **backdrop picking rule** you fall back on whenever you're not deliberately doing something else, plus **seven progressive exercises** that drill it into automatic use. Distinct from [[Angular Reach Exercise]] (same channel, a reach/relaxation-under-tension drill on a fixed angular fret pattern, part of the Warm-up & Technique "always item" rotation on [[The Practice Priority Pyramid]]) — this note is a **picking-direction rulebook + exercise ladder**, not a reach drill, and the source video doesn't reference the Practice Priority Pyramid system at all. Genuinely separate material from the same teacher, not a duplicate.

## The core rule: directional alternate picking

- **Not the same thing as "strict" alternate picking.** Strict alternate picking = literally alternate every stroke (down after up, up after down), no matter where the beat falls. Directional alternate picking = **pick direction is locked to the beat position**, independent of whether you're actually plucking a note there.
- **The rule: downbeats (1, 2, 3, 4) get a downstroke; offbeats/upbeats ("and") get an upstroke.**
- **The picking hand keeps moving through rests and slurred notes even when it isn't plucking** — on a beat you don't pluck, the hand still travels the "right" direction for that beat position (down on a downbeat, up on an upbeat) so the motion stays continuous and the next real pluck is already moving the correct way. Framed with a strumming analogy: constant fluid motion (always moving, only striking the strings when you want sound) beats a jolting stop-start motion where you consciously reposition the hand before every strike.
- **Why it matters:** it's meant as a **default/backdrop mode** — something intuitive enough that you stop spending cognitive energy on pick direction, which matters most once slurs (hammer-ons/pull-offs/slides) start skipping strokes in real playing (scale runs, licks). You're free to break the rule on purpose (deliberate runs of downstrokes, etc.) — it's a fallback, not an absolute rule.
- **Positioned against economy picking** — Jared calls economy picking "a whole different ballgame," a deliberate alternative technique to reach for later; directional alternate picking is presented as the default you return to when nothing else is called for.

## The seven exercises

All demonstrated on a **C major scale, 5th position, one fingering shape** (exercises 1–6), then **A minor blues scale shape** (exercise 7) — same shape works with any scale form. Each demoed at **100 BPM**, then again at **double speed (~200 BPM)**. Pattern notation below: `D` = downstroke (on-beat), `U` = upstroke (off-beat), `–` = rest/ghost-motion (hand moves but doesn't pluck).

1. **Exercise 1 — straight alternate picking (baseline).** Constant eighth notes, every note picked, `D U D U` repeating — this is strict alternate picking, used as the starting reference point before the directional/rest patterns below.
2. **Exercise 2 — `D U D –`.** Down, up, down, then a rest/sustain on the following upbeat (on-on-off pattern, repeating). Feels "easy" because it's close to natural alternation — the entry point.
3. **Exercise 3 — `D U – U`.** Down, up, rest (on a downbeat), up — trains **two upstrokes in a row on strategically placed upbeats**, a less familiar feel than exercise 2.
4. **Exercise 4 — `D – D U`.** Down, rest (ghost-motion upward on the skipped beat), down, up — explicitly calls out watching the picking hand keep moving through the rest so it's already headed the right way for the next real stroke.
5. **Exercise 5 — `– U D U`.** First beat is a rest (click plays, nothing picked), then upbeat, downbeat, upbeat — training **syncopation and internalizing the strong beat without playing it**. Can be played as rests or let-ring (sheet music shows rests; both are valid, since the point is picking direction/timing, not note length).
6. **Exercise 6 — full C major scale run with selective slurs.** All notes still eighth notes, but some are slurred (hammer-on/pull-off), which forces skipping a down or an up in the underlying pattern while the notes keep flowing — the first "real world application" exercise, since scale runs in practice usually mix picked and slurred notes rather than picking everything.
7. **Exercise 7 — A minor blues scale (or any blues-scale shape/position) with selective slurs.** Same slurred-scale-run idea as exercise 6, framed as the "fun"/musical capstone. Worked example: sliding from the 8th fret to the 7th fret on the 3rd string as the slur move (a slide works interchangeably with a pull-off here — any technique that skips the pluck satisfies the pattern).

## Milestone

**Play each exercise clean with a metronome three times in a row, no mistakes** — the stated "leveled up" bar for this system. (Contrast with [[Angular Reach Exercise]]'s single-perfect-pass gate — this is a different milestone shape: a clean-streak count rather than a single take, per [[The Practice Priority Pyramid]]'s taxonomy of milestone types.)

## From videos

- **Sync hand TIMING but deliberately NOT hand tension — plus a small-motion endurance finisher drill.** A real, easy-to-miss habit: players correctly sync picking-hand timing to the fretting hand, but often also unconsciously sync motion *intensity* between the two — digging in harder with the pick at speed makes the fretting hand cramp up too, even though it doesn't need more force to keep pace. Deliberately keep the fretting hand relaxed even while picking harder. Separately: a daily finisher drill at the end of a speed block — minimize picking-motion size while staying fast, across all strings up to the 24th fret — improved endurance, control, and reduced unwanted string noise once adopted (vocal-technique analogy: like a beginner singer who sings louder for higher pitches, guitarists tend to pick bigger for faster, which works against the goal). — BERNTH, [[3 Picking Mistakes That Are Slowing You Down!]]
- **Think in groups of six, not groups of three, when playing 16th-note triplets to a click.** Grouping triplets in 3s puts an upstroke on the second group's first note, which is awkward to lock to a metronome at speed; regrouping the same notes as 6s puts a downstroke on every quarter-note pulse instead, giving a consistent, easier-to-lock accent pattern. A picking-stroke/subdivision-grouping fix specifically for triplet-based material. — BERNTH, [[Top 3 EASY Shred Licks - How To Start Shredding!]]
- **Eliminate individual thumb/index-finger joint movement within the pick grip, for day-to-day consistency — distinct from wrist/arm motion size.** Early on, the thumb and index finger moved independently while picking, producing inconsistent results session to session (good technique one day, falling apart at the same BPM the next) with no repeatable cause. Switching to a stiff, closed-hand grip with no individual finger/joint movement (support the index finger with the other fingers, without full-fist tension) fixed the inconsistency directly — named as the fix for unpredictable practice-to-practice technique variance. — BERNTH, [[10 Life-Changing Guitar Technique HACKS!]]
- **A 7-notes-per-string odd-grouping daily warmup, plus a stated 120 BPM practical ceiling for one-note-per-string arpeggio shapes before switching to sweep picking.** The 7-grouping pattern (across all six strings) is named a personal long-running daily favorite specifically because it trains three things in one pass: picking-hand endurance, string transitions, and timing (odd groupings get noticeably harder to keep locked as tempo rises). Separately: one-note-per-string alternate-picked arpeggio shapes are a genuinely good string-transition workout, but hit a practical wall around 120 BPM in real playing — past that, sweep picking becomes the faster real-world choice for the same shapes, which is itself useful information for deciding which technique to reach for at a given tempo. — BERNTH, [[Guitar Exercises Ranked By Importance]]
- **Deliberately choose the harder picking technique when the point is training that technique.** BERNTH picks a difficult-string-transition arpeggio exercise with strict alternate picking specifically, even though sweep or economy picking would be easier — substituting the easier technique would defeat the training purpose. A stiff full-arm motion gets the pick stuck in the dead zone on these transitions; a wrist motion is the only way through, which is exactly what makes the exercise diagnostic. Worked shape: a minor-add9 arpeggio, also directly usable as improvisation vocabulary over a minor chord. — BERNTH, [[This Is Why You CAN'T Play FAST! (and 2 exercises to FIX it)]]
- **Deliberately flatten picking-hand dynamics as a diagnostic exercise, surfaced via a "human vs. MIDI guitar" comparison video.** Playing a fast lick with heavy picking-stroke accents can mask real timing and note-spacing problems; deliberately mimicking an unhumanized MIDI track's flat, unaccented sound on real guitar forces those problems to the surface instead. Stated goal isn't literally sounding robotic — it's using the flattened-dynamics constraint as a step toward a genuinely fluid, consistent feel. → [[Computer Guitarist VS Real Guitarist (Real Guitar VS MIDI Guitar)]] — BERNTH, [Computer Guitarist VS Real Guitarist (Real Guitar VS MIDI Guitar)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jbkzDAKYiWU)

- **A 9-level beginner-to-advanced shred progression map, with Level 4 named as the actual key unlock.** Levels 1-3 build alternate picking to a metronome with shrinking group sizes (4 notes, then 2); Level 4 requires syncing *every single* picking stroke to a different note — up to that point the fretting hand could mostly coast while the picking hand did the work, and BERNTH names this as the first real technique wall, worth lingering on rather than rushing past. Levels 5-9 build outward from there: clean/inaudible string transitions (2-string, then full 6-string scales), combined note-values/odd-meters/string-skipping/4-NPS phrases, blending legato/alternate/sweep while preserving each one's distinct tonal character, and advanced techniques (tapping, sweep finger-rolls) used musically rather than as flash. — BERNTH, [[10 LEVELS OF SHRED GUITAR - (beginner to pro)]]
- **Deliberately alternate-pick arpeggio shapes that would normally be swept, as a picking-angle-adaptability drill — explicitly not proposed as a replacement for sweep picking at speed.** One-note-per-string arpeggio shapes remove the usual shortcut of settling into one fixed picking angle for a whole run, since the pattern flips which stroke opens the next string almost every time — unlike 3-notes-per-string material where the same stroke tends to open each string. Stated purpose: build a more adaptable angle vocabulary that transfers to other odd alternate-picking patterns, not to out-perform sweep/economy picking on arpeggios themselves (sweep/economy remains faster at real tempo for this exact material). Progression: 2 arpeggio shapes (minor, major) first to isolate the picking problem, then a 3rd (diminished) added for color, tempo pushed until it broke down (honestly reported around 120 BPM 16ths). — BERNTH, [[Best Daily 10 MIN Picking WORKOUT! (Maximum Speed, Control, Endurance)]]
- **Pick grip and pick-escape angle mechanics, in detail — the actual how-to behind this page's existing "one-sided pick-escape bias" bullet.** Grip: small Jazz III pick, index-finger-side-pad grip with thumb on top, only the tip exposed to the strings, held light enough to almost fall out (a calibration drill for over-gripping). Two escape angles exist — **upstroke escape** (pick clears upward after an upstroke) and **downstroke escape** (clears after a downstroke) — and using the wrong one for a given string transition either sticks the pick between strings or grazes a neighbor. A 4-level progression isolates each angle, then combines them within one line, building to a 7/8 arpeggio étude requiring rapid, non-systematic angle switching. Stated goal: master both angles and fast switching between them, since real licks mix transition types unpredictably — framed as the actual bottleneck on tricky licks once raw picking speed is already there. — BERNTH, [[You're Probably Using The WRONG Pick Grip!]]
- **Even-vs-odd notes-per-string as the real reason 3-note-per-string scales are hard to pick cleanly.** With an odd note count per string, alternate picking necessarily starts on a downstroke on some strings and an upstroke on others (only two stroke values exist and 3 doesn't divide evenly) — genuinely harder to keep clean at speed than a picking-pattern that stays consistent across every string. **4-notes-per-string fixes this:** every string starts on a downstroke — down-up-down-up per string, then down again on the next — the same evenly-distributed pattern familiar from chromatic picking warmups, applied to real scale shapes. Tradeoff: the extra note per string demands a real left-hand stretch, described as feeling "almost impossible" at first for smaller hands. → [[4 Note Per String Exercises - Practice THIS To Get Shredded!]] — BERNTH, [4 Note Per String Exercises: Practice THIS To Get Shredded!](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VSGq5OnlKNw)

- **A second independent source naming mid-tempo (~120 BPM) as the most neglected practice zone, plus a "must be wrist-driven by design" exercise principle.** Many players have shred/sweep fundamentals down but struggle to play simple material tight and controlled around 120 BPM specifically, because practice time skews to very slow (learning) or very fast (pushing limits) and skips the middle — despite it being the range most real playing happens in. Two exercises built to be physically impossible with a stiff full-arm/elbow motion (a mid-tempo alternate-picking/string-skipping/muting warmup, and a harder string-skipping arpeggio riff one level up), forcing wrist looseness by construction rather than by instruction. — BERNTH, [[3 EXERCISES I Wish I Knew As A Beginner Guitarist!]]
- **Repeat certain scale phrases across strings so every string starts with the same picking stroke, deliberately trading a little melodic predictability for a much simpler picking-angle problem.** Contrast: a straight 3-notes-per-string scale run constantly flips which stroke (down or up) opens each new string — a materially harder angle problem, since no single fixed angle serves the whole line (matches [[String Skipping Approaches]]'s same angle-flip observation for skipped strings). Repeating phrases so every string opens on the same stroke removes that flip entirely — named as one of BERNTH's own most-used devices. — BERNTH, [[5 EASY Licks That Sound FAST! (Learn To Shred On Guitar TODAY)]]
- **A rhythmic note-grouping-switching drill — trains clean subdivision changes rather than picking direction.** A repeating phrase that switches between different rhythmic groupings (e.g. straight 16ths vs. 16th-note triplets) within one pass, played along to backing tracks at different tempos. What's actually trained: feeling the *next* grouping's pulse before you're already in it — sloppy note spacing right at the switch is the most common failure, not raw speed. Practice method: lock in with a backing track's bass drum built to accent exactly where the groupings change. Also notes the right-hand motion visibly changes between tempo/grouping blocks rather than staying one fixed motion throughout. → [[Ultimate Alternate Picking Exercises - Kickstart Your Speed in 2020!]] — BERNTH, [Ultimate Alternate Picking Exercises | Kickstart Your Speed in 2020!](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wIEnH2sVoi8)

- **A three-tempo-zone reframe of "one perfect picking motion," plus a build-your-own irregular-note-grouping workout method.** Grip and hand shape stay fixed across tempos, but the underlying wrist motion genuinely needs to shift as tempo rises — named zones: slow (below ~90 BPM), mid (~90–160), fast (above ~160). A technique that felt perfect at 90 BPM can suddenly feel "wrong" again crossing into the next zone, which is a zone-boundary effect, not a regression, and is worth recognizing rather than reading as failure. Separately: build custom mixed-grouping workouts by picking a scale and assigning an irregular, non-repeating note count per string (worked example: A minor, 3-2-1-5-2-3 from high E to low E) — deliberately breaks memorized uniform patterns and doubles as a composition exercise. — BERNTH, [[STOP Picking Like This (USE YOUR WRIST!)]]
- **2-notes-per-string workout as a mid-tempo warm-up, deliberately countering a 3-notes-per-string bias.** BERNTH names his own past mistake — favoring fast 3-NPS shred patterns and neglecting 2-NPS drilling — which left a real gap in controlled, tight alternate picking at the mid-tempo range actually used most in real playing. Goal checklist: to a click, no overlapping notes/unwanted noise, 100–120 BPM in 16th notes; works with any chord shape, arpeggio, or cadence. — BERNTH, [[The 7 Guitar Exercises That Will Change Your Life]]
- **Picking-hand anchor and hand-shape setup, presented as the foundation the whole system runs on.** Keep the picking hand physically anchored to the guitar body (resting near the bridge, not floating free) for a stable reference point — contrasted against a floating-hand style that works for some players but isn't recommended here. Form a loose fist while picking rather than letting fingers splay/flop — a direct, immediate control improvement once adopted. Small, wrist-driven motion at all speeds; more arm engages only at higher tempos, but the base motion never becomes full-arm. Jazz III-size pick with a grippy surface (Dunlop Max Grip) for control plus sweat resistance on stage. — BERNTH, [[5 Best Beginner Picking EXERCISES - Learn To Shred TODAY!]]
- **A "walking vs. running" reframe of the classic +1-BPM-a-day metronome method, plus a named short-burst/tension-relaxation/string-pairing speed routine.** Claim: slow and fast picking are mechanically *different motions*, not the same motion sped up — so a linear metronome climb works up to a "speed barrier" and then stalls, because past that point the fix is switching to the fast-tempo motion directly (expect initial sloppiness, same as a first attempt at running). The named daily routine: short, precise 2-string bursts that always end on a downstroke (easy to self-check note count), alternating a fast burst with a deliberate rest so a felt contrast between "tense/fast" and "relaxed" registers — favored over long scale-run drilling for exactly that reason. Closing safety checklist: pick with the tip of the pick, a small pick for control, stay relaxed even at speed (full-arm tension risks tendonitis), stop on pain, keep fretting fingers close to the fretboard, don't lock the wrist or default to full-arm motion. — BERNTH, [[Extreme Guitar Speed in 5 EASY Steps (everybody can do this!)]]
- **Fixed notes-per-string picking drills (pure 2-, 3-, or 7-note-per-string) don't transfer to real material, which mixes counts unpredictably — deliberately practice mixed-pattern workouts as their own category, plus a named 7/4 speed-burst string-transition exercise.** Direct personal evidence: arranging a Paganini caprice for guitar exposed that pure fixed-count drilling hadn't prepared him for the piece's irregular string transitions. The named speed-burst exercise runs in 7/4 (odd meter forces timing focus alongside the technical load) with three escalating blocks per measure: tremolo picking on one string (easy) → a string transition (harder) → one note on a higher string then back down (named one of the hardest transition types to drill). Also debunks two speed myths: linear "+2-5 BPM per day" tempo climbs don't reliably reach a target tempo, and bigger/more forceful picking motions don't produce more speed (and risk tendonitis) — the real fix is short bursts outside the comfort zone, tolerating early sloppiness, then connecting bursts together. — BERNTH, [[6 Guitar Exercises You're Probably Doing WRONG]]
- **Six short, combinable 2-string "building block" patterns in A minor, explicitly meant to be mixed and matched rather than played as finished licks — capped with a 120 BPM solo exercise that chains several of them while switching between plain 16ths and 16th-note triplets.** Notable individual patterns: odd note-count-per-string cycling (3-5-5-3 rather than an even split) for an asymmetric feel from simple back-and-forth motion; starting from the HIGHEST note of a 3-note-per-string position instead of the root; and one pattern that deliberately uses two *different* sub-ideas across its two strings rather than repeating one idea, which is what makes it read as more advanced. Core philosophy stated directly: learn each block in isolation first, then get creative recombining them — the patterns are deliberately simple, the skill is in the combination. — BERNTH, [[Learn To SHRED With Just 6 Patterns]]
- **A one-sided pick-escape-direction bias, named as a self-taught-player-specific gap, plus a rhythm/wrist drill that doubles as a fix for it.** Players tend to develop a comfortable pick-escape direction (up or down out of the strings) and default to it, which limits vocabulary and causes real trouble on material requiring the other direction — fix is deliberately including both-direction exercises rather than only the natural one. Separately: funk-style rhythmic dead-note patterns across multiple strings (worked in for "single-note domination," a self-taught rock/metal habit of neglecting chords/strumming) can't be played with a locked or full-arm picking motion, so they double as a wrist-unlocking exercise alongside their rhythm-timing value. — BERNTH, [[5 BIG Mistakes ALL Self-Taught Guitarists Make]]
- **A 5-step progressive wrist-unlocking exercise ladder, plus the unifying principle that one relaxed wrist motion should serve every picking technique rather than each technique getting its own separate motion.** Steps, in order: (1) a dead-note pattern physically impossible to play with full-arm motion, forcing wrist isolation; (2) the same dead-note approach with a harder 1-2-3 rhythmic grouping; (3) 2-note-per-string patterns from chord/arpeggio shapes, training string transitions; (4) 1-note-per-string versions of the same shapes, a harder transition drill; (5) a minor scale switching subdivision mid-exercise (16ths → 16th-note triplets) without tensing the wrist at the denser subdivision. Stated big-picture lesson: most players develop a *different* motion per technique (alternate vs. sweep vs. hybrid) and fall apart switching between them mid-song — train one relaxed motion and apply it everywhere, backed by a combined ~90-second daily drill touching alternate/economy/legato/hybrid/sweep in one pass. — BERNTH, [[5 Picking Tips To Save YEARS Of Guitar Practice]]
- **A third source for this page's through-rests picking discipline, applied to tied notes specifically: a tied note produces no new pick stroke, but the down/up alternation grid must still be tracked mentally through it as if it had struck** — skipping that phantom stroke desyncs the grid and causes inconsistent, unpredictable pick placement later in the phrase even though the tie itself sounds correct on its own. Chose strict alternate picking over economy/sweep/legato specifically because it best matched a source recording's aggressive articulation. — BERNTH, [[Shred Guitarist Reacts To IMPOSSIBLE Funk Song]]
- **A second independent source for this page's ghost-motion-through-rests rule, worked over a syncopated 7/8 riff rather than a scale exercise: fill rests with dead (muted) notes and keep continuous alternate-picking motion going, so correct down/up stroke placement emerges from the motion rather than conscious pre-planning.** Stated reasoning matches this page's own rule directly — at speed it's too hard to pre-calculate stroke direction around irregular rests, so keeping the wrist in constant motion and memorizing where the strokes land through repetition is the reliable path. — BERNTH, [[2 Amazing Hacks For Better Riffs]]
