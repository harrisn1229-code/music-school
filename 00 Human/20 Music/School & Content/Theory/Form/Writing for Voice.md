# Writing for Voice (Text Setting)

Part of [[Music Theory]]. Setting words to music — distinct from [[Vocal Harmony]], which is about choosing intervals for harmony *parts*; this page is about fitting a melody to lyric text in the first place.

## Scansion
**Scansion** = identifying accented vs. unaccented syllables in the text *before* writing melody. It's usually easier to write words first, then set them to music — but some great songs came from music and lyrics together, or even music first, so don't treat the order as a hard rule.

- Long notes and downbeats are for **accented** syllables.
- A rhythm that matches scansion exactly can still feel dull — adding rhythmic variety (an unaccented syllable landing on a long note, a rest for a breath — singers love to breathe) keeps it interesting without breaking the underlying scansion.

## Melody
- Good vocal melodies are **predominantly stepwise**, with occasional leaps — especially leaping *into* an accented syllable.
- The range and shape of the melody should reflect the tone of the text.

## Notation specifics for vocal parts
- **Dynamics go ABOVE the staff** for vocal parts (opposite of instrumental convention, where they go below) — keeps them clear of the lyrics.
- **Melisma** — a single syllable sung over more than one note, shown with a slur.
- **Word extension line** — when a melisma's last syllable falls on a long held note, extend it with a line so the syllable's duration is clear.
- Separate syllables within a word using a **single centered hyphen**.
- **Don't draw barlines between staves** on vocal parts — makes the lyrics harder to read.

## Accompaniment
- Give the vocal part **room to breathe** — if the melody goes high, have the accompaniment go low.
- Avoid doubling the vocal line outright in the accompaniment — move in **parallel 3rds or 6ths**, or in **counterpoint**, instead.
- Add accompaniment motion during the voice's **long notes or rests** — keeps momentum going when the voice is holding still.
