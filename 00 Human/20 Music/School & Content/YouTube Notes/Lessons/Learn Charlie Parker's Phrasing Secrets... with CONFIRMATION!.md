## Synopsis

Nathan Borton's phrase-by-phrase deconstruction of Charlie Parker's head melody to "Confirmation" — a follow-up to an earlier Parker-melody video (not yet in the vault). Borton walks the A section and B section of the tune roughly in order, pulling out named, reusable devices from each phrase rather than treating the melody as a fixed thing to memorize: the 6th interval as a Parker signature, a minor ii–V lick built from a triad plus the 6th, blues language layered directly into bebop lines, guide-tone connective lines (♭9-down-to-root, 4th-resolving-to-3rd), a 4th→3rd tension-and-release device that then jumps to an altered tone, CESH (chromatic embellishment of static harmony) in the B section, and a couple of "closer" phrases Parker (and Borton) reach for to end a line. The video closes on a practice-methodology point that's as valuable as the licks themselves: build a "lick journal," categorize every extracted phrase by type (5–1 phrases, CESH phrases, altered phrases, etc.), and "plug and chug" them over different changes rather than leaving them tied to this one tune — plus a reminder that Parker's edge is as much about **rhythm** (not playing straight consistent eighth notes) as it is about note choice. Borton performs two short written etudes built from the lesson's own material — one over the first half of the A section partway through, one full chorus over the whole tune at the end — modeling the "write your own etude from the vocabulary" step he asks the viewer to do. Companion piece to Borton's other Parker-melody breakdowns and rhythm-changes/lick-connecting videos already in the vault (see Routed to).

## What's taught

**A section, phrase by phrase:**

- **Opening phrase — a 6th-interval leap right off the top of the melody.** Borton calls the 6th "a very important interval in bebop" and a good interval to train the ear on generally, not just here. **Practice recipe given directly:** (1) practice scales in 6ths; (2) once that's comfortable, start a line with a 6th leap, or place one at the end of a line; (3) as a generative drill, play a major scale straight up or down and randomly jump a 6th somewhere inside it — sometimes a usable line falls out of the random jump itself. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §*From videos*, [[Intervals]]
- **A minor ii–V lick combining the 6th with a triad.** Over a minor ii–V (**E half-diminished (Em7♭5) → A7**, resolving toward D minor): the line walks up the **root of the ii chord**, then the **3rd of the ii chord**, then descends a **G minor triad** — G, B♭, D are the ♭3, ♭5, and ♭7 of Em7♭5 respectively, so the "triad" is really the chord's own upper structure spelled as a minor triad off the ♭3. From there the line jumps from the **root of the dominant (A7) up a 6th to its 3rd** — the same 6th-leap device as the opener, now landing specifically on a guide tone. Borton calls this "a good two-five phrase to learn in as many different keys as possible." → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Guide Tones]], [[Bebop Vocabulary]]
- **Blues mixed directly into a bebop line over B♭7.** Parker plays around the **F blues scale** over B♭7 — F is the **5th of B♭7**, so the device generalizes as: *over any dominant chord, you can play the blues scale rooted on the 5th of that dominant* (not the blues scale rooted on the chord's own root). → [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]] §*From videos*, [[Blues Form and Harmony]]
- **A dominant line resolved two ways — plain 5–1, or stretched into a full ii–V–I.** Borton takes one dominant-chord line and shows it can simply resolve as a **5–1 phrase**, or the same idea can become a **full ii–V–I** by prepending a short line before it — the same lick, two different harmonic scopes depending how much of the progression you want to outline. → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Turnarounds]]
- **A 5–1 phrase built from guide-tone connective lines plus the 6th.** This one layers three devices at once: the 6th interval (again), a short line from the **♭9 of the dominant down to the root**, and a short line from the **4th of the dominant resolving to the 3rd**. Both of the short connective runs are explicitly named **guide tone lines** in the transcript. → [[Guide Tones]] §*From videos*
- **The 4th→3rd resolution device, extended into landing on an altered tone.** Two-part device: (1) start on the **4th of the chord** (major or dominant) and resolve it down to the **3rd** — Borton flags this is "kind of a bad thing to do" on paper (the 4th is an avoid tone against a major/dominant 3rd) but says Parker does it constantly, and the resolution itself creates the tension-and-release; (2) from there, **jump to an altered tone of the dominant chord being played over** — named options given: **♯9, ♭9, ♭5, or ♯5**. Worked example: over **A7**, the line lands specifically on the **♭5**. Confirms the identical device already logged from a different Borton video (see [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §*From videos*, the "THIS Song Turns Students Into BEBOP Pros" bullet) — a second, independent instance of the same 4th-to-3rd-then-altered-tone move. → [[Altered Chords]], [[Bebop Vocabulary]]
- **More blues-over-dominant variation, then an A-section closer.** After the altered-tone move, Parker "mostly plays around with different variations of the same ideas such as playing blues over the dominant" (the F-blues-scale-over-B♭7 device again, transposed to whichever dominant is sounding). The A section then closes with **a strong 5–1 phrase** Borton explicitly recommends as a general line-ending device, independent of this tune. → [[Turnarounds]]
- **A written etude over the first half of the A section**, built entirely from the devices covered to that point — modeling the "compose your own etude from extracted vocabulary" step (see Practice methodology below).

**B section:**

- **CESH — chromatic embellishment of static harmony.** Named and defined directly in the transcript: take one note (in the worked example, **the root of the ii chord**) and move it **up or down chromatically through the changes** while the underlying harmony sits still (or moves slower than the chromatic line). Parker uses the device to open the B section, and Borton gives a second worked example of the same idea afterward. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §6 *CESH*, [[Chromatic Bass Lines]], [[Non-Harmonic Tones]]

Not chord-specific in the transcript beyond "closing phrase," captured qualitatively rather than with invented note names.
- **The B section closes on another 5–1 line**, described as "super cool" — the same 5–1-as-closer logic as the A section, confirming it's a recurring structural habit (end phrases/sections on 5–1) rather than a one-off.

**Practice methodology (the video's real payoff, per Borton):**

- **Build a "lick journal."** After extracting vocabulary from a melody, don't leave it embedded in the tune — **categorize every phrase by type** (all the 5–1 phrases together, all the CESH phrases together, all the altered phrases together, and so on). Stated reasoning: organized-by-function vocabulary is dramatically easier to digest, easier to get "in your fingers" ("in your plane" — captions garble "playing"), and easier to improvise with, because you can "plug and chug" a category of phrase over any changes that call for it rather than searching a whole transcribed melody for the right one. This is a **collection-first variant** of the vocabulary-extraction methods already in [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]] — see the new §*A collection-first variant* added there. → [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]]
- **Rhythm matters as much as note choice — and isn't covered here.** Borton states directly that Parker's edge isn't just theory/note choice, it's rhythm — "make sure that your lines aren't just consistent eighth notes" — and floats a dedicated rhythm-focused follow-up video (unclear if ever made; not found in the vault as of this pass). Flagged here as an open thread rather than expanded on, since the source video itself doesn't develop it. → [[Groove and Microtiming]], [[Syncopation and Metric Displacement]]
- **A full-chorus written etude over the whole tune**, closing the video — the payoff demonstration of "write your own etude using these lines," performed over the entire form rather than just the earlier A-section fragment.
