# Blur's unique chord progression

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WQdeCWnREos)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WQdeCWnREos)

## Synopsis

A close analytical read of Blur's "Coffee & TV" (written by guitarist Graham Coxon, not Damon Albarn), whose verse progression Bennett believes is unique to this one song — walking through each chord's function and, notably, how Coxon arrived at the progression's strangest chord almost by guitar-fingering accident rather than by theory.

## Techniques

- **The progression, in the key of E major**: V (B) → an ambiguous "Aadd♯9" chord → I (E) → ♭III (G, borrowed from E minor) → ♭II add♯11 (Fmaj7add♯11) → ♭V (B♭) → ♭♭VII/chromatic mediant (D♭) → back to B.
- **The "Aadd♯9" chord, explained**: notated as A major with a sharpened 9th (C natural) stacked on top — meaning it contains BOTH the major 3rd (C♯) and the minor 3rd (C natural) of A simultaneously, a deliberately dissonant major/minor blend. On the original record the clash is subtle/blended (much starker when played cleanly on piano); some published sheet music simplifies it (incorrectly) to a plain A minor by dropping the offending C♯. **Coxon's own account** (quoted from an interview): for years he didn't fully understand what the chord was doing — "this chord is totally wrong... but no one said 'geez that's awful'... it creates a little bit of weird tension, a note that does fit but doesn't quite fit."
- **♭III (G) borrowed from the parallel minor** — a standard modal-interchange move, blending E major and E minor.
- **The Fmaj7add♯11 chord, explained via guitar fingering, not music theory**: Coxon played a normal F barre chord on the first fret but left the top two strings open — instead of fretting C and F on top (a plain F chord), the open strings ring B and E, producing a "major 7 add ♯11" color that's unusual on paper but an obvious, almost accidental outcome of *not* barring fully. Those same added notes (B, E) both belong to the song's home key of E major, which is what lets this otherwise non-diatonic F chord blend into the key convincingly.
- **B♭ (the ♭5 chord) via circle-of-fifths logic, not key membership**: B♭ doesn't belong to E major's key at all, but moving TO it from the preceding Fmaj7♯11 chord is a fifth-step (F→B♭) — per [[Circle of Fifths]], any two chords a fifth apart sound consonant/logical in the moment even when the destination chord is totally foreign to the surrounding key. A "local" relationship overriding a "global" one.
- **D♭, a chromatic mediant relative to B♭**: B♭ and D♭ are a minor third apart, both major-quality chords — the textbook shape of a **chromatic mediant** (see [[Chromatic Mediants and Pop Modulation]]) — and they share a common tone (F) that keeps the ear anchored across the otherwise-foreign root motion.
