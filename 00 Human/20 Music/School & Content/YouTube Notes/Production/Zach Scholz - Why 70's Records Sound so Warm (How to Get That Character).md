# Zach Scholz - Why 70's Records Sound so Warm (How to Get That Character)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s6eGa5NOTYg)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s6eGa5NOTYg)

## Synopsis

The single most conceptually important video in this batch. Zach Scholz opens with an A/B test between two of his own mixes — one recorded 100% digitally, one printed to tape — and asks viewers to guess which is which. **The digital mix actually reads as MORE characterful**, and his point is exactly that surprise: nearly everything people credit to tape (personality, warmth, texture) actually comes from decisions made *before* tape ever enters the chain — track-count discipline, broad-stroke by-ear EQ, and above all early commitment. He backs this with his own history (a 2021 session recorded on almost no gear that still has more character than a "proper" tape session), a very specific EQ walkthrough on his TASCAM M-30's 3-knob per-channel EQ, the well-known "Ramble On" muddy-acoustic-guitar anecdote from Led Zeppelin II, and — most actionably — a concrete Ableton technique for simulating tape's forced-commitment workflow entirely digitally (bus an instrument to an audio track, arm + mute it, print every real-time tweak permanently). His thesis: tape is real and does something, but the workflow/mindset shift is the actual source of "warmth," and that shift is available for free, no tape machine required.

## Techniques

- **A/B test result: his 100%-digital mix sounded MORE characterful than his tape-printed mix** — the video's whole premise. Tape didn't add the "warmth" people assume it did; whatever made the digital mix compelling was baked in during mixing, before any tape stage. Directly relevant to the "recreating X without tape" videos elsewhere in this batch — same conclusion from a different angle.
- **His 2021 near-zero-gear session (broken desk, a couple guitars, a MIDI keyboard, a computer — no tape machine) still holds up, because the *limitation* forced creativity** — not having a tape machine at the time pushed him to think more outside the box while mixing rather than relying on hardware to "fix" a boring sound; re-recording it today he'd keep ~90% of the original approach and treat tape as "glimmer on top," not the source of the character.
- **Core 70s constraint #1 — near-zero revisability: EQ/compression/levels were locked in before or during tracking, because once it hit tape it was essentially permanent** — going back to fix a mistake was costly and added noise, so decisions had to be right the first time. Also: much lower headroom than digital, so gain mistakes meant real distortion/blowouts, not a clip warning.
- **The "Ramble On" (Led Zeppelin II) anecdote — the acoustic guitar stem is genuinely muddy and distorted in isolation, and that imperfection is part of what makes the finished song read as iconic** — small baked-in character on individual tracks compounds into the record's overall identity when they're combined, rather than each part being "clean" on its own.
- **Core 70s constraint #2 — track-count discipline (≤24 tracks) forced deliberate sound choices instead of stacking to compensate** — his own before/after: he used to stack many pads on top of each other until something felt "okay"; now he deliberately tries to build a sound in as few tracks as possible — e.g. on a recent 80s-style track, instead of layering more synth basses to thicken a part that felt thin, he added a *piano* underneath the synth bass instead, and it worked better than another layer of the same timbre. → [[Layering and Stacking]]
- **Digital-workflow simulation of committing to tape: print tracks together instead of leaving them as an infinitely-adjustable bus** — rather than just busing instruments together (still fully undoable), physically print the busaed result to a new audio track so it becomes a committed, fixed element — the same idea behind bussing tracks straight to a physical tape machine (see his companion video [[Zach Scholz - Using Analog Tape to Glue a Mix Together]]), just done without hardware.
- **EQ under real 70s constraints was broad-stroke and done entirely by ear, with zero visual feedback** — demonstrated on his TASCAM M-30's per-channel EQ: some old mixers only offer a fixed low shelf + high shelf, so scooping the mids meant boosting BOTH shelves and letting the mid-range read as relatively scooped by contrast, not cutting the mids directly. His own M-30 is a step up with **3 knobs per channel: two swept-frequency bands (60 Hz–1.5 kHz and 1 kHz–10 kHz) plus a fixed 12.5 kHz shelf** — and whatever gets dialed in prints permanently as it records, no redo.
- **Ear-training exercise: practice EQ moves on a simple, feature-limited EQ (a Neve 1073-style 3-band) instead of a full surgical/dynamic EQ** — he specifically recommends Arturia's 1073 replica OR Logic's stock equivalent (both work, no need to pay), used purely to train your ear without visual (spectrum/numeric) feedback; the skill transfers back to make you sharper on precise modern EQs afterward.
- **Concrete Ableton "commit to tape" technique, no hardware required:** (1) set the instrument track's output to an unused bus (he uses bus 10), (2) create a new audio track with its input set to that same bus, (3) arm the audio track and mute it so you only hear the source while adjusting, (4) hit record — every real-time tweak you make gets printed permanently to that audio track as you make it, simulating the "no going back" discipline of recording to tape entirely inside the DAW. → [[Gain Staging]]
- **Central thesis on commitment: unlimited undo/punch-in/redo access is a double-edged sword** — endless revision access breeds second-guessing and a nagging sense of dissatisfaction; deciding something is "done" and moving on is, in his view, the single biggest practical benefit analog gear brings to a workflow — bigger than any tone/coloration it adds.
- **Closing framing: tape is real and does add character, but the workflow change it forces is the actual prize** — "stop with the endless edits, don't overproduce your tracks, instead focus on the playing, the sounds you're creating" — a mindset takeaway that applies with or without owning any tape gear at all.
