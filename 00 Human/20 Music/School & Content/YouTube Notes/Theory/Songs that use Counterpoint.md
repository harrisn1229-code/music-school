# Songs that use Counterpoint

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BGaRq5vi-gA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BGaRq5vi-gA)

## Synopsis

What counterpoint is (independently-moving voices that still produce harmony together), how it contrasts with homophony (the texture most pop/rock actually uses — one clear melody, everything else blending into a unified harmonic bed), and a survey of how pop/rock songs use counterpoint in a "diluted," homophony-supported way rather than the pure Baroque form. Covers the classic parallel-fifths/octaves avoidance rule and closes with a fugue explainer.

## Techniques

- **Definition**: each voice moves with its own independent contour, yet the voices still sound harmonically related together — contrasted directly against homophony, where all voices blend into one unified motion (most pop/rock is "melody-dominated homophony": one clear tune, everything else forming a single harmonic bed underneath it).
- **Pop counterpoint is usually diluted, not pure**: real-world pop counterpoint (e.g. The Beach Boys' vocal stacks) is typically still supported by underlying chords, unlike pure Baroque counterpoint. Demonstrated directly on "God Only Knows" (Beach Boys): the actual recorded vocal counterpoint sits over harmonic accompaniment; stripped down to JUST the interacting vocal lines with no chordal support, the texture becomes far more exposed/delicate — a direct A/B illustration of diluted vs. pure counterpoint.
- **"Introduce separately, then layer" — the most common pop-song counterpoint device**: two (or more) distinct melodies are each introduced on their own earlier in the song, then combined later — satisfying because the listener already knows both melodies individually before hearing them interact. "For No One" (Beatles) — verse vocal melody + a French horn melody, introduced separately then layered (arguably 3-part counterpoint if the bass line counts as a third voice). "Supermassive Black Hole" (Muse) — two melodies introduced separately then combined; one of the two is itself a homophonic 4-part vocal harmony that still reads as ONE voice against the other melody, making the combination 2-part counterpoint overall (a direct example of homophony nested inside a contrapuntal texture).
- **Escalating layered counterpoint**: the middle section of "Paranoid Android" (Radiohead) builds from 2 voices (an octave apart, with a 3rd weaving between) up through 4 progressively-added vocal lines across four rotations. "Country House" (Blur) — each successive chorus adds one more counter-melody (2nd chorus adds one, final chorus adds a brass counter-melody), building song energy purely through accumulating contrapuntal layers.
- **Counterpoint carrying textual/dramatic meaning**: Simon & Garfunkel's "Scarborough Fair/Canticle" weaves a new original anti-war lyric melody ("The Side of a Hill"/"Canticle") into the traditional "Scarborough Fair" tune, creating deliberate lyrical irony between the two simultaneous texts. "One Day More" (*Les Misérables*) — each character introduced singing their own verse in turn, lines progressively layer into dense multi-voice counterpoint, then suddenly resolve into full unison on one shared lyric ("Tomorrow we'll discover...") — the unison moment used dramatically to underline the one thing all the characters share despite their conflicting situations.
- **Fugue**: a formalized, highly developed contrapuntal form — an initial subject stated by one voice, then a second voice enters with the same subject while the first voice continues developing independently; more voices progressively enter the same way. Worked example: Bach's Fugue in G minor builds to 4 simultaneous voices (2 hands + organ pedals for the 4th). Framed as "a much more complicated round" — same basic idea as a childhood round (staggered entries of the same melody), vastly more elaborate.
- **The classic counterpoint convention: avoid parallel/consecutive 5ths and octaves.** When two voices move in parallel 5ths (same direction, same rhythm, fixed 5th apart throughout), the ear perceives them as one fused voice rather than two independent ones — destroying the contrapuntal effect, since counterpoint's whole point is voice independence. Demonstrated with a worked 3-voice example and its fix (adjusting one voice's notes to break the parallel motion). Explicitly framed as a stylistic convention for classic Baroque-style counterpoint, not an absolute rule.
