# Repetition with Manipulation (Getting Past a Plateau)

Jared's (Sound Guitar Lessons) own named practice method, distilled from Anders Ericsson's *Peak: Secrets from the New Science of Expertise* — the research-backed answer to *why* progress stalls at all and the concrete discipline for preventing/breaking through it. This is the theory layer underneath two notes already in the vault built on the same underlying mechanism from a different angle: [[Technique Is Part of the Path (The Red-Flag Repair Loop)]] (Jared's own "go directly to the exact spot" mid-session method — this note supplies the Ericsson research citation for exactly that move) and [[Interleaved Practice, Chunking, and Deliberate-Practice Drills]] (Daniel Seriff's "target the trouble spot directly, don't restart from the top," arrived at independently on a different channel, also citing *Peak*). Where those two notes are session-mechanics toolkits, this note is closer to [[The Practice Priority Pyramid]]'s Stage 5 (Deliberate/challenging practice) — it's the research case for *why* that stage is the hardest one and why almost everyone avoids it.

## Why plateaus happen

Improvement is driven by **challenge**, not by repetition volume. When you first attempt something new, you're forced to think — genuinely challenged — and that's what produces rapid early gains. The instant a skill reaches a "functional/acceptable" level, the brain (evolved to conserve effort and shortcut anything solved) stops treating it as a problem to think through — it becomes a formula, a routine, a habit — and progress halts **at that exact moment**, not gradually afterward. Doing more reps past that point doesn't help, because volume was never the variable driving the gains; novelty/challenge was. Ericsson's own supporting data point: doctors 20–30 years into practice score *worse* on objective performance measures than doctors 2–3 years out of school, because day-to-day practice of medicine mostly doesn't challenge them anymore — a skill can decline, not just stall, once it stops being pushed.

**Don't mistake a plateau for a real skill ceiling.** Ericsson: "When you first start learning something new it is normal to see rapid or at least steady improvement, and when that improvement stops it is natural to believe you've hit some sort of implacable limit... This is the major reason that people in every area stop improving." Jared's gloss: this is the actual mechanism behind "fixed mindset" — not an inherent hard ceiling, but a belief that becomes self-fulfilling the moment you stop pushing because of it. Treat every plateau as "task stopped being challenging," never as "this is my limit."

## Find the actual weak component, not the whole passage

Ericsson's "Steve" case study (a research subject extending digit-span memory): what looked like a hard ceiling turned out to be **one or two specific problem groups within the string**, not overall capacity maxing out — fixing exactly those groups freed up the rest. Same shape in guitar practice: "any reasonably complex skill will involve a variety of components... when you reach a point at which you are having difficulty getting better, it will be just one or two of the components... not all of them."

**Diagnostic method:** push a little past your current comfort/ability and watch for what breaks down *first* — that's the real bottleneck. Worked example: speed up tempo past clean and note which spot goes wrong first, not the whole passage. Then go **one level deeper** than "that spot is hard" — is it a specific finger's technique, a pull-off, picking-hand tone, or time feel? Per Jared, correctly isolating the actual cause (rather than the felt/assumed one) is "the biggest thing" private teaching does — worked anecdote: a student was convinced a hard passage was a left-hand problem; the actual cause was the right hand missing a string, and once *that* got fixed the passage cleared immediately.

**Ericsson's full recipe, stated directly:** "First figure out exactly what is holding you back, what mistakes are you making and when. Push yourself well outside your comfort zone and see what breaks down first. Then design a practice technique aimed at improving that particular weakness." — and this is explicitly *not* the default: "trying this and that and hoping something works" is named as the common failure mode, even among experienced teachers.

## The method: repetition with manipulation

Repetition is necessary to internalize anything, but **identical repetition stops driving improvement once it stops being challenging** — so repeat the material while deliberately varying it: a different spot on the fretboard, a different string set, a different position, a different key. This is the same underlying idea as [[The Practice Priority Pyramid]]'s **Rotation/Variation** mechanic and [[Interleaved Practice, Chunking, and Deliberate-Practice Drills]]'s interleaving, but framed at the level of a single piece of material rather than a whole session's item rotation — "how else can I know this, where else can I play this."

- **Stated personal motivation, not just plateau-avoidance:** Jared is explicitly wary of anything becoming a fully automatic habit — he feels it disconnects him from intentional, expressive playing. Automaticity is treated as a risk to guard against, not only a plateau symptom.
- **It's supposed to feel uncomfortable.** Manipulation works precisely because it keeps you out of the comfort zone that causes plateaus — nobody finds it naturally pleasant, though it can become exciting/stimulating. Stated payoff over time: a genuine craving for the challenge, where a session with nothing to strive for starts to feel wrong.

## Design exercises with strict, specific, pass/fail parameters

A self-designed exercise only works as a diagnostic if success/failure is unambiguous:

- **Concrete examples:** "play this three times in a row at this tempo without buzzing a single note" (one buzz = fail, three clean at tempo = pass — no gray area); "improvise over this progression using only chord tones 3 and 5" (won't produce a great solo — that's not the point — it isolates one specific musicianship muscle, same logic as a weighted exercise targeting one muscle group).
- **Manipulate the rule once the current version is solid**, to keep generating fresh challenge instead of re-running a drill that's already stopped teaching anything — swap chord tones 3/5 for 7/1, change the time signature from 4/4 to 3/4 on the same material, etc. *(General starting point — no fixed rep count given; the constraint is what matters, not a specific number.)*
- **Worked example — scale patterns instead of "just run the scale."** Running a scale straight up and down produces solos that sound like "playing a scale." Fix: a written melodic pattern that forces jumping around inside the scale shape rather than moving linearly. Jared's own free resource for this specifically: a downloadable PDF of his top 3 pentatonic scale patterns for more melodic soloing (off-platform link in the video description, not captured here).

## Self-diagnosis prompt (usable as a standing practice-journal question)

Three-part audit, directly mirroring Ericsson's recipe above: (1) where in your playing have you hit a plateau or felt stale, (2) what do you actually think the weak spot causing it is, (3) what specific exercise could you design to target exactly that. Pairs well with [[Interleaved Practice, Chunking, and Deliberate-Practice Drills]]'s forward-planning practice journal — this question is a good one to answer *before* a session rather than after.
