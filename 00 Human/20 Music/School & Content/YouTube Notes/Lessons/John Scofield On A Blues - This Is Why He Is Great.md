# John Scofield On A Blues - This Is Why He Is Great

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y6n9FE7PDBU)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y6n9FE7PDBU)

## Synopsis

Jens Larsen breaks down what makes John Scofield's playing instantly identifiable, using three phrases pulled from Scofield's solo on "You Speak My Language" — a B♭ blues from the album *I Can See Your House From Here* (a collaboration with Pat Metheny, also featuring Bill Stewart and Steve Swallow, who later formed the core of the John Scofield Trio). Larsen frames the video as a "why he sounds like him" analysis rather than a lick-by-lick transcription lesson: he opens by naming rhythm/timing looseness, dynamic range, legato technique used musically, and a sparser note count (vs. contemporaries like Metheny or John McLaughlin) as the four traits that define the Scofield sound before he does, then digs into three worked phrases to show how those traits actually show up on the fretboard. The through-line across all three examples is **interval- and dyad-based, polyphonic improvisation** — playing two independent melodic voices at once rather than single-note lines or full chords — combined with **harmonic reinterpretation** (skipping expected bebop resolutions, playing lines pulled directly out of a chord voicing rather than a scale, and privately reharmonizing a standard turnaround). This is a short, dense, spoken-analysis video — no tab or notation on screen described in the transcript, and some guitar-demonstration moments are inherently un-transcribable from captions alone (marked below), but every verbal explanation of what's happening harmonically is captured. Compare/contrast with [[Jens Larsen - Practice Advice From Chick Corea]], where the same channel names Scofield (alongside Bill Frisell and Jim Hall) as an exemplar of non-mechanical, "follows the logic of the last phrase" phrasing — this video is the technical mechanism behind that same observation.

## What's taught

**Framing — four traits that define the Scofield sound (stated up front, before the worked examples):**
- **Rhythm and timing looseness** — "quite loose," able to sit noticeably behind the beat (laid back) but also lock in tightly with the group when needed; not a fixed relationship to the beat but a chosen one, moment to moment.
- **Extreme use of dynamics** — large volume swings within a single phrase, including notes that trail off to almost nothing (heard directly in Example 1, below).
- **Legato technique used musically, not just for speed** — heavy use of slurred/legato phrasing, but described as deployed with clear musical intent rather than as a technical flex.
- **Sparser note count than his contemporaries** — fewer notes per phrase than players like Pat Metheny or John McLaughlin; described as part of what makes his melodic language distinctly his own rather than a derivative of the more note-dense mainstream jazz-fusion vocabulary of the era.
- **Stated overall thesis:** phrasing and dynamics, more than note/scale choice itself, are what make a line "sound like Scofield" — the same notes played by someone else with different touch/dynamics would not read as his.

**Example 1 — the opening phrase of the solo (over B♭7/E♭7), timestamp ~2:00–3:15:**
- **Built from intervals and polyphonic improvisation from the very first phrase** — Larsen calls this immediately, unmistakably identifiable as Scofield because of it. Scofield "is not too often playing complete chords" — he plays two-note intervals/dyads very frequently instead of full chord grips, and this solo is "definitely not an exception."
- **Two independent voices moving at once, not just an interval dressing on a single-note line.** The phrase has a **lower layer descending chromatically** while a **separate, independent upper layer plays a blues phrase on top of it** — genuinely two voices moving simultaneously, which Larsen distinguishes explicitly from simply adding intervals to an otherwise single-note line: "this example is using more than intervals because it's really just really polyphonic."
- **Opening interval move over E♭7:** starts on a dissonant 2nd interval, moves to the **tritone on E♭** (banging out E♭7 using its two guide tones — 3rd and ♭7), then only the **top note** of that dyad keeps moving while the bottom holds — landing on a note read either as the ♭4/♯3 (a chromatic passing tone) or a plain **chromatic E natural** (Larsen leans toward hearing it as the latter), then down to a 4th interval.
- **Dynamics inside the phrase, demonstrated concretely:** the last note of this first phrase is played almost inaudibly quiet — "the last note is almost gone" — a direct, audible instance of the "huge difference in volume between different notes" Larsen names as core to the Scofield sound, not an abstract description.
- **Contrast named directly: Bill Frisell does the same polyphonic-interval device, but sounds completely different** — Larsen's point is that the *phrasing and dynamics* applied to the technique, not the technique itself, are what make it identifiably Scofield vs. identifiably Frisell — reinforcing the video's stated thesis.

**Example 2 — later in the same first chorus, over the turnaround into the final cadence (C minor / F7 → D half-diminished → G7 → back toward the ii, B♭ blues), timestamp roughly ~3:15–7:30 (est., not stated exactly):**
- **Opens sitting inside the D half-diminished / G7 chord tones with minimal embellishment** — "clearly in the chord... not a lot happening here."
- **The E over G7 is ambiguous by design** — could be heard as a diminished-scale color tone on G7, or as a leading tone up to E♭ (the chord that follows); Larsen states directly he can't tell definitively which Scofield is "thinking" from the line alone, and treats that ambiguity as part of the point rather than a gap in the analysis.
- **A deliberately un-bebop "bebop skip."** Standard bebop vocabulary here would run 3rd-down-to-5th of the chord (E♭ down to G on C minor) and then continue up to A before landing on F7. **Scofield instead skips up** at that point rather than continuing to the expected A — an explicit, named device for breaking the flow so the line doesn't read as a stock bebop lick, even though it's built from the same underlying chord-tone material.
- **Blue note used as an out-of-chord melodic anchor, not resolved immediately.** The line's next note is the **high E as a blue note over F7♭5** — a note that "doesn't actually fit in the chord" but is chosen anyway for its blue-note color rather than strict chord-tone correctness; from there it descends B → slide to A → slide/pull-off from F to E♭ → B → A → resolving on F, all interpreted as F7♭5 material.
- **Returns to the opening interval device to close the phrase**, now over B♭7: the A♭–B♭ interval reused from earlier in the phrase, then the turnaround moves to G7, where the **voice leads to a major 3rd on G** — landing on a note that clearly announces the G7 sound. Two voices again move in **contrary/opposite directions** through this stretch — Larsen ties this directly back to *why* Scofield leans on two-voice/interval playing so heavily: it gives him independent material to move against itself.

**Example 3 — a related phrase later in the solo, over E♭7 into a turnaround reinterpretation, timestamp roughly ~8:30–13:00 (est., not stated exactly):**
- **Wide-range, deliberately unpredictable melody, stated as the goal rather than a side effect.** Larsen frames this explicitly: not trying to write a vocal-like, singable line, but "creating a different kind of sound" through range and note choice that resist prediction.
- **Opens on E♭7 with the root and the 11th (the "shell 11"), emphasizing the 4th degree** — an unusual melodic emphasis for a blues, since the 4th degree isn't a typical target note; the ascending tritone-power-chord-like shape then descends, sliding down to D♭, into a **flat-5 arpeggio**, ending on E → E♭ — i.e., built entirely from the IV chord's own diminished/altered color rather than a straight blues-scale run over it.
- **Full harmonic reinterpretation of the turnaround, not just melodic reinterpretation.** Standard B♭ blues turnaround here would run: IV → ♯IV° (or a similar passing diminished) → back to I → ii (D half-diminished) → V (G7) → back to I. **Scofield instead:** stays ambiguous on the IV (possibly thinking a straight IV7, possibly an A7-type substitute — Larsen states directly you can't tell for certain from the line alone), skips the expected B♭ landing entirely and goes straight to **D half-diminished → G7**, and **doesn't play the anticipated C7 on beat 1 — he starts it a few eighth notes early**, i.e. anticipating the ii chord ahead of the barline rather than landing it on the downbeat.
- **A line built directly out of a chord voicing shape, not out of a scale.** Larsen calls this "really typical for the way Scofield plays" and notes it's a device more commonly associated with solo chord-melody playing than with single-note soloing: the three notes of this passage come **directly out of a drop-3 D half-diminished voicing**, played as a broken chord/arpeggiated line rather than derived from a scale run. The same principle repeats immediately after for the **G7♭5** that follows — another line pulled straight from a voicing shape rather than a scale.
- **Large, unpredictable intervals as a deliberate device, with an explicit Monk comparison.** Playing lines this way naturally introduces wide, non-stepwise interval leaps; Larsen says this — plus the loose timing feel — reminds him specifically of **Thelonious Monk**, and separately traces the loose-timing feel toward older, "old-fashioned" blues players like **Muddy Waters** and **John Lee Hooker** ("Johnny Hooker" in the auto-transcript, almost certainly Hooker misheard/mis-captioned).
- **Ambiguity between C7 and C minor over the ii chord, played both ways across the solo.** Over this D half-diminished → G7♭5 turnaround, Larsen hears the target functionally as **C7** even without it being stated outright, and notes Scofield actually plays literal **C7 several times elsewhere in the solo** on the same ii-chord slot, but also plays it as **plain C minor** at other points — going back and forth between the two rather than committing to one reading, i.e. deliberately keeping the ii chord's quality (minor vs. dominant) harmonically ambiguous across repeats of the form.

**Reference/context named in the video (not technique, but useful cross-links):** the album *I Can See Your House From Here* pairs Scofield with Pat Metheny on guitar, plus Bill Stewart (drums) and Steve Swallow (bass) — the same rhythm section that later became the John Scofield Trio. Larsen cross-references his own separate video analyzing a Pat Metheny solo on "How Insensitive" from the same album.
