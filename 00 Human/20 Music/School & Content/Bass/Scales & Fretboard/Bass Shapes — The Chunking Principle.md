# Bass Shapes — The Chunking Principle

## The chunking principle — why shapes work

- **A shape is a visual/finger pattern on the neck representing a fixed set of pitch *relationships*, not a fixed set of pitches** — because it's movable to any root, the same shape reproduces the same music starting on a different note every time.
- **Grounded in psychologist George Miller's concept of chunking:** combining many small, hard-to-remember bits of information into one larger, easier-to-remember chunk. Demoed with a random letter sequence (hard to memorize) rearranged into recognizable words (the identical letters, suddenly easy) — the same letters, chunked differently.
- **Applied to bass:** instead of frantically recalling individual note names and theory in real time, a bassist who's internalized a shape treats the whole shape as one pre-computed decision — "find the root, imagine the magic dots, plant your fingers" — freeing attention for everything else happening in the song.
- **Proof the ear tracks relationships, not absolute pitches — the "Another One Bites the Dust" demo:** two completely different sets of notes both read unmistakably as the same bassline, because the *distances between the notes* (the shape) are identical in both — the pitches themselves are irrelevant to recognition, only the relationships are.

## Why bass specifically is built for this (fretboard-uniformity physics)

- **Piano:** five black keys and seven white keys per octave means the keyboard isn't uniform — moving a shape to a new key doesn't reliably reproduce the same music, and a chord's physical hand-shape can look completely different in a different key (worked concretely below, § Shape 3).
- **Vocalists:** no visual or tactile reference at all — nothing to chunk onto.
- **Guitar:** standard tuning is *almost* uniform (perfect fourths between adjacent strings) but breaks that pattern once, between the G and B strings (a major third instead of a fourth) — so a shape doesn't relocate identically everywhere on a guitar neck the way it does on bass.
- **Bass (standard 4-string, E-A-D-G):** every adjacent string pair is tuned in the identical interval (a perfect fourth) with no exception — so any shape found anywhere on the neck gives back the exact same music moved anywhere else on the neck. This is the concrete acoustic reason shapes are an unusually clean chunking tool on bass specifically, more so than on almost any other instrument.

## Where a shape's root comes from

- **Root notes come from whatever chord the guitar or keyboard player is playing** at that moment in the song — that's the bassist's baseline job.
- **Playing just those roots ("chugging") is already a complete, valid bassline** — worked over an E, B, C♯m, A progression (a "Don't Stop Believin'"-style chord sequence) played as plain root notes only.
- **Shapes are the next step up from that baseline:** once the roots are covered, a shape gives a structured way to find *non-root* notes to sprinkle around each root and build something more distinctive than root notes alone, without having to invent those notes from scratch each time.

## Shape 1 — Root-fifth

- **Geometry:** up one string, up two frets from the root. Works identically over a major or minor chord — no need to know which. → full theory (why root-fifth specifically is acoustically stable, the 3:2 frequency ratio, a style-adaptation rhythm formula): [[Root-Fifth Bass Lines and the 3-Step Style Rhythm Formula]].
- **Stated payoff:** the fifth "adds some movement and spice to the bassline without making the harmony sound complicated," and it works "90 percent of the time."
- **Worked demo:** "Rikki Don't Lose That Number" (Steely Dan).
- **Jam demo, over an A–D–E chord progression:** the identical shape, stamped onto each root with nothing else changed, produces a usable rock-flavored line, a country-flavored line, and a more reggae-flavored line — three different genre feels from one shape.

## Shape 2 — Octave

- **Geometry:** up two strings, up two frets from the root (or down two strings/down two frets going the other direction) — the same fret-finding rule as [[Octave Technique — Split-Finger Fretting and Duck-Wrist Prevention]]'s "up two strings and up two frets" heuristic and [[Jamerson's Root-Fifth-Octave Exercise]]'s ingredient 1.
- **Why it's "safe anytime":** an octave is the same note name one register up, so it adds movement without introducing any new note into the harmony — no risk of clashing with the chord.
- **Bonus utility beyond basslines — a note-naming shortcut:** if you already know the name of one note, moving the octave shape gives you the name of the other note for free. Worked: the 3rd fret of the A string is C, so two strings up/two frets up (5th fret of the G string) is also C.
- **Worked demo:** "Hot Pants" (James Brown).
- **Jam demo, over the same A–D–E progression:** a slap-funk-flavored line, a disco-flavored line, and a pop-rock-flavored line, again from the one shape relocated to each root.

## Shape 3 — Major triad

- **Geometry:** the 1st, 3rd, and 5th notes of the major scale built on the root, played as a compact three-note shape rather than a full scale run. → theory basis: [[Chords]] § *Triads (3 notes)*.
- **Theory-optional by design:** "you don't even need to know your major scale in order to use this shape" — memorize the shape, plant it on the root, done.
- **The piano contrast made concrete:** a C major triad and an E♭ major triad are two completely different hand shapes and fingerings on piano — on bass, they're the *identical* shape, just relocated to a different fret. This is § "Why bass specifically is built for this" made concrete on one specific chord type.
- **Worked demo:** "Three Little Birds" (Bob Marley).
- **Jam demo, over A–D–E:** a walking bassline, a ska-flavored line, and a classical Alberti-bass-style broken-triad line, all from the one shape.
- **New to the vault, and a different shape from the existing "triangle":** not the same geometry as [[Bass Fill Writing — The 3-Step Rhythm-and-Shape Method]]'s major/minor "triangle" shape (root-at-top with two scale-color flavor notes below, built for fill-writing) — this shape is the plainer root-3rd-5th triad arpeggio. See § Related for how the two relate.

## Shape 4 — Minor triad

- **Geometry:** the 1st, ♭3rd, and 5th notes of the minor scale built on the root — the same shape as Shape 3 with the third flatted.
- **Open-string equivalence:** the shape works identically whether its root lands on a fretted note or an open string — demoed in E minor first from the 7th fret of the A string, then again from the open low E string: "the same shape, even though one of the notes is open instead of fretted."
- **Worked demo:** "Pipeline" (the Stevie Ray Vaughan / Dick Dale version).

## Shape 5 — "Supershapes": layering and switching shapes mid-line

- **The core insight:** it's more common to switch between shapes within a single bassline than to stay on one shape the whole way through — most real basslines are a sequence of different shapes, not one shape repeated.
- **Worked demo — "Gangsters" (The Specials):** over A minor → A♭ major → A minor → (rest) → A minor → A♭ major → A minor → root-fifth-on-A (the pattern repeating for the second half of the phrase), the line runs the **minor triad shape** on the A minor chord, the **major triad shape** on the A♭ major chord, then the **root-fifth shape** on the return to A.
- **Recipe extracted from that example:** the root-fifth shape (quality-blind) works over both major and minor chords built on the same root; once the chord's quality is actually known, swap in the quality-*specific* shape (minor triad on minor chords, major triad on major chords) for a more colorful line. The same "don't know the quality → use the quality-blind shape; know it → use the quality-specific one" logic [[Bass Fill Writing — The 3-Step Rhythm-and-Shape Method]] uses for choosing between root-fifth-octave and its triangle shape — arrived at independently here for a different pair of shapes.
- **The root movement itself is a shape too:** once a progression's roots are located once (A, D, E in this video's running jam example), the roots' own movement traces a fixed, memorizable visual pattern on the neck (a triangle, in that example) — so a bassist doesn't have to relocate each root from scratch on every repeat, just recall the root-movement shape and stack whichever bassline shape (root-fifth, octave, triad) on top of each point in it. This is "shapes layered on top of other shapes," the chunking principle above applied recursively: the chord progression's shape (which root comes next) and the bassline shape (what to play once you're on a given root) are two independent chunks combined — meaning a bassist only has to remember **two things** per song section (the root-movement shape, and whichever bassline shape is layered on it) instead of every individual note.

## The 3-step process for learning any new shape

Stated directly as generalizing past this video's five starter shapes to any future shape (scales, arpeggios, chord voicings) — a fixed, reusable learning path:

1. **Nail the fingering.** Work out which fingers land where, and drill clean fretting/plucking at different speeds before worrying about anything else.
2. **Memorize the shape.** Look at how far apart the notes sit (frets/strings) until you can see the "magic dots" on the fretboard without conscious effort — naming the shape yourself, if a name helps it stick, is a fine memory aid.
3. **Learn the context.** Work out *when* the shape is usable — this is where theory and ear training come in. Shapes don't replace theory or ear training, they just give those skills a visual/physical home to live in. *(Framed as genuinely open-ended and lifelong — "you'll be learning new ways to use old shapes forever," not a box to check once and move on from.)*

## Bonus: superimposing a shape onto a scale

Closing demo: taking the root-fifth shape (Shape 1) and playing it against **every root of a minor scale** rather than just one static root layers the whole scale's harmonic options on top of the plain two-note shape's usual role, unlocking a much bigger set of playable lines from the same small shape vocabulary.
