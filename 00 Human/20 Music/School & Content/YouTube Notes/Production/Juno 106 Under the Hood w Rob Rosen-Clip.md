# Juno 106 Under the Hood w/Rob Rosen-Clip

## Synopsis

A synth tech's internal tour and demo of the Roland Juno-106, framed as "the next level of technology after the Prophet 5" — genuine engineering detail on the DCO phase-lock, filter chip lineage, and envelope architecture, plus a strong recommendation for the 106 as an ideal first hardware synth and a repairability case study (through-hole components vs. surface-mount) that's more general gear-buying wisdom than a synthesis technique.

## Techniques

- **DCO phase-lock is why the Juno never beats**: all 6 voices share one common clock and stay perfectly phase-locked — the same note played on all 6 voices produces zero beating, unlike a free-running analog design (Prophet-5) where identical notes across voices drift slightly and beat against each other.
- **Filter is a Roland IR3109 chip**, the same chip in the Jupiter-8 and Juno-6/60 — the family-recognizable low-pass character across all three synths traces back to this shared chip.
- **One shared envelope generator drives both VCA and VCF** — simpler than the Prophet-5's separate envelopes, at the cost of not being able to shape amp and filter independently.
- **HPF is stepped, not continuous or voltage-controllable**, and its "0" setting is actually a slight bass boost rather than true bypass.
- **Built-in chorus runs off BBD (bucket-brigade) chips** — the source of the Juno's signature sound, and one of the two most common hardware failure points (the other being the 80017A voice chip); both have modern reproduction replacements available.
- **Digital pitch bend is exact** (a precise octave up/down) — an upgrade over the all-analog Juno-6/60 bender, which drifts unpredictably.
- **Two Juno generations before the 106**: Juno-6 (1982, six-voice, no patch memory) → Juno-60 (added patch memory via a new control board, analog board unchanged) → Juno-106 (added MIDI, refined patch management — described as the most refined/preferred model of the three).
- **Recommended as an ideal first hardware synth, and why**: every parameter is a dedicated physical control with no menu-diving, so cause-and-effect is immediate — contrasted against FM synthesis (DX7), which is programmable in principle but abstract enough that most owners just flip through presets rather than actually program it.
- **Repairability case study, generalizable to any vintage-gear buying decision**: through-hole components (large, hand-solderable) rather than surface-mount devices mean failed parts can be desoldered and swapped even decades later; firmware lives on a separate ROM chip, so even a dead CPU can be replaced by reading the ROM's contents and burning them onto a fresh controller. Gear built up through the mid-1980s tends to share this repairable through-hole design; later gear increasingly uses surface-mount packages that shrink toward physically unsolderable sizes — the actual mechanism behind why some vintage synths stay serviceable forever and others become unfixable.
