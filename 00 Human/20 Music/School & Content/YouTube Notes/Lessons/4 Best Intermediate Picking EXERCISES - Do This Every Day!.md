## Synopsis

Four progressive string-skipping alternate-picking exercises, built as a direct follow-up to a beginner-level octave-interval exercise: a moving-octave "disco" workout, a pentatonic string-skipping pattern, a heptatonic (natural minor) 3-notes-per-string string-skipping pattern in triplets, and a closing composed arpeggio example that spreads chord tones across skipped strings rather than sweeping them in order — deliberately framed as sounding more like a piano player's spread voicings than a typical guitar arpeggio run.

## What's taught

- **Exercise 1 — moving octave interval ("disco workout").** Builds on a simpler fixed-position octave-interval exercise (alternate-pick between two strings a string apart, e.g. D on the A string and D on the G string, skipping the string between) by moving the octave shape around the neck instead of holding one position — same alternating-string picking demand, now combined with left-hand position changes, and deliberately given a rhythmic ("disco") feel to enforce even note spacing rather than random string-hop timing.
- **Exercise 2 — pentatonic string-skipping (B minor pentatonic).** Ascending pattern skips one string per repetition (E→D, skipping A; A→G, skipping D, etc.), same idea reversed for the descending version. Named coordination trap: it's harder to sync the picking hand's skip with the fretting hand's shape change than it looks — a suggested diagnostic is blocking the strings with the fretting hand alone and running just the picking pattern, to isolate whether unwanted string noise comes from the picking hand touching skipped strings.
- **Exercise 3 — heptatonic (B natural minor / Aeolian) string-skipping, 3-notes-per-string, in triplets.** Adds the extra two notes to the pentatonic shape and switches subdivision to triplets specifically because a 2-notes-per-string picking pattern (exercise 2) is genuinely harder to push to high tempo with pure alternate picking than a 3-notes-per-string pattern — named as the reason most fast shred licks favor 3-notes-per-string scales over the pentatonic. Picking-pattern contrast shown explicitly: the pentatonic string-skip always alternates down-up-down-up straight through (one consistent angle usable throughout), while the 3-note-per-string version constantly flips which stroke starts each string (down-up-down / up-down-up alternating) — a materially harder picking-angle problem, since no single fixed angle serves the whole pattern.
- **Exercise 4 (practical capstone) — arpeggio spread across skipped strings, alternating B minor/C major/B minor harmony.** Rather than sweep-picking an arpeggio in string order, the same chord tones are spread out using the string-skipping approach from exercises 2–3 — produces an unpredictable, non-linear melodic contour explicitly compared to how a piano player voices a spread chord with both hands rather than confining it to one register. Stated creative payoff: breaks the habit of always using the same handful of arpeggio/scale patterns, since string-skipping forces visualizing the fretboard in a less linear way.
