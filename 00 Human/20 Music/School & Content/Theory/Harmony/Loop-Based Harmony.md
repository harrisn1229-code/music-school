# Loop-Based Harmony

**How harmony behaves when it repeats instead of developing.** Common-practice theory assumes harmony drives form — a progression goes somewhere and the piece follows. Loop-based music inverts that: the harmony is a *bed*, and everything else moves over it.

That's not a lesser kind of harmony, it's a different job. This page covers the rules that job runs on.

## Vamps and harmonic rhythm

**A vamp** is a short repeating progression or figure — usually one or two bars — looped as a bed under everything else.

**Harmonic rhythm** is *how fast the chords change*, independent of tempo. It's the single most important harmonic parameter in loop-based music and the one nothing in the classical library covers.

- **Fast harmonic rhythm** — a chord or more per bar. Pulls attention *to* the changes. Exhausting over a repeating 2-bar loop.
- **Slow harmonic rhythm** — one or two chords over several bars. Leaves room for drums, timbre and topline to carry interest.

**Loop-based production wants it slow.** The loop repeats dozens of times; if the harmony is busy, the listener is tracking chord changes instead of the vocal and the groove. A 140 BPM trap beat sitting on one chord for eight bars isn't harmonically lazy — it's correctly scoped.

## Why four repeating chords is satisfying

The question worth answering honestly, because it sounds like it should be a problem.

**Predictability is the feature.** Once the ear stops tracking the harmony — which takes about two passes — attention moves to whatever *is* changing: the vocal, the drums, the texture, the arrangement. The progression becomes a **stable bed rather than a series of events**.

That's the trade. You give up harmonic surprise and you buy the listener's full attention for everything else. In a genre where the topline and the production *are* the song, that's the right trade.

The corollary: **if the loop is boring, the problem is usually not the chords.** It's that nothing above them is changing.

## What creates motion when harmony doesn't

Four sources, and a good loop uses several:

1. **Rhythm** — the groove itself, and how it varies. See [[Genre Drum Patterns]] and [[Pocket and Microtiming]].
2. **Melody and topline** — the melody supplies the movement the chords aren't. See [[Melody Writing]].
3. **Timbre** — filter sweeps, wavetable position, automation. See [[LFOs and Modulation]] and [[Automation]].
4. **Arrangement** — scheduled absence. Dropping elements creates as much motion as adding them. See [[Arrangement and Song Form]].

## Pedal point

**A sustained bass note held while the chords above it change.**

Usually the tonic or the dominant. The held note creates tension against whichever chords it doesn't belong to, and releases when they come back into agreement — motion above a fixed anchor, which is exactly the loop-based problem.

- **Tonic pedal** — stability under moving harmony. Very common under intros and outros.
- **Dominant pedal** — mounting tension. This is what an EDM build is doing: sitting on V and refusing to resolve until the drop.

The inverse move is equally useful and already documented: hold the *harmony* and move the *bass* — see [[Chromatic Bass Lines]].

## Four chords with a moving bass

The chords stay the same; the bass walks through inversions underneath them, and it reads as far more harmonic movement than there actually is.

`C – G/B – Am – F` is the standard case: the bass descends C–B–A–F while the chords are still just I–V–vi–IV. All the movement is in the bottom voice.

**Why it works:** the ear takes the lowest note as the harmonic root, so changing the bass changes the perceived chord even when the notes above are static. It's the cheapest way to make four chords feel like eight.

## Reharmonizing a repeated section

Three genuinely *harmonic* ways to fix a loop that's gone stale — not arrangement moves:

1. **Reharmonize the last bar** so the loop resolves differently on each pass. The most effective single change.
2. **Insert a secondary dominant** in front of a chord to pull harder into it — `Am` becomes `E7 → Am`. See [[Secondary Dominants]].
3. **Move the bass** under static harmony, per above.

Also available: swap a chord for its parallel minor or major, or open the voicings into sus/add9 shapes.

## Voicing over a loop

Two practical rules that matter more in loop-based music than anywhere else, because the same voicing is heard dozens of times.

**Use sus2, sus4 and add9 for pads under a vocal.** These remove or blur the **3rd** — the note that defines major or minor. With the 3rd out of the way the pad stops colliding with whatever the topline is doing, and the vocal gets to define the harmony. A plain triad under a busy vocal fights it.

**A topline landing on the 9th** adds colour without changing the chord's function. The 9th isn't in the triad, so it doesn't redefine anything — it just sounds open and unresolved rather than settled. That's why toplines landing on the 9th read as more emotive and less final than ones landing on the root or 3rd.

- **Spread a held interval wide (not close) over a moving bass and let it function as a compound pedal** — from a DnB drop ("Rituals"): hold a single melodic interval (a plain 5th above the bass) sustained while only the bass note underneath it changes on a set path — flat 2→5, then flat 3→5 an octave up, repeated, finally resolving flat 7→1. Spreading the two notes across octaves (rather than voicing them close together) is what lets the ear track the interaction between them as two separate lines instead of fusing them into one indistinct chord — the same "close = fused, spread = perceivable relationship" principle that makes wide voicings read as more dramatic.
