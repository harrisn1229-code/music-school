# Connecting Licks Into Longer Lines

Part of [[Bebop Vocabulary]]'s vocabulary-acquisition material and a cousin of [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]'s "lick-splicing across shapes" section — but where that page splices fragments of **arpeggio shapes**, this note is about splicing whole **memorized licks/vocabulary phrases** (transcribed from a real solo) into new combinations. Distinct from [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]], which reduces a transcribed solo to a named reusable *concept*; this method keeps the actual licks intact as raw material and focuses on the mechanics of gluing two or more of them together into one flowing line.

## The setup: map each lick to a 2-5-1 role first
Before connecting anything, sort memorized licks by which chord function they work over: **minor language → the ii chord**, **dominant language → the V chord**, **resolving/tag phrases → the I chord**. This sorting step is a prerequisite, not optional — you can't usefully connect two licks until you know which chord each one belongs on. → [[The ii-V-I]]

## Four connecting devices, in escalating order of difficulty

1. **Proximity linking (the free move).** Before reaching for any of the tools below, check whether the lick you're ending on and the lick you're starting simply already sit close together — one ending on a note a half step (or otherwise close) from where the next one starts. If so, just play them back to back; no scale or rhythm change is needed. *(general starting point: always check this first — it costs nothing and sometimes the connection is already there)*
2. **Scale walk-up/walk-down as connective tissue.** When the two licks *don't* already sit close together, don't jump straight from one to the other — leave the first lick early (skip its original ending) and walk up or down the diatonic scale that fits the chord you're currently on (e.g. D Dorian over a Dm7) until you land on the exact note the next lick starts on, then play straight into it. The scale isn't decoration here, it's literally the bridge between two otherwise-disconnected phrases. → [[Modal Jazz]], [[Scale Modes Reference]]
3. **Rhythm editing to enable a connection.** Sometimes a lick's own rhythm (e.g. a run of triplets) makes it awkward to interrupt at a useful point. Simplify the rhythm (triplets → straight eighths is the worked example) specifically so you can break out of the lick earlier and walk toward the next lick's starting note using device 2. This is a deliberate edit made *in service of* the connection, not a general "simplify everything" rule — apply it only where the original rhythm is blocking a clean link.
4. **Arpeggio as an on-ramp.** Instead of walking a scale, walk a chord arpeggio (any shape that fits the current chord — e.g. a major 7 or minor 7 arpeggio built off a related root over a ii chord) up or down until you land near the target note the next lick starts on, then pivot straight into it. This works because a well-chosen arpeggio shares most of its notes with the underlying chord while still offering a distinct color (a major 7 arpeggio a third above a minor chord's root highlights its 9th; a minor 7 arpeggio off the 5th highlights the 9th and 11th) — see [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]] for the fretboard-position side of choosing these shapes.

## A fifth move, one level deeper: fragment extraction and shared-note reharmonization
Once individual licks are comfortable, break them into smaller fragments and ask whether a fragment built from one chord's tones will also work over a *different* chord in the same progression — it will, whenever the two chords share enough notes. A fragment built from a major 7 arpeggio (e.g. C major 7) often reharmonizes cleanly over the V and ii chords of the same key (G7, Dm7) because those chords share most of the same notes; a fragment that's mostly a minor 7 arpeggio built a step above the V's root (E minor 7 over a G7-based 2-5-1 in D minor/F major) can work over any of the three 2-5-1 chords for the same reason. Practically: pull the fragment out of its original lick, replay it over the new chord to confirm it still sounds right, then use devices 2-4 above to connect it to the rest of the line. This is the same underlying logic as [[Chord Substitution]] and the shared-upper-structure-triad ii-V-to-iii retargeting already documented on [[The ii-V-I]] — chords that share enough notes are functionally interchangeable for melodic material, not just for comping substitutions.

## A sixth move: transpose a fragment by a fixed interval to a related chord
Distinct from devices 1–4 above (which connect *two different* licks) and from device 5 above (which finds a *different chord* the same fragment already fits) — this device takes one fragment and **moves it by a fixed interval, then simply replays it**, generating a second phrase for free rather than reaching for new material. Two worked forms, both from Grant Green's own playing as transcribed by Jack Ruch:
- **Half-step transposition to reinterpret the same shape over the next chord.** Playing a lick from the B♭ altered scale over a B♭7 chord, then sliding the *identical* shape down a half step the instant the harmony moves to E♭7 — the same fingering now reads as an E♭ Lydian-dominant (♯11) color. Works because a half step is exactly the interval relationship between the two chords' roots.
- **Minor-third transposition as a repeat-and-vary device within one phrase.** A short phrase played over one chord, then immediately transposed up a minor third and replayed — Ruch calls this "a really common theme in a lot of Grant's phrasing," i.e. a genuine recurring habit in Green's improvising, not just a teaching device. Demonstrated three times across one lesson: once inside a 6-2-5-1 turnaround, once reworking a IV-chord lick, and once on a "Blues for Willarine" turnaround line.
Practically: when a lick sits over one chord and a nearby chord is a half step or minor third away (a very common relationship inside a 6-2-5-1 turnaround's internal half-step and minor-third relationships between adjacent altered/target notes), try the identical shape transposed by that interval before composing anything new. → [[🎵 GRANT GREEN - Number One Green Street]], [[🎵 GRANT GREEN - Miss Ann's Tempo]], [[🎵 GRANT GREEN - Blues for Willarine]] — Jack Ruch, [[Transcribing Licks And Making Them Your Own]]

## Why this matters
The headline idea across all six devices: **a memorized lick isn't a fixed object to play start-to-finish, it's raw material.** The actual transferable skill is the connective mechanism — proximity, scale walk-up, rhythm edit, arpeggio on-ramp, shared-note reharmonization, or interval transposition — not any one specific lick. "Steal the licks, mix them like Legos, then bend the rules to make them your own."
