# Zach Scholz - I Tried Recording a Song Like It's 1966

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4JJw_mExqwA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4JJw_mExqwA)

## Synopsis
Zach Scholz sets out to see whether a genuinely convincing 1960s-sounding record can be made by working under the same hard constraints engineers faced in that era, built around a self-imposed four-track ceiling per machine.  He tracks and mixes a full song — claps, tambourine, kick, snare, mono overhead, acoustic guitar, bass, two lead-guitar parts, and Farfisa organ — using a pair of four-track tape machines run in tandem: raw parts get recorded onto the first machine, then groups of them are bounced (summed) down to spare channels on the second, mirroring how larger 60s studios stacked machines to escape the 4-track ceiling without losing the original unmixed tracks.  The exercise surfaces period-specific realities beyond raw channel count: consoles of the era mostly offered hard-left/hard-right/center panning rather than continuous pan pots, so once a group of instruments is committed to a mono-summed channel its pan position is locked in, which forces the entire session — instrument list, pan assignments, bounce order — to be mapped out before a single note is tracked.  It's also a useful lens on the sound of Nate's own core musical interest: this is essentially the same recording ceiling Abbey Road's engineers worked under while tracking the Beatles' 1966 material, and several choices here (spring reverb on drums pushed back in the mix, tight/mono-summed low end, tape-driven saturation used in place of a compressor) map directly onto that era's records.  At the end he A/Bs the 1960s-style bounce-down against a modern digital version of the same song to show how much of that vintage character comes from production discipline alone rather than arrangement or performance.

## Techniques
- **Hard four-track ceiling per machine, matching the core 1960s studio limitation** — the whole exercise is framed around "the biggest limitation being I only get four tracks," even though far more than four total sound sources end up on the finished song.
- **Two four-track machines run in tandem, exactly like larger 60s studios did, to get around the ceiling** — parts are recorded across the channels on machine one, then summed (bounced) down to a single spare channel on machine two, which frees up channels for more material while keeping the original unmixed tracks intact in case something needs rebalancing later. → [[Sony TC-630]] [[Kramer Tape]]
- **Drum bus-down order planned in advance** — claps and tambourine are recorded first and summed to a spare channel on machine one; kick, snare, and a mono overhead then go on the three remaining channels; all percussion is finally summed together onto one channel of machine two.
- **Mic setup kept deliberately mono** — a single mono overhead plus a kick mic and a snare mic, mono by design specifically so the whole kit can consolidate into one track during the bounce.
- **Drum tone built for restraint, not punch** — heavy low-pass filtering strips most of the high end, the kit buses to "a lot of spring reverb," and it's deliberately mixed pushed back with minimal transient punch rather than upfront and punchy; he calls this restraint "the key to tracking a convincing 60s drum." → [[TRANSIENT Slot]] [[BED Slot]]
- **Panning limited to three positions, not continuous pan pots** — 60s consoles mostly used three-way switches (hard left / hard right / center), which is why so many 60s records park drums and bass hard on one side and guitars and keys hard on the other. → [[Stereo Field]]
- **Pan-alignment rule reshaped the whole recording plan** — anything getting summed to a mono channel has to already share the same pan position going in, since panning can't be changed after the bounce; this is why he couldn't group all the guitars together and had to regroup acoustic, bass, and piano instead once he realized their pans matched.
- **Acoustic, bass, and piano treated as one center-panned bed** — grouped together and summed to the second channel of machine two since all three share a center pan, a workaround forced directly by the pan-alignment rule above. → [[BED Slot]]
- **Acoustic guitar tracked to drive tape saturation, not just to be EQ'd afterward** — recorded through a WA87 R2 condenser (Warm Audio), strummed deliberately hard and "a little bit messy" in a rock-and-roll style so the performance would "interact with the tape"; the channel gets a little EQ but very little compression because he wants the natural dynamics of the playing compressed by the tape itself rather than by a compressor. → [[Kramer Tape]] [[Sony TC-630]]
- **Bass pushed toward mids, away from low end** — a Höfner bass (the same brand behind Paul McCartney's iconic Beatles-era violin bass) gets a lot of mid push and a heavy low-end cut rather than being tracked full-range. → [[BASS Slot]]
- **Low-end discipline explained with two concrete, period-specific reasons** — sub content eats up tape headroom and can cause saturation, and too much low end makes vinyl record grooves too wide and causes playback tracking issues, so 60s mixes generally ran tight low end with very little true sub. → [[SUB Slot]]
- **Lead guitars driven for natural amp fuzz, not a pedal** — an old American tweed tube amp is pushed hard enough to break up naturally; a lead line and a harmonizing line are tracked with essentially the same guitar and amp, differentiated only by using a different cabinet on each for separation, then recorded onto channels one and two of machine one and summed to the third channel of machine two. → [[LEAD Slot]]
- **Farfisa organ added late specifically to rebalance the panning** — panned against the electric guitars to give the hard-panned guitar side a little more counterweight rather than added purely for its own tone. → [[TEXTURE Slot]]
- **Session had to be fully mapped before tracking began** — because track count is so tight and mono summing is unforgiving of pan mismatches, he says he repeatedly reached the end of a planned signal chain only to realize an instrument had been left out with no tracks left to give it, which he frames as proof of how important pre-planning the recording map is under these constraints.
