# JAZ-C10 — ♭II–I is not always a tritone sub

**Q.** The same ♭II chord resolving to I can be two different devices. What separates them, and why does ♭II–I sound so complete with no dominant in it?

> [!question]- Answer
> **How you arrive at it.** Dropping into ♭II chromatically — the roots stepping down a semitone at a time into it, as in `Dm7 → D♭7 → Cmaj7` — it is a **tritone substitution**, standing in for a dominant that never appears. Arriving at it from ♭VI by descending fifths on the flat side (♭III → ♭VI → ♭II) it is a **borrowed chord, a Phrygian cadence**. The audible difference is whether the roots move chromatically or by fifths on the way in.
>
> It sounds complete because **every note of a major triad on ♭II falls a semitone into a note of the tonic triad** — ♭2̂→1̂, 4̂→3̂, ♭6̂→5̂. Three simultaneous semitone resolutions, no tritone and no leading tone.

## Why it matters

It is a counter-case to [[HAR-C06 — Why V–I resolves: the tritone]]: a strong cadence built by a completely different mechanism from the one that card explains.

It also sharpens the ear test. A half-step bass drop that is not one of the diatonic landmarks in [[EAR-C10 — Half-step bass motion is a diatonic landmark]] is very often a flat-five substitute arriving at the end of a chromatic run. And ♭II going to **V** rather than to I is a third thing again — the [[Neapolitan Sixth]], the same chord with a different destination.

## Drill

Play ♭II–I twice: once approached chromatically from ii, once approached from ♭VI. Name which is which by ear from the bass motion alone, with the chords voiced identically.
