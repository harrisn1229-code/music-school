# Behind the Track｜'Manchild' by Sabrina Carpenter

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FZCVYCS8x5M)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FZCVYCS8x5M)

## Synopsis

Jack Antonoff walks Mix with the Masters through the build of Sabrina Carpenter's *Manchild*, framed almost entirely around one idea: the feel of a song comes not from what's played but from how the parts are mixed together against each other, and the record's whole identity comes from deliberately blurring a straight LinnDrum against a swung, galloping Juno bass. From there the breakdown turns into a demonstration of playing outboard tape-echo hardware (a Copycat, a Space Echo, a "Benson" unit) live and manually — riding the tape, chasing swells, catching "disgusting" happy accidents — as a genuinely improvised instrument rather than a plugin with presets, explicitly likened to how the electric guitar itself was once dismissed as an inhuman "machine" before players humanized it. The second half covers a live string quartet direction (playing "collegiate," then loosening for a scene change), and a vocal philosophy built entirely around restraint: a nearly-undoubled lead vocal, a four-voice chorus stack standing in for what sounds like "a hundred people," and a stated preference for leaving room for the listener's imagination over pushing a mix to its loudest, densest possible state.

## Techniques

- **Blend a straight LinnDrum against a galloping, swung Juno bass to generate rhythmic tension — "straight and swung are the spectrum... the space between is where the magic happens."** The two founding elements of the track sit at opposite ends of the same rhythmic axis on purpose; the mix decision (how hard to lean into either extreme, and how much to blur the line between them) is treated as the actual songwriting move, not a secondary polish step. → [[Rhythm and Groove]], [[LinnDrum]], [[Roland Juno-106]], [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **Play tape-echo hardware (a Copycat, a Roland Space Echo, and a unit named "the Benson") live and manually during the mix — riding tape speed/feedback by hand in real time rather than dialing in a preset — specifically because it makes the result impossible to duplicate.** "What's cool about things like the chorus echo or the space echo or the Benson, it makes it impossible to be duplicated and it makes it completely unique and it makes it an expression of how you were feeling in that moment." He auditions swells live, keeps some ("that's interesting for something else") and discards others, hunting for "odd moments" that make the track feel like it came from somewhere the listener can't place. → [[Committing and Printing]], [[Modulation]], [[Vintage Gear Worklist]]
- **Treat outboard gear as a played instrument, not a set-and-forget processor — the explicit mental model: "How do I play these machines the same way I play a piano or a guitar?"** Framed against the historical dismissal of the electric guitar as a "bastardization" of the acoustic instrument before players put enough humanity into it that the distinction stopped mattering — the same argument applied here to automation and outboard gear generally. "Touching the tape is like my version of touching grass." → [[Committing and Printing]], [[Automation]]
- **Manually varying tape-echo playback speed on a HARMONIC (not just rhythmic) source deliberately introduces pitch/harmonic drift as the source is echoed** — layered against the LinnDrum/Juno rhythmic treatment, this is a second, harmonic axis of "make it travel in space and time." Explicitly described as a live, ridden effect: "there's moments in there that are like disgusting in the great way and there's moments there that are the wrong kind of disgusting — you got to find them." → [[Modulation]], [[Vintage Gear Worklist]]
- **Most of the track's actual sonic information comes from ONE source instrument (the Juno) rather than being spread thin across many layers** — including the bass, which is not a separate bass patch but the Juno's own low end doubled: "most of the sonic information is really just coming from the Juno... the bass there to gallop... that's not my bass, my bass is this." A concentration-over-proliferation approach to sound selection. → [[Layering and Stacking]], [[Sound Selection Guide]], [[Roland Juno-106]]
- **A live string quartet is directed through two contrasting performance characters for two different structural moments — "collegiate" precision for one section, then a looser scene change for another.** Multiple takes were tracked and comped from ("I have him play a bunch of takes, you can see all the takes and I just grab the bits") specifically to have both characters available for editing rather than trying to get one performer to switch feel mid-take. The vocal melody at this point in the song is described as shifting into something "very classic," evoking "an old English dinner party," matching the string quartet's arrival. → [[Vocal Comping and Takes]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **A guitar part is called out as "one of the bedrocks of this song"** — a harmonic-instrument layer that, like the tape-echoed drums, gets pushed through the same tape-echo speed-manipulation treatment for harmonic movement rather than being left dry. → [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **Deliberately minimal, nearly undoubled lead vocal, with only a "slap" impression of a double rather than a real doubled take, and a four-voice stack standing in for a much bigger sound in the chorus.** "There's basically a lead vocal that tells the story and it's very important for the song to start that way... to double that would just completely lose the intimacy." The chorus reads as "a hundred people singing from a mountaintop" while actually being a four-stack — the size comes from arrangement/performance choices around the stack, not from adding more voices. → [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]], [[Width and Stereo]]
- **A conversational, single-narrator vocal concept drives every vocal-processing decision: avoid over-stacking, doubling, or harmonizing because the song is "one person in conversation."** The lyric's tone (a friend "bitching about dating") is treated as the reason the vocal stays comparatively bare — the processing restraint is in service of the writing's voice, not a generic minimalism rule. → [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]], [[Masking and Carving]]
- **Leave space for the listener's imagination rather than maximizing density/loudness — stated as a direct trade-off, not a vague philosophy.** "A huge chorus... we could have a few things going on and then we could sit with them and treat them in ways to make them feel like the most special thing in the world, because I think that leaves the ability for myself and other people to imagine it bigger than it actually was — which for me is more powerful than slamming it to the very ends of the sonic earth where there's no room for a human being's ear or body to squeeze into that field." → [[Depth and Space]], [[Masking and Carving]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
