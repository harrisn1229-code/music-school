## Techniques

- **Delay vs. reverb — algorithmic reverb IS built from delay taps** — an algorithmic reverb plugin synthesizes room reflections from many delay taps under the hood; "light"/simpler reverb engines use fewer taps, so each repeat reads more like an individual, discrete echo rather than a smooth wash. Reverb and delay can sound very similar with matched settings — the taps are the shared mechanism, only the density and character differ. → [[Reverb]], [[Delay]]
- **Tape delay's origin — two tape heads on one machine, not two effects units** — the earliest delay effects came from running two-track tape through a machine with two tape heads engaged: the first head reads the signal, the second head reads the same signal moments later (because it's physically further down the tape path), producing two near-identical signals in quick succession. The delay time is a direct function of the physical distance between the heads. Modern tape-delay-emulation plugins recreate this mechanically-derived echo digitally.
- **The ~130 ms perceptual threshold: delay time under ~130 ms reads as ONE thicker signal, over ~130 ms reads as TWO distinct signals (an audible echo)** — this is the psychoacoustic line between "thickening" and "echo" use of a delay. Keep delay time under ~130 ms for a fuller, louder-sounding vocal/guitar/any source you want to feel bigger without it reading as a repeat; go longer only when a distinct, heard echo is the actual goal. → [[Delay]] (Ableton), [[Echo]], [[TEXTURE Slot]]
- **Manual tempo-synced delay time via the BPM formula, then detune off it on purpose for push/drag feel** — `60,000 ÷ BPM` = a quarter note in milliseconds (halve/quarter further for 8th/16th note values); once you have that number, shave a few ms off it for a "pushed" delay that rushes ahead of the beat, or add a few ms to it for a "dragged" delay that lags behind — finer, more musical control than a plugin's quantized note-division dropdown alone. → [[Delay]] (Ableton), [[Echo]], [[Rhythm and Groove]]
- **Stereo delay imaging — a mismatched L/R delay time IS a doubler** — setting different delay times on the left vs. right channel of a stereo delay meaningfully widens the stereo image, which is the exact mechanism a dedicated doubler plugin uses on purpose. If widening isn't the goal, keep L/R delay times identical so the stereo image doesn't shift unintentionally. → [[Echo]], [[Width and Stereo]], [[Stereo Field]]
- **Feedback vs. crosstalk — two distinct knobs doing two distinct jobs** — Feedback re-feeds the delayed signal into itself at a level below unity gain, so repeats naturally diminish over time rather than looping away into runaway feedback. Crosstalk is a different function: it bleeds the left channel's delay signal into the right (and vice versa), which widens the image via mild phase cancellation and also emulates a common defect of analog tape-delay hardware, where channel bleed happened as a side effect of the electronics. → [[Echo]], [[Stereo Field]]
- **Delay modulation has three possible domains — time, frequency, and amplitude — but only two have common names** — frequency-based modulation of a delay's repeats is called vibrato, amplitude-based is called tremolo, and time-based modulation (shifting the delay time itself) is the third domain but doesn't have a widely-used separate name. Which domain(s) a given delay plugin's modulation section can actually hit depends entirely on that plugin's design — some only shift time, some can hit all three. → [[Modulation]], [[Delay]], [[Echo]]
- **Delay as a compositional instrument, not just a space effect: an extra snare hit, or a time-based arpeggiator** — a delay tap on a snare that's strong/loud enough reads as an additional snare hit, changing the perceived pattern rather than just adding ambience (the same logic extends to any other instrument). Stacking multiple delays, then adding frequency-based modulation (vibrato) to the repeats, can turn a delay chain into a form of improvised, time-based arpeggiation — with enough frequency modulation added, it can sound like an actual arpeggiator generating new pitched, rhythmic content from a single input. → [[Modulation]], [[Rhythm and Groove]]
