# Music Theory Concepts Tier List

Part of [[Music Theory]]. David Bennett's S-to-F importance ranking of ~28 theory concepts — a **study-sequencing roadmap** for what to actually learn, not a completeness checklist. His framing: work top to bottom, stop wherever you like, and each concept you pick up expands your musicianship regardless of where you stop. Complements [[Ear Training Difficulty Tier List]] (same tier-list shape, applied to ear skills instead of theory topics).

## The ranking, most to least essential

| Tier | Concept | Why |
|---|---|---|
| S | Note names | Can't do anything more advanced (chords, scales) without knowing what note you're playing. |
| S | Triads (major/minor/aug/dim, sus2/sus4) | The essential [[Chords\|chord]] vocabulary — needed to perform or write almost any song. |
| S | Ear training (relative pitch) | Even basic by-ear identification (key, simple progressions) massively expands how you conceptualize music vs. only ever reading it. See [[Ear Training Difficulty Tier List]]. |
| S | Major scales (all 12) | Pre-learns the finger/mental patterns most songs are built from; also the base for improvising. |
| A | [[Seventh Chords]] | Very common, but substitutable — a plain triad gets you through a 7th-chord song in a pinch. |
| A | [[Chord Inversions]] | Same substitutability logic as 7ths — a root-position triad works if you don't know the inversion. |
| A | [[Intervals]] | Names the gaps between notes; essential for spotting them by ear and for transposing on request. |
| A | Pentatonic & [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes\|blues scales]] | Simplified, highly versatile major/minor-scale subsets — the guitarist's entry-level scale. |
| A | Minor scales (natural, harmonic, melodic) | Same pre-learning value as major scales, for minor-key material. |
| B | [[Extended Chords]] (9/11/13, add) | Substitutable down to a 7th chord — "nice to know," not need-to-know. |
| B | Modes of the major scale (Dorian, Phrygian, Mixolydian…) | Skippable if you already know major/minor (a Dorian passage can be heard as "minor with a raised 6th"), but valuable for improvising/writing with a mode's specific flavor. |
| B | [[Transposing Instruments\|Transposition]] | Frequently needed (matching a singer's range on a cover) but not essential to just playing a song as written. |
| B | [[Key Signatures]] | Essential *if* you read sheet music, irrelevant if you don't — a polarized skill, landed mid-table. |
| B | Roman numeral analysis | Abstracts a progression away from its specific key (I–IV–vi–V sounds the same in any key) — very valuable for ear-based thinking, not required to just play. |
| B | [[Time Signatures & Note Values\|Note values]] (rhythm notation) | Reading rhythm off the page matters less than reading pitch — if you already know how a song goes, the rhythm is usually intuitive. |
| B | Clefs (treble/bass/alto) | Essential for reading sheet music at all, irrelevant if you play by ear/tab. |
| C | Extended modes (modes of harmonic/melodic minor) | Same flavor-value as major-scale modes, but come up far less often. |
| C | Exotic scales (whole tone, octatonic, bebop, etc.) | The "miscellaneous" scale bucket — useful for specific flavors, rarely load-bearing. |
| C | [[Dynamics & Articulations\|Articulation]] (staccato/legato) | Matters for how a note is played, but less than playing the right note at the right time. |
| C | [[Time Signatures & Note Values\|Time signatures]] (naming/counting) | You can feel a familiar meter without naming it; mainly useful as a nudge to try unusual signatures (5/4, 7/4) you wouldn't otherwise reach for. |
| C | Tuplets | Same logic as time signatures — play them fine by ear, need the vocabulary only when reading sheet music. |
| C | Rhythmic solfège ("the three," "the and of four") | An efficient shorthand for describing beat placement verbally — mostly a professional-environment skill. |
| D | Cadences / functional harmony | Far more load-bearing in classical/jazz than pop/rock, where it loses relevance. |
| D | [[Dynamics & Articulations\|Dynamics]] (forte/piano) | Matters most for faithfully reproducing a classical composer's intent from the page — if you're playing your own interpretation, you already have an intuitive sense of loud/soft. |
| D | Polymeter (unified tempo, parts cycling out of sync) | Advanced but worth knowing if you'll be playing in a different meter than the rest of the band, so the "falling out of sync" moment doesn't read as a mistake. |
| F | Polyrhythms (e.g. 4-over-3) | Just a tuplet dressed up — playable/writable without the label. |
| F | Tritone substitution | Jazz-specific reharmonization move; irrelevant outside that niche. |
| F | Microtonality | Rare in Western practice; relevant mainly if pushing outside Western tuning. |
| F | Harmonic series (overtones) | Nice-to-know for most instruments; load-bearing mainly for horns/strings tuning against it. |
| F | Temperament (equal vs. just intonation) | Only actionable on instruments that can bend pitch (violin); fixed-tuning instruments like piano can't do anything with it anyway. |
