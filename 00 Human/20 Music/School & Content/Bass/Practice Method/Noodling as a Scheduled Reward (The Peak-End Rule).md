# Noodling as a Scheduled Reward (The Peak-End Rule)

A session-structuring rule for *where* unstructured, no-agenda "noodling" belongs in a practice session — not whether to do it at all. BassBuzz's Josh reframes noodling (aimless, exploratory playing with no goal) from a guilty pleasure or a waste of time into a deliberate, scheduled tool, on the strength of one simple placement rule: **do the structured work first, and save noodling for the very end of the session.** This is a companion to the vault's other session-structuring notes ([[Interleaved Practice, Chunking, and Deliberate-Practice Drills]]'s session-length/timer rules, [[The Practice Priority Pyramid]]'s ordering logic) but answers a different question than any of them: not *what* to practice or *how long*, but **how a session should feel by the time it ends**, and why that matters for whether you come back tomorrow.

## The core mechanism: the peak-end rule

**People's memory and evaluation of an experience is disproportionately shaped by its peak moment and, especially, by how it ends** — not by an accurate average of the whole thing. Illustrated with a movie analogy: a mediocre film with a great ending is remembered fondly; a good film with a bad ending leaves you feeling bad walking out, even though more of the actual runtime was enjoyable. Applied to practice: **your felt memory of "practicing today" is set largely by the last few minutes of the session**, regardless of how the middle of it went.

**The practical rule this produces: structured/hard practice work first, unstructured noodling last.** You still get the productive work done — nothing about this delays or skips the actual exercises, drills, or repertoire — but the session's *last impression*, and therefore the memory you form of "what practicing feels like," is the fun, pressure-free part. That positive last impression is what pulls you back to the instrument again tomorrow, which is the actual leverage point: this is a motivation/retention technique, not a skill-acquisition technique.

**Noodling placed carelessly (e.g., at the start, or scattered with no rule at all) doesn't get this benefit** — Josh names this directly as his own guitar-practice mistake vs. his bass practice, where he applied the end-of-session placement consistently. The noodling itself isn't the variable; *where in the session it lands* is.

## Why noodling itself is worth defending (not just where to put it)

Before the placement rule, the video makes the case that noodling is not wasted time to begin with — worth keeping on the page since the placement rule only matters if noodling stays in the rotation at all:

- **Slash wrote the "Sweet Child O' Mine" riff from a random noodle** — a genuine hit, not a discarded warm-up.
- **Jam bands (Phish, the Grateful Dead) built entire careers on noodling** — extended improvisation *is* the art form, not a departure from it.
- **The Offspring's guitarist performs under the stage name "Noodles"** — a direct nod to the same idea as an identity, not just a technique.

## A ready-to-use application

1. Run the actual planned session first — drills, trouble-spot work, repertoire, whatever the day's plan calls for (pairs naturally with [[Interleaved Practice, Chunking, and Deliberate-Practice Drills]]'s rotation/timer structure for that portion).
2. When the planned work is done, **switch to fully unstructured noodling with no goal** — no metronome, no checklist, no correctness bar — for the last stretch of the session, however long feels natural.
3. Don't front-load noodling as a warm-up *instead of* this closing slot — a warm-up is a different function (see [[Three-Stage Warm-Up (Physical, Mental, Resonance)]]); the peak-end effect specifically needs the fun part to be *last*.
4. Let the session end there, on the noodling, rather than closing with one more "serious" repetition — the last thing you play is the thing that colors how the whole session gets remembered.

— BassBuzz, [[10 Years of Bass Advice in 10 Minutes]]
