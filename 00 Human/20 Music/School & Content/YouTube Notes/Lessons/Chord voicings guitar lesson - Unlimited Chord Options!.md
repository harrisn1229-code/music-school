# Chord voicings guitar lesson - Unlimited Chord Options!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=viTvfArd4Mo)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=viTvfArd4Mo)

## Synopsis

Episode 12 of Jared Borkowski's (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared) guitar-chords-and-theory series, and — despite the "unlimited chord options" title suggesting a broad survey — actually a narrow, single-idea lesson: a deliberately unsystematic, exploratory method for generating new voicings of any chord shape you already know, by moving individual notes up or down an octave one at a time and seeing what turns up. It leans on a prior episode in the series (not yet in the vault) that teaches finding the same note in other fretboard locations via octave/unison interval shapes — this lesson assumes that skill and applies it specifically to chord tones. Before the method itself, Jared draws out a three-way terminology distinction that the rest of the vault's [[Chord Voicings]] page uses constantly but has never stated explicitly as its own thing: a **chord** (the abstract pitch collection, e.g. "C major"), a **voicing** (the specific order/interval spacing those pitches are arranged in), and a **shape** (the specific physical fretboard fingering used to play a given voicing) are three genuinely separate concepts — the same voicing can be played as different shapes in different fretboard positions, or even on a different instrument entirely (a piano player can play the identical voicing with zero relationship to any guitar shape), and the same shape moved to a new root becomes a different chord while remaining the same voicing. He then works the octave-shift method through three open chord shapes (A major, B7, D minor 7) from his own "Chords with Color" chart, turning up several usable inversions per chord, three of which he ties to real songs.

## What's taught

- **Chord vs. voicing vs. shape — the three-way distinction, stated explicitly.** A **chord** is the abstract pitch content (e.g. "C major" = C-E-G, any order, any octave). A **voicing** is the specific order and interval spacing those notes are arranged in — e.g. root-3rd-5th stacked close together is a different voicing from 3rd-5th-root-up-an-octave, even though both are "C major." A **shape** is the specific physical fretboard fingering used to realize a given voicing — the same voicing (say, 1-5-3 bottom to top) can be fingered as an open-position shape, a 5th-fret shape, or a 6th-string-rooted shape, all genuinely different shapes playing the identical voicing; a piano player can play that same voicing with no shape-relationship to the guitar at all. Moving a whole shape up or down the neck by a fret keeps the voicing identical while changing which chord it is. This is the vocabulary this whole lesson (and most of [[Chord Voicings]]) runs on, named directly rather than left implicit.
- **Close position vs. open position, defined.** Close position = all the voicing's notes packed as close together as physically possible. Open position = the same notes with real space between some of them (e.g. moving a middle voice, like the 3rd of a triad, up an octave while the outer notes stay put opens a gap between the bottom two notes). Directly maps onto [[Chord Voicings]]'s existing "Close" row in its named-voicing-families table, but that page didn't previously define *how* an open voicing is derived from a close one — this lesson supplies the mechanism (move one interior note up an octave).
- **Inversion clarified as a byproduct, not a goal.** An inversion is simply "the root is not the lowest note" — nothing more. The method in this lesson produces a lot of inversions incidentally (because notes keep getting shuffled to different octaves) but inversions and their functional/voice-leading behavior are explicitly deferred to a future video in the series; this lesson is scoped to just finding voicings, not using them functionally.
- **The core method — octave-shift voicing discovery, worked as a "puzzle":** start from a chord shape you already know (root position is the natural starting point, since it's usually already memorized). Pick one note in the chord and move it up or down an octave, using the octave/unison-interval fretboard-navigation skill from the prior episode in the series. If the moved note now collides with another note on the same string (you can't play two notes on one string at once), move the colliding note to its own nearest open octave too, and keep going note by note until every note in the chord has a distinct, playable string. There's no fixed order or target outcome — it's explicitly framed as loose, playful exploration ("moving the puzzle around") rather than a systematic drill, in contrast to some of his own more rigorous prior lessons. Full worked-example writeup and the collision-resolution mechanic on the new technique note [[Octave-Shift Voicing Discovery]].
- **Worked example 1 — C major triad, showing the method from scratch.** Root position C-E-G (close position). Move the root (C) up an octave → E-G-C, still close position, now a different inversion (same voicing family, notes reordered). From the original root position instead, move the *middle* note (E) up an octave → C-G-E, this time with real space between the C and G — an open-position voicing, the 1-5-3 voicing. That exact 1-5-3 voicing is then shown playable as three different shapes: an open-position fingering, a shape at the 5th fret, and a shape rooted off the 6th string — same voicing throughout, three different shapes. Sliding any one of those shapes up a half step keeps the voicing (1-5-3) but changes which chord it is.
- **Worked example 2 — A major, open chord.** Starting complete triad: 5th-root-3rd (open A shape stripped to one each of 5th/root/3rd, no doubles). Move the 5th up an octave (collision resolved as it comes up), then move the 3rd down an octave, then relocate the root using a "find the 5th below" fretboard move (from the prior episode) → lands on **3rd-root-5th, first inversion, no open strings — fully movable to any major chord.** Named song ties for this exact voicing/progression: it's the **I → V(1st inversion) → vi** voice-leading move (I chord, then the V chord voiced in first inversion, resolving smoothly into vi) used in **"Tears in Heaven" (Eric Clapton)**, **"Don't Think Twice, It's All Right" (Bob Dylan)**, and **"Somebody to Love" (Queen)** — the first-inversion V chord is what makes the connection to vi sound smooth rather than a plain I-V-vi jump. A thicker variant is also shown: doubling the 3rd an octave up (3-1-5-3) for a fuller strum, demonstrated on the Queen example.
- **Worked example 3 — B7, dominant 7th.** Starting complete four-note chord: root-3rd-♭7-5th (open-B-string open chord stripped to one each). Several octave-shift passes are worked in sequence, each producing a distinct usable voicing: (1) move the 3rd up an octave → **root-♭7-3-5** (barred), a standard root-position dominant-7 shape; (2) move the root up an octave, then resolve the resulting collision by moving the 3rd down an octave → **♭7-3-5-root, third inversion, ♭7 on the bottom** — named as the opening chord of **"Águas de Março" ("Waters of March," Antônio Carlos Jobim)**, a famous bossa nova standard; (3) continue shifting (3rd down an octave, then ♭7 up an octave, then 5th up an octave to resolve the collision) → **3-♭7-1-5, first inversion**, which he notes would resolve naturally to an E chord (as V7 → I); (4) one further shift (root up an octave, 3rd up an octave) → **3-♭7-5-root** — the shape used (fingered as B7, functioning as G7 in the demo) for the intro to **"Wave" (Antônio Carlos Jobim, famous Frank Sinatra recording)** — another bossa nova standard.
- **Worked example 4 — D minor 7, the companion chord from the "Wave" example.** Starting shape: root-5th-♭7-♭3 (open D shape). One pass (♭3 down an octave, open D moved to resolve, top note down an octave to resolve again) turns up an initially unplayable shape that's discarded as impractical — flagged directly in the video as a normal outcome of this exploratory method ("sometimes it will be unplayable... you have to keep moving"), not every octave-shift attempt lands on something usable. A further shift from there → **root-♭3-5-♭7, third inversion, ♭7 on the bottom** — the functional partner to the B7 third-inversion voicing found above (♭7-on-bottom Dm7 → ♭7-on-bottom-style B7/dominant → resolves to C, matching the "Wave" progression). A second, separate voicing found by shifting the bottom note up an octave (with a cascading collision-resolution move) → **♭3-♭7-root-5, first inversion, 3rd in the bass** — noted to sound like a major 6 chord because the pitch collection of a minor-7 first inversion (3-5-7-1... reordered) overlaps with a major-6 chord's notes (deferred to a future video on 6th chords rather than explained fully here). Stated as a general-purpose substitution: this shape can replace essentially any plain Dm(7) voicing when a more colorful sound is wanted, and it resolves functionally the same way (to the same dominant-7 third-inversion shape found above, then to C).
- **Meta-point about the lesson's own approach:** explicitly contrasted with some of Jared's more "systematic and heavy duty" prior lessons (fixed chord-shape drilling in a set order) — this method is deliberately loose, playful, and puzzle-like, with no guaranteed outcome each time you try it. Framed as "teaching you how to fish" rather than handing over a fixed shape diagram (a "fish").
- **Resource plug:** his free "Chords with Color" chart (soundguitarlessons.com/chordswithcolor) supplies root-position starting voicings — described as colorful but deliberately limited to one fixed position each — meant to be fed into this exact octave-shift method to multiply into many more usable voicings and shapes.
