# Keyscape — Rhodes LA Custom E & Mark I

**Instrument:** [[Keyscape]] · **Slot:** [[BED Slot]] · **Used by:** [[1 Electric Piano Track|Electric Piano Track]]

## What it's for
THE neo-soul/hip hop Rhodes — Keyscape's definitive e-piano box. LA Custom E is the modern, cleaner voicing; Classic Mark I is the vintage bark-and-bell tone. Default reach for any warm, sustained comping part in that lane.

## Settings
- ⑥ Patch: LA Custom E (modern) or Classic Mark I (vintage) — audition both, pick by how much bark the part wants
- ② Custom controls page: Bark + Bell are the tone controls on Rhodes patches — push Bark for grit/attack, Bell for the metallic top
- ⑤ Drive/EQ knob: a little of the modeled tube drive for extra bark, before any Ableton saturation downstream — it responds like the real amp
- ③ Tremolo: OFF if [[PanMan]] is doing the movement downstream — don't double-tremolo
- ⑧ Internal FX: reverb OFF always; chorus can stay on for Mark I when it *is* the sound (Mark I + chorus = the neo-soul default)

## In context
- Warm sustained low-mid harmony — same arrangement role as a synth bed or [[Model 84 — Juno Pad]] ([[Sound Palette]]: interchangeable BED-slot occupants).
- If PanMan is used downstream on [[1 Electric Piano Track|Electric Piano Track]], confirm tremolo is off at the patch first — it's the most common double-processing mistake here.

## From videos

- **The real mechanism behind "Bark," explained from an actual Rhodes cutaway/dealer-demo unit — and why it's coupled to more than just tone.** A Rhodes key is a seesaw: pressing it lifts a hammer to strike a tine while simultaneously pulling a "bridle strap" that releases the damper felt so the tine can ring — a single orchestrated linkage, no separate escapement (so the hammer can hyperextend if the tine's tip isn't there to stop it). The tine sits in front of a pickup with a curved (V-shaped) profile: as the tine's position moves toward the center of that curve, the tone reads more "nasally"/overtone-rich — this is the actual physical basis for what a Bark control is modeling. **The coupled part worth knowing**: on real hardware, adjusting tine-to-pickup position for more bark ALSO changes damper tension, because the same mechanical assembly governs both — everything on a real Rhodes is interrelated in a way a digital Bark knob simplifies into one clean parameter.
- **Why a Rhodes tine decays instead of ringing forever, and where "Bell" partly comes from.** The tine is deliberately built as an UNEQUAL-leg tuning fork — a true symmetric tuning fork rings almost indefinitely with an organ-like sustain and no natural decay; making the two legs unequal introduces the decay curve that reads as a piano-like note rather than a drone. The tine assembly is spring-mounted on isolation grommets, decoupled from the wood body specifically so vibration doesn't get sucked into (and damped by) the wood, and so each note stays acoustically independent rather than the whole cabinet "homogenizing" every note together.
- **Why Rhodes tines need individual per-note pickups (unlike a Wurlitzer's flat reeds, which can share a comb-style pickup)**: a Wurlitzer reed is flat, like a ruler, and only moves in one plane when struck. A Rhodes tine is round and, as it rings longer, its oscillation becomes genuinely circular — which is exactly why each tine needs its own separate, position-adjustable pickup rather than a shared comb pickup; a circularly-oscillating tine in a shared comb would physically clang into its neighbors.
- **Real-hardware tuning behavior, useful context even for the modeled instrument**: tines sit on a pressure-fit spring, so tuning is done by physically sliding the tine forward/back — Rhodes generally hold tuning very well because it's a mechanical spring-tension system, not humidity-sensitive. It IS temperature-sensitive though, since the tine is metal and expands/contracts — a cold Rhodes can genuinely read flat (one tech's anecdote: 5 cents flat in a cold garage) and self-corrects back into tune as it warms up.
- **Historical note on hammer-tip material — felt vs. neoprene, and why it matters for tone**: early Rhodes (pre-late-1960s-ish) used felt hammer tips like an acoustic piano, but where an acoustic piano hammer strikes 2–3 strings (spreading wear), a Rhodes hammer strikes a single tine at one contact point — felt would develop a groove/notch from repeated impact, degrading and changing the tone, requiring techs to periodically reshape hammer tips by hand. The switch to neoprene/rubber hammer tips (still deliberately "hardened" for point-contact durability) traded a slightly different tone for dramatically better mass-production reliability and easy replacement — worth knowing when a source recording specifies an earlier felt-tip-era Rhodes vs. the more common later rubber-tip units the Mark I plugin models.
- **A defense of CBS-era Rhodes production, worth knowing when shopping for a real unit to sample/reference**: unlike "pre-CBS" Fender guitars being the prized era, there were no full-size, mass-produced Rhodes pianos AT ALL before the CBS era — Leo Fender hadn't put one into real production yet. CBS-era units catch flak for perceived cost-cutting, but because they were genuinely mass-produced, real Rhodes pianos are findable and affordable today in a way rarer boutique-era electric pianos (an RMI, for instance) simply aren't — and a properly serviced CBS-era unit can still sound excellent. → [[Vintage Gear Worklist]]
