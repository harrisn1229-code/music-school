# Arrangement and Music Mixing: Understanding the Relationship

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_--yEbcNjAs)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_--yEbcNjAs)

## Synopsis
A Sage Audio conceptual breakdown arguing that arrangement (what notes are played, on what instruments) IS most of the mix's frequency balance and dynamic character before any processing happens — mixing is finishing a job arrangement already mostly did, or fixing a job it did badly. Builds the case through the ADSR framework (transient vs. tonal content occupy different, fairly predictable frequency zones across almost any instrument), then works through concrete arrangement-driven mixing decisions (an acoustic guitar's fundamental competing with a fuller arrangement, a synth's overtones masking a vocal formant, filling a thin mid-range via saturation/reverb instead of EQ boost), and closes on how arrangement determines the peak-vs-RMS relationship that dynamics processing then has to manage — including a genre-specific caution about over-processing performances (jazz) where the dynamics were the point.

## Techniques
- **Every instrument splits into transient (attack/decay) and tonal (sustain/release) content, and the two usually carry different frequency information — know this before reaching for a processor.** Tonal content: a note's fundamental plus its harmonics/overtones (whole-number multiples of the fundamental), generally declining in amplitude the further they sit from the fundamental — so a bass playing A3 (220 Hz) is still primarily a low/low-mid signal even though its harmonics extend into the mids and high-mids. Different instruments have different overtone amplitude profiles for the same note (a piano's fundamental is often lower in amplitude than its overtones, so the same A3 occupies more high-mid/high content on piano than on bass). Transient content: less tied to a specific note — a snare's fundamental may be tuned, but most of its overtones aren't whole-number multiples of it; transient/percussive content in ANY instrument (a vocal's consonants/sibilance, a guitar string's pick scrape, a snare/kick's snap) sits mostly in the high-mids/highs. The exception: slow-attack pads/synths designed with minimal transient content. → [[EQ]], [[TRANSIENT Slot]]
- **A well-arranged mix should already read close to a pink-noise-shaped frequency response (amplitude generally decreasing from lows to highs) before any processing — arrangement, not EQ, is doing most of the balancing.** If instruments are chosen for their distinct overtones/timbres and notes are arranged to avoid heavy overlap, the frequency response is already close to correct going into the mix; EQ still helps control specifics, but a conflicting arrangement produces a conflicting mix no matter how it's processed. Concrete failure case: kick and bass both playing/emphasizing the same note/frequency zone produces mud that EQ alone struggles to fully undo. → [[Masking and Carving]], [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **Transient content's concentration in the high-mids/highs is a genuine mix asset, not just a byproduct — it lets the top end stay full while giving the ear "relief" from constant tonal energy up there.** Since transients are naturally dynamic (loud briefly, then gone), high-frequency transient content fills the top of the frequency response without keeping it constantly loud the way sustained tonal high-frequency content would — this is why an uncontrolled, overly loud transient region (e.g. harsh, unmanaged sibilance) reads as fatiguing or painful: it breaks the dynamic relief the ear is expecting up there. → [[De-essing]], [[TRANSIENT Slot]]
- **Arrangement changes what EQ decisions make sense — worked example: an acoustic guitar's low-mid fundamental only becomes a problem once the arrangement gets denser.** A solo acoustic guitar (± a vocal) can occupy the low-mids fine — nothing else is competing there. Add bass, kick, snare and other low-mid-occupying instruments and the same untouched acoustic now reads as muddy — either attenuate its fundamental/low-order harmonics, or (if you control the arrangement) voice/play it an octave higher to sidestep the collision at the writing stage instead of the mixing stage. → [[Masking and Carving]], [[EQ]]
- **A synth with overtones concentrated in 2–5 kHz will mask the vocal's third formant (the vowel/consonant cluster the ear is most sensitive to) — either dip the synth there or pick a different synth patch at the arrangement stage.** If the synth's own character lives in the same 2–5 kHz range the vocal needs for intelligibility, two fixes at two different stages: mixing-stage — dip the synth there while boosting the vocal there; arrangement-stage (if you're the one choosing the patch) — pick a synth with different overtone content so the collision never happens. → [[Masking and Carving]], [[EQ]], [[LEAD Slot]]
- **Fill a thin mid-range by generating NEW content there through saturation or reverb on a low/low-mid source, rather than only reaching for an EQ boost on an already-thin range.** If the overall arrangement is light in the mids, saturating an instrument with a fundamental in the low-mids generates harmonics that land in and fill the mids as a byproduct; reverberating the vocal's mid content produces the same effect via early reflections (strongest near the source, tapering off). Either move fills frequencies the arrangement itself left empty, rather than boosting a range that has nothing there to boost. Caveat: if a range is already balanced, don't add more processing that amplifies it further UNLESS it's a deliberate creative effect (e.g. a distinctive delay) — in that case, attenuate the range before or after the effect to preserve overall balance. → [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[Reverb]], [[Masking and Carving]]
- **Peak level is usually driven by transients, RMS (average loudness) by tonal content — and dynamics processing exists to close that gap when a recording arrives peaky and quiet-on-average.** For most unmixed recordings, peak level is too high relative to RMS, which causes temporal masking (a loud transient covers up what follows it). Compression's actual job: attenuate the peak-driving transient content, which frees up headroom to raise the overall signal level — lifting the RMS-driving tonal content and reducing that masking. Worked example: peak-compress an acoustic guitar's transients specifically to gain the headroom needed to bring its sustain/tonal content up. → [[Compression]], [[Compression Staging]], [[Gain Staging]]
- **Genre dictates how much dynamics processing is appropriate, because genre dictates how intentional the recorded peak/RMS relationship already is.** Talented performers with proper gain-staged recording can establish much of the ideal peak/RMS relationship live, before any compression. In genres like jazz, that dynamic relationship IS the performance — over-compressing frustrates the musicians and undoes something deliberate. In genres like rock, fewer sources (typically kick + snare) drive the peak level while everything else (vocal, bass, guitar) drives the RMS — so vocal/bass/guitar can usually take heavier compression while kick/snare get compressed more conservatively, in a way that retains their dynamics rather than flattening them. → [[Compression Staging]], [[Genres Index]]
