# As I Hear It  - The Secrets of Great Music | Ep. 01

📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5nYaY-7fTA8)

## Synopsis
A different format from the rest of the batch — instead of teaching a method, Max Konyi picks five moments across five very different pieces of music that he personally finds brilliant, plays them, and thinks out loud about *why* they work and what a composer/producer could take from them. It's explicitly a first attempt at the format ("if you like the video it would be really great... let me know"), and the five picks span funk (Nate Smith, "Bounce"), Baroque classical (J.S. Bach, violin arrangement of a harpsichord concerto), cool jazz (Dave Brubeck Quartet, "Take Five"), electronic/neurofunk (Frequent & Camo, "Battery"), gospel/R&B, and video-game chiptune (Jake Kaufman, Shovel Knight) — genuinely wide-ranging, unlike the rest of the channel's more method-focused catalog. Each example yields one concrete, reusable compositional/arrangement device rather than a vague appreciation note.

## Techniques
- **Self-referencing repetition as deliberate musical humor: restate a phrase, then drop the whole thing a half-step down, fully out of key, once.** Nate Smith's "Bounce" states a melodic hook, develops it through a back-and-forth "oh yeah, oh yeah" call pattern, then on one single pass transposes the entire phrase down a half-step — completely outside the established key — before snapping back to normal. Reads specifically as musical humor/"tongue in cheek" because everything else around it has stayed diatonic; funk as a genre is unusually tolerant of this kind of wink. → [[Motivic Development]] (sequence-like motion pushed deliberately non-diatonic), [[Chromatic Mediants and Pop Modulation]]
- **Resolve a big, bombastic buildup into ONE sparse, tense melodic line instead of another big hit.** In the Bach violin concerto movement, a long dense/heavy build resolves not into a climactic hit but into a single falling violin line moving through close dissonant intervals (major/minor 2nds rubbing against each other, "opening and closing" as it descends) over a repeating pedal tone — described as feeling like "a tense little snowflake falling." The device: **buildup into reduction**, not buildup into escalation — saved specifically for the last third/quarter of a ~7-minute piece, so the contrast against everything preceding it actually lands. → [[Depth and Space]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **Let a drum solo's power come from radical space, not fills.** The "Take Five" (Dave Brubeck Quartet) drum solo's most-loved stretch drops to just kick drum hits, everything else (piano, bass) holding a completely static loop underneath — the solo develops by slowly, sparsely iterating on that one simple kick statement rather than filling the space with independent polyrhythmic material. The surrounding silence and the other instruments' stillness is explicitly what gives each hit its meaning — reads almost as musical humor in its own understatement, working against the expectation of a "fancier" drum-solo payoff. → [[Rhythm and Groove]]
- **Reprise a drop with the SAME melody/bass sounds but a tighter, more driving groove, for a second "free" high without new material.** Frequent & Camo's neurofunk/glitch-hop track "Battery" repeats its drop with near-identical melody and bass sound choices the second time, but reworks the groove underneath: the kick lands squarely on the beat (vs. more sporadic the first time), hi-hats switch to straight 8ths, and the bass pattern locks onto the downbeat instead of scattering — described as "getting double mileage" out of the same drop by making it feel "focused and aggressive" purely through a groove-intensity change, with no new melody, harmony, or instrumentation. → [[Rhythm and Groove]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **A tritone-sub reharmonization used exactly once in a whole song, saved for maximum impact.** A gospel/R&B tune loops a progression in B minor — heard as E add9/G♯ – G minor 6 – A♯dim7 – B minor (roughly I–♭III–iv° over a chromatic descending bass) — and on one single pass late in the song substitutes, in place of the expected A♯dim7, a **C7♭13♭9** (the ♭II of B — i.e. the tritone sub of the V, F♯7). The substitution keeps every other note of the surrounding voicing identical and changes only the bass/root underneath, "recontextualizing" the whole stack into something new. Deliberately withheld from the chorus and every earlier loop — saved for one high-stakes moment late in the song specifically so it lands as a surprise rather than wearing thin through repetition. → [[Chromatic Mediants and Pop Modulation]], [[Extended Chords]], [[Chord Progressions]]
- **A clean call-and-response hook resets the ear after a maximalist section, and is worth an immediate repeat because it IS the payoff.** Jake Kaufman's Shovel Knight soundtrack (cited as a personal favorite composer, praised for tracker-based "maximalism" — constantly shifting harmony, interweaving melodies, changing keys and time signatures throughout) briefly drops into a simple, clear call-and-response figure — a melody phrase, then a stabbed chord response — and repeats that exact figure once more before diving back into denser material. The moment of clarity works specifically *because* of the density surrounding it; repeating it once immediately (rather than moving on after a single pass) is what lets it register as a hook rather than a passing detail. → [[Motivic Development]]
- **The format itself is a transferable listening habit, independent of any single example**: pick a piece of music you genuinely love, isolate the specific moment that does it for you, listen to it repeatedly with focused attention, and articulate in words what it's actually doing structurally/harmonically/rhythmically — turning passive appreciation into an analyzable, reusable compositional idea. Explicitly framed as a first attempt at this format that depends on audience response to continue.
