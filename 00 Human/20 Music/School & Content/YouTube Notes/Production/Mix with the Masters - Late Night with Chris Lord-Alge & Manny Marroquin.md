## Synopsis

A 25-minute, late-night, off-the-cuff conversation between Chris Lord-Alge and Manny Marroquin at La Fourmi during one of Marroquin's Mix with the Masters seminar weeks — CLA interviewing his friend and peer, though the questions run both directions. This is almost entirely career history and mixing philosophy, not a technical breakdown: how each of them got into mixing (Marroquin cites Bob Power and Jimbo Barton as his first studied influences, CLA cites Bob Clearmountain via "Good Times" by Chic), the records that put them on the map (Marroquin: Whitney Houston's "Heartbreak Hotel"; both reflect on John Mayer's *Continuum* with Michael Brauer as a career highlight), a nostalgic detour into early-'90s tape-format history (ADAT vs. DA-88 vs. analog multitrack, and the 15–20-minute manual process of building a reverse-reverb effect on tape before digital samplers made it a one-second job), and a closing shared philosophy that individual style — not copyable settings, plugins, or EQ curves — is what actually separates one mixer's work from another's. There is essentially no concrete signal-chain, plugin, or setting information in this video; it is captured here for the vault's completeness (as a Mix with the Masters source and for Nate's own career/craft-philosophy reference) rather than for technique extraction.

## What's taught

- **Career origin stories, not technique.** Marroquin: studied Bob Power's kick/snare sound early on, was mentored by Jimbo Barton on *Operation: Mindcrime* and *Empire* (watching Barton move mics inches at a time while EQing live, swapping through five snares and ten kicks per session on a real budget). CLA: traces his own start to Bob Clearmountain via "Good Times," "Let's Dance," "Reckless," and Springsteen.
- **A minor historical/technical aside on tape-era transfers — kept qualitative, era-specific, not a modern actionable technique.** Marroquin recalls that transferring ADAT tracks through the mixing console's EQ and gain stage on the way to 2-inch analog tape (rather than a clean digital transfer) audibly improved the sound: "we just transferred everything through the desk, a little bit of EQ to the two-inch, and it sounded considerably better... because everything got a little bit of [analog coloration] to it." The general principle — summing/transferring through an analog stage adds coloration that a clean digital bounce doesn't — echoes ground this vault already covers via saturation/tape-emulation plugins, but the specific ADAT-through-console workflow has no direct modern equivalent worth documenting as its own technique.
- **A 15–20-minute manual reverse-reverb build on tape**, described as a period curiosity rather than a technique to adopt: sample the reverb into an Eventide H3000, record it backward to tape, physically flip the reel, and re-record — versus a one-second flip in Pro Tools today.
- **Shared closing philosophy: individual style, not copyable settings, is what actually distinguishes one mixer's work.** "No matter how many of you copy my plugins, my settings, his settings, his plugins, his EQs, his panning, his monitoring, his compressor setting — it doesn't matter. What matters is here [ears/mind] and today." And on mixing on unfamiliar gear with students: doing so is "a real mix" because you're forced to actually mix rather than lean on a familiar rig's muscle memory. A career-craft point, not a mix-chain one — kept here rather than forced onto a technique page.
- **"We put a little piece of our heart into every mix we've ever done."** Both frame mixing as an emotional act performed *for* the artist (treated like a family member's work, not a technician's task) rather than a purely technical exercise — the same theme Marroquin develops at length in [[Mix with the Masters 2012 - Manny Marroquin]].
