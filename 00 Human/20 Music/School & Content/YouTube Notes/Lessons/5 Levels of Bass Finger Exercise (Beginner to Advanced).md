The five levels build in a clean, deliberate order: level 1 — right fingers, right frets, aim for the end of the fret; level 2 — fingers-down-as-you-ascend, release-one-at-a-time-as-you-descend; level 3 — minimize "flying fingers" range of motion on release; level 4 — a new two-string version of the exercise, where the level-4 goal is lifting each finger *toward* the next string it's needed on rather than just off the fretboard; level 5 — a full one-octave chromatic scale across three strings, with a "system reset" minimum-pressure technique layered on top of all the previous levels' goals. ## What's taught

**Setup (all levels).** Frets 9–12 on the G string (levels 1–3), chosen as "the easiest spot on the neck for most people to use all four fingers" regardless of hand size. Plucking hand: alternate index-middle throughout — this lesson is explicitly fretting-hand-only, plucking mechanics aren't taught here. Tempo tool: a drum-loop app (Drum Beats Plus on iPhone, Loops on Android, ~$5) so practice happens against a beat rather than free-time — framed as both making the drill feel more musical and building the separate skill of "locking in with a drummer." Find a tempo that's your "edge" — mostly clean with occasional mistakes, not sloppy the whole way through.

- **Level 1 — Basic pattern, aim for the end of the fret (~0:50–4:40).** Index-middle-ring-pinky ascending (frets 9-10-11-12), then pinky-ring-middle-index descending, same G-string position. **Fret placement is the actual technique point:** press right behind the fret wire, not mid-fret-space — that's what gets a clean note with minimum pressure, vs. a "rattling" sound from fretting too far back. If a rattle persists after correcting placement, add a bit more pressure (not a lot). **Start absurdly slow** on any new level before adding tempo. **The single biggest mistake named across the whole video:** trying to perform the entire exercise start-to-finish out of the gate, instead of **bite-sizing** — isolating the exact 2-3-note transition that's failing (e.g. ring-pinky, ring-pinky on a loop) until it's consistently clean, *then* reintegrating it into the full pass. Repeating the whole exercise while one micro-element is still wrong just "bakes mistakes and bad habits" in rather than fixing anything.
- **Level 2 — Fingers down ascending, release one at a time descending (~4:40–8:00).** Ascending: add each finger without lifting the ones already down (index stays as middle lands, both stay as ring lands, all three stay as pinky lands) — the hand progressively closes onto the position. Descending: the reverse — release fingers one at a time (pinky, then ring, then middle) rather than lifting and re-pressing simultaneously. Payoff stated directly: this halves the coordination demand, because each transition becomes a single motion (add one finger, or subtract one finger) instead of a simultaneous lift-and-press. **Tempo gate to move to level 3:** half notes at 120 BPM for beginners (one note every two beats), quarter notes at 120 for advanced. Explicitly normal to spend a month or two stuck at level 2 before passing that gate — "the point is not to blow through all five exercises as fast as you can."
- **Level 3 — Reduce flying-fingers range of motion (~8:00–11:10).** Same riff, same fingers, new goal: keep released fingers close to the neck instead of springing away from it ("flying fingers"). Named payoff: less wasted motion = both energy savings and a shorter trip back to the next note, i.e. a direct speed lever. The pinky is named as the specific finger that resists this the most. **Isolation fix, chained two levels deep if needed:** first isolate down to just the pinky (all other fingers held down, only the pinky presses/lifts); once that's under control and the *ring* finger's release starts dragging the pinky along with it, isolate that specific pair (ring-pinky ring-pinky, or ring-middle ring-middle) before reintegrating. **Progress-tracking tip:** once tempo starts moving, log it on paper — date, exercise name, BPM, and a felt-sense note — as visible proof of improvement for days practice feels stuck. Tempo gate: same half-notes/quarter-notes-at-120 ladder as level 2.
- **Level 4 — New pattern: string-crossing with directional lift (~11:10–15:40).** The exercise changes shape for the first time: D string fret 9, index-middle-ring-pinky, then shift to the G string, index-middle-ring-pinky; reverse coming back — G string pinky-ring-middle-index, then D string pinky-ring-middle-index. **The level-4 goal:** lift each finger *in the direction of the string it's next needed on*, not just off the fretboard. Ascending case: the instant the pinky note on the D string plays, the index finger should already be moving toward the G string so the other fingers can follow it into position. Descending case (named as the trickier direction): after the pinky's D-string note, the pinky's release motion should aim back toward the D string (where it's needed two notes later), not just lift straight up — same logic applies finger-by-finger through ring and middle, and once the index lands the 9th-fret D-string note, the pinky should already be arriving over the 12th fret of the G string, "set behind" the index. Explicitly named as a level a lot of gigging professional players never fully nail — worth doing regardless, both for physical ease over a set and as groundwork for future speed/shred goals. Tempo gate: same 120 BPM ladder, now judged against all four accumulated level goals at once.
- **Level 5 — One-octave chromatic scale + minimum-pressure "system reset" (~15:40–20:30).** The riff itself: a full 12-note chromatic scale, G to G, across three strings — A string fret 10 (index) through fret 12 (pinky, ascending index-middle-ring-pinky), shift to D string fret 9 (index) through fret 12 (pinky), shift to G string fret 8 (index) through fret 12 (pinky) for the first half; then descending back down pinky-ring-middle-index through the same three strings in reverse (G string fret 12→9, D string fret 13→...→10 on the way, A string fret 14 down to the starting fret), more string-crossing and vertical shifts than level 4. **The level-5 goal — minimum pressure via a "system reset":** deliberately relax the fretting hand to *zero* pressure first (fingers touch the string but produce no sound), then add pressure back in very small increments until the note just goes clean — that minimum-clean-pressure point is usually noticeably *lighter* than the pressure a player habitually uses. Hold that new lighter pressure for the whole exercise, and periodically re-check that grip hasn't crept back up ("gripping the neck like you're trying to murder it"). Demonstrated on all three riff versions (levels 1-3's G-string pattern, level 4's two-string crossing, and level 5's full chromatic run) — the reset is a technique to reapply at any level, not a level-5-only trick. Tempo gate: same half-notes/quarter-notes-at-120 target, held across all five accumulated goals simultaneously.
- **Beyond level 5.** Once level 5 is clean at target tempo, go back to level 3 (the last version before string-crossing was added) and push the BPM higher, using quarter notes at 120 (or even sixteenth notes) as one concrete advanced target. All five level *goals* generalize to any other bass finger exercise — the video is explicit that the specific riff is disposable, the transferable skill is the goal stack: end-of-fret placement → progressive add/subtract fingering → minimized flying-fingers range → directional string-crossing lifts → minimum pressure, plus the bite-sizing method for isolating and fixing whatever specific transition is failing at any level.
- **Closing quote (Victor Wooten, cited):** "technique is very important because you can have all the ideas in the world, but if you can't express them then you're going to be frustrated" — framed as the actual stakes behind grinding through five levels of a fretting drill: the frustration of a hard exercise is temporary, the frustration of bad technique blocking self-expression is ongoing.
