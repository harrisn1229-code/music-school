# Octave and Unison Shapes for Fretboard Navigation

Part of [[Guitar Positions]]. Jared Borkowski's (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared) two prerequisite skills for "taking any chord you can play and finding many other places to play that exact same chord" — stated explicitly as groundwork for a follow-up video on manipulating chord voicings around the neck. Unlike [[Root-to-Root Scale-Form Mapping Method]] (learning a scale shape's five positions), this pair of drills is about **relocating a single already-known pitch** elsewhere on the fretboard — either an octave away or the identical pitch (unison) on a neighboring string — which is the actual mechanism underneath moving a chord voicing to a new string set or neck area. Episode 11 of the channel's chord-theory series; references episodes 2–3 for the "major scale as a measuring stick" counting method reused in skill 2 below.

## Skill 1 — The Closest Octave Exercise

For any fretted note, learn the **nearest available octave shape(s)** relative to it — not the general/skip-a-string octave shape most players already default to, but a full drill through every string that makes you consciously see an octave in **both directions** (toward the thicker/lower strings and toward the thinner/higher strings) wherever both exist.

**How to drill it:**
1. Start on the 6th string (low E), any fret (worked example: 7th fret). Play the note, then play the octave that is closest to it — there will be an octave shape available toward the higher strings.
2. Repeat starting from the 5th string, same fret. **The octave shape itself changes** compared to the 6th-string version — Jared calls this out explicitly as the reason to drill *every* string rather than learning one octave shape and assuming it transfers: "the octave shape changes... that's why we want to do this throughout all these strings."
3. Continue through the 4th, 3rd, 2nd, and 1st strings. **The shape changes again specifically around the G (3rd) → B (2nd) string transition**, because that's the one pair of adjacent strings tuned a major 3rd apart instead of a perfect 4th — "because of the tuning of the guitar it's going to always throw things off when we're mapping stuff out."
4. **Edge cases:** off the 3rd string there is no octave available toward the lower strings (you run out of string) — only the higher-string octave. Off the 4th string (working the descending pass) there's no octave available toward the higher strings — only the lower-string octave. Treat these as one-directional stops in the drill, not errors.
5. **Do the whole thing twice — ascending (start on the 6th string, work up to the 1st) and then descending (start on the 1st/highest string, work back down to the 6th).** Descending forces you to "think in a different way" rather than just running the ascending pattern backward from memory — different angle, different context, same shapes.

**Why it works (stated purpose):** most players know *one* octave shape (root here, octave there, done) and stop. Drilling every string in both directions builds the habit of noticing "that same note is an octave above over here, *and* an octave above over here" reflexively — described as foundational for fretboard analysis generally and for seeing that the same notes exist inside a chord in multiple places, which is the actual prerequisite for relocating a chord voicing.

**Practice note:** don't worry about which fingers you use for this drill — it's a seeing/hearing exercise, not a fingering study.

## Skill 2 — Cross-String Unison Finding via Major-Scale Chunking

Finding the **exact same pitch** (not an octave — the literal same note) on the string immediately above or below, **without counting frets one at a time.** Jared's stated problem with fret-counting: "it's very kind of vague feeling to count a number of frets... it still kind of feels like guessing." His fix: use major-scale scale-degree counting as a chunking device — the same "major scale as a measuring stick" concept from earlier in the series (episodes 2–3) — even when you aren't in a major key or thinking about a real chord's root. The numbers 1–7 here are **not** scale degrees of any actual key; they're just a fast, chunkable way to count two whole-steps' worth of distance instead of "four or five frets, I think."

**Finding the unison toward the body (the next string up in pitch):**
1. From your starting note, locate scale-degree **3** on the next string up (count "1, 2, 3" — two whole steps from an imaginary "1" a step below your note, on that string).
2. Step back down through **2**, then **1** — landing on **1** gives you the same pitch as your original note.
3. Chunk it as "two whole steps" rather than a raw fret count — that's the whole point of using the scale-degree frame instead of counting frets one at a time.

**Finding the unison toward the nut (the next string down in pitch):**
1. Locate scale-degree **5** on the string below — Jared gives the direct shortcut: "if one is here on a fifth string, five is always on the same fret below it," i.e. no separate counting needed once you know the interval between that string pair.
2. Walk up through **6**, **7**, and back to **1** — landing on **1** gives you the unison.

**Both patterns shift by one fret across the G (3rd) → B (2nd) string pair** — same caveat as Skill 1's octave shapes, and for the same reason (major-3rd tuning interval instead of perfect-4th). Verify the pattern on that specific string pair rather than assuming it copies straight over from the others.

**Two-stage practice method (do both, in this order):**
1. **Walk it — play every in-between note.** Play your starting note, then physically play 3-2-1 (or 5-6-7-1) as real notes on the adjacent string, landing on the unison. This is the "seeing the bridge" stage — you're building the connective tissue between the two pitches, not just memorizing a jump.
2. **Visualize it — jump straight to the target.** Once the walked version is comfortable, skip playing the in-between notes and jump directly from the starting note to the unison, but keep **thinking through** the 3-2-1 (or 5-6-7-1) bridge mentally as you jump. Explicit warning: don't just memorize "I jump to fret X" as a bare number — out of the context of this specific drill (e.g. mid-piece, on a different string than where you last drilled it) you won't have that memorized number available, so the mental bridge has to actually be there, not a shortcut around it.

**Drill through all six strings, ascending then descending — same "different angle, different direction" repetition logic as Skill 1.**
