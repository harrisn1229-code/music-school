# How much music theory did The Beatles know?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HmjRM3AziTY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HmjRM3AziTY)

## Synopsis

A biographical/philosophical piece rather than a technique breakdown: using interviews and quotes, argues the Beatles had a real working grasp of music-theory *concepts* (key, chord function, rhythm, reharmonization via bass notes) without the vocabulary to name them — and that producer George Martin functioned as the "translator" who converted their intuitive ideas into formal terms and notation for outside musicians. Closes with a broader argument for why analyzing a song with theory is valid regardless of whether its writer could name what they were doing.

## What's taught

- **Rhythm/meter awareness without formal terms**: Paul's off-beat count-ins (e.g. "1-2-3-4-1" for *All My Loving*, which starts on beat 3) show real bar/beat awareness; "24 bars" used as a placeholder count during *A Day in the Life*'s recording. Time signatures described by feel/analogy rather than name — the 3/4 middle-eight of *We Can Work It Out* described by Harrison as "waltz time." Ringo Starr, on *Here Comes the Sun*'s irregular meters, describes working entirely by feel rather than counting beats.
- **Chord/reharmonization awareness**: George Harrison describing *A Hard Day's Night*'s opening chord as "F with a G on the top string" (an F add9, though he didn't have that name for it) while correctly understanding that McCartney's bass note underneath determines the chord's actual sounding quality (with a D bass, the same shape sounds as Dm11). Paul independently arrived at the same "bass note changes the chord" idea after noticing the same technique on the Beach Boys' *Pet Sounds*. Paul describing *She Loves You*'s closing G6 chord as "that tight little sixth cluster."
- **Key-change awareness without the term "key change"**: Paul's account of writing *From Me to You*'s middle eight (a ii-V into F major) shows he understood the harmonic *effect* of the modulation ("going to a G minor and a C takes you to a whole new world") without using the phrase "key change." George Harrison shows more explicit key-change awareness discussing why a tambura couldn't be added to *Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds* (a chord-changing, modulating song).
- **Modal awareness without the term**: Paul on *I'll Get You*'s A minor-in-a-major-key move (a Mixolydian-adjacent modal interchange) — he recognized it as unusual/fascinating without identifying it as modal.
- **George Martin's role as translator**: classically trained (Guildhall School of Music), Martin transcribed the band's ideas into sheet music for session musicians, pushed back on/negotiated their more unconventional requests (Paul insisting on an "un-string-quartet" major 7th in the *Yesterday* string arrangement), and made structural suggestions of his own (shortening a repeated intro from 8 to 6 bars on *Can't Buy Me Love* to speed the transition into the verse). The band reportedly didn't even know the word "tempo" before working with him.
- **Closing argument**: music theory functions as a *language* for describing/communicating musical ideas, not a rulebook that constrains them — the Beatles had the underlying musical instincts and concepts but lacked that shared vocabulary, which is precisely the gap Martin filled; analyzing their songs with theory after the fact is still valid regardless of what terms they themselves used while writing.
