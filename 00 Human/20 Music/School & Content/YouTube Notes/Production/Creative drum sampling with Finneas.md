# Creative drum sampling with Finneas

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CPROY0-0T2U)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CPROY0-0T2U)

## Synopsis

A short Mix with the Masters breakdown of Finneas walking through one song's drum-and-sample build, start to finish: the track began with him alone, programming a drum beat that became the song's core percussion, then grew outward — a heavily-processed "old sounding" string patch, an 808 built inside a deep parametric 808 synth, and a kick/snare/clap/tom kit built around finding good individual sounds and layering them. The most valuable thing in the video isn't any single setting, it's the stated philosophy that runs through the whole thing: a raw starting sound (a self-played patch, a purchased sample) is only ever a starting place, and the satisfying part of production is dressing that raw sound up until it's "kind of unrecognizable." He also names a direct compositional lesson pulled from writing "bad guy" — make the bass line itself singable, not just the vocal — and describes how the drum kit's real job is to coexist with that bassline's pocket rather than stand alone. Tool-wise it's a useful snapshot of a working producer's engine migrating over time: Battery was his primary sampler when this song was written; by the time of the interview he'd moved to Logic Pro's then-new Drum Machine Designer/Quick Sampler update instead.

## Techniques

- **A raw sound (self-recorded, a patch played live, or a purchased sample) is a starting place, never the ceiling — the goal is to process it until it's "kind of unrecognizable."** Stated directly as a core production lesson: "the starting place for a sound... doesn't mean that you can't sort of change it into something kind of unrecognizable... when you take something where you're like okay this is what it looks like when you don't dress it up and then you dress it up, it becomes — it takes on so much character and totally changes the way that it actually is." Demonstrated on the song's string layer (below). → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]]
- **The song started from a solo drum-programming session** — "the very first piece of it was I was alone... sitting and making a drum beat which became the sort of main percussion element of this song" — everything else (strings, 808, additional layers) was built on top of that foundation afterward, not composed first and drummed-to.
- **A "crazy kind of messed up old sounding" string layer, built from Output's Analog Strings run through a five-stage processing chain: sample delay → clip distortion → Valhalla Room → a channel EQ → Little AlterBoy** — the combined result read as cinematic, "some Hans Zimmer type things," entirely from stacking simple effects on one patch rather than sourcing a different, already-cinematic sound. → [[ValhallaRoom]], [[Little AlterBoy]], [[Plugin Wishlist]], [[Finneas Drum Sampling Chain]]
- **Output Analog Strings' own onboard Dirt, Delay, and Pitch faders do real "imaging" work on their own, before any outboard FX are added** — called out by name as one of Output's best plugins specifically for how satisfying those three controls are to move; a reminder that an instrument's built-in character controls can carry real weight in a chain, not just outboard sends. → [[Plugin Wishlist]], [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **Battery was the primary drum-sampling engine for this song, but has since been replaced by Logic Pro's newly-updated Drum Machine Designer + Quick Sampler** — a real example of a tool preference shifting over time inside one working producer's setup, not a one-off comment: "at the time I was using Battery, that was my primary engine... that's all I use now." → [[Drum Rack]], [[Simpler]]
- **The actual Battery workflow described is pure auditioning, not deep sound design** — "I adore Battery as a plug-in, so I'll just go through my library and pick out a sound... this felt really cool, this just felt like really interesting" — browse the library and stop when something grabs you, rather than building the sound from raw synthesis. → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]]
- **PanMan run on a picked drum layer with an always-on, continuously-moving "hard bounce MIDI" pattern (not a one-off swipe), purely for imaging depth.** "I popped this plug-in, PanMan, on it — which again just for imaging, just takes things out of just like sort of mono mania... I just thought that adding this sort of — it's just called hard bounce MIDI, so it's happening all the time — I just thought that added a real sort of like depth to it that was really exciting." *(qualitative only — no Offset/Width/Rate given)* → [[PanMan]], [[Width and Stereo]]
- **Two layered kicks — a sparse "one and one" (downbeat-only) kick plus a second kick pattern hitting beats 1 and 3 — with the primary kick chosen for its own sound first, then evaluated by how it layers with the rest.** "This primary kick... it's really about like the actual sound itself and the sounds that I'm layering it with, so once I find a good sound then I'm pretty excited about it." → [[Layering and Stacking]], [[Kick Design]]
- **Claps layered directly onto the backbeat (2-and-4) snare as one composite hit, plus a second snare doubled with a deliberately "crappy"-toned tom sound reserved for a specific section (named as chorus) rather than run through the whole song** — described as sounding like "20th century fox or something," i.e. reached for as a fanfare-adjacent character hit, not a generic fill tom. *(auto-captioning is ambiguous on whether the tom layer is chorus-exclusive or chorus-absent — logged qualitatively rather than guessed)* → [[Layering and Stacking]], [[Sound Selection Guide]], [[Snare Design]]
- **A stated minimalism preference for drum programming: "I love minimalism, I love simplicity — anything that's just like a wonderful foundation for other things to develop is really exciting to me."** The drum kit's actual job, in his framing, is less about standing on its own and more about how it "coexists" with the rest of the arrangement (the bassline specifically, below).
- **The pocket comes from the bassline sitting on the eighth note, locked together with an eighth-note hi-hat pattern (referred to on-screen as "zipper").** "That bass is really happening primarily on the eighth note, essentially in time with 'zipper,' which is really what — what is the sort of eighth-note hi-hat." → [[Pocket and Microtiming]], [[Bassline Writing]]
- **A direct compositional lesson credited to writing "bad guy": make the bass line itself singable, not just the vocal melody.** "That's sort of a lesson I learned from, like, 'bad guy' — like how fun it is to make an instrumental part that's like really hooky, and you can sort of sing it in your own right, as opposed to only thinking of the vocal line as the thing that you'd sing along to." → [[Bassline Writing]], [[Melody Writing]]
- **808 Studio (and its since-released sequel, 808 Studio 2) valued specifically for its deep parametric envelope control, over the more common "find an 808 sample and pitch it on the keyboard" approach.** "This song before 808 Studio 2 came out... I tweaked all the envelopes on this, the sub box — there's just a lot of different parameters that you can tweak on this plug-in, which is great, as opposed to... people would just be sort of finding an 808 sample and then throwing it on the keyboard, and this engine is just really really fun." → [[Roland TR-808]], [[Bass Design]], [[Plugin Wishlist]], [[Sound Selection Guide]]
