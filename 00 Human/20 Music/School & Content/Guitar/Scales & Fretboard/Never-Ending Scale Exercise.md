# Never-Ending Scale Exercise

Part of [[Guitar Positions]] and [[Jazz Harmony]] — Kurt Rosenwinkel's own stated practice method (relayed by Jens Larsen from master classes he attended as a student), for building the skill of a **melodic idea that survives a chord change** instead of resetting direction at every barline. Adjacent to, but distinct from, [[Continuous Scale Exercise]] (Jared Borkowski's drill for tunes that change keys often): that exercise trains finding the *physically closest correct note* the instant the harmony changes and moving by step only; this one deliberately **decouples the scale-change moment from the actual chord-change moment** — you keep a line going as if the progression were static, then swap scale on your own schedule (roughly every 8 notes), not the harmony's. The goal in both cases is melodic freedom across changes, but Rosenwinkel's version trains *pre-planning ahead of the harmony* rather than *reacting instantly to it*.

> Transcript note: the source captions render Rosenwinkel's name for this as "the never-ending scale of cosines" — almost certainly a mishearing/mistranscription of the actual term (possibly "concepts"). Kept here verbatim as heard rather than guessed at; the mechanics below are unambiguous regardless of the exact name.

## The core idea
Take a chord progression and keep a scale (or other harmonic unit) running continuously **as if the progression were static/"in time"** — i.e. don't stop and re-derive the correct scale/arpeggio the instant a new chord arrives. Instead:

1. **Play roughly eight notes through the harmony** (Rosenwinkel's worked example: a turnaround with altered dominants), thinking of the line as a continuous run rather than one scale-choice-per-chord.
2. **Change scale whenever you've played about eight notes** — on your own note-count schedule, not necessarily synced to the actual chord boundary. The chord may already have changed underneath you by the time you swap; that's the point.
3. **The practice target is thinking ahead** — pre-planning the next scale/unit before you need it, and keeping the melodic movement going, rather than stopping to solve "what's the right scale for *this* chord" reactively at each change.

## Generalizes beyond scales
Rosenwinkel states the same "keep it moving, swap the unit every ~8 notes rather than at the chord boundary" logic works with other harmonic building blocks, not just parent scales:
- **Diatonic triads** — run the same continuous-movement, delayed-swap approach using triads stacked from the underlying scale (see [[Harmonized Scale Triads for Soloing]] for how to derive the seven triads of a scale).
- **Diatonic seventh chords** — same approach, one harmonic step up in density from triads.

## Why this works (the melody-first payoff)
This is the mechanical practice version of the broader trait documented on [[Solo Dynamics and Arc]] and [[🎵 KURT ROSENWINKEL - Nefertiti (Ballad Session)]]: an ascending or descending melodic idea that keeps its direction *through* a chord change, rather than resetting at the barline, reads as far more vocal/coherent than note-perfect but direction-resetting playing — "you can keep the melodic idea going across the bar line." Practicing the never-ending-scale approach is what builds the reflex to let a line's own momentum outrank the temptation to "correctly" restart at every chord.

## Worked example — Rosenwinkel's Nefertiti solo (Mark Turner, *Ballad Session*)
The opening phrase of the analyzed excerpt is a direct real-world instance: a fairly plain ascending D major scale run starts over an A sus4 chord, and **keeps ascending in the same direction once the chord moves to D7alt/B♭** instead of turning around or restarting — exactly the "idea becomes free of the changes" behavior this exercise trains.
