# Why Elton takes 2½ minutes to get to the chorus

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Mf3-8KlZ3wE)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Mf3-8KlZ3wE)

## Synopsis

A structural deep-dive into "Tiny Dancer" (Elton John, 1972), which deliberately withholds its chorus until 2:33 — against the "hook in the first minute" convention — and runs 6:12 with no possible radio edit. Breaks down the specific arrangement devices that keep the long wait from feeling boring, and the commercial cost the band accepted for the choice.

## Techniques

- **Deliberately restructuring a lyric to withhold the chorus, against the songwriter's own default instinct**: Bernie Taupin's original lyric draft was closer to a standard verse/pre-chorus/chorus layout; Elton specifically chose to run two verses back-to-back before the first pre-chorus, pushing the chorus back — a conscious structural rewrite of the given material, not an accident of the lyric's shape.
- **A verse built as a miniature AABA (32-bar/American Songbook) form, giving the listener a "preview chorus" early even though the real chorus is far off**: each "Tiny Dancer" verse runs A (line 1) - A (repeated melody/harmony) - B (new melody/harmony) - A (return, and the line containing the title itself) — meaning the listener gets something chorus-shaped, including the title hook, within the first minute, even though the true chorus doesn't land until 2:33. Cites the technique's history: standard structure for 1920s-40s American Songbook standards ("Over the Rainbow," "I Got Rhythm," "Blue Moon," "Anything Goes"), rare in modern pop but not extinct ("Make You Feel My Love," Dylan/Adele).
- **A pre-chorus doing double duty as a dynamic AND tonal contrast device, specifically to "reset" listener attention before finally paying off**: at 2:12, the first pre-chorus breaks the song's flowing legato piano texture with bright staccato chords, AND simultaneously shifts from the song's home key (C major) to the parallel minor (C minor) — the first harmonic departure in the entire song up to that point. Both changes are deliberately positioned as "something is coming" signals right before the long-withheld payoff.
- **Using a same-scale-degree major/minor pivot to make the chorus's arrival read as maximally bright**: the pre-chorus's move to C minor sets up the chorus's return to C major — because the chorus re-enters in the parallel MAJOR relative to what was just heard, the tonal shift itself (not just melody/dynamics) makes the chorus feel unusually "bright and brilliant," a direct payoff of the darker pre-chorus that preceded it.
- **A tempo/dynamic slowdown placed directly before the payoff moment, functioning as word painting AND anticipation-building at once**: the lyric "I say softly, slowly" is set to genuinely slower, softer music right before the chorus — described by the songwriter himself as intentional, and by the video as "the musical equivalent of a rollercoaster slowing just before it plummets" — the deceleration is what stretches out the anticipation in the final seconds before release.
- **A doubled chorus once it finally arrives, extending runtime further specifically to make the long wait feel "worth it"**: rather than a single chorus pass, the first arrival is immediately doubled — explicitly framed as Elton wanting the payoff to be "something special" given how long the build-up lyric took to earn it.
- **Naming the actual commercial mechanism behind the "hook in 60 seconds, under 4 minutes" convention, and the real cost of breaking it**: radio formatting, not an arbitrary aesthetic rule — songs over ~4 minutes are rarely played on radio at all, or get cut down to a radio edit; "Tiny Dancer" has no radio edit (only the 6:12 album version exists) and can't be faded early since a 3-minute fade would leave only one chorus. Consequence, with numbers: the song only reached #41 on the US chart on release (vs. Elton's typical top-10/#1 run at the time) and wasn't released as a UK single at all; it took until 2005 (34 years) to reach 500k US sales, a pace dramatically slower than same-era Elton hits, before eventually becoming one of his most enduring songs.
- **The explicit songwriting principle stated as the video's takeaway**: conventions are "a great framework to refine songwriting" but not "a foolproof formula" — arrangement should serve the individual song's needs (a long, cinematic lyric warranting a long build), not a one-size-fits-all commercial template, even when that means accepting worse initial chart performance.
