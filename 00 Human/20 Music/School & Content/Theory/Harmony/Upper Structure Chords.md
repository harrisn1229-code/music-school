# Upper Structure Chords

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Chords]] / [[Chord Naming by Context]]. An **upper structure** is a chord (usually a triad, but any chord type works) played over a *different* bass note. The same upper voicing gets a completely different name, function, and color depending purely on which bass note sits underneath it — the jazz/jazz-fusion cousin of a [[Chord Inversions|slash chord]], pushed further: the bass note isn't necessarily even a tone from the upper chord.

## The idea
- Pick any chord as the "upper structure" (triads are typical, but 7th chords and beyond work too).
- Put a bass note underneath it. If that bass note is already a tone of the upper chord, you just get an inversion. If it isn't, you get a genuinely new composite chord — a different name, a different function, sometimes a different key implication entirely.
- **Where to place it**: the most common, most reliably useful placement is the **♭6 degree of a major key** (or the natural 7 of a minor key) — put the upper structure's root there and build out from that home position.

## The six bass-note options (worked from an E-minor upper structure on the ♭6 of E major = C)

| Bass note relationship to the upper structure's root | Resulting chord (in E major, upper structure = Em, bass on C) | Function / typical resolution |
|---|---|---|
| **Same as the upper structure's root** | ♭6 major chord — Cmaj7♯11 (= maj7♭5) | No fixed resolution; can sit unresolved. Optional smooth move: ♭6 → 5 (half step down) |
| **A 4th below the root** (upper structure read as the bass note's ♭7) | ♭7 dominant 13 — D13 | Dominant function: backdoor resolution to I, tritone sub into the chord a half-step up, straight V7 into a new key/modal chord |
| **A major 3rd below the root** (upper structure read as the bass note's ♭3) | iv minor6/9 — Am6add9 | Dramatic, tension/tragedy color; commonly resolves to I but forgiving — works resolving almost anywhere |
| **A major 3rd above the root, enharmonically** (upper structure read as an altered dominant's upper triad) | Altered dominant ♭7♯5♯9 — G♯7♯5♯9 (= V7alt/vi) | Classic jazz altered-dominant sound; typically resolves down a 5th into a minor chord, but can target IV, ii, ♭III (as a tritone sub), or the relative/parallel major |
| **A ♯4 above the root** (rarer) | "Spanish dominant" — 7sus4♭9-type (F♯7sus4♭9) | Suspended-dominant color, resolves similarly to a plain V7sus |
| **A ♭9/♭2 above the root** (rarer) | Same shape a half-step away (B7sus4♭9) | Functions similarly to the ♯4 option above |

## Using it
- **Chain multiple bass-note readings of the same upper triad through one progression** — alternate a plain diatonic chord with an upper-structure reading, cycling through 2+ of the six options above, connected by smooth bass motion/voice-leading between the bass notes. This is how a full upper-structure progression gets built rather than using just one option in isolation.
- **Not every dominant-function upper structure needs to resolve where you'd expect** — especially in fusion, deliberately NOT resolving an unstable-sounding upper structure (or resolving it somewhere unexpected) is a stylistic choice, not an error.
- **Practice method**: pick one upper-structure triad, place it on the ♭6 (major) or natural 7 (minor) of a key, and write a progression using at least two of the six bass-note options above — the real skill is the voice-leading/bass-motion connecting them, not just knowing the chord names.

> [!note] Two different "upper structure" devices — don't conflate them
> This page's upper structure is a **different chord dropped over a foreign bass note** (a reharmonization/polychord device). [[Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords]] uses the same term for something narrower and more literal: the triad already sitting inside a seventh chord's own 3rd-5th-7th, extracted as a lighter-weight stand-in for the full chord. Same word, two jazz devices.
