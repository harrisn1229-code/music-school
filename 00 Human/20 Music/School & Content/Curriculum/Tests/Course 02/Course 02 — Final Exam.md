> **Q1.** **Tonic I, vi, iii · subdominant IV, ii · dominant V, vii°**
> vi and iii substitute for I because they share notes with it.
>
> **Q2.** **T → SD → D → T — home, departure, tension, return**
> Most of Western popular music in four symbols.
>
> **Q3.** **SD – D – T – T**
> F is IV, G is V, Em is iii and Am is vi — the last two are both tonic substitutes.
>
> **Q4.** **The authentic cadence — the dominant holds the key's maximum tension and the tonic answers all of it**
> It's the default ending of most Western phrases for that reason.
>
> **Q5.** **A leading tone**
> Nothing in IV pulls up by a semitone into the tonic, so the arrival happens without tension being discharged.
>
> **Q6.** **vi shares two of its three notes with I**
> The ear is prepared for I and gets a chord that's mostly I, so it accepts the near-miss.
>
> **Q7.** **It leaves the phrase unresolved, so it reads as a question**
> Ending *on* the dominant rather than resolving to it keeps the tension standing.
>
> **Q8.** **The antecedent phrase asks and the consequent answers**
> A 4-bar phrase ending on V demands a second 4-bar phrase that ends on I, and the pair reads as one 8-bar unit.
>
> **Q9.** **B and F — B rises a half step to C, F falls a half step to E**
> Both resolve inward, by the smallest possible distance, onto chord tones of C.
>
> **Q10.** **It's the most unstable interval available, and it sits inside every dominant 7th**
> Six semitones, splitting the octave exactly, with no consonant reading.
>
> **Q11.** **Both tritone notes sit a semitone from a tonic chord tone. Tritone substitution shares the fact.**
> *Rubric (5):* names the tritone as the source of the instability (1) · says each note resolves by half step into a chord tone of I (2) · names tritone substitution, or another technique built on the same interval (2)
> A dominant 7th contains a tritone, the most unstable interval in the system, so the chord is heard as needing to go somewhere. The resolution is inevitable rather than merely likely because it's maximally efficient: both voices move a semitone and both land on tonic chord tones. Tritone substitution follows directly — two dominant chords a tritone apart share the same tritone, so they can stand in for each other and resolve to the same place.
>
> **Q12.** **E7, made by raising the 3rd of Em to G♯ — labelled V/vi**
> *Also accepted:* E7 · five of six
>
> **Q13.** **A borrowed leading tone for the target chord**
> It sits a semitone below the target's root and pulls into it.
>
> **Q14.** **A non-diatonic dominant 7th that resolves down a fifth**
> The quality is the tell — pull needs the tritone only a dominant 7th supplies.
>
> **Q15.** **Fm (iv) · A♭ (♭VI) · B♭ (♭VII) · E♭ (♭III)**
> *Also accepted:* any four parallel-minor triads with correct numerals
>
> **Q16.** **Because the tonic never moves — only the available notes change**
> A key change relocates home. Interchange keeps home where it is and repaints around it.
>
> **Q17.** **Secondary dominants add pull; borrowed chords add colour**
> *Rubric (5):* states the pull-versus-colour distinction (2) · gives a correct secondary-dominant example in C with its target (1) · gives a correct borrowed-chord example in C (1) · says one creates motion and the other mood (1)
> A secondary dominant inserts tension aimed at a specific chord: E7 in front of Am pulls into it, because the G♯ is a borrowed leading tone. A borrowed chord recolours a slot without redirecting anything: swapping F for Fm keeps the same subdominant job and the same destination, and only the shade changes. One acts horizontally, on where the progression is going; the other vertically, on how a moment feels.
>
> **Q18.** **Approached by step, left by step, same direction — and they're what lifts a melody past arpeggio**
> A line of chord tones only outlines the harmony; the notes in between create the momentary friction that reads as melodic motion.
>
> **Q19.** **Tonicization points briefly; modulation relocates the tonic. Duration and confirmation decide.**
> How long you stay, and whether you cadence in the new key. It's a spectrum, not a binary.
>
> **Q20.** **Am — vi in C and ii in G — confirmed by D7–G; without a pivot the new key arrives unprepared**
> *Rubric (5):* names Am and gives both functions correctly (2) · gives D7–G or an equivalent confirming cadence (2) · says that without the pivot the change is abrupt or unconvincing (1)
> Am is diatonic to both keys, so it can be approached as vi in C and heard as ii in G without anything sounding wrong at the moment of reinterpretation. Following it with D7–G supplies the cadence that resets the ear's sense of home, which is what makes it a modulation rather than a visit. Without the pivot you'd be dropping into G with no shared ground — either an abrupt lurch, or a passage the ear keeps trying to hear as V of C rather than as a new tonic.
