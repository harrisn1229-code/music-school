# Why does Germany have an H note?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hlafE9fm4_g)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hlafE9fm4_g)

## Synopsis

Traces the German/Central European "H" note name back to 9th-century Gregorian chant notation, and reveals the same history as the direct origin of the ♭, ♮, and ♯ symbols used everywhere today — with a payoff in the famous BACH motif.

## Techniques

- **The historical origin, precisely**: 9th-century chant notation used 7 diatonic notes, but the B was sometimes swapped between two forms to avoid an "undesirable" tritone against F — **B durum** ("hard B," our B natural) written as a squared/Gothic ("black letter") symbol, and **B molle** ("soft B," our B♭) written as a rounded, Latin-style symbol. **Those two written symbols are the direct ancestors of modern accidentals**: the rounded soft-B symbol became the ♭ (flat) symbol; the squared hard-B symbol became the ♮ (natural) symbol; an adapted form of the same squared symbol most likely became ♯ (sharp) too.
- **Why German-system countries call B natural "H"**: the leading theory is that the Gothic/black-letter hard-B symbol visually resembled the letter H (and/or H stood for "hart"/"hard" in German), so hard-B started being written as literal H — leaving the letter "B" free to be reassigned to what English speakers call B♭ instead. English/Dutch kept "B" for B natural and used "B-flat" for the altered note (there was no other altered note yet to name, since chant only ever altered the B) — two independently-workable solutions to the same 9th-century naming problem, which is why they still diverge today. Later, when 12-tone chromatic naming needed a name for every altered note, English generalized "flat"/"sharp" as suffixes to any note; the German-tradition system generalized "-is" (sharp) and "-es" (flat) suffixes instead — except B, which keeps its irregular historical name (B♭ = "B," not "Bes") as a fossil of the older two-symbol system.
- **The BACH motif — only possible because of this naming quirk**: in the German system, B♭-A-C-B natural spells B-A-C-H, letter for letter. J.S. Bach used this exact 4-note motif to musically encode his own name in several works, and later composers reused the same motif as a direct nod/homage to Bach — a device that literally cannot exist in the English naming system, where the same four notes would spell B♭-A-C-B, not a name.
