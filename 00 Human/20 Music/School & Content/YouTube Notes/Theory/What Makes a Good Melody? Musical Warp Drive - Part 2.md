## Synopsis

The free lesson video for week 2 ("Melody") of composer/teacher Max Konyi's paid "Musical Warp Drive" course — a 33-minute, studio-demonstrated deep dive into what separates a strong melody from a weak one. Konyi works entirely inside a single chord progression played on solo piano/clarinet in Studio One, playing four contrasting melodies over the identical chords (a singable one, a deliberately hard-to-sing one, a deliberately bad one, and edited variants) so every concept — singability, steps vs. leaps, contour, range, climax, outlining chord changes — is heard as a direct A/B comparison rather than described abstractly. The back half covers compositional process: whether to write chords or melody first, and how genre constrains melodic vocabulary. ## Techniques

- **Melody is a linear (one-at-a-time) succession of notes; a chord is the same notes played simultaneously** — "line" and "tune" are plain synonyms for melody. Chords are, in a real sense, multiple melodies stacked and played at once — the top note of a chord progression traces its own melody, and so does every inner voice, which becomes explicit once you study voice-leading note-by-note instead of chord-by-chord. → [[Chord Inversions]]
- **Even unpitched drum patterns read as a kind of melody** — kick/snare/hats/toms create a relative high-low contour the ear tracks and latches onto, the same way it tracks pitch contour in a sung line, even though there's no discrete pitch involved.
- **Melody is the single most magnetizing element for a listener** — above chords, basslines, and drum patterns, it's what a non-musician listener is almost exclusively tracking; treat every other part of the arrangement as in support of the melody, not competing with it.
- **A melody carries expression through five simultaneous dimensions: pitch, rhythm, timbre, articulation, dynamics** — directly analogous to speech prosody (pitch inflection, variable rhythm/stress, tone of voice, how a word/note is attacked, loud vs. soft), which is why melody reads as the most "human" and connective element in music.
- **Design for singability as an explicit goal, and test it by asking whether the melody could be hummed from memory** — demonstrated directly: an already-good melody was revised down to just one moderate leap (a 6th, at the very end) with everything else shrunk to small stepwise intervals, made noticeably easier to sing without losing its identity.
- **Steps are easy to sing, leaps are hard — the best melodies blend both rather than committing to one.** A stepwise-only melody has no drama; an all-leaps melody (demonstrated: constant large jumps including a full-octave leap) can still be pretty and instrumentally playable but is genuinely difficult for a human voice, which is a real tradeoff to weigh when writing for a singer vs. an instrument.
- **Contour is the melody's pitch shape traced over time — literally visualize it like a mountain silhouette on the horizon.** A melody with rhythm but no pitch movement (repeating the same note) has zero contour and reads as flat/boring regardless of how interesting its rhythm is; a good contour has real up-and-down shape, not necessarily smooth, but present.
- **Range: an octave to an octave-and-a-half is the practical ceiling for a strong, singable melody** — this is the most common range great melodies actually use; pushing past ~2 octaves starts to break most listeners' ability to track the contour and starts to compromise singability, even though wider-range melodies certainly exist and can work.
- **A strong melody usually has one climax — a single high point it rises to and falls from, occurring only once** — the climax doesn't have to sit in the middle of the phrase; it can land at the very start, the very end, or anywhere between. Look for whether a melody you're analyzing has a note that appears once, at its peak, rather than being revisited repeatedly.
- **Outlining chord changes: build the melody substantially from the underlying chords' own tones (arpeggiate the harmony) so the melody alone implies the chord progression even with zero chords sounding.** Demonstrated as an A/B/C test — both the "singable" melody and the deliberately "hard to sing" (heavily arpeggiated) melody clearly implied the chord changes when played completely alone, while a third, deliberately "bad" melody (constant note repetition, static/repetitive rhythm, notes scattered without purpose across the bar) implied none of the harmony at all in isolation. Outlining is one of the clearest structural differences between a strong melody and a weak one. → [[Non-Harmonic Tones]], [[Chord Progressions]]
- **Bad-melody anti-pattern, named explicitly: constant pitch repetition + static/repetitive rhythm + notes that land in the bar without evident purpose.** Useful as a direct triage checklist against a melody that isn't working — see also [[Melody Writing]]'s existing "fixing a boring melody" checklist, which this reinforces from a different angle (chord-outlining rather than register/rhythm).
- **Order of operations, chords first (the majority approach, especially in modern music): write the full chord progression first, then compose the melody on top of the already-sounding harmony.** The melody that results is naturally well-integrated with the chords because it was written in direct response to them — this was the method used for all three of the direct-comparison melodies above.
- **Order of operations, melody first: write the melody with zero chords sounding, from an internal/felt sense of harmony only, then compose new chords underneath it afterward.** Produces a genuinely different, sometimes more original melodic line, precisely because it isn't pre-shaped by pre-existing harmony. Demonstrated directly: recycling the OLD chords underneath the NEW melody-first line produced an audible clash even though both were in the same key — confirming a melody-first line generally needs freshly-composed harmony written to fit it, not old chords reused just because the key matches.
- **Genre constrains what melody is "good" — there is no universal best melody, only one appropriate to its context.** The demo's melodies were deliberately traditional/classical-triadic and would likely not fit hip-hop, dubstep, house, or metal; the fix isn't a theory rule but active listening — consciously study melodies within whatever genre you're writing for so its vocabulary becomes internalized.
- **Practice method: sing along with melodies you love to physically internalize their qualities**, and separately, **listen to melody actively and consciously (eyes closed, mentally stripping away lyrics/full arrangement) rather than passively in the background** — specifically listening for contour, range, singability, and climax so those qualities surface naturally in your own writing later without having to consciously apply rules while composing.
- **Given exercise: find 3 favorite songs with a clearly discernible melody and analyze them against contour, range, singability, and climax** — noted that some genres (heavy ambient, a lot of metal) often lack a clear melodic line at all, so choose songs where the melody is actually the point.
