## Synopsis

Daniel Seriff introduces **shell voicings** — the stripped-down 3-note version of a 7th chord (root, 3rd, 7th — no 5th) — as the entry point into jazz-guitar comping, explicitly framed as workable "beginner, intermediate, or advanced." This is the first video in a four-part series (the next three cover applying these exact shapes to a jazz blues and other jazz tunes). The whole lesson is two movable hand shapes — one rooted on the low E string, one rooted on the A string — each manipulated through five 7th-chord qualities (major 7, dominant 7, minor 7, minor 7♭5, diminished 7) by flatting the 3rd and/or 7th one half step at a time, rather than learning five unrelated grips. ## What's taught

- **What a shell voicing is and why it works.** A shell voicing = the smallest usable version of a 7th chord: **root + 3rd + 7th only, no 5th**. The 3rd and 7th are the guide tones that actually define a chord's quality (major/minor, dominant/major-7), so dropping everything else still reads unambiguously as the chord — "that's all you really need in this voicing in order to kind of hear the tonality of it."
- **Two movable shapes cover the whole neck** — one with the root on the low E string, one with the root on the A string, a fifth apart (worked here as C-rooted and F-rooted, but both are fully movable to any root). Having both means never having to jump far for the next chord in a progression.
- **Playing technique:** strum with all non-shape strings muted, or pluck with hybrid picking (pick plus fingers) since only 3–4 of the 6 strings are used per shape.
- **E-string-root shape family (root-7th-3rd string order, low E–A–D–G–B–e):**
  - **Cmaj7** — `8 x 9 9 x x` (root C on low E fret 8, D string fret 9 = major 7th B, G string fret 9 = major 3rd E).
  - **C7 (dominant)** — `8 x 8 9 x x` (lower the 7th one half step: D string 9→8 = ♭7 B♭).
  - **Cm7** — `8 x 8 8 x x` (also lower the 3rd one half step: G string 9→8 = ♭3 E♭).
  - **Cm7♭5** — **identical shape to Cm7** (`8 x 8 8 x x`) — the shell voicing has no 5th at all, so there's nothing to flat; minor 7 and minor 7♭5 are indistinguishable in this voicing.
  - **Cdim7** — `8 x 7 8 x x` (flat the 7th again: D string 8→7 = ♭♭7/major 6th A).
- **A-string-root shape family (root-3rd-7th string order, x–A–D–G–B–e):**
  - **Fmaj7** — `x 8 7 9 x x` (root F on A string fret 8, D string fret 7 = major 3rd A, G string fret 9 = major 7th E).
  - **F7 (dominant)** — `x 8 7 8 x x` (lower the 7th: G string 9→8 = ♭7 E♭).
  - **Fm7** — `x 8 6 8 x x` (lower the 3rd: D string 7→6 = ♭3 A♭) — flagged as a bit of a finger stretch, worth building the reach deliberately.
  - **Fm7♭5** — **identical shape to Fm7** (`x 8 6 8 x x`), same no-5th logic as the C shape.
  - **Fdim7** — `x 8 6 7 x x` (flat the 7th again: G string 8→7 = ♭♭7 D).
- **The build order is one-note-at-a-time, not five memorized grips:** start from major 7, flat the 7th → dominant 7, flat the 3rd too → minor 7 (same shape covers minor 7♭5 for free), flat the 7th again → diminished 7. Practicing the move as a single half-step slide between adjacent qualities (rather than five isolated shapes) is the actual skill — explicitly recommends drilling major7↔dominant7 back and forth since chords change quality constantly in real jazz tunes.
- **Series roadmap:** this video teaches the two shapes and five qualities in isolation; the next three videos in the series apply them to a real jazz blues and other jazz standards, and get "a little more challenging" by bouncing between the two shapes mid-progression.
