## Synopsis

Jared's case for treating **singing/vocalizing along with your own improvising** as a dedicated practice method for musical phrasing, not just a party trick some players happen to do. He opens by naming the four things that actually make phrasing sound good (strong/concrete rhythm, repetition, ideas reacting to what came before, and ideas reaching conclusions), argues singing along enforces all four automatically because you physically have to breathe and naturally simplify when singing, and cites Keith Jarrett, Kurt Rosenwinkel, and Glenn Gould as known practitioners of audibly singing while playing. ## What's taught

**Why singing along works — the four target elements of good phrasing:**
1. Strong/concrete rhythm (not ambiguous timing)
2. Repetition (rhythmic and/or pitch/note repetition)
3. Ideas reacting to/building on what was just played
4. Ideas getting concluded, not just trailing off

Singing along is claimed to push all four automatically — breathing forces pauses/space, the voice naturally simplifies and repeats more (catchier singing = more repeated pitches/rhythms, more locked-in timing), and there's a felt desire to resolve a sung phrase. Cited practitioners: Keith Jarrett, Kurt Rosenwinkel, Glenn Gould.

**The 4-step method (~4:40–15:00):**
1. **Constant notes, no breaks, on purpose** — over a Cmaj7–Fmaj7 (I–IV) loop, play continuously with zero rests at any tempo/rhythm, varying note durations (triplets, 8ths, quarters). Deliberately the *opposite* of phrasing — the goal is proving your fretting hand can go anywhere without conscious note-hunting, which is the technical prerequisite for the rest of the method. Requires touch/feel/technique/accenting to actually sound good as a "wall of sound," but the accuracy of pitch isn't the point yet.
2. **Vocalize out of time, no backing track, pitch-matching not required** — very slow, on your own, sing/hum along with your playing. The singing does not need to be technically good or match pitches exactly; stay relaxed — forcing it "too perfect" reads as tense. Let the vocal loosely track the contour; if it runs out of vocal range, let it jump octaves rather than forcing a match.
3. **Do it for real, in time, targeting the four elements** — with a backing track or on your own, consciously aim for non-ambiguous rhythm, repetition, reacting to what you just played, and concluding ideas, all while singing along. Reported effect: playing becomes noticeably simpler and more relaxed/"lyrical"/groovy compared to playing the same material without singing.
4. **Embody the same feel silently, without singing out loud** — the actual end goal: "sing in your mind" while playing, staying as connected to the four elements as in step 3 but without vocalizing. Described as genuinely hard — expect to lose the thread and catch yourself drifting back into step-1-style note-hunting; cycle back to some out-loud singing and retry, repeating over time in the woodshed.

**The three broad ways to work on phrasing (framing, not fully covered here):**
1. Phrasing models — choose and repeat a rhythm, alter it slightly, build compositional structure (A-phrase/B-phrase/A-phrase/C-phrase, where C contrasts or concludes). Referenced as a separate video.
2. Borrow rhythms from spoken sentences/phrases or existing melodies — play the borrowed rhythm over a different scale/tonality so it doesn't resemble the source, purely as phrasing practice. Referenced as a separate video.
3. Singing along with your own playing — this video's full method, above.

**Bonus — melodic patterns as the "ultra combo":** combining phrasing work with melodic patterns/sequences (breaking note selection into a logical, repeating pattern run through a scale/key) is named as the single most game-changing combination for making playing "unique and musical and expressive." Free PDF offered (top 3 pentatonic-scale melodic patterns, linked in the video description) as material to practice singing along with — the patterns themselves aren't detailed in the transcript.
