# The BEST Arpeggio Trick! (ONE easy pattern for ALL shapes)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6KODqYqfXPo)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6KODqYqfXPo)

## Synopsis

BERNTH teaches a single 2-notes-per-string, one-octave 4-note arpeggio shape (root–3rd–5th–7th spread across two adjacent strings), then shows that every common 7th-chord quality — minor 7, major 7, dominant 7, minor(major 7), half-diminished, and an augmented triad — is reachable from that ONE shape by changing just one or two fretted notes. He then applies it chord-by-chord over a real E harmonic minor cadence (Em(maj7)–Am7–Gaug–F♯m7♭5–B7–Em) to prove the system holds up on a genuinely "intimidating"-looking progression, versus the lazy default of just running the E harmonic minor scale over the whole thing. The core reusable content is the one-shape-many-qualities arpeggio system; the worked cadence is a strong concrete example to reuse as a practice etude.

## What's taught

- **The base shape: E minor 7 arpeggio, 2-notes-per-string, one octave.** Root E (A string, 7th fret) – ♭3 G (A string, 10th fret) – 5th B (D string, 9th fret) – ♭7 D (D string, 12th fret). Explicitly framed as a rejection of the usual "Google the chord name + arpeggio, memorize the easiest shape" habit — that approach never engages with the actual notes/interval structure, so nothing sticks and nothing generalizes.
- **Octave-doubling extension.** The same 4-note shape copies directly up an octave (repeat root–♭3–5th–♭7 starting from the next E) to build a bigger, more melodic-sounding arpeggio line across more of the neck — no new shape to learn, same 4 notes repeated.
- **One-note-changes-per-quality — the core trick.** Starting from the E minor 7 base shape: raise the ♭7 to a natural 7 (D→D♯) → **E major 7**. From E minor 7, raise the ♭7 the other way (already ♭7, so this direction gives dominant): keep the ♭3 but raise it, i.e. from the **major 7** shape lower the 7th back to ♭7 → **E dominant 7 (E7)**. From minor 7, raise ONLY the ♭7 to a natural 7 while keeping the ♭3 → **E minor(major 7)** (a minor triad with a major 7th — the exotic-sounding chord used as the tonic of the cadence below). From minor 7, lower the 5th (B→B♭) → **half-diminished (m7♭5)**. **Augmented** is built differently — stack major 3rds continuously from the root (G–B–D♯–G…) rather than modifying the minor-7 shape, since an augmented triad has no natural minor-7-family relative.
- **Applied to a real progression: E harmonic minor cadence Em(maj7) – Am7 – Gaug – F♯m7♭5 – B7 – Em.** Chord-by-chord arpeggio choice, all derived from the same base shape/family: **Em(maj7)** = minor-7 shape with the 7th raised (root-♭3-5-7). **Am7** = the plain minor-7 shape moved to A, played in reverse order (5th-♭7-root-♭3 descending) for phrasing variety rather than always ascending root-first. **Gaug** = the continuous-major-3rds augmented shape. **F♯m7♭5** = minor-7 shape with the 5th lowered. **B7** = major-7 shape with the 7th lowered back to ♭7 (dominant). Framed explicitly as more musically engaged than the "safe/lazy" default of running the parent E harmonic minor scale over the whole cadence without accenting each chord individually — the individual chord colors (the exotic m(maj7), the symmetrical augmented, the half-diminished) get lost under a single blanket scale.
- **Practice framing:** breaking down every chord this explicitly is a learning-phase step — with repetition this chord-by-chord arpeggio selection becomes an automatic improvisational reflex rather than a conscious per-chord lookup.
