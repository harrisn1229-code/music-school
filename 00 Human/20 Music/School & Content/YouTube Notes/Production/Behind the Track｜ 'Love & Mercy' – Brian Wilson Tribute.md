## Techniques

- **Score a film about a musician by requesting the ACTUAL original multitracks and building new material around/out of them, rather than imitating the artist's sound.** Ross's stated reasoning: "to make a score that sounded like the Beach Boys, that would have sucked, because you're not going to be the Beach Boys" — so instead he told the director the only way he'd do it is if Brian Wilson gave him all the original recordings. ~2 days later, a box arrived containing the multitracks to *every album* Wilson had recorded, including **68 different versions of "Good Vibrations"** alone. *(~0:00–2:30 in the interview portion)*
- **Treat hundreds of unglamorous session stereo files — band warm-ups, talkback chatter, players noodling — as usable sound material, not just alternate mixes to sort through.** Alongside the finished multitracks, Wilson sent "hundreds of these stereo files where it would be like the drama from the Wrecking Crew warming up... the various musicians playing little bits and pieces" — described directly as "magic," and folded into "a world of sound that we could reach for or play on top of," not discarded as off-cuts.
- **Any creative processing done to the original artist's music was run past the artist himself before use, framed as essential to the collaboration working ("a journey we took together").** Not a specific technique so much as a workflow/permission discipline worth naming when reworking a living artist's original masters for a new context.
- **Build a scene's "inner voice" from a vocal collage: several differently-processed takes/treatments of the SAME lines, stacked, rather than one clean vocal reading.** The sequence opens on the actor Bill Camp reciting lines Wilson said had "always resonated with him... haunted him through his life," voicing his father — "a number of different vocals obviously being treated in different ways to try and form a collage," stated to read as literally surrounding the listener in a cinema context. → [[Layering and Stacking]]
- **Leave a decades-old archival vocal completely untreated — "straight off the tape... no autotune involved" — as a deliberate authenticity choice, not an oversight.** The "In My Room" flashback runs Wilson's original vocal take exactly as recorded against newly-composed modular-synth/guitar material built around it. → [[Vocal Tuning]]
- **Pair a tempo acceleration with hard editing and a time-signature change to mark the exact moment a scene snaps into a different (psychedelic) headspace**, rather than relying on instrumentation or processing alone to sell the shift. At the moment the film reveals Brian was hit by his father and lost hearing in one ear, "we're using that opportunity to accelerate the BPM... using clever editing and time signature changes to get us into the places that we need to be." *(qualitative only — no BPM numbers given)* → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **Loop a chunk of the ORIGINAL vintage backing track (1970, 8-track "Till I Die") and sidechain a newly-added horn stab against it, so the archival lead vocal — also pulled from the same old tape — still reads clearly on top.** Described only qualitatively on camera ("I think we were sidechaining," flagged as "a bit corny these days"): "we added the horn but it's processing, grabbing a piece of Till I Die of the backing track and then just looping it. But what's cool is the vocal can work over the top of that." → [[Sidechaining]]
- **Brian Wilson's own late-1960s "modular" composition method — write self-contained sections independently, then fit them together afterward — is direct historical precedent for loop-based, section-based modern beatmaking.** Described on camera as working "much the way that we might work on a verse or a chorus or a bridge and be able to loop around it," decades before DAW looping made that workflow the default. Flagged as double-edged: for a period it let Wilson "look into the future" of his own music-making, but taken further "it could get out of hand and it did get out of hand and it did basically take him down." → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **Instrumentation added on top of the original material was deliberately restrained rather than a full new score: mostly guitar and modular synths, chosen "consciously" of the archival material underneath.** No specific settings given — a stated philosophy (serve/extend the original recordings, don't compete with or bury them) more than a worked recipe.
- **Sourcing constraint as a historical lesson, not nostalgia: *Pet Sounds* was recorded entirely on four-track, forcing every decision to be final and deliberate** — offered directly as a contrast to the modern default of unlimited tracks, worth remembering as a discipline exercise (try scoring/writing with an artificially low track count) rather than just trivia.
