# Guitar Inversions - Music Theory Guitar Lesson

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sYdPl43BZ80)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sYdPl43BZ80)

## Synopsis
An earlier (2020-05-05), purely mechanical companion to Jared's later ["Inverted Guitar Chords" video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xIwjyUOpiHE) — where that one is about *hearing* the difference between shape-level and functional inversions in real songs, this one is the how-to-actually-find-them-on-the-fretboard tutorial the sibling video gestures at but doesn't demonstrate. It teaches a single systematic method — take a chord shape, identify the chord-tone role of each string, and on each string move up to the next available chord tone — and works it in full, step by step, on three concrete guitar shapes: an open D chord (two different subset voicings), a C chord on the 5th string, and a Cmaj7 (adding the 7th to see how a seventh chord's third inversion behaves). Closes with a beginner-friendly generalization — that the same one-string-at-a-time method applies to *any* three-note (or four-note) piece pulled out of a bigger chord shape, not just to a small triad grip — and a practice philosophy: work the derivation out slowly and repeatedly on *new* chords rather than memorizing a few shapes to mastery, because the value is in re-deriving quickly, not in the specific shapes landed on.

## What's taught
- **Definition and naming, precisely:** an inversion just reorders which chord tone is lowest. Root on the bottom = root position (not "un-inverted," just its own category). First time you invert = **1st inversion** (3rd in the bass). Second time = **2nd inversion** (5th in the bass). For 4-note (7th) chords only, a third inversion exists with the **7th in the bass**. Naming is **not** "3rd inversion because the 3rd is on the bottom" — it's counted by *how many times you've inverted*, which is a common beginner mix-up Jared calls out directly. [00:01:37]
- **The core derivation method — advance every note to the next chord tone, one string at a time:** for a given voicing, identify each string's current chord-tone role (root/3rd/5th, or root/3rd/5th/7th), then move the note on each string up to the **next closest instance of the next role in the chord's own formula** on that same string (triad cycle: 5→1→3→5…; add the 7 for a seventh chord: 5→7→1→3→5…). Repeating the cycle walks through root position and every inversion in order before landing back on the starting shape an octave up. **This is the same "advance every note one slot" method already documented on [[Chord Inversions]] and [[Chord Voicings]] from Jack Ruch's "Must Know Chords for the Blues" video (worked there on E7)** — this video is a second, independent, guitar-teacher's-eye source for the identical mechanism, worked on different chords (D, C, Cmaj7) and slower, beginner-paced. [00:00:56]–[00:07:00]
- **Why the major scale structure matters here:** finding "the next chord tone up on this string" is fastest when you already know the major scale's whole-step/half-step pattern (1-2-3-4-5-6-7-1, half steps only 3→4 and 7→1) cold — you can count "5, whole step to 6, whole step to 7, half step to 1" along a single string without ever naming actual note letters. Framed as a standing recommendation across "so many videos": know the scale's *structure*, not just its note names. [00:06:49]–[00:07:21]
- **Worked example 1 — open D chord, upper 3-string voicing (5-1-3):** starting shape omits the doubled open-D root, using just the top three strings (5th, root, 3rd). Walked note by note: 5→1 (whole step), 1→3 (two whole steps, skipping the non-chord-tone 2 and 4), 3→5 (half step then whole step, skipping 4) → lands on 1-3-5, the same D chord an octave up. Full three-shape cycle demonstrated and named at each stop. [00:03:53]–[00:09:47]
- **Worked example 2 — same D chord, second voicing (open D root retained + 1-5-3 on strings 4/3/1):** a second, differently-fingered subset of the same chord, walked through the identical method (1→3, 5→1, 3→5) to show the method is shape-independent — any valid chord-tone subset can be inverted the same way, not just one "canonical" grip. [00:11:20]–[00:12:47]
- **Worked example 3 — C chord, 5th-string voicing (1-5-3 on strings 5/4/3, skipping the 2nd string):** same method again (1→3, 5→1, 3→5), explicitly tied back to hearing the inversion "in relation to a root nearby somewhere" even when that root isn't actually being played — i.e., even while working pure shape mechanics, keep a mental map back to the underlying root. [00:13:23]–[00:14:20]
- **Worked example 4 — Cmaj7 (adding the 7th to the C 1-5-3 shape):** middle finger drops onto the fret between existing fingers to add the major 7th, producing a **1-5-3-7 close-interval voicing with a half step between the 7 and the root** ("this kind of creepy gorgeous voicing"). Walking the four-note cycle (adding 7 as a distinct role: 5→7 instead of 5→1, 7→1, 1→3, 3→5) produces all three inversions of the 7th chord. **The 3rd inversion (7 in the bass) is called out as genuinely awkward — both to finger ("not great for the technique and for the wrists") and to hear ("quite unstable, doesn't really sit there that nicely")** — an honest, non-idealized assessment of when a mechanically-valid inversion isn't actually a good voicing to reach for. [00:15:45]–[00:18:23]
- **Generalizing to any chord, however big:** for a large full chord (demonstrated on a big E-shape barre chord), you don't invert the whole six-string chord at once — you find **any 1-3-5 (or larger) chord-tone subset within it** and invert just that piece, using the identical one-string-at-a-time method. Different 3-note subsets pulled from the same big chord (top three strings, a middle cluster, etc.) each generate their own family of inversions, and some of those families turn out to be **physically identical to shapes already found earlier in the video** on D and C — a concrete demonstration that there are only so many usable close-voicing shapes and they recur across different "parent" chords. [00:19:20]–[00:20:59]
- **Applications named (not deeply worked, but flagged as reasons to bother with this):** classical guitarists benefit from recognizing inversions as they appear written in a score; songwriters use inversions to change which note sits on top as the melody, or to write independent-sounding accompaniment parts for multiple instruments/voices; improvisers benefit because scale-form soloing and chord-tone/arpeggio soloing are the same underlying shapes seen from a different angle. **Voice leading (connecting chords via their nearest inversion) is explicitly named and teased as a separate future video's topic**, not covered here. [00:10:00]–[00:11:19]
- **Practice philosophy — process over memorization, explicit and repeated:** (1) take real time to work out where the root/3rd/5th/7th are yourself before checking a diagram — "even if that took you like an hour, that's amazing study time" — because the derivation itself is the skill, not the resulting shape; (2) once mapped, it's fine to also drill bouncing between the inversions as a vocabulary/muscle-memory exercise, **but** the higher-value practice is doing the *derivation* on a brand-new chord repeatedly, not memorizing three shapes to mastery — "teaching yourself to fish instead of just getting a fish." Framed against a specific failure mode: spending a long time deriving something once, then drilling it into muscle memory and losing the ability to re-derive quickly next time. [00:02:59]–[00:03:53], [00:21:07]–[00:22:43]
