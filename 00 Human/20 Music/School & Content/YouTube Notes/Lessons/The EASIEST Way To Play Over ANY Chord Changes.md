# The EASIEST Way To Play Over ANY Chord Changes

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ak_iM6H9rxE)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ak_iM6H9rxE)

## Synopsis

Daniel Seriff (professional guitar coach, 25,000+ one-to-one lessons taught since 2007) teaches the smallest possible move that makes a single pentatonic box sound like it's tracking a moving chord progression: instead of learning a new scale or arpeggio for every chord, stay in one minor pentatonic box and change (or add) exactly **one note** the instant each chord arrives. Worked over a **12-bar minor blues in C minor** — explicitly the same changes as BB King's "The Thrill Is Gone" — the lesson walks all four chords of the form (Cm7, Fm7, Ab7, G7) and shows that C minor pentatonic already matches the tonic chord exactly, then derives a single new target note for each of the other three chords using Seriff's own "diagonal pentatonic" fingering (a movable, string-pair-based system he's built other lessons and a paid course around). The throughline is deliberately anti-complexity: rather than "chasing chords around the fretboard," find the one note that's actually different about the new chord and change only that. ## What's taught

- **The form: 12-bar minor blues in C minor, same changes as BB King's "The Thrill Is Gone."** Four chords: **Cm7 (i), Fm7 (iv), Ab7 (♭VI), G7 (V)**. Bar layout: **4 bars Cm7 → 2 bars Fm7 → 2 bars Cm7 → 1 bar Ab7 → 1 bar G7 → back to Cm7.** Note: BB King and others sometimes drop a second, quicker turnaround into bar 12 — flagged but not covered in this lesson. The chords are kept plain (no extensions) on purpose, to keep the focus on the soloing method rather than harmony complexity.
- **Why C minor pentatonic is already the right scale for the i chord, before any substitution.** Cm7 = **C – E♭ – G – B♭** (1, ♭3, 5, ♭7). C minor pentatonic (**C E♭ F G B♭**) contains all four chord tones plus **F, the 11 (4th)** — called out as "super colorful," safe to hit freely over Cm7 without needing to resolve it.
- **The "diagonal pentatonic" fingering used throughout.** Seriff's own movable, string-pair-based pentatonic system (built and named across multiple of his lessons — see also [[Dorian from the Minor Pentatonic Box]] for a second worked example of the same system, applied to modal color instead of chord-tracking). In C minor, worked on the **D and G strings**: a **two-note set at frets 8, 10 on the D string**, then a **three-note set at frets 8, 11, 12 on the G string**. *(Fret numbers as given in captions — treat as unconfirmed until checked against the video; the relational moves below — "half step above the three-note set," "wedged between the two highest notes" — hold regardless of the exact frets.)* Its selling point over a fixed box: because it's movable, every substitution below "copy-pastes" to any root or neck position without re-deriving the shape.
- **Fm7 (iv chord) — add A♭, one half step above the three-note set.** Fm7 = **F – A♭ – C – E♭** (1, ♭3, 5, ♭7 of F minor). Every note is already in C minor pentatonic except **A♭**, F minor's own ♭3. On the diagonal fingering, A♭ sits exactly **one half step higher than the highest note of the three-note set** — no new scale shape, just one adjacent fret, hit right when Fm7 lands. All the other pentatonic notes still sound fine against Fm7, so nothing else in the line has to change. Demonstrated live: playing C minor pentatonic (diagonal) through 4 bars of Cm7, adding A♭ exactly as Fm7 hits, described as sounding like deliberate, prepared changes-tracking from one added note.
- **Ab7 (♭VI chord) — substitute the blue note (G♭) for the pentatonic's plain G.** Ab7 = **A♭ – C – E♭ – G♭** (1, 3, 5, ♭7 of A♭ dominant). The new note, G♭, is Ab7's own ♭7 — and it also happens to be **the blue note of the home key, C minor**. On the diagonal fingering, the blue note sits **wedged between the two highest notes of the three-note set** — a fixed, findable location in the shape. Sub G♭ for the plain G of C minor pentatonic for the Ab7 bar: G natural "would sound really bad" over Ab7; G♭ "sounds perfect." Every other pentatonic note carries over unchanged. Framed as the biggest distinguishing chord of a *minor* blues vs. a dominant blues — the ♭VI-to-V turnaround is the minor blues's signature move (most dominant blues progressions instead go V-down-to-IV).
- **G7 (V chord) — simplify to three notes and add the leading tone (B natural).** G7 = **G – B – D – F** (1, 3, 5, ♭7). Rather than adding one note on top of the full pentatonic, this move **drops two weak notes and changes one**: drop **B♭ and C** (neither fits G7 well), add **B natural at the 9th fret, D string** — the **major 3rd of G7** and, in the key of C minor, the **leading tone** (major 7th of the key), giving a strong pull back into the following Cm7. Leave the G-string three-note set as-is: root (G), ♭7 (F), and the outlier **E♭/D♯** — technically the chord's ♯5, adds "a little extra tension and pull back to that C minor." B natural is explicitly optional ("you don't have to hit that B every time") — variations offered: bend up into it, or play it an octave higher.
- **The general method, stated directly:** compare each incoming chord's notes against the pentatonic box already under your fingers, find the one note that's actually different, and change only that — "you don't have to arpeggiate some crazy arpeggio or even play a whole new scale shape." Framed as applicable to *any* progression, not just this one minor blues.
- **Practice recommendation: build/use backing tracks broken into individual chord sections**, so each part of a progression can be drilled on its own rather than only ever practiced inside the full form. Seriff's own backing tracks for this exact progression are offered as a free download in the video description (not captured in the transcript).
