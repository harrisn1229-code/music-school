# I Am the Walrus — The Beatles

> Intentionally nonsensical lyrics written to confuse literary analysts. Mike Sammes Singers provide the 'oompah' vocal parts. A BBC radio broadcast of King Lear fades in during the outro. George Martin's orchestration.

## Chords
**Key:** A major, but built on parallel major triads marching up and down the scale

**Verse:** A – C – D – E – A
→ **I – ♭III – IV – V – I** (Lennon climbs through chromatic major triads, not diatonic chords)

**"I am the eggman / walrus":** C – D – E
→ **♭III – IV – V**

**Intro / outro riff:** root-position major triads sliding by step — B–A–G–F–E descending, A–B–C–D–E ascending (**II – I – ♭VII – ♭VI – V / I – II – ♭III – IV – V**).

## From videos

- **The intro's key ambiguity, the chorus's misdirected secondary dominant, and the outro's Shepard-tone illusion.** Intro sits in B (not the verse's A), swaying B major/B minor (modal interchange) — a D major chord (shared by both B minor and A major) pivots smoothly into the verse's A. Chorus opens on B major functioning as a textbook secondary dominant (V/V, "should" resolve down a 5th to E) but instead climbs B→C→D→E as parallel major triads, deliberately subverting the expected resolution — notably the song uses **zero minor chords** despite constant minor-key color from modal interchange. Bridge pivots back to A via an F major chord — a tritone from B, the harmonically furthest possible chord from that key center — which then moves to E to imply A minor/major without ever playing an A minor chord. Outro: a 7-bar loop descending the A minor scale with a **major** triad forced onto every degree; the cello follows the descent down to its lowest note then jumps up an octave while the violins rise in **contrary motion**, producing an effect often compared to a **Shepard tone** (a note that seems to endlessly ascend or descend). ## Production & Arrangement
A chaotic orchestral collage — one of the great "throw everything in" productions. George Martin's dense strings + brass, the **Mike Sammes Singers** chanting nonsense ("oompah, everybody's got one," rising "ho-ho-ho"), and — most famously — a **live BBC radio broadcast of King Lear tuned in on the spot and recorded straight into the fade** (pure chance/aleatoric; it's different every time it was mixed).

- **Lennon's vocal is heavily compressed/distorted** through a cheap mic for the detached, sneering tone.
- The harmony **slides chromatically** (the ascending "sitting in an English garden" line) — restless, unresolved, matching the collage.

**Full from-scratch recreation, instrument by instrument (David Bennett Music Theory)** — a copyright-safe rebuild for use in analysis videos, useful as a virtual-instrument matching reference: **Pianet** (the original's early Hohner electric piano) approximated via a Keyscape pianet patch. **Horns**: BBC Symphony Orchestra doubled with Session Horns Pro for extra bite. **Violin**: no dedicated violin instrument owned, so BBC Symphony Orchestra doubled with Spitfire Solo Cello for texture (a cello isn't a violin, but layering it in thickened the sound usefully). **Cello** (the arrangement's key harmonic-space instrument, playing almost throughout): BBC Symphony Orchestra layered under Spitfire Solo Cello as the main voice — prioritized for realism since it's so exposed. **Strings/horns transcribed by ear into MuseScore, then exported as MIDI** and imported into the DAW rather than programmed directly — useful workflow for matching an orchestral arrangement precisely. **Drums**: Abbey Road 60s Drummer (a period-correct kit/mic emulation). **The King Lear radio broadcast**: recreated literally, by tuning an actual AM radio (bought secondhand) live and capturing whatever happened to be broadcasting, rather than sourcing/simulating old BBC audio — same aleatoric spirit as the original. **Backing vocals**: recreated solo via pitch-shifting a single performance into a mock-choir, standing in for the original's 16-voice Mike Sammes Singers arrangement. → [[Layering and Stacking]] (the doubled-library technique used on horns/violin/cello) — David Bennett Music Theory, [[I recreated "I Am The Walrus" from scratch]]

## Pollack's analysis

*Extracted from [[AWP — I Am The Walrus]] — Alan W. Pollack, "Notes On…" #121.*

**Form.** Underneath the outrage, **a talking blues** — patter declamation, phrases grouped in threes, and an exposed V–IV–I inside a song whose harmony is otherwise nowhere near a key. Two structural moves have Lennon precedents: verses followed by refrains are subtly different from verses followed by other verses, and **the intro comes back mid-song**. Intro **7½ bars**, with a perfectly traditional AABB melodic phrasing hiding inside the strangeness; first verse 6 bars, its second appearance extended by five; second verse melodically the same but harmonically rewritten to travel halfway to V; refrain only **3 bars**, the third taking one extra that establishes the precedent for how the outro grows. The radio interlude is **exactly one measure in tempo**, which is nothing like what it sounds.

**Harmony.** `Verse 1: I (G in bass) – ♭III – IV – I · ♭III – IV – I` · `Verse 2: I (G) – vi – ♭VI – ♭VII – I · ♭VI – V/V (2)` · `Refrain: ♭VI-of-V – ♭VII-of-V – V` · `Outro loop: I – ♭VII – ♭VI – V7 – IV9 – ♭III11 – v – I`. Nominally A major, but **V-of-V is given tonal focus** and **V the sense of arrival**, so the refrain reads less as a half cadence than as an outright modulation to the key of V ([[HAR-C07 — Secondary dominants]], [[HAR-C05 — Half cadence]], [[POP-C09 — Mixolydian ♭VII]]). Two passages honestly refuse Roman numerals: the intro chords exist because bass and top voice walk down together in **parallel tenths**, and the outro is a Möbius strip where **contrary motion generates the chords** rather than the other way round ([[POP-C04 — Four chords with a moving bass]]) — the loop resets on its eighth move against four-bar phrases, so a different chord lands on the downbeat of every third phrase, which is what makes it feel limitless.

**Notable.** The first verse establishes the tonic **without ever using V**. Verse 2's block slide f♯m – F – G – A, then the tritone leap F to B, is the harmonic signature. The tune has two modes only — hammering one pitch, or bluesy licks on ♭3 and ♭7 — and **the radio is withheld until half the song has gone by**, then allowed to dominate the last forty seconds.

**Against this page.** Agrees on A major as the nominal key, and on the parallel-triad reading in the Chords section.
