# How Radiohead use Modes

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CEi9ecBRR_4)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CEi9ecBRR_4)

## Synopsis

Six Radiohead examples of modal color, spanning Dorian, Lydian, Mixolydian, and modal interchange — each demonstrated with an A/B comparison (the actual recording vs. the same passage manually altered back to plain major/natural-minor) to isolate exactly what the modal note is doing perceptually.

## Techniques

- **Dorian, demonstrated via A/B removal, two songs a decade apart.** "Lucky": verse is E Aeolian (natural minor); chorus swaps to E Dorian — the i→IV move (Em→A major, not the Aeolian-diatonic A minor) raises the 6th degree (C→C♯), and the vocal lands directly on that C♯. Flattening it back to C natural (author's A/B demo) makes the chorus sound as depressed as the verse instead of "opening up" — the raised 6th is specifically what reads as lift. "Lotus Flower" (a decade later): same i→IV Dorian move transposed to D minor→G major, vocal marking the B natural this time. Author's stated mechanism for WHY Dorian feels less resolved than Aeolian: in natural minor the ♭6 sits a semitone below ♭5, creating a strong pull to resolve down to 5; raising that note a whole step away from 5 (Dorian's natural 6) removes that pull, so the 6th can be rested on without wanting to fall — the source of the "free-floating" feel.
- **Dorian, demonstrated via ambiguity/deferred confirmation instead of A/B removal.** "15 Step": the vocal-only intro (percussion, no harmony instrument) uses the minor PENTATONIC scale, which omits both the 2nd and 6th degrees — the exact degree (6th) that distinguishes Dorian from Aeolian. Result: the intro is modally ambiguous by construction (no instrument states the deciding note), so the ear defaults to hearing plain natural minor until the chords enter afterward and retroactively reveal Dorian — a case where WITHHOLDING information (not stating a note) is itself the compositional device, deliberately reordered (chords-after-vocal, not chords-first) to create that reveal.
- **Lydian via a chord-only shift under a fixed, diatonic vocal line.** "Airbag" verse: the vocal melody never leaves the major scale, but the chord underneath moves from a diatonic Add9 to a ♯11 chord (the ♯11 = a sharpened 4th = Lydian), recontextualizing the same 3 sung notes as Lydian-flavored purely via the harmony change — melody stays constant, only the chord tells the ear "Lydian." The ♯4 then chromatically descends ♯4→4→3, creating a walking line-cliché through the progression as a side effect (see [[Line Cliché]]).
- **Modal interchange, two songs.** "Everything in Its Right Place": F major → C major (solid major, I-V) → D♭maj13 and Cm/E♭ (both borrowed from parallel Aeolian/minor) — first half of the progression major, second half minor/Aeolian, on the same tonic. Later, dropping the F major chord entirely (leaving only C sustained) pulls the tonal center toward minor even though the remaining chord (C) is itself major, because F major was the thing anchoring the major-key feel. "Burn the Witch": verse vamps F♯6/9 ↔ E major — E is a ♭VII relative to F♯, giving **Mixolydian** (major scale with a flat 7). Chorus drops the 3rds from the chords (power chords, tonally ambiguous major/minor) and introduces a D chord (foreign to both F♯ major and F♯ Mixolydian, native to F♯ **Aeolian**) plus an A natural in the vocal (F♯ Aeolian's minor 3rd) — modal interchange pulling the song from a Mixolydian verse into a minor/Aeolian chorus, explicitly tied to the song's darker chorus theme.
