# Root-to-Root Scale-Form Mapping Method

Part of [[Guitar Positions]]. Jared Borkowski's (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared) standard method for internalizing **any** scale's five positional shapes across the neck — he states he applies this identical method to every scale type in his channel's ongoing position-mapping series (major scale, natural minor, pentatonic, blues scales, harmonic minor, and an announced upcoming melodic minor entry), so this page is the reusable method extracted once rather than re-documented per scale. **Distinct system from this vault's own fret-span [[Guitar Positions|Position 01–12]] pages** — Jared's "five scale forms/positions" are five shapes anchored to a scale's own recurring root pattern (loosely related to, but not identified by him as, the CAGED system — see caveat below), not the vault's 12 fixed fret-span zones; also distinct from [[CAGE System Scale Shapes]]'s five *chord-shape*-anchored positions (C-A-G-E-D grips). Three different position-numbering conventions can all describe the same stretch of fretboard — check which system a given note/video is using before cross-referencing.

## The method
For each of the five scale-form shapes in turn:
1. **Start on the lowest root** in that shape (not the lowest note of the shape — the lowest note that is specifically the scale's root/tonic).
2. **Play up the shape in order.** Every time you land on another root (any octave), **repeat it** — pause on it briefly, staying in time (or play it twice back-to-back without a pause; both are valid, pausing is the more common choice) — before continuing upward.
3. **Never repeat or pause on any non-root note**, including the highest note of the shape if it happens not to be a root — treat the top of the shape as a turnaround point, not a stopping point.
4. **Play the entire scale form** — don't stop early because your ear wants to resolve to a root that's outside the shape; the point of the drill is locking in *this specific shape's* full range, not just the nearest-sounding resolution. (Worked caveat from the harmonic minor video: on the 4th-string-root shape, the ear may want to resolve one note early to a root that's actually outside the shape's range — stick with the exercise's own shape instead.)
5. **At the top edge, bounce back down** through the same rules (repeat every root, skip everything else) until you're back at the bottom root.

**Why it works (stated purpose):** running a scale shape purely low-to-high-to-low is fine finger practice, but it doesn't teach you *where the root sits inside the shape* — you come out of it not knowing where the ♭3, the 5th, or any other scale degree falls relative to the tonic. Root-to-root practice forces that awareness in, because every pass makes you consciously locate and land on the root multiple times per shape, and builds the actual sound of the scale rooted correctly (as opposed to, e.g., accidentally internalizing one of its modes by ear).

## Fingering caution
Not every shape is ergonomically even — flag awkward shapes explicitly rather than assuming uniform fingering. Worked example from harmonic minor's 4th-string-root shape: finger pattern 1-3-4 / 1-3-4, then a repeated 3-4 stretch — called out as ergonomically tricky and worth deliberate slow practice rather than assuming it will fall under the hand the same way the other four shapes do.

## Add-on once the shape is solid: melodic patterns
Once a shape's plain root-to-root pass is comfortable, break up the straight in-order run with a **melodic pattern** — see [[Scale Sequences in Thirds]] for the broken-thirds version of this idea (skip up a scale degree, step back, skip up again) and the accompanying "maze view vs. map view" framing: playing a scale strictly in order only ever shows you the *next* note (maze view — you can see one step ahead and nothing else); deliberately skipping around inside the same shape builds a "map view" — an overhead sense of every note's location so you can jump to any of them on demand, not just step to the adjacent one. Melodic patterns are the bridge from the first to the second. Confirmed on a second scale (the blues scale) as the **"melodic thirds"/"skip up, come down one"** pattern — up a scale step (skipping one), then back down one note, repeated through the shape — plus a second, **"every other note" pattern** — now worked out directly from its source video, along with a third pentatonic-specific pattern: see [[Top 3 Pentatonic Melodic Patterns]] (the every-other-note pattern, a back-neighbor pattern, and a five-note pentatonic descending/ascending lick, all from [[Melodic Patterns for Pentatonic Scale Soloing]]).

## Handling a scale note that falls outside the shape (e.g. a blues scale's blue note)
For scales where one note doesn't fit cleanly inside the five-shape boxes — the blues scale's added ♭5 "blue note" is the worked example — the note sometimes sits **outside** the main box shape to the left or right rather than inside it. Rule: play that outside note **on the same string as the note you're resolving to next**, and **slide/slur into it** rather than picking it separately. Which side depends on direction: **ascending**, play the outside note on the **left** side of the shape; **descending**, play the same pitch on a **different string** on the right side. Both spots are equally "out of the way" from the core shape and both are needed — know the note in both places, not just one, since a player will need it in whichever position the direction of travel puts them in.

## Full practice progression (blues scale video's fuller list)
The harmonic-minor video (above) covers root-to-root plus the melodic-pattern add-on; a companion video on the blues scale spells out the same method's full progression in more detail:
1. **Root-to-root through all five shapes**, rooted on the scale's written root (C in both worked videos).
2. **Every root, every key** — repeat step 1 transposed to every root/key, tracked with a simple checklist; expect it to take multiple practice sessions (loosely, "12+," possibly spanning weeks/months) to cover every shape in every reachable neck location and key.
3. **Melodic patterns** (see above) — deliberately not root-to-root's repeat-the-root rule; that rule is specific to the internalization pass, not carried into melodic-pattern practice.
4. **Root-to-root again, with a metronome, locked to an even pace** — not a speed drill; used as a smoothness/hesitation test (can you play it evenly without stopping to think).
5. **Improvise with the shape while deliberately targeting/returning to the root** as home base — the step that actually makes the tonality audible in real playing, not just in the drill. On the blues scale specifically, this final step is what makes the *identical* physical shape read as **minor blues scale** vs. **major blues scale** — the notes don't change, only which one gets treated, in practice and in the ear, as home base.

**Three general supporting habits** (stated as secondary, not method-specific, but recommended alongside any of the above): alternate picking as much as possible (pick down/up, i-m fingerstyle alternation, or thumb alternation); tone/tension awareness (watch for buzz/rattle from excess pressure, notice warm vs. bright tone); work toward legato/connected phrasing, expecting gaps between notes to shrink with technique over time.

- **The "inchworm technique" — a named left-hand fingering discipline for the scale forms that shift position mid-shape, replacing an awkward reach or jump with a contract-then-expand motion.** Of the five major scale forms, two stay entirely within one position (index-finger fret placement never moves) and three shift position partway through and then return. For the shifting shapes: instead of reaching the 3rd finger across the string change or jumping the whole hand, play the note just before the shift with the **4th finger** instead of the 3rd — the hand briefly "contracts" (fingers crunch together), then "expands" back to normal spacing on the new string in the new position, like an inchworm's contract-then-extend motion. Explicitly framed as a tension/efficiency habit, not a fix for something unplayable — a reach or shift may feel fine slow, but building the contract-expand habit means the efficiency is already automatic when speed later demands it.
