# Chromatic Root-to-Third Connector Lick

Part of [[Guitar Positions]]. The lightest-weight member of the "target one chord tone, then get out" family: instead of a full arpeggio ([[Dominant 7 Arpeggios Over Blues Pentatonics]]) or even a single swapped scale note ([[Single-Note Pentatonic Substitutions Over Changes]], [[Diagonal Pentatonic Blues Soloing]]), this technique targets **one chord tone per chord, connected to the next by a single chromatic half-step**, then drops straight back into ordinary pentatonic-blues phrasing. Worked over the **V→IV→turnaround chunk of a 12-bar blues shuffle in G** (D7→C7→turnaround back to D7/G) — bars 9–11 of the form documented on [[Blues Form and Harmony]] — and then relocated, note-for-note, to **four different fretboard positions**, the same relocate-the-lick instinct as [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]] (same channel/teacher), but anchored on a single note visualized against a chord shape rather than a full 3-note triad grip.

## The core idea: anchor on V's root, chromatic-step to IV's 3rd, then get out
1. **Over the V chord (D7), lean hard on its root (D).** Repeating/circling the root note is what "drives home the sound of that chord" against generic pentatonic noodling — a few variations on the same idea: **D–F–G–A**, or a small **chromatic pull-off between A and G**.
2. **Walk that same note up a half step into the IV chord's 3rd.** As the progression moves to C7, take the D and **walk it up chromatically to E** — the **major 3rd of C7**. One note, one half-step move, and the chord change is "hinted" without needing the full arpeggio: "we don't have to get too deep into outlining the full arpeggio, we're just kind of using these basic chord shapes as a visual guide to see these chord tones."
3. **Return to ordinary G minor pentatonic blues vocabulary immediately after the hit** — the connector is a brief target, not a scale substitution to sustain through the bar.
4. **Turnaround: stay in G blues, but land on D.** The closing turnaround phrase is plain G blues language aimed to resolve onto a **D note**, outlining the V7 (D7) chord one more time right before the 12-bar form repeats.

*(general starting point, portable to any key: over any V7→IV7 move, root of V walked up a half step lands on the major 3rd of IV — this works because a V7 built a 4th above the I and a IV7 built a 4th below the I sit a whole step apart, so V's root sits a half step below IV's 3rd. Confirm against the actual chord qualities before assuming it holds outside a standard dominant blues.)*

## Four-position relocation drill
The identical lick (D-root-of-V → chromatic-step-to-E-3rd-of-IV → turnaround-landing-on-D) is replayed from four different starting points on the neck, each visualized against a chord-shape landmark rather than found purely by ear:

1. **D on the 3rd fret of the B string** — the position the lick is introduced in.
2. **Open D string** — same chromatic pull-off idea from the open string; tonal note: **pick down near the bridge** for this open-string version, for a brighter/different attack than the fretted positions.
3. **D on the 5th fret of the A string** — a different part of the neck that "lends itself to stretching out" differently, with different surrounding notes and directions available.
4. **D on the 7th fret of the G string** — visualized specifically as a **D chord shape adjacent to a C bar-chord shape**, so both chords' tones are seen as two neighboring CAGED-family grips rather than located by ear alone (the same "closest adjacent shape" logic as [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]'s CAGED-adjacency method, but for a single note pair instead of full arpeggios).

**Why relocate at all:** "a big part of improvising is tying together ideas in different spots on the neck — depending on where you're coming from and where you're going to, you want to have access to these different ideas all over the fretboard." Matches [[One-Position Fretboard Overview Drill]]'s and [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]]'s framing directly: position fluency, not the specific lick, is the actual goal.

## Practice philosophy: chunk the form, don't solo the whole 12 bars at once
Stated explicitly at the top of the source lesson: break a long progression into small 2–3-chord chunks (I→IV, V→IV→turnaround, etc.) and drill each chunk in isolation before trying to solo over the whole form — the same chunk-then-connect instinct as [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]] § *Chunk-then-connect* and [[Woodshedding Changes with a Looper]], applied here to a single-note connector instead of full arpeggio vocabulary.
