# What is Syncopation?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_uqu-aD9HpU)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_uqu-aD9HpU)

## Synopsis

A foundational explainer for the whole syncopation topic — not a single-song breakdown but the definitional groundwork this channel's other syncopation-adjacent videos assume. Covers what syncopation actually is (accenting off the main beats), the "needs a straight reference to push against" principle, the taxonomy of syncopation by how far off the beat a note lands (8th/16th/quarter-as-backbeat), anticipation/the push as a related-but-distinct device, why syncopated rhythms should be notated with ties, and a performer's-eye note on the difficulty of singing over a syncopated accompaniment.

## Techniques

- **Core definition**: syncopation is a note landing off the main beats (the quarter-note pulse in 4/4) — on an "and" (8th subdivision) or further (16th subdivision) — rather than on beat.
- **Needs a straight reference to feel**: demonstrated on Gorillaz's "Feel Good Inc." (de-syncopating the bassline by moving every offbeat note onto the beat kills the bounce) and Billie Eilish's "bad guy" (the bassline is syncopated but the kick drum underneath holds a strict four-on-the-floor — stripping the kick and playing the bassline alone sounds noticeably flatter, because the friction between the two parts is what's actually felt as "bounce," not the syncopation alone).
- **Jungle Boogie (Kool & the Gang)** — a clean textbook example: most of the riff's notes fall between the quarter-note pulses rather than on them.
- **Anticipation / "the push"**: chords land early, on the last 8th of the previous bar, instead of squarely on beat 1. Examples: "Ain't No Mountain High Enough" (Diana Ross), "He's the Greatest Dancer" (Sister Sledge), "Let's Get It On" (Marvin Gaye), "Free Fallin'" (Tom Petty — guitar riff), "Satisfaction" (Rolling Stones — melodic anticipation, not just chordal). Common in disco/soul/pop.
- **The Marvin Gaye estate v. Ed Sheeran ("Thinking Out Loud") case, rhythm angle**: the disputed shared element was specifically this pushed-rhythm pattern — the estate lost because a pushed/anticipated rhythm was ruled too generic a device for any one song to own.
- **Displacement taxonomy** (mildest to most extreme): quarter-note syncopation = a backbeat (snare on 2 and 4 — so common some don't even count it as "real" syncopation since it still lands on a main beat); 8th-note syncopation = the default/most common form; 16th-note syncopation = a "second degree" of displacement, twice as far off-grid, reads as more extreme/funky ("Give It Away" by Red Hot Chili Peppers bassline, "Pick Up the Pieces" by Average White Band guitar); 32nd-note syncopation exists in theory but is essentially never used, since at normal tempos it's indistinguishable by ear from 16th-note syncopation and a live performance drifts between the two anyway.
- **Tresillo rhythm** (3 notes, middle one syncopated) demonstrated on "All of Me" (John Legend), and its "clave" cousin (tresillo layered over a straight four-on-the-floor kick) on Radiohead's "Myxomatosis," which also extends the tresillo cell across the bar line into a string of dotted quarters for an especially disorienting feel.
- **Notation convention — always tie a syncopated rhythm across the beat, never write it as one merged note value**, even though the two notate identically in sound. The tied version lets a sight-reading performer still see where the "hidden" beat falls; the untied, simpler-looking version erases the beat grid entirely and forces the reader to count blind. This distinction only matters for a human reader — notation software plays either version back the same, so it's invisible unless the chart is meant to be sight-read.
- **Performance difficulty note**: singing a vocal melody over your own syncopated accompaniment is measurably harder than over a straight strumming pattern. David Bennett cites his own song "Blue" — the piano part he wrote after the fact forced him to learn to interlock a new vocal rhythm against its syncopation, and argues the added difficulty is itself often what produces a melody the writer wouldn't have arrived at over a simpler accompaniment.
