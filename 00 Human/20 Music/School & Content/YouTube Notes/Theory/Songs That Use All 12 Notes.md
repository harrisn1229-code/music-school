# Songs That Use All 12 Notes

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=waTCJ1ZuZjY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=waTCJ1ZuZjY)

## Synopsis

A survey of songs that manage to sound all 12 chromatic pitches at some point — genuinely rare, since most songs stay within the 7 diatonic notes of their key. Draws a key theoretical distinction along the way: physically hitting all 12 notes ACROSS a modulation isn't the same as genuine single-key chromaticism, since what listeners track is a note's scale-degree position, not its raw frequency.

## Techniques

- **The core distinction, proven with Twinkle Twinkle Little Star**: transpose the melody up a semitone partway through and you'll physically sound all 12 pitches across the two keys — but you're still only ever hearing 7 diatonic scale degrees at any given moment, because the ear tracks a note's position in the current key, not its literal frequency. This is why a transposed repeat still sounds like "the same song."
- **Genuine single-key chromaticism** (all 12 notes, no key change required): "Hedwig's Theme" and the "Imperial March" (John Williams — film scoring leans on chromaticism more than pop/rock); "Master of Puppets" (Metallica, within its opening 30 seconds); "In Bloom" (Nirvana, once chords are counted alongside melody).
- **All-12-only-via-modulation** (doesn't meet the strict single-key definition above): "Still Crazy After All These Years" (Paul Simon) and "Something" (The Beatles) — both only complete their 12th note by moving to a new key.
- **Melody + harmony combined**: "Just" (Radiohead) builds a chord on 11 of the 12 notes (only B natural missing) — confirmed intentional; quotes Thom Yorke describing Jonny Greenwood "trying to get as many chords as he could into a song… like writing a medley."
- **The full 12-out-of-12 chord cases**: "All the Things You Are" (jazz standard) builds at least one chord on all 12 notes across the tune (via modulation); "Tracy" (The Cuff Links) goes further, fitting a MAJOR chord specifically on all 12 notes within just 2 minutes (also via key changes).
- **Chromatic runs** (walking every note in sequence): Chopin's Étude No. 2 in A minor ("the chromatic étude"), "Entrance of the Gladiators," "Flight of the Bumblebee" (a common speed-run showpiece), Bruce Springsteen's "Born to Run" as a pop/rock example.
- **Traveling the full circle of fifths as an alternate route**: "Awaken" (Yes) moves anticlockwise around the circle of fifths, landing a chord on all 12 notes without repeating any — cohesive-sounding because adjacent chords are always a 4th or 5th apart.
- **12-tone technique / tone row (Schoenberg)** name-checked as the formal 20th-century method requiring all 12 notes before any repeats, specifically to deny any one note tonal preference.
- **Practical heuristic**: an artist known for heavy chord variety is statistically likely to have a song that hits all 12 notes somewhere, purely because enough chord variety eventually spills outside the diatonic set.
