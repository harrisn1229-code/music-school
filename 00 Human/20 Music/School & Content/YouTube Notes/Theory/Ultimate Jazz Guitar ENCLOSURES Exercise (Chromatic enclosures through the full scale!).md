# Ultimate Jazz Guitar ENCLOSURES Exercise (Chromatic enclosures through the full scale!)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-T-wnZUjP2g)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-T-wnZUjP2g)

## Synopsis

A short, tightly-scoped drill from Jared Borkowski that takes the enclosure device already documented on [[Bebop Vocabulary]] and [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] — surrounding a target note chromatically before landing on it — and applies it systematically to **every note of a scale form**, not just chord tones. This is the key distinction from the earlier videos already in the vault: those worked enclosures onto chord tones within changes; this one strips chord function out entirely and drills the mechanical shape as a pure scale-degree exercise, "without needing to think of anything as chord tones." He states explicitly that any note of a scale can be enclosed this way and it will "sound fantastic and very jazz" regardless of whether the landing note happens to be a strong chord tone — the ear resolves the tension melodically either way. He demonstrates in C major (his default demo key) descending from the top of a scale form all the way to the bottom, applying one fixed rule at every step, and is explicit that the principle transfers to any scale form once you know it well enough to "see it as a map." The video is short and process-focused — no new theory beyond the one rule, repeated and varied across the scale — which makes it a clean, drillable companion piece to the deeper chord-tone-targeted enclosure material already on [[Chord-Tone Soloing]].

## What's taught

- **The core rule, stated in full and repeated at every step of the descending run — this is the whole video:** starting on the highest note of a scale form, descend note-by-note until you land exactly **one half step above** the note you're about to enclose (the target). From there, **skip DOWN past the target to the next note that is actually IN THE SCALE** (never skip to a chromatic note — it has to be a diatonic scale tone), then **walk chromatically UP** from that scale tone through however many half steps sit between it and the target, landing on the target. That target note then becomes the new starting point and the whole process repeats on the next scale tone down. [~1:00–2:30]
- **Explicitly decoupled from chord tones — the video's one real theoretical claim.** "Without needing to think of anything as chord tones, we can still do enclosures on any note of a scale that you're in, and it's going to sound fantastic and very jazz." Landing an enclosure on a scale tone that isn't a strong chord tone or "good note" still works, because the ear is carried melodically away from it right after — the enclosure's *motion* is doing the work, not the landing note's harmonic weight. This is a deliberately simpler entry point than [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]'s per-chord, function-aware enclosure shapes (which do depend on knowing whether you're over a dominant/Dorian/Aeolian chord) — a good on-ramp to drill the shape before adding chord-tone targeting on top. [~5:30–6:30]
- **Always descending — direction is a fixed constraint of this specific drill,** unlike the general enclosure device on [[Bebop Vocabulary]] which is direction-agnostic. Start at the top of whatever scale form/box you're working in and enclose your way down to the bottom, one scale tone at a time, never skipping a scale tone in the descent itself (only the target-note skip described above skips anything). [~1:15]
- **Variable enclosure length falls naturally out of the rule — not a separate case to memorize.** How many chromatic notes land between the skipped-to scale tone and the target depends entirely on how many half steps separate them in that stretch of the scale: where two scale tones are already a half step apart (e.g. the 3rd–4th or 7th–root gaps in a major scale), the "walk up chromatically" collapses to zero filler notes — you land on the target directly off the skip, no extra notes needed, "there's not any chromatic movement other than that's just that." Where a whole step or more separates the scale tones, one or more chromatic passing notes get inserted on the way up. He calls out at least one case in the middle of the C-major run where this collapse happens and treats it as the same rule, not an exception. [~4:00–4:45]
- **Prerequisite before this drill is worth doing: know the scale form as a map, not a memorized sequence.** He's explicit that you need to already "see the scale so well" that at any point mid-descent you instantly know what the next scale tone down is, in position, without hunting for it — "you have to know the scale so well that you just see it as a map." Looking at a scale diagram while first working this out is explicitly sanctioned as a crutch, not cheating. [~2:00–2:15]
- **String-crossing side benefit, named but not developed.** He flags in passing that the descending-enclosure pattern is "so good for string crossing, too" — the skip-down-then-chromatic-walk-up shape naturally forces position and string changes that a straight scalar descent wouldn't, though he doesn't demonstrate this as a separate technical point beyond the one mention. [~2:45]
- **Practice framing: solve it, don't just copy it, and don't force mastery.** He frames the whole video as a puzzle to work out yourself ("I want you to solve the puzzle of this and take your time") rather than a lick to memorize outright, states the principle generalizes to **any scale form**, and explicitly gives permission to only take a single favorite enclosure spot from the drill into a real tune rather than mastering the whole descending run — "don't worry about mastering it... if you're having a positive experience, do what is best for your musical expression." A soft opt-out for anyone finding the full systematic version too mechanical: pull one shape you like out of it and use just that one spot in real playing. [~1:40–2:00, ~6:30–7:30]
- **Applies to any scale, not just the C-major demo key.** He states the rule set once, demonstrates in C major "where I like to mostly demonstrate things," and is explicit the same principles need to be worked out by hand on any other scale form the player wants — this video doesn't hand-hold through other keys. [~1:35]
