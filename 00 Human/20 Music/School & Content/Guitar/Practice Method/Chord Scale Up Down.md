# Chord Scale Up Down

> [!warning] Reconstructed from the standard form — confirm against the video
> The source short (**[Chord scale up down](https://youtube.com/shorts/_GwQ9lEL3CM)** — Chapman Welch, 57s) **could not be retrieved** on 2026-08-14: YouTube refused its captions and its audio stream, so there is no transcript and no Whisper fallback. What follows is the **standard chord-scale up/down exercise** as documented in the sources below, not a transcription of that video. Check it against the short and correct anything that differs — particularly the **direction rule** and whether he runs it **one chord per bar** or two.

One scale per chord, played **up from the root to the 7th, then down from the 7th to the root**. The point is not the scales — you already know them. It is hearing *which* collection each chord is asking for, in time, without stopping to work it out.

A **chord scale** is "the appropriate scale for each chord in a song's progression" — the exercise makes that mapping automatic rather than analytical.

## How to run it

- Take a progression you already know. Start with a diatonic one — **ii–V–I in G: Am7 · D7 · Gmaj7** — before anything that borrows.
- **One chord per bar.** Ascend the chord's scale from its root to its 7th; on the next chord, descend from the 7th back to the root. Alternate direction chord to chord so you are never restarting from the same place.
- **Play it against the harmony.** Record the progression first and loop it. Without chords underneath, a descending A Dorian starting on G just sounds like G major, and the whole exercise collapses into "one scale, seven starting notes" — which is exactly the misunderstanding it exists to break.
- Once diatonic is automatic, use a progression where the answer is *not* the parent key — a **Cmaj7♯11** (Lydian, for the ♯11) or a borrowed **Fm** in C. That is where this stops being finger work and starts being [[SCL-C08 — Matching a scale to a chord|chord-scale matching]].

## The failure mode

Playing the parent major scale from seven different starting notes and calling it seven chord scales. If every chord in the progression gets the same collection, the routine is doing nothing — the test is whether you can say, for each chord, **which note makes that scale the right one** ([[SCL-C05 — Characteristic notes|the characteristic note]]).

## Progress

Track by tempo. Update `tempo_current` when a full pass is clean and in time at that tempo; do not rate it and do not run it through spaced repetition — like every routine here it wants daily reps at a rising tempo, which is the opposite of a widening SR interval.

Tempo is per quarter note with one chord per bar. **50 → 120 bpm**; at 120 with a four-chord progression you are naming and playing a new collection every two seconds, which is the point at which it has become automatic.
