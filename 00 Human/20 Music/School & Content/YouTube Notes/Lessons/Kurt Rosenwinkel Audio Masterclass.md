## What's taught
- **How the "pianistic," soloing-while-playing-chords sound is built — split first, blend second.** Continuously track how active the harmony needs to be in the moment; when a melodic line feels like it needs support, stop and fill in a chord, then return to the line. Starts as two visibly separate actions (line, then chord, then line, then chord) and blends with practice into one texture where the notes around a melody note simultaneously state the chord underneath it — can spontaneously lead into in-the-moment reharmonization. Full extraction, generalized into a standalone technique note (no prior home in the vault for this): [[Melodic-Harmonic Blending (Comping While Soloing)]].
- **The every-beat-chord passing-chord drill.** Devised specifically to surface unfamiliar neck territory the blend above exposes: take a simple tune and force a chord on every single beat, stride-style. A slow harmonic rhythm forces two beats of the same written chord in a row, which is the point — find a second voicing that voice-leads toward the next chord instead of repeating the first, which is where passing chords live. → [[Melodic-Harmonic Blending (Comping While Soloing)]], [[Chord Substitution]]
- **Phrasing/rhythmic freedom — no deliberate practice, but self-recording caught a habit.** States plainly he doesn't consciously work on his open, asymmetrical phrasing; it's just what he hears. The one piece of concrete feedback came from listening back to his own recordings and noticing his phrases tended to start and end in the same rhythmic places — noted, but not developed into a dedicated drill. A general, transferable practice tip (record yourself specifically to catch phrase-boundary habits) that stays a light touch here rather than a routed technique.
- **Career advice: give up "making it" as the goal, optimize the music instead.** Blunt framing — success is defined entirely by how good the music is, not by external outcome; "be prepared to be poor," take opportunities to improve your situation but don't let chasing success replace making the music as good as it can be. General life/career advice, not routed further.
- **Learning a new tune: a five-step, repetition-first process.** Melody + chords off the page, repeated until comfortable; as comfort grows, notice alternate voicings and passing chords; listen to as many recorded versions as possible for passing-chord vocabulary and rhythmic approaches specific to that tune; repeat until it becomes genuinely meditative (an hour or more) — "you have to unlock the secrets of the song"; only once memorized, bring in the lyrics as a separate pass, which can pull toward vibe-driven harmonic choices (e.g. reaching for a more open voicing because the lyric calls for "opening up") rather than pure substitution theory. Full extraction: [[Internalizing a Tune Through Repetition]].
- **Symmetric diminished scale as a source of interconnected triads across the neck.** General scale-work habit: pick a scale (e.g. a chord's own chord-scale, then its altered-dominant counterpart), find the triads sitting inside it, note that a different chord quality over the same root exposes different triads. Applied specifically to the B♭ symmetric (half-whole) diminished scale, which contains four triads/dominant-7-type chords a minor 3rd apart (B♭7, D♭7, E7, G7 family) — the real stated practice goal is mapping how those triads interconnect through their different inversions across the fretboard, not just deriving them. → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Chord Substitution]] §2/§5, [[Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords]]
- **The bebop scale explained as a harmonic-rhythm fix, with a second confirmed chromatic-tone placement.** Harmonic rhythm framed directly as "be strong, be weak" — a plain seven-note scale in steady eighths drifts chord tones onto weak beats every bar because seven doesn't divide evenly against a duple pulse; the bebop scale's added chromatic tone corrects the drift, landing chord tones back on strong beats. Names two valid insertion points for a bebop-major-type scale (matching the vault's existing table): between the 6th and 5th degrees, or between the 5th and 4th — both said to "work, more or less." Generalizes the same strong/weak-beat logic to full chord progressions: passing chords belong on the weak beats between two structural chord tones, the same mechanism as the every-beat-chord drill above, described as what makes a line sound more modern than strict diatonic passing tones. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]], [[Melodic-Harmonic Blending (Comping While Soloing)]]
- **Three-tonic-system (Coltrane-changes-adjacent) exercise, worked from scratch and directly generative of an original composition.** All 12 keys divide evenly into four three-tonic systems, each spanning three key centers a major 3rd apart (worked system: F, C♯, A). Practice method: write the note names of all four systems on paper, drill through them with a metronome playing short chord ideas (roughly two chords per tonal center) moving between tonal centers — training the physical/mental reflex for jumping harmony mid-line rather than memorizing one lick. States this exercise directly generated one of his own compositions, whose A section cycles through all four systems (all 12 keys) as a result. *(Exact chord degrees per tonal center are garbled in the auto-captions — captured qualitatively on the routed note rather than guessed; the tune's own title is also too garbled to confidently identify.)* → [[Chord Substitution]] §5
- **Individuality as an emergent, not deliberately cultivated, property — the "draw a circle" framing.** Quotes Picasso ("don't try to be an individual, just try to draw a circle — nobody draws it the same way twice") as his answer for why he doesn't have a deliberate account of what makes his playing sound like him; distinctive phrasing is framed as inevitable byproduct of consistent practice, not a style choice layered on top. Philosophical, not routed to a technique note.
- **Classical listening as mood-reference for composition, not technique study.** No formal classical study, but names Ravel, Shostakovich, Schoenberg, Ligeti, Messiaen, Mahler and specifically Prokofiev's piano concertos as sources of *aesthetic mood* he returns to when composing — his tune "Dream of the Old" (from his first Verve record) is cited as directly inspired by wanting a longer, Prokofiev-like multi-movement, orchestral-feeling structure. Also mentions playing Bach's lute suites on guitar specifically to study how Bach's left-hand fingering accommodates multi-linear (fugue-style) phrasing — a genuine technical takeaway, but tied to a specific repertoire choice rather than a generalizable drill, so it stays here rather than being routed.
- **Real-time reharmonization "in the holes" of a progression — a chromatic-mediant substitution chain built on the fly.** Rather than playing every diatonic chord of a progression, let chords surface somewhat freely in the mind and play over whichever one is currently present, holding until the next arrives — deliberately skipping some diatonic chords ("playing over the chords in the holes"). Worked over a 1–6–2–5 (`E♭maj7 Cm7 Fm7 B♭7`): substitute the vi with a chord a half step above it (C♯m7 standing in for Cm7), then substitute the ii–V with chords each a half step below their diatonic counterparts (Bm7–E7 standing in for Fm7–B♭7), resolving back to E♭maj7 — the whole progression becomes `E♭maj7 → C♯m7 → Bm7 → E7 → E♭maj7`, described directly as playing "not-vi" rather than the plain vi. Full extraction: [[Chord Substitution]] (new "From videos" entry, alongside the existing entry on reharmonizing purely in the soloist's head).

- **Chord/scale-degree awareness before literal note-naming — a stated developmental sequence for fretboard knowledge.** Awareness of what's being played started purely as physical/visual shapes, then shifted to thinking in scale-degree/chord-degree numbers (1-3-5-7-♭9-♯13 etc.) rather than shape recognition alone, and only later did literal note names become part of real-time awareness — offered as the order competence tends to develop in, not a specific drill to run.
- **A stated long-term goal for fretboard mastery: full-neck access to any chord, freedom to move or stay still by choice.** Describes wanting to instantly "see the whole [fingerboard]" for any given chord rather than being tied to one position, so that staying in one spot through a busy harmonic passage — or moving freely across the neck — becomes a deliberate choice rather than a forced one. Ties this to steady, balanced practice of the seven scale positions plus the neck's diagonal "fault line," and frames whole-neck thoroughness as something that becomes possible only with years of practice, when earlier-life practice necessarily stays more fragmented because too many separate things need simultaneous attention. General practice philosophy — not a specific enough drill to route, but consistent with (and worth reading alongside) [[Continuous Scale Exercise]] and [[Three-Stage Warm-Up (Physical, Mental, Resonance)]].
- **The three-stage physical/mental/resonance warm-up framework, plus a retuning-as-mental-pattern-reset story.** Warm-up isn't a fixed exercise list but three named principles, each mapped to one of rhythm/harmony/melody: physical (right-hand/left-hand coordination — chromatic scale and major scale run the full length of the neck shifting position, major pentatonics through the cycle of fifths), mental (the same drills, but speeding up real-time decisions by changing key mid-pattern instead of always completing a full up-and-down run in one key first), and resonance (singing while playing, described as having "its own intelligence" independent of conscious harmonic knowledge). The practice discipline is to keep cycling among all three throughout playing, not just at the start of a session. A vivid extended story: after years of technical study, seeing a chord symbol meant already knowing exactly how it would sound before playing it — described as "dry" and boring, evidence the mental faculty had become too dominant over the other two — so the fix was retuning the guitar to an unfamiliar tuning, which wiped the accumulated pattern-recognition knowledge (a known shape no longer produced the known sound) while leaving the physical connection intact; strikingly, singing every note remained completely accurate throughout, offered as direct evidence the resonance principle really is independent of the mental faculty rather than downstream of it. Full extraction: [[Three-Stage Warm-Up (Physical, Mental, Resonance)]].
