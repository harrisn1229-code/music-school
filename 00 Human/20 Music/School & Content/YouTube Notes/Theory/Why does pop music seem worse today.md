## Techniques

- **Key ambiguity is a real, definable phenomenon, not just "it's subjective": a piece can genuinely lack enough context (chord/melody notes) to confirm one tonic over another.** Cited examples: "Karma Police" (Radiohead, some listeners hear A minor, others E minor — same underlying ambiguity mechanism documented on [[Is Karma Police in E minor or A minor]]) and "Sweet Home Alabama" (Lynyrd Skynyrd) as a second famous instance of the same effect. → [[Diatonic Modes]]
- **A key signature doesn't uniquely determine the key — it only fixes which notes are altered, and up to 7 different modes can share the identical signature.** The 3-sharp signature (C♯/G♯/F♯) is taught as "A major or F♯ minor," but any of the 7 modes built on that same note collection could use it — a piece genuinely in E Mixolydian or B Dorian would still be notated with the same 3-sharp signature, and confusion arises because there's no strong convention for which key signature "belongs" to a modal piece the way there is for major/minor. → [[Diatonic Modes]]
- **Maj7 chords sit most naturally, without leaving the scale, on scale degrees 1 and 4** (Cmaj7 and Fmaj7 in C major) — venturing further out, a ♭III maj7 (E♭maj7 in C) or ♭II maj7 (D♭maj7, resolving back to the tonic maj7) reads as more "exotic"/chromatic. **Sus2 chords have no 3rd, so they're neither major nor minor — which makes them close to "foolproof": they tend to work stacked or substituted almost anywhere** because they can't clash on the major/minor axis, only functioning as a color/ambiguity device.
- **"What makes a song sound epic" — two separable levers, not one.** Instrumentation/arrangement does most of the work (the example given: "Final Countdown" played on recorders loses essentially all of its epic quality despite identical notes/harmony). Independently, **minor keys read as more serious/dramatic/epic than the parallel major** — demonstrated by imagining "Final Countdown" (originally F♯ minor) in F♯ major instead, described as losing its impact.
- **A bridge's structural job, stated plainly: refresh the listener's ear after roughly two minutes of the same harmonic/melodic material**, functioning as a deliberate break from what's already been established rather than a place for new hooks to compete with the chorus.
