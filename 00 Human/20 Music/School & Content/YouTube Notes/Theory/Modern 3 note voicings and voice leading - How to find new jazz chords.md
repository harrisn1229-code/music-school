# Modern 3 note voicings and voice leading - How to find new jazz chords

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QgK7c7ZGD44)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QgK7c7ZGD44)

## Synopsis

Jens Larsen demonstrates close-interval, second-heavy 3-note voicings drawn from modern jazz guitarists Gilad Hekselman, Lage Lund, and Nelson Veras — the same voicing family already covered generally as "cluster-adjacent/2nd-interval voicings" in [[The Ultimate Jazz Chord Guide - 12 Most Important Voicing Types]]. The real content here is the **exploration method itself**, worked in detail across several ii-V-I cadences: start from one incomplete, dissonant 3-note voicing, and generate a whole family of usable voicings by (a) moving it through the scale, (b) finding a different chord it also works for, or (c) voice-leading it as a single unit — moving every note the same direction by the same interval — into the next chord.

## What's taught

- **A single 3-note voicing moved as a rigid unit through an entire ii-V-I, one whole step at a time.** Starting from a close-interval Dm-family voicing, raise every note a whole step to land on a usable G7♭9 voicing, then raise every note a whole step again to land on a usable Cmaj7(♭13/6, no 3rd) voicing — the same physical shape mechanically transposed twice covers all three chords of the cadence, rather than deriving three unrelated voicings.
- **Incompleteness is fine, and gets MORE fine as tension increases.** In a higher register, or on an altered dominant, a voicing can skip the root (or even the 3rd) and still read clearly — alterations/tensions on a dominant chord already signal "this is altered," so a fully spelled-out chord matters less there than in a lower-register, more foundational voicing.
- **Contrary motion as a deliberate voice-leading choice**, not just a byproduct — resolving a G7 to a Cmaj7 by having the top voice descend while the lower voices ascend (voice splitting) is called out explicitly as a reason a particular cadence "sounds nice."
- **Exploration method, generalized:** (1) take a voicing found in a real context; (2) move it through the chord's scale (major or melodic minor, matched to the chord) on more than one string set to map which shapes are physically playable vs. too much of a stretch; (3) from the resulting set, hunt for a second usable voicing containing the same key note (e.g. any diatonic voicing containing the 7th) to build alternate cadences; (4) separately, ask what OTHER chord (not just the original one) each newly-found voicing could serve — e.g. the original Dm-family voicing also works, unmodified, as part of a G7altered voicing.
- **Second-interval voicings tend to want an outer major-7th or minor-7th interval alongside the inner 2nd** — named as the reason these dissonant, second-heavy shapes still read clearly as tension that wants to resolve, rather than as pure noise.
- **Teased, not covered: pentatonic-scale-derived upper structures.** Some of the Cmaj7 voicings used in this video's examples are described as coming from moving a shape around inside an E minor pentatonic scale — pentatonic scales work well as a generator for upper-structure voicings — flagged as a topic for a future video, not developed here.
