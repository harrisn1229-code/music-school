# 3 easy chromatic approaches to chord tone soloing (to stand out & sound less vanilla)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wnkbyGmPkH8)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wnkbyGmPkH8)

## Synopsis
Jared Borowski (Sound Guitar Lessons) teaches three simple, drillable ways to decorate chord-tone-arpeggio improvisation with chromatic notes, aimed at players who've already got basic chord-tone soloing down and want to move past a "vanilla," straight-arpeggio sound. All three are variations on the same underlying move — a chromatic note a half step below a chord tone — applied three different ways: as a plain approach before the tone, as a turn that leaves and returns to the tone, and as a fuller "up-then-down" enclosure around it. He works them over a 12-bar blues (Sonny Rollins' "Tenor Madness," in B♭) using the B♭7, E♭7, F7 dominant chords and the Cm7 ii-chord, then extends the third technique with a genuinely useful piece of theory: the same minor-7 chord shape gives you a different available upper-neighbor note depending on whether it's functioning as a ii chord (Dorian, whole step above the 5th) or a tonic minor chord (Aeolian/natural minor, half step above the 5th) — worked on the A-minor tonic chord in "Manhã de Carnaval" (Black Orpheus). The teaching method itself is as reusable as the licks: deliberately overuse a new device, mapped onto every chord tone of a progression exclusively, until it's automatic — only then does it start showing up tastefully and expressively in real playing. Video closes with a plug for his free chord-tone arpeggio-shape PDF pack and a teaser for next week's lesson on comping over Miles Davis' "So What."

## What's taught
- **Shape 1 (~0:00–3:58) — approach every chord tone of the arpeggio from a half step below.** Map the chord's 1-3-5 (and optionally the 7) with each tone preceded by the chromatic note a half step below it — over B♭7 that's A→B♭, D♭→D, E→F, and (if including the 7th) G→A♭; same pattern moved to E♭7. He usually applies it to the triad (1-3-5) and skips the 7th, but says it sounds great on the 7th too when included.
- **Shape 1 practice note — play it as a slur.** Pick the approach tone, then hammer on to the chord tone rather than picking both — described as "pretty cool" and the preferred feel for this shape once it's under the fingers.
- **Shape 2 (~3:58–5:19) — same half-step-below relationship, but as a turn: land on the chord tone, dip to the neighbor a half step below, then return to the chord tone before moving to the next one.** Worked over E♭7 and Cm7. Same "doesn't matter what's in the scale" framing as Shape 1 — the neighbor is always chromatic (half step), regardless of the underlying scale/mode.
- **Universal practice method for Shapes 1 & 2 — deliberately overuse a new device first.** When a chromatic-approach shape is new, map it onto *every single* chord tone of the progression, exclusively, even though that's more than you'd ever actually use in a real solo — "just overuse it just to get it down is kind of fun." Only after it's second nature does it start appearing tastefully and expressively in real playing, in the right spots, without having to think about it. *(This is the generalizable teaching principle underneath both shapes — deliberate, temporary over-application as the on-ramp to tasteful, occasional real use.)*
- **Shape 3 (~5:19–8:20) — enclosure: chord tone → scale step above → chord tone → chromatic half step below → chord tone.** The fullest of the three: land on a chord tone, move up to the note directly above it *in the chord's own scale/mode*, back down to the chord tone, then further down to the half step below, and back to the chord tone. Two rules govern which note sits "above":
  - **Over a dominant 7 chord (Mixolydian)** — whole step above every chord tone EXCEPT the 3rd, where the chord's own scale puts the 4th only a half step above the 3rd. Worked over B♭7: root (B♭) → whole step above (C) → B♭ → half step below (A) → B♭; 3rd (D) → half step above (E♭, the mixolydian 4th) → D → half step below (D♭) → D; 5th (F) → whole step above (G) → F → half step below (E) → F; 7th (A♭) → whole step above (B♭) → A♭ → half step below (G) → A♭. Same pattern moved to E♭7 and F7.
  - **Over a ii-chord minor 7 (Dorian) — whole step above every chord tone, including the 5th.** Worked on Cm7 as the ii of B♭: the note above the 5th (G) is A natural, a whole step, because Dorian's natural 6th sits a whole step above the 5th. "You don't need to know all this, you can just map it out and go by ear" — but the theory explains why it's a whole step here specifically.
  - **Over a tonic-minor chord (natural minor/Aeolian), same chord shape — the note above the 5th shrinks to a HALF step,** because Aeolian's ♭6 sits only a half step above the 5th (not a whole step, as in the Dorian/ii case above). Worked on the A-minor tonic chord in "Manhã de Carnaval": five, half step up, five, half step down — described as "very much the sound of a minor sound off the five of that chord." **The takeaway: the chord symbol alone (e.g. Cm7) doesn't tell you whether the note above the 5th is a whole or half step — the chord's function (ii vs. i) does, because that's what determines which mode it's actually drawn from.** → [[Modal Jazz]]
  - Played with a slur (as in Shape 1) for a fluid, connected feel; described as a common lick/turn in jazz vocabulary, used off any of the four chord tones (root, 3rd, 5th, 7th).
