# Bebop Vocabulary Practice (Joe Pass Method)

Part of [[Song Practice]]'s practice-methodology material. Where [[Etude-Based Vocabulary Practice]] documents how to absorb a *composed comping etude* (Galbraith), this page documents the sibling method for **Joe Pass's *Guitar Style*** (Alfred/Pacific Jazz) — a graded sequence of blues, minor blues, modern blues, rhythm-changes, and 3/4-blues solo studies (p.33–54) that culminates in a full, chord-symbol-free transcription of Pass's own solo on "For Django" (p.55–58, Pacific Jazz PJ-85). Full book synopsis on [[Joe Pass Guitar Style - Enrichment]]; the melodic devices this material trains (enclosures, approach notes, guide tones, line closers, CESH) are catalogued on [[Bebop Vocabulary]].

## 1. Take the "For Django" solo apart phrase by phrase (p.55–58)

The solo is printed with **no chord symbols at all** — the book states this outright: "so you must do your own analysis" (p.55). A separate chart on the same page shows "some of the basic chordal thinking used in the solo," but explicitly frames it as a skeleton, not the full picture: "with chord embellishment/substitution, variations are almost limitless" (p.55). Treat that chart as a starting point for your own analysis, not the answer key.

**Work it in the chunks the book itself gives you**, rather than top-to-bottom in one pass:

- The notation is broken into **rehearsal letters (A, B, C, D)** across the four pages — use each lettered section as one practice unit.
- A **`Tacet`** marking partway through (end of the first page) shows where the recorded guitar drops out — worth noting as a natural section break, and a reminder that the transcription follows an actual performance, not a composed study.
- Several passages are marked **`8va`** (with a dashed continuation line back down to **`loco`**) — get the octave placement right before working the phrase into a line at all; an 8va passage played in the wrong register won't line up with the guide tones underneath it.
- A **`Bridge`** label appears partway through the transcription — "For Django" has its own bridge-shaped harmonic motion, so treat that stretch as its own vocabulary chunk rather than folding it into the surrounding blues-adjacent material.

**For each rehearsal-letter chunk:**
1. Play it exactly as written first — no analysis, no chord symbols filled in yet. Same play-before-analyze order as [[Etude-Based Vocabulary Practice]]'s step 1.
2. Go back and write your own chord symbols underneath the notes, phrase by phrase, checking your reading against the book's own partial chordal-thinking chart.
3. Once the harmony is nailed down, name the devices already tracked on [[Bebop Vocabulary]] as they show up: enclosures (§2), approach notes (§3), guide-tone targets (§4), line closers (§7), CESH (§8). File short extracted phrases into those buckets rather than keeping the whole page as one long memorized block — the same "collection-first" filing method documented on [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]].
4. Move to the next lettered section only once the current one is clean at a real tempo.

## 2. What the graded study sequence is teaching, in order (p.33–54)

The sequence isn't just "more reps" — each stage removes a different piece of scaffolding. Read it as training-wheels removal, in this order:

**Blues (p.35–36) — rhythmic density first.** The first chorus is written in **straight 8th notes**, then the identical form is rewritten **in 16th notes**: "This one is in 16th notes. It gives you more to play on each chord" (p.36). The book's own stated reason for starting with straight 8ths: eliminating rhythmic variety forces the ear to build better melodic lines and "avoids the practice of playing memorized licks" (p.35) — chord symbols are given "for your analysis, not necessarily for accompaniment" (p.35), i.e. read them, don't assume they're the comping part.

**Minor Blues (p.37–39) — same drill, new tonic.** Identical progressive-difficulty approach (straight 8ths → denser lines) reapplied over a 12-bar minor blues in C minor. Nothing new mechanically — the point is proving the method transfers to a different tonal center before the harmony itself gets harder.

**Modern Blues (p.39–44) — the harmony gets harder, the line stays put.** A single 12-bar blues form is given as **three parallel reharmonizations**: Basic (plain blues changes), Optional B (adds ii–V motion and secondary dominants over what were static chords), Optional C (a heavily altered/chromatic reharmonization — passing diminished chords, ♭9/♯5 dominants, chromatic ii–V insertions). The solo studies that follow are written to be playable against any of the three — the teaching point is that **one melodic line can sit under multiple harmonizations**, and that a line's "correctness" depends on how aggressively the accompaniment reharmonizes underneath it. This section also introduces **blank measures with only chord symbols given** ("Improvise in the blank measures," p.42) — the first place in the book where notation is deliberately withheld and you supply the line yourself.

**Rhythm Changes (p.43–48) — fast tempo, then withheld notation on the bridge.** AABA "I Got Rhythm" changes, given as **two BASIC rhythm-pattern chord charts** (p.43) that every player varies from. The **bridge (starting bar 17)** is a single two-bar phrase repeated through a descending cycle of dominant 7ths a fourth apart (p.46–49) — and instead of notating it out, the book gives two example two-bar phrases and an instruction: **"Finish the chorus with something of your own"** (p.48). This is the sequence's clearest scaffolding-removal moment: you're handed the harmonic device (a two-bar cell recycled through a dominant cycle) and two worked examples, then told to generate your own.

**3/4 Blues (p.49–54) — the same method in waltz time, run as a continuous performance.** Two different 12-bar blues chord-change sets in 3/4, each preceded by an **INTRO** vamp and followed by six labeled passes — **Improvising, Harmony, 2nd Chorus, 2nd Solo, 3rd Chorus, 3rd Solo** (p.49–51) — written so the last measure of one chorus feeds directly into the pickup of the next. Nothing here is conceptually new; the point is stamina and continuity — playing multiple choruses back-to-back as one performance rather than isolated 12-bar excerpts, in an unfamiliar meter.

**What makes it "graded," stated plainly:** the time signature and form stay fixed within each section while (1) rhythmic density escalates (8th → 16th notes), (2) harmonic density escalates (Basic → Optional B → Optional C), (3) written notation is progressively withheld (full notation → blank "improvise here" measures → "finish this yourself" prompts with only two worked examples), until the sequence culminates in the **fully chord-symbol-free, fully unnotated-analysis "For Django" solo** — the graded studies are rehearsal for exactly the skill the capstone solo demands cold.

## 3. Working it as practice material

1. **Pick one stage** — a single 12-bar blues chorus, one Modern Blues reharm level, one rhythm-changes chorus — and play it exactly as written before analyzing anything.
2. **Fill in what the book withholds.** For the Modern Blues blank measures and the Rhythm Changes bridge, write your own two-bar phrase before checking it against the book's worked examples. For "For Django," write your own chord analysis under each rehearsal-letter chunk (see §1).
3. **Transpose.** The book states its own intent directly: every study is "written to be transposed to all keys and analyzed by ear, not memorized as licks" (synopsis, [[Joe Pass Guitar Style - Enrichment]]). Move a cleaned-up stage to at least two or three other keys before calling it done — a phrase that only survives in its original key hasn't actually been internalized as vocabulary.
4. **Extract and file.** Pull two or three short phrases per stage and file them into [[Bebop Vocabulary]]'s device buckets (enclosure, approach note, guide-tone line, closer, CESH) rather than keeping the whole page as one long memorized unit.
5. **Advance stages only when clean.** Move Blues → Minor Blues → Modern Blues → Rhythm Changes → 3/4 Blues → For Django only once the current stage is comfortable at tempo in the original key and at least one transposed key — the sequence is ordered for a reason; skipping ahead skips the scaffolding.
6. **Work "For Django" last**, one rehearsal-letter section at a time (§1), since it's the sequence's own capstone — everything upstream of it (rhythmic density, reharm density, withheld notation) is rehearsal for reading and playing over changes with no chord symbols at all.
