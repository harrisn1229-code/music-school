# Synthet - The Sus Chord

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xW1LljOK_A0)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xW1LljOK_A0)

## Synopsis

A compact sus-chord primer: what sus2 and sus4 are, why sus4 is the more common of the two, the two standard usage patterns (suspension-then-resolution, and a V chord holding a sus4 under a tonic melody note), and a bonus tangent into quartal chords — stack a second 4th on top of a sus4 and the chord stops reading as major or minor at all, described here as a "seasick," rootless quality that's rare in pop but usable for color.

## What's taught

- **Sus2 vs sus4 construction** — replace the 3rd with the 2nd (sus2) or 4th (sus4) above the root; neither has a 3rd, so the chord is neither major nor minor, feeling open/undefined. Sus4 has the stronger pull back to its "real" chord; sus2 progressions on their own are rare — it's more common as a passing variation on a normal chord. → [[Suspended Chords]]
- **Two usage patterns** — (1) **suspension → resolution**: play the sus4 then resolve to the normal triad a beat later (classic "special effect," e.g. Csus4 → C); (2) **held tonic over the V**: when the melody sits on the tonic note over a V chord, swap the V's 3rd for the tonic note itself (Vsus4) so the melody note isn't a clash — both patterns already documented on [[Suspended Chords]].
- **Quartal chords (new mention)** — a sus4 is already built from a 4th interval instead of a 3rd; stack *another* 4th on top and you get a quartal chord — rare in pop, deliberately ambiguous/rootless, described as "seasick" because it doesn't resolve toward major or minor. Worth a short mention on [[Suspended Chords]] or [[Extended Chords]] as a further-out cousin of sus4.
