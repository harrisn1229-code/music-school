## Techniques

- **Dual-channel "auxiliary input" tracking setup — record/monitor a live-processed (autotune) feed on one channel while the actual take used for editing/comping comes off a separate, effectively clean channel.** Described as putting the mic in and adding autotune "simply to have a kind of monitoring of what will be left once we have the vocals" — the point is explicitly NOT to record the auto-processed version as the keeper; one named channel (heard in captions as "Red") carries the normal take, a second named channel (heard as "Gire," likely a garbled name — treat as unconfirmed) carries the auto-tuned monitor feed, used only to judge in the moment whether more takes are needed. → [[Latency-Free Tracking]], [[1 Main Vocal Track]]
- **Stated reasoning for why the auxiliary/monitor feed doesn't need to be "correct":** the real editing/comping pass afterward can do more than live autotune ever could, so the only thing that actually matters while tracking is whether the performance itself is good and emotional — pitch is fixable later, delivery isn't. "The only important thing is if the singer isn't managing to get emotional. It's very difficult to fake it. You can spend hours so that nobody notices, but it's not the same as when the magic is there." Framed around Luis Fonsi as an example of a singer who needs almost no correction because he's simply an excellent singer. → [[Vocal Tuning]], [[Vocal Comping and Takes]]
- **Never interrupt/correct-talk a singer mid-take over the mic — let the auxiliary-input setup itself keep the control room quiet rather than constantly stopping to comment.** Stated benefit of the same dual-channel setup: "you never let the singer be heard" from the booth side — i.e. the engineers aren't cutting in to react to every take, which keeps the session from becoming "a stressful process" for the singer. → [[Vocal Mic Technique]], [[Vocal Comping and Takes]]
- **Record continuously rather than stopping and restarting per take, and don't actively monitor every pass in real time — let it accumulate into a "playlist" of takes and only check in occasionally.** "They're always on the network, the recording channel records absolutely everything, and it makes a playlist, and we don't have to listen. We only go down occasionally... so she can hear what she did." → [[Vocal Comping and Takes]]
- **Named tradeoff: this workflow takes LONGER overall than recording straight to a single mixed-on-the-fly channel, because of the extra comping/mixing pass it creates afterward — worth it anyway for how it treats the singer.** Explicit acknowledgment that convenience isn't the reason for the setup; singer experience is. *(qualitative — no time figures given)*
- **Avoid stopping a singer to chase a "perfect" take — instead let them sing the same phrase repeatedly (stated as seven times) without commentary, unless there's something specific to say.** "There are singers that you don't want to start correcting and looking for the perfect take because it starts to stress the singer out... it becomes a beautiful process if you make it something mechanical, singing that part seven times without needing to correct it, unless you need to tell them something specific." If the singer "has the magic," the right take arrives on its own rather than through direction — repeated generic notes ("asking them, asking them, asking them, asking them") are framed as worse than just letting more passes happen. → [[Vocal Comping and Takes]]
- **General production philosophy, stated to apply beyond vocals: hearing a polished, processed sound WHILE performing changes and improves the performance itself.** Explicit non-vocal example given directly: "if you're going to play a guitar and you're using compression and equalization, the guitar is sounding spectacular, you play differently, you play better." Applied back to vocals: feed the singer delay, reverb, and stereo widening ("we put stereo on the voices") live in the monitor during the take, specifically so they can perform without thinking about pitch or timing — "he closes his eyes, and he gives the best performance he can... not thinking about anything." → [[Latency-Free Tracking]], [[1 Main Vocal Track]], [[Delay]] (effect), [[Reverb]] (effect), [[Width and Stereo]]
- **A massive (60–90 channel) layered background/harmony vocal stack, built specifically to recreate the feeling of a crowd singing along at a live show — credited to a Dr. Luke studio video as the inspiration.** "We were inspired by a video we saw of Dr. Luke recording... [it] always obsessed us because... at least in our heads, [it] makes people want to sing and be part of it, like it's created in a concert, and shout the song with the artist." On a specific song (context implies a Fonsi track, likely "Despacito" given the surrounding conversation, but not stated outright — flag as unconfirmed) they describe roughly 60, "probably 90" channels of these layered vocal parts. → [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]], [[Layering and Stacking]]
- **Deliberately minimal processing on that massive harmony stack — light EQ and a low cut, nothing else, before it gets combined down.** "A little bit of equalization, we take away the bass, nothing special... a little bit of equalization and it becomes a very cool channel." No frequency/dB given — logged qualitatively as a high-pass/low-mid trim rather than a full chain. *(general — no numbers given)* → [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]], [[EQ]] (effect)
- **A dedicated printed "effects channel," referred to as something like "Via Fex," used for word-specific delay/reverb moments instead of leaving those effects as an automatable recall to revisit later.** Workflow: duplicate the vocal channel, load whatever process is wanted on a specific word or moment (density compared directly to "a kind of Skrillex drop with a ton of channels" collapsed down to one), and PRINT it as its own standalone channel — a mixer can then choose to use that channel's effect on the word it's tied to, or simply leave it muted/unused if it's not wanted, without reopening automation. Stated reason: avoiding a future "email doing a recall about a delay" — i.e. not wanting word-specific effects left as editable automation that someone has to revisit and re-decide later. Said to be used far more now than in earlier years of their work. → [[Committing and Printing]], [[Delay]] (effect), [[Reverb]] (effect)
- **Named artist/context throughout: Luis Fonsi**, discussed repeatedly as an exceptionally strong, low-correction singer ("Despacito" is name-checked directly) — the whole clip's examples are drawn from his sessions specifically, not a generic client.
