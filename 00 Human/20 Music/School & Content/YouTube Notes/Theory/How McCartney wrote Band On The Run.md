# How McCartney wrote Band On The Run

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W7gHlC_muXs)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W7gHlC_muXs)

## Synopsis

An analysis of "Band on the Run" (Wings) as an episodic, multi-movement song in the same lineage as "A Day in the Life" and "Happiness Is a Warm Gun" — three distinct, non-repeating sections tied together by key relationships, tempo change, and a thematic recall of the opening chord progression transposed into the final section.

## Techniques

- **Episodic structure: a song built from several unique, non-repeating sections (distinct mood/key/instrumentation each) rather than the standard verse-chorus-verse repeat model** — cited alongside "A Day in the Life" and "Happiness Is a Warm Gun" as the same device. Three sections here: D major (intro/verse) → A minor (second section, more serious mood) → C major at a jumped tempo (80→125 BPM, "the escape").
- **Modal interchange in the opening progression: Imaj7 → IV → iv, the iv borrowed from the parallel minor.** In D major, G minor isn't diatonic — it's borrowed from D minor (parallel key), giving the "bittersweet twang" that closes the intro progression. → [[Modal Interchange]]
- **A key change used as a literal narrative device, not just a musical color change**: the shift from A minor (planning to escape) to C major at the tempo jump (the actual escape) is timed to the lyric's story beat — the harmonic and tempo shift IS the moment of escape, not just underscoring it after the fact.
- **A closing section can reprise the opening section's harmonic content transposed into a new key, functioning as a "we're back home, but changed" callback**: the final section reuses the SAME Imaj7→IV progression from the D major opening, now transposed into C major — different instrumentation, different tempo, but audibly the same harmonic shape, tying the episodic sections together into one song rather than three unrelated fragments.
