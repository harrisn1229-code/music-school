# Jack Ruch — Dial In Your Amps and Pedals for Great Clean and Overdriven TONES!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=45s77xBLZBU)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=45s77xBLZBU)

## Synopsis

A short, dial-in-focused companion to Jack Ruch's ([[Jack Ruch - My Home Recording Setup for Guitar|My Home Recording Setup for Guitar]]) full studio-chain walkthrough — this one skips signal routing entirely and goes deep on the actual knob-turning: how he sets a clean amp platform, what he wants from a pedal (specifically the low-to-medium-gain overdrive + clean boost combo), and — the most reusable material in the video — the *relative volume* discipline for balancing a clean-boost stage against an overdrive stage against the clean rhythm tone underneath both. Framed around a demo pedal (the "Vertical" from Apollo Approved) but the actual lesson is entirely tool-agnostic: it's about *how* to dial any boost/overdrive pedal, not about that specific pedal. Reinforces (rather than replaces) the clean-platform-first amp philosophy already documented from Kenny Beats and Jack Ruch's other video, and adds genuinely new material: the boost/overdrive volume-balancing method, tone-control-per-pickup guidance, and live-vs-studio headroom/gain-staging notes for real amps and pedals.

## Techniques

- **Amp choice — vintage-style Fender amps, small for the studio, bigger for the stage.** Studio: [[Fender Blues Junior|a Headstrong amp]] or a vintage 60s Fender Princeton Reverb, volume set to about 3–4, for a "nice balanced clean tone." Live, especially with a louder drummer, he wants more power/headroom — a vintage Fender Pro Reverb. General rule stated directly: "all these old Fender amps have a great balanced, clean tone and are a really nice starting place for adding pedals" — the small-clean-blackface-Fender-as-pedal-platform pattern repeated a third time across this vault's Jack Ruch/Kenny Beats sources, same lane as the owned [[Fender Blues Junior]].
- **What he actually wants from a pedal: a low-to-medium-gain overdrive AND a clean boost** — calls out two-in-one pedals as the efficient way to get both (demoed: the "Vertical" from Apollo Approved — clean boost into a smooth, warm overdrive; also namechecks his long-time board pedal, the Analog Man King of Tone, which pairs a low-gain overdrive with a clean boost the same way).
- **Clean boost — live use case: cut through the mix, not add dirt.** A clean boost raises volume for melody/solo parts so they cut through a full band without breaking up (assuming the amp has enough headroom) — his "level one" boost for soloing with a still-clean tone before reaching for overdrive.
- **Overdrive — what it's actually doing besides adding grit.** Beyond distortion/grit, the overdrive's main job (per Jack) is **compression** — it makes the note feel "squishier" under the fingers and helps sustain. Also: most overdrive pedals **cut some high end**, which tames the bright/spiky character of a clean electric guitar — a second, often-overlooked reason overdrive tames a bright guitar/amp combo beyond just "less clean."
- **Boost/overdrive volume-balancing method (the core reusable tip):** set the **boosted volume slightly louder than clean rhythm volume**. Set overdrive gain to taste, but push the pedal's own volume **up more than you'd expect** to compensate for the *perceived* volume loss caused by the pedal's own compression — solo by yourself it can sound too loud, but once a full band (especially drums/cymbals) comes in, that same setting reads as correctly balanced, not too hot. This is a live-band-context correction, not something you can reliably judge alone in a quiet room.
- **Tone controls set per guitar/pickup, not fixed once.** A brighter amp, brighter guitar, or bridge-pickup use calls for different tone-control settings than a darker guitar/neck-pickup use. Jack stays mostly on the neck pickup (demoed on a 335) and sets pedal tone controls so they work reasonably well across both pickups rather than dialing exclusively for one.
- **Amp volume changes the whole downstream calculation — live-vs-studio headroom note.** At a quiet studio volume (Princeton around 3), pedal boost/overdrive settings are more subtle. At a louder live volume (amp cranked to 5–6+), the amp's own tubes/speaker are already being pushed and compressing on their own — headroom shrinks, so pedal volume/boost settings need to come back down from where they'd sit at bedroom volume. **Re-tune pedal settings any time the amp's own volume changes**, don't assume one dial-in transfers across venues.
- **Demo settings on the Princeton Reverb (concrete numbers, general starting point on THIS amp — not a universal preset):** amp volume ≈ 3–3.5, treble ≈ 5, bass ≈ 2.5, a little reverb; guitar volume and tone controls all the way up. Order demoed: clean rhythm (no pedals, neck pickup) → clean boost engaged for a cleaner solo ("level one") → overdrive + boost together for the full boosted-overdriven solo sound, switching to the bridge pickup partway through to show the same settings working across pickups (brighter/more aggressive on bridge, as expected).
- **Stated framing: tone is subjective, genre-dependent — this is "what works for me," not a universal recipe.** Explicitly pitched at blues/jazz/R&B players; the method (relative-volume balancing, clean-platform-first, tone-per-pickup) generalizes even where the specific gear (Fender combo, Analog Man/Apollo Approved-style overdrive+boost pedal) doesn't.
