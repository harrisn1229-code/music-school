# Chromatic Bass Lines (Line Clichés)

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Chord Progressions]]. A **chromatic bass line** is a bass that walks by half-steps while the chord above it stays (mostly) the same or moves slowly. The stepwise motion creates a strong, smooth pull that carries a lot of a song's emotion. The classic version is the **line cliché**.

## The line cliché (descending)
Hold a chord and walk one voice — usually the bass — **down chromatically**: root → maj7 → ♭7 (dom7) → 6.
- In C: **C → Cmaj7 → C7 → C6** = bass **C – B – B♭ – A**.
- Harmonized as chords over a descending bass: **I – Imaj7 – I7 – IV/A** (or vi) — the bass is the melody.

## Why it works
- The ear locks onto the smooth half-step descent; each passing note adds a little tension/color before resolving. It feels inevitable and melancholic.
- Works ascending too (root → ♯root → 2…), and on minor chords (**Im – Im(maj7) – Im7 – Im6**, the "James Bond"/"My Funny Valentine" line).

## In practice
- **Voice it in the bass** and let the top notes stay static — the movement is felt more than seen.
- Great under a held vocal note: the harmony moves while the melody doesn't.
- Pairs naturally with **[[Seventh Chords]]** (the maj7/dom7 are *made of* the walking note) and often sets up a **[[Secondary Dominants|secondary dominant]]** at the bottom of the descent.

- **[[🎵 THE BEATLES - Something]]** — verse bass **C – B – B♭ – A** (the "beating heart" line cliché) under "Something in the way she moves."

- **Chromatic approach walks as a melodic connective device, distinct from this page's harmony-side reharmonization/line-cliché use.** James Jamerson's practice exercise (taught to Ron Brown, documented in *Standing in the Shadows of Motown*): starting **three frets below** a target note (the fifth or the octave of the current chord) and walking up chromatically into it, with the fifth re-inserted partway through the walk-up to the octave rather than a plain chromatic run — the notes outside the scale create tension that resolves the instant the target chord tone lands, which is the whole payoff. Worked note-for-note against real Motown lines: on "Bernadette" (Four Tops), moving E♭→D♭, root-fifth-octave accounts for nearly every note except a lone **D natural**, which only makes sense as a chromatic approach tone connecting the two chord centers; on "For Once in My Life" (Stevie Wonder, key of F), a three-fret chromatic walk up to the fifth (C) and a second three-fret walk straight to the octave (F) reproduce the exercise almost literally. The same device turns up outside Motown too: Paul McCartney's chromatic walk up to the fifth in The Beatles' "Nowhere Man," Robert DeLeo's three-fret walk to the fifth in Stone Temple Pilots' "Plush," and Geddy Lee's chromatic walk into the A in Rush's "The Spirit of Radio." Full formula (plus the syncopation ingredient and gear notes) → [[Jamerson's Root-Fifth-Octave Exercise]]. → [[Non-Harmonic Tones]], [[Syncopation and Metric Displacement]] — BassBuzz, [[Jamerson's Stupidly Simple Exercise (for Killer Bass Lines)]]
- **Voice a composed chromatic descending bass line explicitly, even without a bassist, because it's part of the harmony's actual character.** Under otherwise static or slow-moving chords, a jazz standard's slash chords spell out a deliberate stepwise-descending bass motion (root → down a whole step to the ♭7 in the bass → down another half step) — always played out loud rather than implied, "because I just love that intentionality." A concrete case of this page's "the bass is the melody" idea showing up as a lead-sheet instruction (slash chords) rather than a freely-chosen arranging device. → [[Seventh Chords]] — Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared, [[BODY AND SOUL Guitar Lesson (chords, tab, scales, melody) No. 18 of Top 25 Jazz Standards for Guitar]]

- **The mirror-image principle stated as a general reharmonization habit, not just a substitution trick: whenever the expected move is a descending chromatic bass line, deliberately try running it UP instead, as a surprise device.** Framed directly against tonal music's default: "descending" chromatic bass motion under a static passage is the expected/typical move, so reversing it is a reliable way to make a familiar cadence sound unexpected. Worked over a passage in "Night and Day" starting from a chord read as a **♯4 minor 11** (explicitly *not* the half-diminished reading the same root would usually get): instead of letting the bass fall as the original changes do, the bass climbs step by step through the passage, landing eventually on a **7♯5-type dominant with an added upper-structure tone** before resolving back to the tonic. Soloing options named across the ascending stretch: **Dorian** and a **whole-tone**-flavored choice over different chords in the passage, with **C harmonic minor, C melodic minor, or plain C major** all viable over the plain dominant spot depending on how far out to go. This is the same descending↔ascending mirror mechanic as the entry below (driven there by tritone substitution, driven here by simply choosing the opposite direction outright) — read together they cover both *why* the reversal works harmonically and *that* it's worth trying as a default move. *(Caption-heavy garbling on this video — see full flag on the video page; the specific chord spellings in this passage are the most internally-consistent reading recoverable, not a verified transcript quote.)* → [[Reharmonization]] — Jens Larsen, [[Re-harmonizing Standards - Modern Jazz Progressions and Jazz Chord Sounds]]
- **A general rule for choosing between a descending and an ascending chromatic bassline under the same tonic→dominant target, via §2-style tritone substitution: whenever the descending version works (tonic down to the dominant that resolves to the next chord), the ascending version usually works too, because moving up a major 3rd from the tonic lands on the tritone-sub note of that same dominant.** Worked over "Body and Soul" as D♭maj7 → D7 in place of the plain descending option into the same target — giving an arranger both a chromatically descending and a chromatically ascending bassline choice under one melody. - **A "dominant diminished" passing chord — a dim7 a half step below the target, functioning as its V7 — used specifically to fill a static chord with an ascending chromatic bass line.** Worked example: a chord standing still through the second half of a bar gets a **C♯dim7** inserted a half step under the following **Dm7**, resolving up a half step into it; framed explicitly as sounding like a rootless A7 (Dm7's own V7) rather than a "true" diminished chord. **The payoff, stated directly, is the chromatic bass it produces: C → C♯ → D** — matching this page's line-cliché mechanic exactly, just ascending instead of descending and generated by a diminished chord instead of a maj7/dom7/6 walk-down. Because this type of passing chord changes the actual bass motion of the progression (not just the comping color), it's usually written into the chart rather than left as optional decoration — unlike the diatonic and dominant passing-chord types on [[Passing Chords]].
