# You Suck at Music Theory No. 2: Creating Tension

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9eTRtWcuzWc)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9eTRtWcuzWc)

## What's taught
Underbelly explains WHERE consonance and dissonance actually come from, using a spectrum analyzer on a single sine-wave note: the overtone/harmonic series above a fundamental. Playing the fundamental against each successive overtone shows that intervals close to the fundamental in the series (octave, 5th, major 3rd) sound the most naturally consonant, while intervals further out (the 7th, the 9th) sound progressively more dissonant — because the note pairs' own overtones stop lining up as neatly. That physical basis is then turned into a practical tool: specific dissonant intervals (a 4th, a raw 7th) "want" to resolve to specific consonant ones (down to the 3rd, up to the octave), and suspended chords are built and resolved using exactly that pull.

## Techniques
- **The overtone/harmonic series explains why some intervals sound consonant** — a single note (A1, ~110 Hz) contains not just its fundamental but a stack of overtones above it: octave (A2, 220 Hz), perfect 5th (E3, 330 Hz), octave (A4, 440 Hz), major 3rd (C♯5, ~550 Hz), 5th again (E5, 660 Hz), a 7th (G, ~770 Hz), octave (A5, 880 Hz), 9th (B, ~990 Hz). Playing the fundamental against each of these shows a clear consonance gradient: octave and 5th sound the most naturally resonant (they're the foundation of ancient/Western music), the major 3rd still sounds pleasant, and the further up the series you go (7th, 9th) the more dissonant/tense the interval sounds — because the two notes' own overtone stacks stop aligning as cleanly. → [[Intervals]]
- **Specific dissonant intervals have a "natural" resolution direction** — a perfect 4th above a root wants to resolve DOWN a half step to the major 3rd, or UP a whole step to the 5th; a raw 7th above the root wants to resolve UP to the octave. Demonstrated as literal tension-then-release pairs (play the tense interval, then its resolution). → [[Chord Progressions]]
- **Build a suspended chord from this exact tension** — an "8 chord" (root + 5th + octave) with a 4th added on top is a sus chord; that 4th wants to fall a half step to the major 3rd, giving a clean tension-and-release. → [[Suspended Chords]]
- **Keep the suspension while ALSO adding the resolution, for tension that doesn't fully release** — instead of removing the suspended 4th when it "resolves," keep it sounding at the same time as the newly-added major 3rd; the chord still reads as resolving but the leftover 4th keeps a residue of tension alive. → [[Suspended Chords]]
