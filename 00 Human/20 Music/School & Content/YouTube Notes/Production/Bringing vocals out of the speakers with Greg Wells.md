# Bringing vocals out of the speakers with Greg Wells

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LdZoJttfTss)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LdZoJttfTss)

## Synopsis

A short, talking-head philosophy piece (no on-screen mixing, all spoken) from producer/mixer Greg Wells via Mix with the Masters, built around a single core idea: **the best vocal effects are the ones you never consciously notice while the track is playing.** Wells opens with a formative story — hitting stop mid-mix on sessions with engineers like Chris Lord-Alge, Spike Stent, Michael Brauer, and jbird ("cban" in the auto-captions) and being surprised to hear long reverb/delay tails ringing out that he'd never registered while the track was actually playing — and traces his own vocal-FX approach back to that realization. From there the video moves through his go-to *kind* of delay (tape, deliberately inaccurate/mid-focused, lightly modulated), his reverb taste (dead on top, favoring the physical Capitol Chambers over anything clinically accurate), a long detour into compression as the real "effect" on a vocal — including the WWII-radio origin of leveling technology, Michael Brauer's compressors-as-tone-machines philosophy, and Chris Lord-Alge's habit of compressing a signal AFTER it's already been limited — and closes with his rules for doubling and pitch-tuning: both should be inaudible as *techniques* while being missed the moment you take them away. A second, distinct thread runs alongside the effects discussion: a confession that he under-mixed vocals earlier in his career because he was too focused on the instrumental (the hi-hat, specifically), and a stated mix philosophy — vocal and band on "the same stage," not a Sinatra-style vocal-way-up-front-band-way-back balance. No product names, plugin brands, or numeric settings are given anywhere in the clip — this is a pure feel/philosophy piece, and every technique below is logged qualitatively as a result.

## Techniques

- **The core test for ANY vocal effect: if you'd notice it by hitting stop mid-track, it's too much.** Wells describes repeatedly hearing reverb and delay tails ring out when engineers like Chris Lord-Alge, Spike Stent, and Michael Brauer hit stop mid-mix — tails he'd never noticed while the track was actually playing — "I wasn't hearing it in the track at all... and I kept noticing that again and again." That gap (audible in isolation, inaudible in context) became his working definition of a correctly-set vocal effect: something that "hugs and holds and supports" the lead vocal rather than being heard as its own event. → [[Depth and Space]], [[Reverb]] (effect), [[Delay]] (effect)
- **Tape delay as the default vocal delay type, specifically BECAUSE it's inaccurate — mid-focused, rolled off top and bottom, "dreamy" reflections.** "I'm a big fan of kind of tape delay on a vocal because it's not very accurate on top or bottom — it's all sort of mid-range, just like weird kind of dreamy Echo Reflections." A little modulation on top, deliberately slow rather than fast, "so it swims a little bit" rather than warbling. *(qualitative — no time/feedback/mod-rate values given)* → [[Delay]] (effect), [[EchoBoy]]
- **Reverb taste: dead on top, and a strong preference for the real Capitol Chambers over anything clinically accurate.** "I like reverbs to be sort of dead on top" — paired with a named favorite, the live echo chambers 30 feet under the floor in Capitol Studios' machine room ("sounds really old-fashioned but it's just musical and incredible"). Directly opposed to "really clean and accurate reverbs," which he says "sound goofy" to him. Capitol Chambers is a physical room, not a purchasable unit — logged here as a reference point, not routed to [[Vintage Gear Worklist]] (see Routed to). → [[Reverb]] (effect), [[ValhallaVintageVerb]]
- **Compression, not reverb, is "the best effect on a vocal" — and its power comes from how compressors interact with each other, not from any single unit's gain reduction.** "The right kind of and the right combination of compressors and how they react to each other" is named directly as more important than reverb. Frames compression's whole existence as inherited WWII radio technology — the reason "stay level" is called that — built to keep one soldier's voice from blowing out another's ear on a battlefield radio link; "trickle-down military technology." *(historical framing, not a settings tip — logged for the "why compression does more than volume" context it gives the two technique bullets below)* → [[Compression]] (effect)
- **Michael Brauer's "compressors are big tone machines" philosophy — routed through multiple compressors specifically for their COLOR, sometimes without meaningfully engaging gain reduction at all.** "No one's going to give you a better answer than Michael Brauer about how compressors really are big tone machines, and I love how he uses them to create different colors and different vibes — even if he's not even really engaging the gain reduction in the compressor, he'll just route things through different compressors." A compressor chosen and used as a coloration stage first, a leveling tool second. → [[Compression Staging]], [[UAD Teletronix LA-2A]], [[UAD UA 1176LN]]
- **Chris Lord-Alge's aggressive gain-reduction habit, as described by Wells: limit a vocal, then compress the ALREADY-limited signal — stacking a compressor after a limiter to hear how it reacts, for extra energy a single stage can't produce.** "Chris is quite a big fan of using gain reduction pretty aggressively... he will limit something and then compress the limiter after — you know, like, limiting it's like there's a ceiling, you wouldn't think it could be compressed more, but he wants to hear how that reacts to a compressor, because it sort of adds this other crazy energy to it." Compression here is framed as adding energy to a vocal, "far more important than reverb or whatever." *(secondhand — Wells describing Lord-Alge's approach, not Lord-Alge's own demo; no ratio/threshold/GR values given)* → [[Compression Staging]], [[Compression]] (effect)
- **Doubler discipline: subtle enough to never sound like chorus/phasing, but load-bearing enough that pulling it out is audibly worse.** "A little bit of kind of a doubler effect is nice if it doesn't sound phasey, if it doesn't sound like a chorus thing." On a raging rock track specifically, a little doubling avoids having to turn the vocal fader up — the target is that you never consciously hear the doubler, but "if you take the doubler out, it's like — what happened, why doesn't it sound as good." Same "invisible until removed" test Wells applies to reverb/delay, applied here to doubling. → [[Artificial Double Tracking (ADT)]], [[Doubler2]]
- **Confession/warning: he under-mixed vocals for a long time earlier in his career, specifically because he was too focused on the instrumental — the hi-hat is the named example.** "I think I was guilty of under-mixing vocals for a long time when I was younger, because I was so obsessed with what the hi-hat was doing." Framed as a byproduct of being an instrumentalist himself and genuinely loving the sound of the band, not a technical mistake — a bias worth watching for in anyone who mixes from the instrumental side first. → [[Depth and Space]]
- **Mix-balance philosophy: vocal and band on "the same stage," explicitly against a Sinatra-style vocal-forward/band-way-back balance.** "I want the vocal to not be lost, but I want the vocal to not get in the way of the accompaniment either... I want everyone to kind of be on the same stage" — contrasted directly with "a Frank Sinatra record where it's all vocal and Nelson Riddle or Count Basie are way in the background conducting the band." Subtle vocal effects (not fader level) are named as the actual mechanism for achieving vocal presence within that balance — see the doubling/delay/reverb bullets above. → [[Depth and Space]]
- **A ping-pong, rolled-off delay, doubled up with a second independent mono delay running behind it, purely for a "weird depth perception" effect the listener isn't meant to consciously register.** "The right sort of spatial stuff that you might not even notice in the mix — like a ping-pong kind of rolled-off delay, or sometimes I'll do that with another mono delay behind it, just to add some sort of weird depth perception thing." Framed as compensating for the fundamentally unnatural listening situation of two speakers with no visual/performance cues — "the weird stuff you got to do to make the music just sound natural." *(qualitative — no time/feedback values given)* → [[Delay]] (effect), [[Depth and Space]], [[EchoBoy]]
- **Tuning philosophy: he tunes vocals "all the time" because it sounds better, but the artifact/transparency bar is total — "I never want to hear tuning on a vocal," same standard he applies to doubling.** "Same with tuning on a vocal — I never want to hear tuning on a vocal. I tune vocals all the time because it sounds better, but I don't want to hear any artifacts or any unnatural thing of tuning — there's a way to use the tuning plugins [transcript cuts off here]." The clip ends mid-sentence before naming a specific technique for achieving that transparency. → [[Vocal Tuning]], [[Pitch and Formant]] (effect), [[Auto-Tune Pro]]
