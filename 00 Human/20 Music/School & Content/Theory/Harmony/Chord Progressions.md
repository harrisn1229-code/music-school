# Chord Progressions

Part of [[Music Theory]]. How the diatonic chords from [[Chords by Key]] actually move — Roman numeral analysis, harmonic function, and the stock progressions.

## Roman numeral analysis
- Replace each chord's root letter with its scale degree in the song's [[Keys|key]]: in C, `C Dm F G` → `I ii IV V`
- Same numerals describe the progression in **every key** — instant transposition ([[Circle of Fifths]])
- Only label chords that ARE diatonic; a D major chord in the key of C isn't "II", it's non-diatonic (borrowed/secondary)
- Two systems: classical (lowercase = minor: ii, vi) vs Berklee/pop (always uppercase + suffix: IIm, VIm; minor keys numbered from the major scale: ♭III not III). See [[Chords by Key]]

## Harmonic function (T / SD / D)
Every diatonic chord has a job:

| Function | Chords (major key) | Feel | Tension |
|---|---|---|---|
| **T** Tonic | I | home, at rest | lowest |
| **(T)** Substitute tonic | vi, iii | semi at-rest | low |
| **SD** Subdominant | IV, ii | moved away from home | medium |
| **D** Dominant | V (vii°) | wants to resolve home | highest |

- vi & iii substitute for I because they share 2 of its 3 notes; vi is stronger (contains the tonic note, and = the relative minor's home chord — [[Keys]])
- ii ≈ IV (share degrees 4 & 6): jazz prefers ii (as ii7), rock/folk/country prefer IV, blues IV7
- Minor keys: i = T, ♭III = (T), iv & ♭VI = SD, V/v = D, ♭VII = (D)
- The archetypal cycle: **T → SD → D → T** ("advancing"); reversing it feels like retreating
- Analyze songs by graphing the tension story: start at rest → climb → peak on D → resolve. (Dylan's *Like a Rolling Stone* chorus = I IV V = T SD D, 2 bars, repeated 6×. Gnarls Barkley's *Crazy* = Im ♭III ♭VI Vsus4 V = T (T) SD D, with a [[Suspended Chords|sus4 resolution]])

## Stock progressions
**Major keys**
- I–IV–V — the backbone (blues, rock, folk)
- I–vi–IV–V — '50s doo-wop
- I–V–vi–IV — modern pop 4-chord loop
- ii–V–I — jazz cadence (as ii7–V7–Imaj7 with [[Seventh Chords]])
- I–IV–I–V or 12-bar blues (all dom 7ths)

**Minor keys**
- i–♭VI–♭VII · i–iv–♭VII · i–iv–v (or V) · i–♭VI–♭III–♭VII · iiø7–V7–i (jazz minor)

## Reverse-lookup: finding the key from a bare progression
Everything above assumes the key is already known. Run it backwards instead: given only a handful of chords and no other context, rotate the [[Circle of Fifths]]'s diatonic window around until you find every key whose 7-chord set contains all of them. A three- or four-chord fragment is rarely locked to just one key — seeing every candidate window at once beats guessing off the first or loudest chord.

**Worked example** — a riff built from **Am, C, G** and nothing else. Scanning the windows around the circle, all three chords fit (either diatonically or as a secondary-dominant borrow — see the II/III note above) inside three different keys:

| Key | Am reads as | C reads as | G reads as | Rest of that key's chords |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| F | iii | V | II *(borrowed — acts as V of V)* | I=F, ii=Gm, IV=B♭, vi=Dm, vii°=E° |
| C | vi | I | V | ii=Dm, iii=Em, IV=F, vii°=B°, plus II=D, III=E *(borrowed)* |
| G | ii | IV | I | iii=Bm, V=D, vi=Em, vii°=F♯°, plus II=A, III=B *(borrowed)* |

**Disambiguating between the candidates**: add a 7th to the chord you suspect is the tonic and see which color fits the riff better. If **Cmaj7** sounds right, the progression is centered on C or G (C is a diatonic I or IV in both). If **C7** (dominant 7th) sounds closer, it's centered on F instead — C is F's V chord, and a dominant-seventh color is exactly what a V chord wants to sound like on the way to resolving.

The move generalizes past this one example: treat an unfamiliar chord set as a *shape* to rotate through the circle looking for windows it fits inside, not a formula to solve from the first chord. — Jim Fleser, [[The Chord Wheel - Enrichment|The Chord Wheel]], p.6

## Songwriting: hook chord phrases
From drawmusic's method — a chord-based hook is a short progression + melody phrase, repeated a lot:
1. Pick a 2–8 bar progression (3–5 chords), loop it
2. Write melody/lyrics over it
3. Steal structure, not chords: analyze hits for chord count, length, T→SD→D shape, twists (minor key, substitute chords, sus effects)

Pairs with /song-seed captures (`20 Music/Song Seeds/`) and the Chords sections on Library song pages.

## Chord function names (classical)
Each scale-degree triad has an official name, not just a Roman numeral: **I** tonic · **ii** supertonic · **iii** mediant · **IV** subdominant · **V** dominant · **vi** submediant · **vii°** leading-tone. The subdominant triad is always major and the leading-tone triad is always diminished, in every major key. Every progression that resolves ends in a [[Cadences|cadence]] — see that page for the 4 named cadence types (authentic/plagal/half/deceptive) and their strict "perfect" criteria.

## From videos

- **I-IV-V as music's "primary colors," and "three chords and the truth" as the genre-linked reason to use only them.** The Roman-numeral framing IS the point: 1-4-5 sounds the same relationship in any key (C-F-G, E-A-B, F-B♭-C), which is why the numbers name function, not pitch. Phrase credited to country songwriter Harlan Howard: country, folk, and punk lean on I-IV-V specifically because simple, functionally-complete harmony doesn't compete with lyrics/storytelling for attention (Motown's "My Girl," Pulp's "Common People"). **Disguised simplicity via mid-song key change**: Paul Simon's "Diamonds on the Soles of Her Shoes" plays I-IV-V in E, then shifts the SAME pattern up a semitone to F — looks harmonically rich on paper, is actually one simple progression transposed. **Minor-key version, with a harmonic-minor variant on V**: natural minor i-iv-v (Gorillaz's "Feel Good Inc."); raising just the 7th degree turns v major for a "classical" sound while i/iv stay minor (Muse's "Uno"). → David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs That Only Use 3 Chords]]
- **A graded tension tool for a song's OPENING chord, distinct from never resolving to the tonic at all — the song still resolves eventually, the delay is what creates momentum.** Opening on **IV**: mild tension, not urgent — "Time After Time" (Cyndi Lauper), "Bad Romance" (Lady Gaga), "Walking in Memphis" (Marc Cohn), "Viva la Vida" (Coldplay), "Anti-Hero" (Taylor Swift). Opening on **ii**: similar mild-tension effect — "All My Loving" (Beatles), "Love Song" (Sara Bareilles). Opening on **V — strongest tension**: the dominant wants to resolve back to I more than any other chord, so opening there creates maximum "we can't stay here" urgency — "This Love" (Maroon 5, C minor) opens on a first-inversion G7, the added 7th and inversion intensifying the instability further. → [[Cadences]] for the companion "never resolves to tonic at all" and "avoids the tonic chord entirely" devices — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that don't start on the tonic chord]]
- **A recurring personal songwriting fingerprint: climbing 5→6→7 over otherwise plain I-IV-V.** John Lennon used this melodic/harmonic climb (5th degree up through the 6th to the 7th) across multiple piano songs — "Mother," "I Am the Walrus," and later "Imagine" — a reusable, distinctive way to add motion over simple primary-chord harmony rather than changing the chords themselves. → [[Modulation (Key Change)]] (this same video's chained-modulation example) — David Bennett Music Theory, [[4 underappreciated John Lennon songs]]

- **A reusable classical-to-pop borrowing toolkit, extracted across a large catalog of songs directly built on public-domain classical pieces.** Change tempo (Billy Joel's "This Night" speeds/swings Beethoven's Pathétique 2nd movement); change rhythm while keeping pitch content (same example); alter a figure's note count (Lukas Graham's "Better Than You" turns Beethoven's 3-note Moonlight Sonata arpeggio into 4 notes); slow a source down enough to obscure it (Phil Collins' "A Groovy Kind of Love," Clementi's Sonatina No. 5 — speeding the pop version back up is what reveals the likeness); borrow texture/style rather than melody (Zeppelin's "Stairway to Heaven" intro imitates Bach's Bourrée's two-voice melody-over-steady-bass shape without quoting its actual tune, vs. Jethro Tull's "Bourée," a fairly direct adaptation); transpose one chord by changing only its bass note (John Williams' Star Wars score reuses a Holst "Mars" chord, C bass → G bass); **reverse the usual direction — borrow a classical piece's MELODY and set it against a new, changing chord progression, rather than borrowing its chord progression under a new melody** (K-pop's "Last Goodbye" reharmonizes Pachelbel's Canon melody differently each time it recurs — the inverse of the far more common pattern of new melodies over a borrowed Canon progression) → [[10 K-pop songs based on Classical music]]. **Legal framing**: this borrowing is uncomplicated specifically because the source material is old enough to be public domain — contrasted against Sia's "Breathe Me" vs. Philip Glass's then-recent, still-copyrighted "Metamorphosis One," which would carry real legal exposure under the same borrowing pattern. → David Bennett Music Theory, [[28 Songs That Are Based On Classical Music]]
- **A functional-harmony breakdown using the minimal two-chord case, where every bit of a song's tension/release has to come from just two chords — making each function unusually easy to isolate.** I-V (strongest pairing: V's leading tone "demands" resolution, especially as a dominant 7th) vs. I-IV (subdominant "recommends" rather than demands, more relaxed) — and I-ii is functionally EQUIVALENT to I-IV, not a third distinct option (ii and IV share subdominant function, explained by sharing 2 of 3 notes). A minor v (rather than major V) weakens dominant function by flattening the leading tone — "Falling" (Alicia Keys) uses I-v for a gentler pull than a "real" I-V song. Philosophical aside worth keeping: chord extensions (C→C6→C7) are argued to be flavor variations on ONE chord (same function), not separate chords — "You Never Can Tell" (Chuck Berry) counts as two-chord (C, G7) even though the piano alternates C/C6. Two reputed "two-chord songs" that don't actually qualify: "Dreams" (Fleetwood Mac, resolves to vi later) and "Tomorrow Never Knows" (Beatles, a C drone plus a superimposed B♭/C11 — arguably one chord with an added color, not two). → David Bennett Music Theory, [[26 Songs That Only Use Two Chords]]
- **The "blues rock" progression — I–♭III–IV–I** (A-C-D-A), the workhorse of blues-rock/hard rock. ♭III is borrowed from the parallel minor (modal mixture) against an otherwise-major I/IV — that major/minor mix is the mechanism behind the "bluesy" sound. Straight uses: "Are You Gonna Be My Girl" (Jet), "Free Bird" (Lynyrd Skynyrd), and three Paul McCartney Beatles songs ("Back in the U.S.S.R.," "Magical Mystery Tour," "Helter Skelter" — the latter possibly influenced by "Purple Haze"'s use of the same shape plus a "Hendrix chord" ♯9). **Halfway-start variant** (♭III-IV-I): "Get It On" (T. Rex), "Born to Be Wild" (Steppenwolf), "Seven Nation Army" (The White Stripes). **All-minor variant** (i-♭III-IV, darker/Dorian): "School's Out" (Alice Cooper). **Pedal-point variant**: "Let Me Entertain You" (Robbie Williams) holds the tonic in the bass under all three chords in the verse, releasing in the chorus. **Climb-to-♭VI variant, a musical sequence** (same minor-3rd motif repeated a 4th higher): "Gimme Some Lovin'" (Spencer Davis Group), "Welcome to Paradise" (Green Day), "Love Shack" (The B-52s). **Why it's "guitar-native"**: no single scale contains I, ♭III, and IV together on paper, but the five CAGED open-chord shapes map directly onto the notes of the pentatonic scale — playing major chords on all five pentatonic degrees hands you this exact progression with zero modal-mixture theory required, which is why it "shouldn't work" yet feels obvious on guitar. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use the Blues Rock Chord Progression]]
- **The "4-3-2-1" progression — IV–iii–ii–I, the descending mirror of the 1-2-3-4 climb documented below.** Almost always dressed in 7ths for a jazzy color: "Loving You" (Minnie Riperton) and "If I Ain't Got You" (Alicia Keys) share the identical maj7/min7 voicing (Bennett suspects direct inspiration). **Reusable voicing trick**: a IVmaj7 chord voices cleanly as a minor triad (built on the 3rd) over the IV root in the bass — e.g. E minor over C reads as Cmaj7. **Dominant pedal variant**: "Blinded by the Light" (Manfred Mann, not the Springsteen original, which re-harmonized entirely) holds the 5th degree constant in the bass under the whole progression in the verse, then drops the pedal in the chorus — same chords, different tension via the bass alone. **Turnaround variant**: "Zelda's Lullaby" (Koji Kondo) detours to land on V as a turnaround on the repeat instead of resolving to I. **Secondary dominants added on a repeat** (a reusable "vary the repeat" device): "Oh My Love" (John Lennon) and "Long, Long, Long" (George Harrison) both insert a secondary dominant just before the final I chord the second time through. → [[Secondary Dominants]]. **iii-for-I-first-inversion swap**: F-C/E-Dm-C (swapping the iii chord for I in first inversion) — "Life's Been Good" (Joe Walsh) and "Let It Be" (The Beatles) share this exact shape; McCartney later transposes it down a 5th for an elongated variant. **Songs using BOTH the climb and the descent**: "Lean on Me" (Bill Withers) and "Like a Rolling Stone" (Bob Dylan) each climb up the scale then climb back down within the same phrase. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use the 4-3-2-1 Chord Progression]]
- **The "4-5-6-1" progression — IV–V–vi–I, Bennett's "climb back home."** Starts away from the tonic and climbs up to land on it, giving a clear departure/arrival shape. "Heat Waves" (Glass Animals, bridge) doubles the harmonic rhythm partway through for a rise in energy. "When You Were Young" (The Killers, key of B) rushes V and vi to half a bar each, buying time to return to IV earlier. Straight uses: "Who Knew" (Pink), "Beauty and the Beat" (Justin Bieber), "Apt." (Bruno Mars & Rosé). **Same chords, different label by context**: reads as vi–VII–i–III in the relative minor instead when the song has already established minor tonality — "Time Is Running Out" (Muse) is F-G-Am-C heard as 6-7-1-3 in A minor, not 4-5-6-1 in C, because the verse fixes A minor as home first. Elliott Smith's "Say Yes" bridge climbs PAST the I arrival point to a G7 (the major-II "Elliott Smith chord") before resolving. **Why it isn't heard as Lydian I-II-iii-V**: Lydian is too unstable a mode to read as home without a pedal tone reinforcing the tonic underneath each chord — "Sarah" (Fleetwood Mac) is the closest real example to that pedal-tone Lydian treatment. → [[Diatonic Modes]] (Lydian instability) — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use the 4-5-6-1 chord progression]]
- **The minor-key "climbing" progression — ♭VI–♭VII–i**, a two-step climb (neither ♭VI nor ♭VII is a point of rest) to a satisfying tonic arrival. Song: "Running Up That Hill" (Kate Bush). **Naming-convention side note, useful generally**: this same three-chord shape can equally be described as IV–V–vi in the relative major key (identical chords, different label) — Bennett's rule of thumb is to name whichever key the song is *actually* tonally centered on (per melody, resolution point, overall gravity), not whichever relative key happens to produce cleaner-looking Roman numerals. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use the Running Up That Hill chord progression]]
- **This chord progression is Madness! — a doubled-minor variant of the "Believe" progression.** The vanilla shape (documented above, [[The Axis Progression]] family survey) is I–V–ii–IV, all diatonic. Madness's "Our House" instead makes BOTH the V and the IV chords minor — **I–v–ii–iv** — rare enough for such an otherwise-familiar pop shape that it reads as unique to this song. Modal mixture (see [[Modal Interchange]]): the melody stays fully diatonic to the major scale throughout, never landing on a note that would clash with the borrowed minor chords, which is exactly what lets the harmony mix flavors without sounding dissonant. **Arrangement device layered on top**: the identical chord shape repeats verse (key of C, one chord/bar) → chorus 1 (key of D, TWO chords/bar — the transposition and the doubled harmonic rhythm compound for an energy jump) → chorus 2 (key of B, same doubled rhythm) → back to C for the next verse — a reusable technique: repeating an identical chord *shape* in a new key (often at a new harmonic rhythm) reliably signals "new section" without needing genuinely different chords. → [[Modal Interchange]], [[Musical Form]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[This chord progression is Madness!]]
- **Three progressions that are each (as far as documented) used in only one song apiece.** (1) **"Black Hole Sun" (Soundgarden) verse** — blends parallel G major and G minor (see [[Modal Interchange]]): tonic chord is G major, but the next chord (B♭6) is borrowed from G minor, followed by another minor-key borrow (F); a consistent tonic-note common tone (G) glues the blend together across the borrowed chords; ends on a Dsus4 (avoiding the F♯/G-minor clash) and a **Phrygian dominant** turnaround. (2) **"You and Whose Army?" (Radiohead, *Amnesiac*)** — resists analysis as one static key; is actually a chain of **ii–V–I's marching through different keys roughly every two bars** (the same device as "Giant Steps"), each landing chord serving as the "ii" of the next ii-V-I, eventually looping back to the start. → [[The ii-V-I]]. (3) **"All the Young Dudes" (David Bowie/Mott the Hoople)** — starts as a plain descending-stepwise walk down the major scale (see the entry above), then detours at the halfway point into a G-minor-family borrow (Gm–E♭–B♭, same [[Modal Interchange]] device as Black Hole Sun) rather than a genuine key change — the vocal melody staying entirely within the original major scale through the "borrowed" half is the tell that it's modal mixture, not modulation. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[3 Songs with unique chord progressions]]
- **"La Folia" — classical music's own answer to the 12-bar blues.** A minor-key **ground bass** (a repeating bass MOTIF that gets harmonized, rather than a "chord progression" conceived chord-first — compare Pachelbel's Canon, the major-key equivalent of a famous ground-bass work, documented above): i–V–i–♭VII–i–V, looping, traditionally in 3/4 (tied to the sarabande dance, though not a hard requirement of the harmony itself). Originated as a Portuguese Renaissance folk dance, spread across Europe to become one of Baroque music's most reused compositional templates — many pieces are literally titled "La Folia"/"Folia" because the form was explicitly used as a theme to base new works on. Also found in Beethoven and Liszt (his "Rhapsodie espagnole" is named "Spanish" specifically for La Folia's Iberian association). Modern film/TV usage as a period/genre signifier: Vangelis (*1492: Conquest of Paradise*), Nicholas Britell (*Succession*), Adam Crystal (*Bonding*), James Newton Howard (*Restoration*, explicitly set in the 17th century, La Folia's peak-popularity era). **Only one confirmed pop crossover found**: "Oops!... I Did It Again" (Britney Spears). — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Classical music's favourite chord progression]]
- **Blur's "Coffee & TV" — a one-off progression (written by guitarist Graham Coxon, not Damon Albarn).** In E major: V (B) → a deliberately dissonant "Aadd♯9" chord (A major's major 3rd AND minor 3rd stacked simultaneously — Coxon's own account, quoted: *"this chord is totally wrong... but no one said 'geez that's awful'... it creates a little bit of weird tension, a note that does fit but doesn't quite fit"*) → I (E) → ♭III (G, borrowed from E minor — [[Modal Interchange]]) → an Fmaj7add♯11 that Coxon arrived at essentially by guitar-fingering accident (a partially-unbarred F chord leaving the open B and E strings ringing, both of which happen to belong to the home key of E, letting the otherwise-foreign F chord blend in) → B♭ (the ♭5 chord, reached from F via a fifth-step per [[Circle of Fifths]] — locally consonant even though B♭ is foreign to the wider key) → D♭ (a **chromatic mediant** relative to B♭, sharing the common tone F — see [[Chromatic Mediants and Pop Modulation]]) → back to B. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Blur's unique chord progression]]
- **Two songs sharing an unusual chord progression, confirmed by a RipX vocal/instrumental mashup: "Karma Police" (Radiohead) / "Sexy Sadie" (The Beatles)** — both use C–D–G–F♯, notable for the F♯ major chord (built on the raised 7th degree, an unusual choice); the shared progression, including that specific chord, has long been cited as evidence Radiohead drew direct inspiration from "Sexy Sadie" when writing "Karma Police." — David Bennett Music Theory, [[These songs have the same chords... let's swap them! (Mr Blue Sky)]]
- **Four more RipX-mashup-confirmed shared progressions.** (1) **"My Heart Will Go On" (Celine Dion) / "In the Air Tonight" (Phil Collins)** — both the **Aeolian vamp** (vi–V–IV–V in the major key, documented above). (2) **"Black or White" (Michael Jackson) / "I Still Haven't Found What I'm Looking For" (U2)** — both plain 12-bar blues (documented above), evidence the form shows up well outside its rock/blues home genre. (3) **"Watermelon Sugar" (Harry Styles) / "Blinding Lights" (The Weeknd)** — dm–Am–C–G, a genuinely uncommon shared coincidence, both usually performed in different keys from each other; this is the pair that produces **modal ambiguity** — see [[Diatonic Modes]] for the full mechanism (both songs withhold the one note, B vs. B♭, that would disambiguate A natural minor from D Dorian, until the 4th chord). (4) **"Mr. Brightside" (The Killers) / "Shut Up and Dance" (Walk the Moon)** — both in D♭ major using IV–vi–V, both popular wedding-DJ staples; tempos had to be met in the middle for the mashup since Mr. Brightside runs faster live. **Bonus — same-artist self-reuse: "Can't Stop" / "Zephyr Song" (both Red Hot Chili Peppers)** — same progression (vi–V–III–IV / i–VII–V–VI read from the relative minor), but in very distant keys (E minor vs. A minor), which is why this mashup is judged the weakest-sounding of the batch — extreme digital pitch-shift on either the vocal or the instrumental introduces audible artifacts, a useful cautionary data point about how much key distance a mashup/reharm can tolerate before quality degrades. → [[Diatonic Modes]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[These songs have the same chord progression... let's swap them! (My Heart Will Go On)]]
- **Three more RipX-mashup-confirmed shared/reused progressions.** (1) **"Otherside" (Red Hot Chili Peppers) / "Numb" (Linkin Park)** — both the **minor Axis progression**, the minor-key-rooted relative of I–V–vi–IV — see [[The Axis Progression]]. (2) **"Havana" (Camila Cabello) / "Smooth" (Rob Thomas & Santana)** — both a Latin-styled i–VI–V-family move (minor tonic descending to the major V via the vi chord), a common Latin-harmony shape. (3) **"I Will Always Love You" (Dolly Parton/Whitney Houston) / "Earth Angel" (The Penguins)** — both the classic doo-wop I–vi–IV–V (documented above); the mashup is used as a direct style-independence demonstration — Whitney's vocal over Earth Angel's 1950s instrumental "sounds like it could have genuinely come out in the 1950s," while Earth Angel's vocal over a modern instrumental reads as an '80s/'90s ballad instead: chords "only contribute so much" to perceived era/style, with rhythm, instrumentation, arrangement, and timbre carrying most of that signal. **Same-artist self-reuse**: Coldplay's "Speed of Sound" reuses the same unusual progression as "Clocks" (documented in full under the dedicated Coldplay entry below) — similar piano-riff arrangement too, read as a deliberate "Clocks 2." → [[The Axis Progression]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[These songs have the same chords... let's swap them! (Numb)]]
- **The Andalusian Cadence, in full (expanding the brief mention above).** i–♭VII–♭VI–V, a strong descending-scale-degree "countdown" to a major-V resolution. **Harmonic rhythm changes its character**: one chord/bar (weighty — "Look What You Made Me Do," Taylor Swift) vs. two chords/bar (driving — "Hit the Road Jack," Ray Charles) vs. deliberately uneven, rushing the first three chords then resting on V ("Sultans of Swing," Dire Straits, for anticipation before resolution). **Major vs. minor V — the "is it really Andalusian" question**: the textbook form specifies a MAJOR V (via harmonic minor's raised 7th); two songs use both within the same track — "A Hazy Shade of Winter" (Simon & Garfunkel, minor v in the verse, major V later) and "Genie in a Bottle" (Christina Aguilera, chorus repeats the progression twice, minor v then major V, the raised 7th also audible in the vocal melody the second time). **Power-chord ambiguity**: "Citizen Erased" (Muse) — the guitar riff alone (power chords, no 3rds) tracks the progression's root contour without being definitively major or minor until Matt Bellamy's vocal adds the thirds; "The Globalist" (Muse) uses the same progression in the same key, explicitly called a sequel to "Citizen Erased" by the band, read as a deliberate callback. Muse's cover of "The Man with a Harmonica" (Ennio Morricone) also uses it, live-linked to introduce "Knights of Cydonia." **Flamenco origin and dual conceptualization**: named for its strong association with flamenco (Andalusia, Spain); flamenco musicians sometimes reframe the SAME four chords by treating the final (major V) chord as the true tonic instead — reinterpreting the whole passage into **Phrygian mode** rather than minor. "Innuendo" (Queen) descends through the cadence while Steve Howe (Yes) plays a flamenco-styled solo over it, explicitly evoking both traditions at once. → [[Diatonic Modes]] (Phrygian dual-conceptualization) — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use the Andalusian Cadence]]
- **The "Just the Two of Us" progression — vi–V–i (minor key) with a jazzy ii-V-of-vi "enclave" detour.** In F minor: the base loop is Db–C–Fm (vi–V–i); right when Fm sounds fully "arrived," the song instead treats the NEXT chord (D♭) as a temporary tonic and approaches it with its own ii–V (Ebm7–Ab7 → Db) — two chords foreign to F minor — before returning to the original loop. Bennett's shorthand: "2-5-1 of 6." Songs using the identical enclave-detour version: "Nonsense" (Sabrina Carpenter); "Stronger Than You" (Estelle, *Steven Universe*). Close variants: "Emotions" (Mariah Carey, V sometimes minor for a mellower color); "Teddy's Jam" (Guy) and "Finesse" (Bruno Mars) — the latter explicitly a stylistic homage to New Jack Swing, the R&B subgenre (blending hip-hop drum-machine/sample elements with conventional R&B) whose use of this shape earned it the nickname "New Jack changes" from a viewer comment. New Jack Swing's influence carried into '90s boy-band pop — "Everybody" (Backstreet Boys) uses the same vi-V-i chords reoriented to start on i instead. → [[The ii-V-I]] (secondary ii-V tonicization) — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use the Just The Two Of Us chord progression]]
- **The "Where Is My Mind?" loop (Pixies) — I–vi–III(major)–IV, a genuinely rare shape.** The non-diatonic III (a MAJOR chord on the 3rd degree) functions as a secondary dominant of vi ("5 of 6"); instead of resolving there (the expected perfect cadence), it goes to IV instead — a **deceptive/interrupted cadence** that's specifically what keeps the loop perpetually unresolved rather than sounding "done." "Actually Romantic" (Taylor Swift, released the same year as the source video) — same loop, same key (E major), same guitar-rhythm feel and tempo, close enough that direct inspiration is plausible (though, as always, not provable or legally relevant on chords alone). - **Five progressions that are each unusual enough to be essentially one-offs — different devices per song.** (1) "Subterranean Homesick Alien" (Radiohead) — Dm → A♭ (second inversion, E♭ in the bass) → Dm, a tritone-related pair with no shared diatonic key; already logged in full on [[Circle of Fifths]]. (2) **"Michelle" (The Beatles)** — F → B♭7♯9 (the "Hendrix chord," combining the major 3rd D and the ♯9/D♭ simultaneously for deliberate dissonance — frequently MISlabeled as the smoother, more obvious B♭m7 in chord charts, but the Beatles chose the harsher voicing on purpose) → E♭6 → Ddim7 → Bdim7 → C → Bdim7 → C. **Diminished-7th inversions are enharmonically other diminished-7 chords** — Ddim7 and Bdim7 share the exact same four notes; only the bass note (which one is voiced lowest) differs. (3) **"Mrs. Robinson" intro (Simon & Garfunkel)** — F♯7 (5 bars) → B7 → E → A → D (1st inversion) → Bm → F♯7 → E7, a near-total **circle-of-fifths chain** (F♯→B→E→A→D, each a descending 5th) that also pivots the key from F♯ (intro) to A (verse) via the closing E7. (4) **"Sir Duke" chorus (Stevie Wonder)** — B → Fm7 → Emaj7: B→Fm7 is a tritone leap (same "maximally unrelated" relationship as the Radiohead example), made to work by a shared common tone (D♯/E♭ present in both the B triad and Fm7) gluing the two chords together across the harsh root movement. (5) **"Light My Fire" verse (The Doors)** — Am7–F♯m7, a **chromatic mediant** (roots a major 3rd apart, no shared diatonic key) that still works because the two chords share 50% of their notes (A and E — the root and 5th, arguably the two most structurally important tones); alternatively readable as modal interchange, since F♯m7 is the diatonic vi of A MAJOR even though the verse is in A minor. - **The "Royal Road" progression — IV–V–iii–vi** — Japan's own equivalent of the Axis Progression: extremely common in anime, J-pop, and video-game soundtracks, and largely uncommon in Western pop by comparison (the reverse asymmetry from [[The Axis Progression]]'s I–V–vi–IV, which dominates the West but barely appears in this form in Japan). Name translates as "the easy way" — the effortless, go-to route to a melodramatic-but-not-serious mood. **Why it feels the way it does**: starts on IV (subdominant, mild tension) → V (dominant, maximum tension) → iii (weak tonic-function, "at rest but not satisfied") → vi (tonic-function, "wistful" rest) — and **crucially never resolves to I at all**, so the loop just keeps circling without ever fully landing, which is exactly what gives it that ongoing, momentum-driven melodrama. **Near-miss variant**: "Never Gonna Give You Up" (Rick Astley) is often cited as an example but actually swaps the IV chord for a ii7 built a third below it — the same notes minus the root, just a different bass note (one note different: a G♭ bass would make it IV, an E♭ bass makes it ii7 instead). The genuine Royal Road progression does appear in Astley's other Billboard #1, "Together Forever." → [[The Axis Progression]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Japan's favourite chord progression and why it works]]
- **The "Creep" progression — I–III–IV–iv** (G–B–C–Cm, in G) — a distinctive four-chord loop with surprisingly few real-world users (Bennett notes he had to "scrape the barrel" for examples beyond the famous plagiarism-lawsuit cluster). **The I→III move** (G to B major, chromatic — B minor would be diatonic) reads two ways: as a **chromatic mediant** (two same-quality chords a 3rd apart), or — Bennett's preferred read — as a **secondary dominant with a deceptive cadence**: B major is the V of vi (E minor), but instead of resolving to the expected vi it resolves to IV (C) instead, "faking out" the expected destination while still functioning as a dominant pull away from I. **The IV→iv→I close** is a **minor plagal cadence** — turning the subdominant minor for a bittersweet, nostalgic color — glued together by tight voice-leading (C-E-G → C-E♭-G → B-D-G, only one note moving at each step). **Songs**: "That's When Your Heartaches Begin" (Ink Spots/Elvis, 1937 — the earliest known use); - **The "Magnolia" progression — I–I7–IV–iv** (C–C7–F–Fm) — named (per jazz-circle usage, not Bennett's own coinage) after the 1928 tune "Magnolia's Wedding Day" (Jimmy McHugh/Dorothy Fields), and common in older/jazz-standard repertoire. **I7** is a secondary dominant (V of IV) pulling into F; **iv** (minor four) then guides back home via a minor plagal cadence, same device as the Creep progression above. **A genuine top-voice line runs through it**: C→B♭→A→A♭(→G), independent of which register it's voiced in. **Reharmonized variant**: swap the plain iv for a ♭VI augmented triad built on the SAME notes minus the root (used in "Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds," Beatles) — same harmonic content, different bass note. **Pedal-point variant**: hold a single bass note underneath all four chords (all four share that scale degree as a chord tone) for grounded tension — heard in "That's the Way of the World" (Earth, Wind & Fire, D♭ pedal). **Another reharm**: swap iv for a ii7♭5 built a third above it (same notes as a first-inversion vi6 chord) — heard in "White Christmas." **Songs**: "Dear Prudence," "Magical Mystery Tour," "Hold Me Tight" (The Beatles); Ringo Starr's "What Goes Around"; "When the Saints Go Marching In"; "Santa Claus Is Comin' to Town"; "When the Swallows Come Back to Capistrano"; "The Fear" and other tracks (Lily Allen). → [[Secondary Dominants]], [[Cadences]] (minor plagal), [[Augmented Chords]], [[Chord Inversions]] (pedal point, reharm variants) — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use the Magnolia chord progression]]
- **The "Plagal Cascade" / Wonderwall / Imagine Dragons progression — i–III–♭VII–IV** (Am–C–G–D, in A) — the IV chord is major (D, not diatonic Dm) via the same Dorian borrowing as the Dorian-vamp entries above (raised 6th degree). Named "Plagal Cascade" (coined by the YouTube channel 12tone) because after the opening i→III (a 3rd up), the rest of the loop is a chain of descending PERFECT 4THS — C→G→D→Am, three 4th-drops in a row — a plagal (IV→I-type) cadence repeated/cascaded rather than occurring once. **Harmonic rhythm changes the character of the identical chord sequence**: one full bar per chord (True Faith, New Order) reads as a straightforward progression; half a bar per chord — twice the speed (Run to You, Bryan Adams) — makes the same four chords read more like a riff/hook than a harmonic backdrop, with an added sense of forward momentum. - **Seven common progressions in one survey — the Axis family, two Spanish/modal vamps, and a doo-wop mix-up worth correcting.** (1) **Axis Progression — I–V–vi–IV** — see [[The Axis Progression]] for the full writeup; noted here as the most "loopable" 4-chord shape because I→V moves maximally away from home, then vi and IV each offer partial (not full) resolution, keeping the loop wanting to continue rather than settling. (2) **vi–IV–I–V** — the SAME four chords started on vi instead of I, giving a minor-key-flavored variant of the Axis progression; the Axis of Awesome comedy sketch (the progression's namesake) explicitly used this relationship to chain two "different" progressions into one longer medley. (3) **Andalusian cadence — i–♭VII–♭VI–V** (with the V chord made major/dominant) — flamenco-associated descending progression; the major V pulls hard back up to i, keeping the 4-bar loop tightly closed. (4) **"Aeolian vamp" — i–♭VII–♭VI** — the same three opening chords as the Andalusian cadence, but WITHOUT the V chord at the end — removing that dominant pull leaves the loop floatier/less directional, staying in pure Aeolian color rather than resolving. (5) **Doo-wop/"50s" progression — I–vi–IV–V** — often mislabeled the "Blue Moon" progression, but "Blue Moon" itself actually uses **I–vi–ii–V** (ii and IV are both subdominant-function chords, only one note apart, so the confusion is understandable but the two progressions are not identical). (6) **I–V–IV–V** — all-major-chord, major-scale-only loop; simple, upbeat, functional bed. (7) **"Mixolydian vamp" — I–♭VII–IV–I** (F–E♭–B♭–F) — three chords are enough to establish Mixolydian color (♭VII borrowed from the major scale's flattened 7th); also reads as circle-of-fifths root motion (♭VII→IV→I are each a 4th/5th apart), giving it an "epic, open" quality on top of the modal color; **also the identical progression behind "Hey Jude"'s outro coda (The Beatles) and "One Day Like This"'s outro coda (Elbow)**, confirmed by a RipX vocal-swap mashup — Elbow's outro is judged "almost definitely" a direct homage to the Hey Jude coda, both simple, uplifting, singalong-melody outros on the identical loop. → [[The Axis Progression]], [[Diatonic Modes]] (Aeolian, Mixolydian) — David Bennett Music Theory, [[7 super common chord progressions and why they work]], [[These songs have the same chords... let's swap them! - **The Pachelbel's Canon progression, spelled out — I–V–vi–iii–IV–I–IV–V** (in D: D–A–Bm–F♯m–G–D–G–A), 8 bars, entirely diatonic. **What makes it work: harmonic sequence in the bass**, not just the chords — the first 6 bars are the SAME interval movement (a descending 4th) repeated three times from different starting points (D→A, B→F♯, G→D), which is what gives the loop its "meant to go together" logic; the final 2 bars break the sequence deliberately with a IV–V turnaround specifically because a sequence can't loop back on itself — you need a genuine cadential move to restart it. Genuinely unusual for a classical piece to be chord-loop-based at all (closer to how pop music is built) — one reason it ports into pop so easily. **Common pop alterations** (the first 4 bars are usually kept intact — "that's where the character comes from" — changes cluster in bars 5–8): swap the iii chord for major III; replace the penultimate IV with ii (minor or major) instead. Songs in the ORIGINAL unaltered form: "All Together Now" (The Farm); "Love in the First Degree" (Bananarama, cited by producer Pete Waterman as directly modeled on the Canon progression — "almost the godfather" of the Stock Aitken Waterman sound). → [[Chord Progressions]] § *descending-stepwise-bass progression* (near-identical, differs only in the penultimate chord) — David Bennett Music Theory, [[21 Songs that use Pachelbel's Canon chord progression]]
- **The descending-stepwise-bass progression — a full harmonized walk down the major scale, most often heard as a partial 4- or 5-step excerpt rather than the complete 7-step form.** Full form: I → (V or iii, in inversion) → vi → (IV or ii, in inversion) → V (the most consistent/fixed step of the whole progression) → I (1st inversion) → ii → V–I turnaround — every chord voiced so the walking bass note lands on each consecutive major-scale degree in order. **5-step is the most common real-world length** (I down to IV, five bass notes, then the progression moves elsewhere) — e.g. the verse of "Piano Man" (Billy Joel). **6-step**: "Can You Feel the Love Tonight" (Elton John). **4-step** (down to V) also occurs. **Why it works**: it's literally a harmonized major scale, so it inherits the scale's own maximally-predictable, satisfying motion — and it's easy to stumble onto as a songwriter, since each next chord is just "whatever harmonizes the next scale degree," not a leap of imagination. **Close relative**: the Pachelbel's Canon progression (I–V–vi–iii–IV–I–IV–V) is nearly identical to this one — they differ only in the penultimate chord. → [[Chord Inversions]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[40 Songs that use Descending Stepwise chord progressions]]
- **Two progressions that are the same four chords, just started at a different point — both driven entirely by circle-of-fifths root motion.** **I–vi–ii–V** (C–Am–Dm–G): jumps away from home (C→Am, a 3rd) then walks back in a chain of perfect 5ths (Am→Dm→G→C, each a 5th down) — starts at home, wanders, returns. **vi–ii–V–I** (Am–Dm–G–C): the same four chords starting one position later — pure 5th-chain the whole way through — starts away from home and works steadily back to it. Both read as satisfying for the same underlying reason: the perfect 5th is the simplest consonant interval after the octave (a 3:2 frequency ratio), so stepping around the [[Circle of Fifths]] one hop at a time always sounds "correct," even when a step briefly leaves the diatonic key. **Dressed up with extensions**, the vi–ii–V–I form becomes the "Isn't She Lovely" progression (Stevie Wonder): C♯m7–F♯7(secondary dominant, V of V)–B11/B9sus4–C♯. **General writing tool**: from ANY chord, moving a 5th up or a 5th down is close to foolproof — you can chain several 5th-moves in a row even straying outside the starting key (demonstrated: Am–F–B♭–E♭, walking further from A minor with each hop) and it still glues together, because each individual step is consonant; the further you wander, the more deliberate you have to be about the 5th-move back home when you want to resolve. → [[Circle of Fifths]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[2 Super Satisfying Chord Progressions And How To Write Your Own]]
- **Three minor-key progressions, each mixing (or not mixing) a different pair of minor-scale flavors.** (1) **"Minor climb" / "House of the Rising Sun" progression — i–III–IV–V** in natural minor with a Dorian tint (e.g. Am–C–D in A) — mixes Aeolian (i, III) with Dorian (the D major IV chord, which needs a raised 6th/F♯ that plain natural minor doesn't have) — a walking-up-the-scale feel with one modal-interchange chord for extra brightness. (2) **Aeolian–harmonic-minor blend — i–iv–III–V** (Am–Dm–C–E) — stays in natural minor for the first three chords, then the final V chord borrows harmonic minor's raised 7th (G♯) to become a real dominant — added drama/seriousness right before the loop restarts. (3) **"Aeolian closed loop" — i–iv–i** (Am–Dm–Am) — pure natural minor throughout, and a CLOSED loop: it begins and ends on i, so unlike most 4-chord loops it doesn't need repetition to reach resolution — the tonic return is already built in. → [[Diatonic Modes]] (Dorian), [[Harmonic Minor Modes]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[3 Minor key chord progressions you should know]]
- **Four progressions, each named after its most famous song.** (1) **"Kiss From a Rose" — ♭VI–♭VII–I major** (E♭–F–G, in G) — sets up like a minor-key progression (the ♭VI and ♭VII of the relative minor) then resolves to the MAJOR tonic instead of the expected minor one — a **Picardy third** (a minor progression resolving major) for unexpected uplift/brightness. - **The "Eight Days a Week" progression — I–II–IV–I (D–E–G–D), all four chords MAJOR** — a borrowed II chord (E major instead of the diatonic E minor) is what gives it a bright, uplifting, unusually uniform sound. Two ways to hear the II chord: (1) **temporary Lydian** — E major contains G♯, the raised 4th degree of D, so the progression briefly tips into D Lydian before the following G chord restores the natural 4th and pulls back to plain D major — a deliberate uplift-then-settle arc; (2) **secondary dominant, interrupted** — II is the V of V (E is a fifth below A, the key's actual dominant), so the ear expects a II→V (E→A) resolution, but the progression goes to IV (G) instead — an **interrupted cadence** that redirects the expected pull rather than completing it. **Voicing choice matters**: root-position throughout gives pure parallel motion (all notes moving the same direction together — powerful/unified, reads as one big riff rather than four distinct chords) vs. a voice-led arrangement with a stepwise-descending top line (A→G♯→G→F♯) for more independent, "each chord has its own identity" motion — a deliberate choice between unity and voice independence, not a right/wrong. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band" (title track); also several Beatles solo-career tracks. Other artists: Queen's "It's a Kind of Magic" (the vocal melody explicitly leans into the raised-4th/Lydian moment on the II chord); also used in the *Undertale* and *Sonic* game soundtracks. → [[Diatonic Modes]] (Lydian), [[Secondary Dominants]], [[Cadences]] (interrupted cadence) — David Bennett Music Theory, [[the "Eight Days a Week" chord progression]]
- **Four more common progressions, minor-key and modal this time.** (1) **Harmonic-minor vamp — i–VI–V** (e.g. Am–F–E in A minor) — the raised leading tone from harmonic minor sharpens V into a real dominant pull even in a minor-key loop. Heard in "Sweet Dreams (Are Made of This)" (Eurythmics). (2) **The "Viva la Vida" progression — IV–V–I–vi** — the same four chords as the I–vi–IV–V "50s" loop, just rotated to start on IV instead of I; the climb (IV→V resolving up to I) then the vi chord begins climbing back down, so the loop restarts smoothly. Heard in "Viva la Vida" (Coldplay), "I Want to Hold Your Hand" (The Beatles), "Walking in Memphis" (Marc Cohn). - **Five progressions analyzed for WHY they work, not just what they are.** (1) **I–V–♭VII–IV** (C–G–B♭–F) — a **harmonic sequence**: a major chord followed by another major chord a 4th down, repeated once — the repetition of the same shape (just transposed) is what makes it sound cohesive/inevitable rather than arbitrary; also **blends two scales**, since I and V are diatonic major-scale chords but ♭VII belongs to Mixolydian (major scale with a flattened 7th) borrowed in for a "cooler, more mellow" color. (2) **I–ii–IV–V "major scale climb"** (C–Dm–F–G, the "My Girl" progression, The Temptations) — a plain diatonic ascent whose single defining feature (climbing stepwise up the scale degrees used) mirrors an uplifting/rising lyrical mood. (3) **I–IV–I–V** (C–F–C–G) — the T→SD→T→D functional cycle in its most bread-and-butter form: I is fully at rest, IV (subdominant) introduces mild tension without demanding resolution, back to I for release, then V (dominant) creates real pull back home — the alternation is what keeps a simple loop feeling like it's going somewhere. - **Four standing chord-substitution rules, all reversible, plus a "reduce to basic form" quick-ID table.** (1) Major → its relative minor or secondary relative minor (for C, use Am or Em). (2) Minor → its relative major (for Am, use C). (3) Dominant 7th → the dominant minor a fourth up (for C7, use Gm — a ii-for-V substitution). (4) **Any chord → the chord rooted a flat 5th away** — the tritone substitution, generalized past dominants to majors and minors too. Worked example: over a static Am–Dm–G7 vamp where the melody tolerates it, a dozen altered-7th substitutes (♭9, ♯5, etc.) can all stand in for the plain G7 turnaround chord — there's no single "correct" reharmonization, only ones that fit the melody in hand. Reduce any embellished symbol to its basic form first to see which rule applies (Cma7/C6/C6-9 → C major; C9/C11/C13 → C7; Cm7/Cm9/Cm7♭5 → Cm). **Back-cycling**: add harmonic interest to a static chord by cycling backward through the circle of dominants before it, one fingering shape carried through the whole line. **Symmetric chord movement**: a chord can move up/down the fretboard by any interval (half-step, whole-step, 3rd) as a single shape, provided the final chord in the run resolves properly into what follows — ear decides which intervals work, not theory. → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Altered Chords]] — [[Joe Pass Guitar Style - Enrichment|Joe Pass Guitar Style]], p.3–10
