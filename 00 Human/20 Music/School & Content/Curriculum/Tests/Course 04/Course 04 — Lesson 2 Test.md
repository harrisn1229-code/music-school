> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **It sounds an octave LOWER than written**
> Written pitch sits an octave above sounding pitch. Direction is the whole content of the fact — "an octave higher" is the reverse and is wrong.
>
> **Q2.** **A transposing instrument**
> *Also accepted:* effectively notated 8vb
>
> **Q3.** **Because on a non-transposing instrument the note sounds where you wrote it**
> That's an octave above where guitar habits expect it, so parts written from those habits come out high.
>
> **Q4.** **The same written pitch exists in five or six different places on the fretboard**
> Notation tells you *what*, not *where*, so every note carries a location decision.
>
> **Q5.** **Ledger lines — wrong because they're just as common in piano music, which is easier to read**
> Ledger lines are a legibility nuisance, not the mechanism, and `8va` exists precisely to manage them.
>
> **Q6.** **The mapping is one-to-one — each pitch has exactly one key**
> The decision never comes up, so the reader converts symbol straight to motion.
>
> **Q7.** **A fixed four-fret window, one finger per fret — it makes the mapping unambiguous again**
> Committing to a position removes the choice, which is what restores piano-like directness.
>
> **Q8.** **Removing the location variable so the reading skill can be trained on its own**
> Twenty-four pages in one position means every note has exactly one place, so attention goes to reading rather than to choosing.
>
> **Q9.** **The symbol fixes a pitch but not a fingering — and a wrong belief is at least correctable**
> *Rubric (5):* explains that notation encodes pitch and duration but not physical location (2) · says guitar has multiple locations per pitch, so the reader supplies what the page omits (1) · says a wrong belief is a target you can argue with, while a blank offers nothing to correct (2)
> Notation encodes what to sound and for how long. On an instrument where each pitch has one home, that's a complete instruction. On guitar it's underspecified: the same written E might be five places, so the reader has to supply a decision the composer never made, in real time, while also reading. That's the load. As for the belief — someone who thinks it's ledger lines has a specific, checkable claim, and showing them a first-position passage that's still hard immediately dislodges it. Someone with no theory of the difficulty has nothing to test and tends to conclude they're simply bad at reading, which points at no fix.
