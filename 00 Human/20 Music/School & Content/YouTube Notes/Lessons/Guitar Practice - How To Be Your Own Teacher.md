# Guitar Practice - How To Be Your Own Teacher

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KUFfHa09LM0)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KUFfHa09LM0)

## Synopsis

Jens Larsen lays out a complete self-diagnosis method built around recording yourself practicing — the substitute for "what you pay a teacher to do," namely listen to your playing, identify what to work on, and evaluate whether it's improving over time. The video moves through why recording works (you can't objectively hear yourself while you're busy performing), practical how-to (gear, metronome-vs-backing-track), the psychological obstacle almost everyone hits (nobody likes hearing their own recorded playing, even famous players), and finally three disciplines for actually getting value out of the recordings once you have them: don't get distracted from the one thing you're evaluating, be specific rather than vague about what's wrong, and keep sessions short and archived so you can measure progress over time. This is a genuinely different, self-contained self-teaching *process* rather than a technique or vocabulary lesson — it slots directly alongside the practice-methodology material already in the vault (recording-based self-diagnosis is a gap the existing [[The Five Pillars of Jazz Guitar Practice]] framework doesn't cover) rather than the tune/theory/vocabulary content most guitar lesson videos carry.

## What's taught

**Why record yourself at all**
- The core reframe: a huge part of what a teacher does is *listen* to your playing, tell you what to work on, and evaluate progress over subsequent lessons — recording yourself lets you do all three of those roles for yourself, because it lets you take a step back and listen without also having to perform.
- While actually playing you're spending your focus on performing (correctly) — you can't simultaneously give full analytical attention to *how it sounds*. Recording separates the two tasks in time.
- Recording is specifically good at catching **subtle things that are hard to hear while playing**: swing feel (are eighth notes landing as a triplet feel or straight/even), general phrasing habits, getting stuck playing in one register/octave of the instrument, and other phrasing issues.

**How to record — practical setup**
- **Use your smartphone, video not just audio.** Phones are already good at this; get a clear enough image and adequate framing.
- **Get a decent audio signal — this is the part people skip.** If the audio is distorting, you can't judge finer details of timing or phrasing and some notes become inaudible — a bad recording actively defeats the exercise. Basic care (mic distance/level) matters more than gear quality.
- **Bonus use: record for pure technique review** — e.g. a video specifically to watch your right-hand (picking-hand) motion on an exercise or phrase type, independent of how the notes sound.
- Minimal gear needed: "a phone and a coffee mug on the table" is explicitly framed as sufficient.
- **Practice against a metronome before a backing track when starting out.** A metronome is harder — it forces you to generate and feel time/groove yourself rather than riding a backing track's groove, so it's a better test of whether your own sense of time and phrasing is solid. Once that's established, backing tracks (or a groove app like Drum Genius) are good for testing whether you actually lock in with a real rhythm section feel.

**The obstacle: nobody likes hearing themselves back**
- Named explicitly as near-universal, not a personal flaw — cited examples: Allan Holdsworth and Michael Brecker were both famous for disliking listening to recordings of their own playing.
- Everything you play already feels like a "tired cliché" to you because you've already practiced it, even if it sounds fresh/good to someone else.
- **This gets easier with repetition** — the discomfort is real at first but fades as you get used to it; treat it as a tool to push through rather than a signal to avoid recording.
- **Concrete counter-move: keep a running list of three things you're working on AND three things you already do well.** Don't let the self-critical listening turn into pure self-flagellation — the list format forces balance and keeps the process usable rather than demoralizing.
- **Don't listen back immediately — put time/space between recording and listening.** Right after playing you still remember what you *meant* to play and where you flinched, which distorts how you hear the take (bias toward "I know that chord sounded bad because I remember flubbing it"). Waiting removes that memory bias so you hear what's actually on the recording, not what you remember attempting. Explicitly compared to how record-making works — artists give finished takes a few weeks of distance before deciding what makes the album.
- **The deeper payoff:** there's a gap between how playing *feels* in the moment and how it actually *sounds* — recording yourself repeatedly shrinks that gap over time, so your internal sense of your own playing gets more accurate and useful even outside the recording exercise itself.

**Three rules for getting real value out of self-recorded evaluation (the core actionable checklist)**
1. **Don't get distracted from what you're actually evaluating.** If you're recording specifically to check eighth-note swing feel, play long eighth-note lines and listen *only* for that — don't get pulled into judging phrase interest, melodic content, or anything else in the same take. Practicing/evaluating one specific thing means tuning out everything adjacent to it, even other real flaws you notice along the way (save those for their own dedicated pass).
2. **Be specific about the target sound, not just "good" or "bad."** Vague self-feedback ("that was bad, make it better") is explicitly called out as the single worst thing a teacher can say — it gives you nothing to act on. The fix: name the actual gap — e.g. "I need to play more evenly on a swing groove" or "I need to play more laid-back" — specific enough that you can then go find or design an exercise that targets exactly that gap. Precondition: you have to already know *what you want it to sound like* and be able to hear the difference between that and what you're doing — self-diagnosis fails if you can't yet hear the target sound in your head.
3. **Measure progress over time, and keep the recordings short.** A single day's practice-and-record cycle won't show improvement — change shows up across multiple sessions, so record regularly (daily is a good habit) but **keep each recording short** — a few choruses or a single chorus of a standard, not hours of footage to sit through, or the review process itself eats practice time. Archive recordings and periodically compare old vs. new to actually see the progress, which is what sustains motivation — hearing real improvement is what keeps you coming back to the process; without that feedback loop, motivation to keep grinding drops off.
