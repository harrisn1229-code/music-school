# Octave-Shift Voicing Discovery

A deliberately unsystematic method for turning up new voicings of any chord shape you already know — Jared Borkowski's (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared) exploratory counterpart to the more rigorous drilling in [[Hub-Latching Approach]] and [[Chord Inversions]]. Where those work a chord's voicings by known theory (four inversions per chord tone, a fixed practice path), this method finds them by playful trial and error: move one note of a shape up or down an octave, see what turns up, and keep going. Directly demonstrates the mechanism behind [[Chord Voicings]]'s existing "close position" and "open position" rows and its chord/voicing/shape distinction — this page supplies *how* to move between them, not just what they're called.

## Prerequisite: seeing a note in other places

Assumes the ability to locate the same pitch class in other fretboard positions via octave and unison interval shapes — see [[Octave and Unison Shapes for Fretboard Navigation]]. Concretely, this means being able to answer "where else on the neck is this exact note" fast enough to move a chord tone without stopping to calculate — the same fretboard fluency [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]] and [[Guitar Positions]] build toward from other angles.

## The method

1. **Start from a chord shape you already know** — root position is the natural starting point since it's usually the one already memorized.
2. **Pick one note in the chord and move it up or down an octave**, using the octave/unison-shape skill above to find where that same pitch lands elsewhere on the neck.
3. **If the moved note now collides with another note already on that string** (you can't play two notes on one string at once), move the *colliding* note to its own nearest available octave too — cascading as many times as needed until every note in the chord has a distinct playable string.
4. **Repeat with a different note**, or repeat the whole cycle from the new shape, treating each result as a fresh starting point for the next move.
5. **Some results will be physically unplayable or just bad** — that's a normal, expected outcome of moving things around blind, not a sign the method is broken. Discard and try a different note.

There's no fixed order, no target voicing, and no guarantee of what you'll land on — that's the point. Jared frames it explicitly against his own more "systematic and heavy duty" prior lessons (memorize a fixed set of shapes in a set order): this is loose, playful, puzzle-solving exploration, closer to noodling with intent than a drill. "If teaching you a chord by giving you a chord shape diagram is giving you a fish, this lesson is teaching you how to fish."

## Worked examples

**C major triad (the from-scratch demo).** Root position C-E-G, close position. Move the root (C) up an octave → E-G-C — still close position, a different inversion of the same voicing family. From the original root position instead, move the *middle* note (E) up an octave → C-G-E, this time with real space between the C and the G — an **open position** voicing, specifically the **1-5-3** voicing. That 1-5-3 voicing is then shown realized as three different physical shapes (open position, a shape at the 5th fret, a shape rooted off the 6th string) — same voicing, three shapes, directly demonstrating [[Chord Voicings]]'s voicing-vs-shape distinction. Sliding any one of those shapes up a half step keeps the voicing (1-5-3) intact while changing which chord it is.

**A major, open chord → movable first-inversion 3-1-5.** Starting triad: 5th-root-3rd. Move the 5th up an octave (resolve the resulting collision), move the 3rd down an octave, relocate the root via a "find the 5th below" fretboard move → **3-1-5, first inversion, no open strings, fully movable to any major chord.** Named as the **I → V(1st inv.) → vi** voice-leading move used in "Tears in Heaven" (Eric Clapton), "Don't Think Twice, It's All Right" (Bob Dylan), and "Somebody to Love" (Queen) — full song ties on [[Chord voicings guitar lesson - Unlimited Chord Options!]].

**B7, dominant 7th → four usable inversions from one shape.** Starting shape: root-3rd-♭7-5th. Sequential octave shifts turn up, in order: (1) root-♭7-3-5 (barred, standard root-position dominant-7 shape); (2) ♭7-3-5-root, **third inversion, ♭7 on the bottom** — the opening chord of "Águas de Março" (Jobim); (3) 3-♭7-1-5, **first inversion**, resolves naturally to a I chord; (4) 3-♭7-5-root — the shape used for the intro to "Wave" (Jobim/Sinatra).

**D minor 7, the "Wave" companion chord.** Starting shape: root-5th-♭7-♭3. One pass turns up an unplayable shape — discarded, a normal outcome. A further shift → **root-♭3-5-♭7, third inversion, ♭7 on the bottom** — the functional partner to the B7 third-inversion shape above (resolves the same way, to I). A second, separate shift → **♭3-♭7-root-5, first inversion, 3rd in the bass** — noted to sound like a major 6 chord (a first-inversion m7 and a major-6 chord share overlapping pitch content), usable as a general-purpose colorful substitute for any plain Dm(7).

## Collision-resolution mechanic, generalized

The one piece of real mechanical rigor inside an otherwise loose method: whenever an octave shift lands two notes on the same string, the fix is always the same — move the *newly-colliding* note (not the one you were originally moving) to its own nearest available octave, and repeat if that triggers another collision. This cascading fix is what turns "randomly move notes around" into something that reliably terminates in a playable four-or-fewer-note voicing rather than a dead end.

## Why this differs from the hub-latching approach

[[Hub-Latching Approach]] and this method solve adjacent problems from opposite directions. Hub-latching starts from *known theory* (a chord has N inversions, defined by which chord tone is lowest) and drills a fixed path through all of them systematically, always anchored back to a "hub." This method has no theory prerequisite and no anchor — it's a way to *discover* voicings you didn't already know existed by physically moving notes and listening, useful specifically when you already have a shape memorized and want more options from it without deriving them analytically first. Both feed the same destination: more voicings under the fingers, reachable from different starting skill levels.

## Practice framing

Feed it with any root-position shape you already know — Jared's own free "Chords with Color" chart supplies deliberately limited, single-position root-position voicings specifically as raw material for this method to multiply. Treat each session as exploratory rather than comprehensive: work one chord shape at a time, keep what sounds good, discard what's unplayable, and don't expect (or need) to exhaust every possible octave permutation.

— Jared Borkowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared), [[Chord voicings guitar lesson - Unlimited Chord Options!]]
