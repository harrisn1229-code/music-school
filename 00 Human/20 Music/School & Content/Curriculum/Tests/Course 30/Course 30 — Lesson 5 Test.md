> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **Assign the roles out loud, at the top of the tune**
> Who comps the head, who comps each solo, who takes it out. Called like a bandleader calls a form, so nothing has to be negotiated mid-tune.
>
> **Q2.** **Piano is primary; the guitarist behaves like a horn player until cued**
> Solo when called, otherwise lay out rather than comp. It is a practical jam-session default, not a claim about instruments in general.
>
> **Q3.** **Place your hits in the holes the motif leaves, rather than doubling it**
> A predictable, repeated idea is exactly what lets you hear where the gaps will be by the second or third repetition, so you can land in them instead of on top.
>
> **Q4.** **Move opposite the soloist — they go up, you go lower — and going higher is the delicate one**
> Comping above the soloist risks pulling listener attention off them, so it is used sparingly.
>
> **Q5.** **Legitimate — silence in a conversation is confidence, and it buys thinking time**
> Leaving room lets the soloist define what is actually needed, so you re-enter with something that fits instead of reflexively filling. It is also a practised skill: a player used to covering all the harmonic space alone finds laying out effortful at first.
>
> **Q6.** **Because it has stopped being comping and become a pulse — a second drummer**
> All quarter notes, metronomically predictable, harmonically unassertive. The role is so defined that the piano can comp freely on top of it.
>
> **Q7.** **Closed triads — three notes, no doubled notes, no full six-string chords**
> They are lean on purpose, and that leanness is exactly why three of them can stack without collapsing into mud.
>
> **Q8.** **String group, neck position and tone — not different material**
> One triad vocabulary relocated across four string groupings and three positions, then separated by pickup and tone choice.
>
> **Q9.** **A bed, a backbeat part and a melody part**
> The bed is arpeggiated closed triads low on the D-G-B strings, the backbeat is accents on the top three strings, and the melody is a double-stop line above both.
>
> **Q10.** **Both are solved by dividing register and role, and neither is solved by writing better parts**
> *Rubric (5):* identifies the shared problem — two harmony parts in the same register and rhythmic space read as clutter (2) · names division by register and role as the shared solution (1) · gives at least one real difference between the live and recorded cases (2)
> In both cases the failure is the same: two harmony voices occupying the same register and the same rhythmic space cancel each other out, and no amount of quality in either part fixes it. The solution is the same too — decide who owns which register, which rhythmic role and which moments, then hold to it. What differs is the negotiation. Live, it is real time and social: the roles get called at the top of the tune, and if they were not, someone has to lay out or track the other player's register on the fly, with laying out always available. On a recording nothing is real time, so the division is arranging: one triad vocabulary spread over different string groups, positions and tones, and any part that crowds another can simply be re-voiced or removed. The live version needs an agreement; the recorded version needs a plan.
