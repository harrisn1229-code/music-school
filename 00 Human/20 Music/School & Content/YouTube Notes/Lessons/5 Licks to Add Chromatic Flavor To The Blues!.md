# 5 Licks to Add Chromatic Flavor To The Blues!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WHku52jEKLY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WHku52jEKLY)

## Synopsis
Jack Ruch's five go-to chromatic blues licks, taught individually with fingerings and settings and then stitched into full 12-bar-blues-shuffle solo examples in the key of G — a tighter, more concentrated companion to his other blues breakdowns ([[Blues Lead Phrasing and Touch]]'s worked examples, [[B.B. King Guitar Lesson - 5 Licks To Get Started!]]'s box-building primer). Where those videos build phrasing/touch and hand-position vocabulary broadly, this one is narrowly focused on **chromaticism as a connective device**: half-step runs and pull-offs that link scale degrees, chord tones that voice-lead between the I and IV chords, a CAGED-triad-based line for outlining chord tones across the whole form, and — the most advanced of the five — a D altered-scale phrase over the V chord that explicitly nods to jazz vocabulary (Joe Pass is named directly). Each lick is shown as a movable, relocatable shape rather than a fixed lick tied to one spot on the neck, and the video closes with a full solo combining all five over a 12-bar shuffle.

## What's taught

- **Lick 1 — a chromatic ♭5-passing-tone connector, playable in two positions off the same shape.** Position A (3rd fret, key of G, I chord): starting on **D (the 5th of the G chord)**, play D→F→G→A, then pull off **A♭→G** (the chromatic passing tone between the 4th and 5th), then back down F→D. Position B (8th fret, same fingering, moved up): starting on the **root (G)** instead of the 5th, the identical shape now yields root–♭3–4–5–♭5 — described directly as giving "that nice kind of bluesy sound with the flat fifth." Explicitly framed as one movable building block that other material can be attached before/after, not a complete phrase on its own. → [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]], [[Blues Lead Phrasing and Touch]]
- **Solo example 1 — lick 1 stated as a motif.** Over a 12-bar blues shuffle in G, the 3rd-fret version of lick 1 opens the solo; the second time through the form the same shape is developed into a small motif rather than replayed verbatim.
- **Lick 2 — a long ascending chromatic run from the root to the 5th of the current chord shape, three-notes-per-string.** Starting on **G**, run chromatically **G→A♭→A→B♭→B→C→D♭→D** (root up to the 5th), fingered as three notes on the G string then crossing to the B string for the last two (D♭, D) rather than staying on one string the whole way — chosen purely to keep the run inside one comfortable position. Fits directly out of the underlying chord shape (root of the shape up to its 5th), and because it's built from a movable shape rather than fixed frets, it relocates cleanly up through the I, IV and V chord positions as the form moves — described as "a great lick" for building repetitive motific material. → [[Blues Form and Harmony]]
- **Lick 3 — a chromatic I→IV voice-leading connector, placed specifically in bar 4 of the 12-bar form.** Over the G (I) chord: walk chromatically **G→F**, jump up to the **9th (A)**, come down to the **6th (E)**, then a **half-step hammer-on E→F** followed by a **slide down to D** — landing exactly as the IV chord (C7) arrives in bar 4. Once on the IV chord, walk chromatically **up** to its **3rd (E of C7)**. Framed directly as a voice-leading device — "little chromatic things to pull your ear from one chord to the next" — rather than scale-based improvising; demonstrated placed precisely at the I→IV turn of a 12-bar form so the phrasing lands "properly" against the chord change. → [[Blues Form and Harmony]]
- **Lick 4 — an E-minor-triad-based line (relative minor of G) plus two chromatic-approach variants, built from CAGED triad groupings.** Base version: slide up an **E minor triad** (relative minor of G major, so it sits naturally over a G/G7 chord), then walk **D→C**, resolving into G major. Named alternative: walk up chromatically from **B to D** (the 3rd of the G major chord) instead of the triad slide. A third variant used in the solo example: chromatic approach on the B string up to the **3rd of the G major chord**. All three are framed as reorderable notes from the same **CAGED triad grouping** (3rd–5th–root of the chord, optionally adding the ♭7) rather than fixed licks — the same shape retargets the V or IV chord just by recognizing that chord's own CAGED triad shape, including jumping down a string set for the IV chord specifically. → [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]], [[Harmonized Scale Triads for Soloing]]
- **Lick 5 (most advanced) — a long chromatic descending line into a D-altered-scale phrase over the V chord (D7), resolving back to the I chord (G).** Placed at the very end of the 12-bar form, over the V chord. Starts by sliding into the **3rd of the G chord (B)**, up to **D**, then a long chromatic descent **E→E♭→D**, jumps down a string, and continues chromatically **D♭→C→B→B♭** — one continuous chromatic line spanning roughly a 4th. From there the line moves into **D altered-scale** territory over the D7 (V) chord: described in the transcript as moving through the **♯9 → ♭9 → root**, then down to the **♭7**, before resolving to the **G chord by landing on B (the 3rd of G)**. Explicitly credited as the kind of altered phrasing "guys like Joe Pass" play over a dominant V chord in a blues context, tying it to jazz vocabulary rather than straight blues scale material. → [[Altered Chords]], [[Blues Form and Harmony]], [[Scale Modes Reference]]
- **Closing full solo (all five licks combined).** The final worked example strings together phrases from all five licks over a complete 12-bar shuffle in G, demonstrating how the individually-taught building blocks recombine into one continuous solo rather than staying as isolated exercises — the same "learn the pieces, then combine them" framing used across Jack Ruch's other lesson videos ([[Chord-Tone Soloing]], [[B.B. King Guitar Lesson - 5 Licks To Get Started!]]).
- **Stated practice framing.** All five licks are pitched as **movable shapes**, explicitly encouraged to be relocated around the fretboard and reordered/recombined rather than played verbatim — "take these ideas and move them around the fretboard... create something new out of it."
