# Songs to help you recognise chords in a progression

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fxNRcKnMF-c)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fxNRcKnMF-c)

## Synopsis

A song-anchor system (same method as the interval videos, applied to chord function) for recognizing where a chord sits relative to the key by ear — hearing chords as scale-degree functions (I, ii, iii, IV, V, vi, plus several chromatic/borrowed functions) rather than as fixed note names, since a given function (e.g. I–V–vi–IV) sounds recognizably similar regardless of what key it's transposed into. Each example is chosen to start on the tonic chord and move directly to the target function, to isolate that one relationship. Major-key functions only — a minor-key companion video is referenced as planned.

## Techniques

- **The core idea — relative not absolute pitch**: hearing "I" and "V" instead of "C" and "G" lets the same function be recognized transposed into any key (D–Bm–G sounds like the same progression as C–Am–F because both are I–vi–IV).
- **V (dominant)** — "Summer of '69" (Bryan Adams), verse and post-chorus riff both move I→V. Also any I–V–vi–IV "Axis of Awesome" song, most famously "Let It Be" (Beatles).
- **IV (subdominant)** — "Born in the U.S.A." (Springsteen) — the whole song oscillates between I and IV.
- **vi** — "Stand By Me" (Ben E. King), I→vi. "Send My Love" (Adele) — oscillates I↔vi for its entire duration.
- **ii (minor)** — "I'd Rather Go Blind" (Etta James) — oscillates I↔ii throughout.
- **iii (minor)** — "Help!" (Beatles) verse, I→iii — described as a "halfway house" feel, neither resolved nor tense.
- **♭VII (borrowed/chromatic, most common non-diatonic function)** — "Hey Jude" outro: I→♭VII→IV→I.
- **Major II (borrowed)** — "Eight Days a Week" (Beatles), I→II (major, not the diatonic minor ii).
- **Major III (borrowed)** — "Creep" (Radiohead), I→III (major).
- **Major VI (borrowed)** — "Maxwell's Silver Hammer" (Beatles), I→VI (major, not the diatonic minor vi).
- **Minor v (borrowed)** — "The Only Exception" (Paramore), I→v (minor version of the usually-major dominant).
- **Minor iv (borrowed)** — described as having a particularly melancholic/"longing" sound. "No Surprises" (Radiohead) opening riff: I→iv (minor).
- **♭II (Neapolitan-adjacent, "exotic")** — "White Rabbit" (Jefferson Airplane) pivots almost its entire duration between I and ♭II.
- **12 functions covered total**, explicitly flagged as not exhaustive but "the most common types" worth learning first — all major-key.
