# Flying — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

A jam frame more than a composition — too choreographed to pass as real improvisation, and less developed than anything else they were writing by 1967. Pollack covers it largely because it's the rare mid-period trace of a habit that bookends the career: set a simple progression, usually a twelve-bar blues, and play over it until you're done. The 1960 Quarrymen tapes are full of it and so are the later session reels; the middle years show almost nothing.

**Form.** Three twelve-bar choruses and a fade. **The intro is itself a complete twelve-bar frame** — arguably a third verse, and parsed as an intro only because the tune hasn't shown up yet.

**Harmony.** A straight **twelve-bar blues**, in the variant where measure 12 takes a V chord rather than holding I over from measure 11 ([[BLU-C01 — The 12-bar form]]).

**Melody.** The tune runs an AA'BA pattern across the first eight measures and then simply stops — **the last four bars are left with no melody at all**, the instrumental equivalent of scat-mumbling through a lyric you haven't finished writing.

**Arrangement.** The thick, heavily processed Mellotron mix of the period. The rhythm guitar works a 4–3 appoggiatura figure through the intro ([[HAR-C09 — Non-harmonic tones]]). Verse 1's tune goes to English horn because an oboe can't reach its lowest note; verse 2 is all four of them in choral unison. The music stops dead at the end of verse 2 and the track runs on for roughly another thirty seconds of Mellotron noodling and tape noise — an available outtake carries a New Orleans jazz coda in that spot instead, dropped in favour of the effects version.
