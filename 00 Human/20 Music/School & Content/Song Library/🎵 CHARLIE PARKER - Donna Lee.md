# Donna Lee — Charlie Parker

> A bebop standard — one of the "aural transcription" tunes named directly on [[The Five Pillars of Jazz Guitar Practice]] § Pillar 2 as a canon solo worth transcribing by ear, alongside Miles Davis's "So What" and Grant Green's "Cool Blues." First given its own page here from [[How To Improve Your BEBOP LINES and Phrases with DONNA LEE - The Ultimate Bebop Guide...]] (Nathan Borton), which teaches the head melody itself plus a "DNA" breakdown of the four devices Parker leans on throughout — arpeggios, chromaticism, the major bebop scale, and line closers. The tune's own harmonic quirks (a non-functional B♭7 and a backdoor dominant) were already logged on [[Modal Jazz]] and [[Turnarounds]]/[[Diminished Chords]] from a separate, companion Borton video ("THIS Song Turns Students Into BEBOP Pros...") — both video sources are consolidated here.

## Chords
**Key:** A♭ major · **Form:** 32-bar contrafact of "(Back Home Again in) Indiana," transposed down a whole step from Indiana's usual key of B♭.

**A section (×2):** a turnaround shape reused across the section — **A♭maj7 (I) → F7 (VI7, a secondary dominant standing in for the diatonic vi) → B♭m7 (ii) → E♭7 (V7)** — the same I–VI7–ii–V skeleton that underlies rhythm changes' own A section (hence this page's "rhythm-changes-adjacent" structure tag, despite not sharing Gershwin's actual changes). One pass swaps the diatonic ii chord for a **static, non-resolving B♭7** in its place instead — Nathan Borton's "tonic dominant" reading, logged above.

**Bridge:** cascades through a rapid chain of secondary ii–V's outside the home key — **Gm7 → C7 → Fm7 → B♭7**-type motion, touching several key centers two bars at a time — before a **backdoor dominant, D♭maj7 → G♭7 (♭VII7)**, pulls it back toward home, landing the form on **A♭maj7** via a final ii–V–I.

Extremely fast harmonic rhythm (Parker's original recording sits around 220–225 BPM) is as much the tune's identity as the changes themselves. See [[Bebop Vocabulary]], [[Turnarounds]], [[The ii-V-I]].

**Additional harmony logged from a companion source video** (also working example-by-example off named chord targets rather than a full re-derived lead sheet):

- **Fm6 – C7 – Fm6 – B°**, where the B° is read as a rootless **G7♭9** resolving up a half step into Cm7 — the general spotting rule stated directly: "when you see a diminished chord moving up a half step below a chord, it usually acts as the five of that next chord." → [[Diminished Chords]]
- **A non-diatonic B♭7 sitting where a diatonic ii chord (Bbm7) would normally go** — flagged as "weird" because it doesn't belong to the key, then reframed as a static **tonic dominant**: its own bebop-dominant scale, or the melodic minor built on its own 5th (F melodic minor over B♭7). → [[Modal Jazz]]
- **D♭maj7 → G♭7, a backdoor dominant** (♭VII7→I) resolving back to D♭maj7 — soloed either as another static tonic dominant, or by substituting D♭ minor 6 and borrowing minor-key language over it. → [[Turnarounds]] § *Backdoor*

## Melody & line-building breakdown (Nathan Borton)

- **Learn the melody first, by ear, before touching the analysis.** Stated explicitly as the priority: sing it, memorize it away from the page — the eighth-note feel and phrasing are the point, and reading it defeats the purpose. Borton notes a couple of corrected notes he plays (matching Parker's actual recording) that differ from the standard real-book lead sheet.
- **Arpeggio → line closer, the default 5–1 shape.** Start on the 3rd of the dominant (F7's 3rd, A), walk up the arpeggio, then drop into a fixed **line closer** — a short, largely memorized cadential tail — that resolves down into the target chord: this example starts the closer on F7's ♭9 (G♭) and walks down to the 3rd of the I chord (B♭). The arpeggio doesn't have to voice-lead smoothly into the closer; you can jump straight to wherever the closer starts. New device for the vault, full writeup → [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §7.
- **The same shape decorated with extensions.** A second line uses an arpeggio with the 9th and 11th thrown in — named directly as "a sound that Wes Montgomery and Grant Green like to use a lot" — before its own line closer. → [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]
- **The formula flipped — closer opens, arpeggio extends.** The same closer Parker usually uses to *end* phrases opens this one instead, with the arpeggio then extending the line rather than resolving it — named directly as **tonic dominant** language ("a dominant chord that doesn't resolve to the one chord... it just kind of stays in the sound of the dominant"), useful for stretching a dominant line before an eventual resolution or over a static dominant section like the B section of [[Rhythm Changes]]. A fourth independent confirmation of the tonic-dominant concept already logged three times on [[Bebop Vocabulary]]. → [[Modal Jazz]]
- **Chromatic enclosures around chord tones AND extensions, generalized into a repeatable pattern.** One line encloses the **root** of the dominant chord; a second part of the same line encloses the **♭5**. Both are shown to generalize: the root-enclosure pattern can be walked up or down through the whole arpeggio (enclose the 3rd, jump down to the ♭7, repeat the pattern from there), and the ♭5-enclosure pattern extends to every other altered chord tone in turn — ♭5, ♯5, ♭9, ♯9. Stated payoff: combine different combinations of these enclosure patterns, close with a line closer, and "you have some really great sounding [lines]." → [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §2
- **The major bebop scale, right out of the gate in the melody itself.** Major scale plus one added note (the ♯5), which lets a line start on any chord tone and land back on the *same* chord tone one measure later. **The caveat stated directly:** don't count the 7th as a chord tone when using this scale — move it to the 6th, so the "arpeggio" in play is root–3rd–5th–6th, not root–3rd–5th–7th. Matches [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §1's bebop-major row (Mixolydian... wait, major + ♯5, passing tone between 5 and 6) exactly. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §1
- **A worked 3-6-2-5-1 line in the key of A♭, built from the bebop-major rule above.** Starting on C over A♭maj7 (I, one measure) — because the rule guarantees landing back on C a measure later — then into Cm7 (the 3, leading to the 6, then the 2-5-1). Stated shortcut: if you already know short 2-5 vocabulary that starts on the root, you can "plug and chug" it straight in at that point. The line finishes over the final 2-5-1 by pulling directly from Donna Lee's own melody. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §1, [[The ii-V-I]]
- **A short etude over the first half of the tune**, deliberately combining melody licks with the newly-built vocabulary above, closing the lesson.
- **Practice recommendation, stated directly:** take this melody further than just learning it — go through line by line, extract the licks you like, and move each one through all 12 keys. Framed as a transcription "everybody knows," dense enough with reusable material to be worth this treatment on its own.

## Steal this

- **Build a phrase from just two pieces: an arpeggio (with or without extensions) and a memorized line closer** — you don't need to compose a fresh cadence every time, just chain a chord-outlining half into a fixed cadential tail.
- **Flip any closer to open a phrase instead of ending one** whenever you want a tonic-dominant, non-resolving feel rather than a cadence.
- **When using the major bebop scale, remember the 6th-not-7th swap** — the "arpeggio" underneath the scale is root-3rd-5th-6th, not the usual 7th-chord shape.
- **Move a favorite lick through all 12 keys once it's under your fingers** — the stated payoff for treating a well-known transcription (this tune, or any other) as a genuine practice resource rather than a one-time listen.
