> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **Two chords can substitute when they share enough tones and the same harmonic function**
>
> **Q2.** **Function — function first, tones second**
> A substitute sharing three notes that resolves the wrong way is worse than one sharing two that resolves right.
>
> **Q3.** **iii lacks the root and contains the leading tone, which drags it toward dominant function**
> Substituting iii for I at a final cadence usually sounds unresolved — which is sometimes the point.
>
> **Q4.** **They contain the same tritone — B and F, since D♭7's C♭ is B**
> The 3rd and 7th swap roles: G7's 3rd is D♭7's 7th and vice versa.
>
> **Q5.** **Only dominant 7ths — and always a semitone above the target of resolution**
> The tritone is what is being preserved, and only a dominant 7th has one.
>
> **Q6.** **Dm7 – D♭7 – Cmaj7, roots walking D – D♭ – C**
> That descending chromatic bass is one of the most recognisable sounds in jazz and neo-soul.
>
> **Q7.** **♭II-of-ii — because it is undramatic, reading as a chromatic step from iii down to ii**
> Most listeners never register it as a substitution at all.
>
> **Q8.** **Any major triad a semitone above the target**
> *Also accepted:* a plain major triad a half step above the chord you are going to
> It does not have to be altered, and it does not have to be a dominant 7th.
>
> **Q9.** **Any dominant can be substituted, secondary dominants included**
> *Rubric (5):* says any dominant — not only the V — is a candidate (2) · says the substitute is labelled relative to its target, as ♭II of that chord (1) · gives a concrete edit, such as a major triad a semitone above the ii, V or vi (2)
> A secondary dominant is a dominant, so V/ii, V/V and V/vi can all be swapped for the chord a tritone away — which is why the label is written relative to the target rather than to the home key. In C, the substitute for the A7 heading to Dm is E♭7, or just an E♭ triad. The practical version is one edit: find a chord you are moving to, put a major triad a semitone above it in the bar before, and listen. That works in a piano roll with no altered-chord vocabulary at all.
>
> **Q10.** **A tritone sub if you arrive chromatically; a borrowed Phrygian cadence if you arrive from ♭VI by fifths**
> The audible difference is whether the roots move chromatically or by fifths on the way in.
>
> **Q11.** **Every note of the ♭II triad falls a semitone into a note of the tonic triad**
> ♭2̂→1̂, 4̂→3̂, ♭6̂→5̂ — three simultaneous semitone resolutions, no tritone and no leading tone.
>
> **Q12.** **The Neapolitan — ♭II going to V**
> *Also accepted:* Neapolitan sixth · Neapolitan chord
>
> **Q13.** **Fm7 – B♭7 – Cmaj7, both borrowed from C minor**
> *Also accepted:* iv7 – ♭VII7 – I
>
> **Q14.** **When V7 sounds too final or too bright — the backdoor has no leading tone**
> *Rubric (5):* says it is for when V7 is too final or too bright (2) · says it is borrowed from the parallel minor and has no leading tone rising into the tonic (2) · names the ♭6 falling to the 5th, or names the plagal flavour (1)
> V7 resolves by discharging a tritone and pushing a leading tone up a semitone into the tonic, which is maximum finality. The backdoor has neither: B♭7 in C carries A♭, the ♭6, which falls to G, and D, which is already in the tonic chord once you land on Cmaj9 (B♭7 and a plain Cmaj7 share no notes at all). The arrival is plagal-flavoured, warmer and more ambiguous, which is why gospel, soul and R&B live on it — and why it works in a loop that is not supposed to sound closed. The shorthand, ♭VII7 straight to I with no minor iv in front, does the same job in one chord.
