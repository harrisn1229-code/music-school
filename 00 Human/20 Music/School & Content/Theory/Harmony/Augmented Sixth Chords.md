# Augmented Sixth Chords

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Chords]] / [[Altered Chords]]. A family of **predominant** chords built to approach the dominant with maximum half-step tension — named for the augmented 6th interval between their top and bottom notes.

## Construction
Start from a doubled root of a V chord; approach the upper note of that octave with a half-step **below**, and the lower note with a half-step **above**; add the tonic as a third note. The interval between the resulting top and bottom notes is an augmented 6th.

## The three flavors

| Type | Symbol | Built by |
|---|---|---|
| Italian | It.6 | 3 notes only, doubled tonic |
| French | Fr.6 | adds the 2nd scale degree instead of doubling the tonic |
| German | Ger.6 | adds the lowered 3rd scale degree instead |

## Resolution
- Normally resolve to a **dominant triad** — not a dominant 7th, because the doubled roots present in a dominant triad would otherwise create parallel-motion problems.
- Also commonly resolve to a **tonic chord in 2nd inversion** (which itself has a doubled 5th scale degree, avoiding the same problem).
- Rarely, an augmented 6th chord is found **transposed down a perfect 5th** ("on ♭2"), used to approach a root-position tonic chord instead.
- Resolving a **German 6th → V** creates textbook-illegal parallel 5ths — but it's accepted as idiomatic anyway ("Mozart did it all the time").
- A Romantic-era-specific variant resolves the Ger.6 to a **dominant seventh** instead of a plain triad, which makes the augmented-6th interval resolve *obliquely* rather than outward to the octave.

## Enharmonic pivot trick
A **German 6th is enharmonically identical to a dominant 7th chord** (respell the augmented 6th as a minor 7th). This makes it a ready-made pivot chord for [[Modulation (Key Change)|enharmonic modulation]] — approach it like a Ger.6 in the old key, resolve it like a V7 (or a secondary dominant) in the new key, or vice versa. Beethoven used this move.

## Real-tune example: "double diminished" in Out of Nowhere
Jazz guitarist Jens Larsen analyzes the recurring **E♭7** in the jazz standard **Out of Nowhere** (key of G major) as a **"sharp-4 double-diminished chord"** — his own working term for the same sound this page catalogs as a **German augmented sixth chord**. Derivation as he explains it: the sound is closely related to **C♯ diminished** (the sharp-4 diminished triad of the key); "double-diminish" that triad's 3rd (E → E♭) and the resulting voicing, played an octave up, reads as an **E♭7**. In the tune, the same E♭7 chord/sound appears **twice for two different structural reasons** — once as this sharp-4 double-diminished predominant resolving straight back to the tonic (bars 3-4), and once, later in the form, as a **tritone substitute for A7** (the dominant of the dominant, bars 9-10) — the same four notes doing two different jobs depending on context, a real-world instance of this page's "It's just the sharp-4, differently named/used" point.
