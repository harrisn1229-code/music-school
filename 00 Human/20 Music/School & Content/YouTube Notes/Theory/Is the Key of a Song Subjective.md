# Is the Key of a Song Subjective?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wYV1ThJr-J0)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wYV1ThJr-J0)

## Synopsis

A follow-up to a "Clocks" video that provoked comment-section disagreement over what key the song is actually in — generalizes that disagreement into a proper theory of WHY and WHEN a song's tonic can be genuinely, unresolvably ambiguous, naming three contributing factors and demonstrating both the ambiguity and its resolution with real songs.

## Techniques

- **Core claim**: because all 7 diatonic modes share the same 7 notes and differ only in which note is treated as tonic, a song's "true" key can genuinely be a matter of legitimate listener disagreement, not just a fact some listeners get wrong.
- **"Clocks" (Coldplay) case**: comment-section disagreement between E♭ (Mixolydian), F minor (Aeolian), and A♭ (Ionian) — all three are valid readings of the identical 7-note collection, not unrelated guesses.
- **"Get Lucky" (Daft Punk) case, with a live demo**: the Bm7-D-F♯m7-E loop is disputed between B (Dorian) and F♯ (minor). Demonstrated by composing two different original melodies over the SAME chord loop — each melody alone pulls the listener's ear toward a different tonic note, proving melody (not just harmony) drives the perceived key.
- **Three named factors that create/resolve the ambiguity**: (1) a repeating chord loop that never resolves to a cadence, so no chord ever gets to read as a definitive "arrival"; (2) the melody's own implied tonic, independent of the chords underneath; (3) whether the melody happens to use the ONE note that would distinguish two neighboring candidate keys (e.g. the D natural vs. D♭ that's the only difference between E♭ major and A♭ major) — if that note never appears, there's no evidence to settle the dispute.
- **The first chord has outsized influence** simply by being heard first, before the listener has any other context to judge by — a "home-field advantage" independent of the three factors above.
- **The resolving counter-example, "Touch" (Daft Punk)**: same album, same chord progression and key as "Get Lucky," but this time the song eventually breaks out of the loop into new material that clearly resolves to F♯ minor — direct proof that it's specifically the never-resolving LOOP that manufactures the ambiguity in "Get Lucky," since the same starting material resolves cleanly once it's allowed to.
- **"Sweet Home Alabama" (Lynyrd Skynyrd)** cited as the same D-Mixolydian-vs-G-major phenomenon (credits Adam Neely's video on it rather than covering it independently).
- **Framed as a perceptual switch, like a duck/rabbit optical illusion**: a listener can consciously shift which note they hear as tonic, but can't hold both readings at once — recontextualizing lets you flip between them.
- References an existing "Karma Police" (Radiohead) video as a companion case that ISN'T loop-based, showing the phenomenon isn't exclusive to rotating chord progressions.
