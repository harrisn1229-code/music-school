# Gain Staging

**What it is:** managing signal level at every handoff — interface → track → each plugin → group → bus → master — so each stage receives the level it was designed for.

## Why it matters in a 32-bit world
Live's float engine can't clip internally, so gain staging isn't about digital overs anymore. It's about two things:
1. **Analog-modeled plugins are calibrated to hardware sweet spots.** UAD, Decapitator, Kramer, API-2500 expect ≈ −18 dBFS average (hardware "0 VU"). Feed them 10 dB hotter and their modeled nonlinearity turns to choke; 10 dB colder and they're asleep.
2. **The louder-is-better illusion.** Any un-level-matched A/B is rigged — louder always wins. Unity through every device is what keeps your ears honest on every knob.

## The house convention (from the working chains)
- **Meter sandwich:** [[Utility]] (gain in) + [[mvMeter2nogpu]] (VU) at the chain head · Utility (trim) + meter at the tail · extra meters between sections of long chains ([[Swiss Army Meter]] alternates).
- **Target ~0 VU** (≈ −18 dBFS RMS) into everything. Peaky sources (drums): *average* 0 with peaks flying above. Background elements: looser is fine.
- **Unity out of every device:** set each plugin's output so bypassing it changes nothing but the effect. The 1176's input/output pair, Inflator's output fader, Kramer's linked record/playback all exist for this.
- **Clip-gain first:** pull raw recordings to ~0 VU average *by hand* (per phrase for vocals) before any plugin — the compressor should meet a signal that's already musically level (see [[1 Main Vocal Track|Main Vocal Track]] Step 0.5).

## Downstream
- Groups/buses also want ~0 VU at their heads — if every track obeys, sums land close automatically.
- Mix into the master with peaks ≈ −8 to −6 dBFS before the loudness stages (the Disclosure habit — headroom to "mix into the limiter").
- The fader is for *balance*; the Utility gain is for *calibration*. Don't fix calibration with faders.

## From videos

- **Record vocals within a −18 dB to −3 dB range, chosen by INTENT — not just "hot enough."** Nearer −18 dB gives a cleaner, more transparent signal; nearer −3 dB (via higher preamp gain) captures more preamp coloration/character. Below this range, any digital gain added later amplifies the noise floor along with the signal. Once recorded, ride CLIP GAIN on specific loud/quiet sections BEFORE reaching for a compressor — an unevened performance hits a compressor harder on its loud moments, making the compression more audible than necessary; manually balancing with clip gain first (using the waveform display) lets the compressor do less work and stay more transparent. → [[Vocal Mic Technique]], [[Compression]] — Sage Audio, [How to Process Vocals Like a Pro](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=I06KKVnCAVU)
- **Always start a mix with the drums, regardless of genre or whether the song even has drums.** "Now when I start a mix, being a drummer, I always start with the drums. It could be a ballad, it could be a song without drums, and I'll still start in the drums." A personal, stated-as-fixed entry point rather than a per-song decision — the same instinct as Mosty's "always start with the bass" habit on [[Bass Design]] (there, because bass is what "moves the song" in reggaeton), each engineer anchoring the build-up on the element their own background makes fastest/easiest to judge. → [[Bass Design]] — Mix with the Masters (Manny Marroquin), [[Mix with the Masters 2012 - Manny Marroquin]]
- **Mix-bus fader "juggling": push all channel faders up together to find the balance, routinely end up collectively too hot, then select every fader at once on an automated desk and pull them down together, rather than re-balancing tracks individually.** Nick Launay: "my mixing process is a bit of a juggling thing — push the faders up, play around with them a bit, and very often I will end up with everything being too loud, but on all automated desks we can just select all the faders and bring them all down a bit so it's not overloading the bus." Owned-lane equivalent: select every track's fader in Ableton (or group them) and trim as a block rather than nudging one at a time. → [[Session Templates]] — Mix with the Masters (Nick Launay), [[Mixing Nick Cave & The bad seeds - Nick Launay]]
- **Do a track's gain trim with the plugin's own input/output control, not the fader — specifically so the fader position stays a reliable visual reference.** Tom Lord-Alge on a guitar track: "you can see I do a little bit of gain trim — remember, I let the plugin do the work so that my fader doesn't have to, so in case I bump it, I know where it is." A distinct rationale from the usual "unity gain in/out of every device" convention above — this is about *which control* absorbs a trim, kept separate on purpose so the fader always tells you at a glance whether it's been bumped. → [[1 Electric Guitar Amp Track|Electric Guitar Amp Track]] — Mix with the Masters (Tom Lord-Alge), [[Bass and Guitar sound with Tom Lord-Alge]]
- **Drums-first gain structure, verified by exactly where the drum bus hits its compressor — a concrete numeric checkpoint, not a vibe check.** Route the drum bus through an analog-console-emulation stage into a mastering-style bus compressor and set gain so it **hits the compressor at roughly −1 dB** (a little over/under is fine) — since drums are deliberately the loudest element in the record, that reading confirms the whole drum bus is properly gain-staged, which in turn guarantees headroom for everything layered in after. Check the master/2-bus next: sweet spot **−5 to −6 dB** (roughly −5, −4 to −6 acceptable), leaving ~5 dB of headroom before 0 so vocals and instruments can be added without clipping risk. → [[Master Chain]] — Jaycen Joshua (Mix with the Masters), [Mixing Summits with Jaycen Joshua](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L6xx7DWKzng)
- **Set outboard mic preamps as hot as possible without clipping — "as loud as they can get without hitting the red" — and call the result an audibly better upgrade over interface-only gain, not just a habit.** "You want to hit your recording software, the computer, with as much signal as possible. That's going to give you a nice loud full sound that's really going to sit in the track the best." Stated with a direct comparison: "I've done it without the mic preamps and there's a noticeable difference." Re-tune per guitar/amp-volume combination rather than a set-once value. → [[1 Electric Guitar Amp Track|Electric Guitar Amp Track]] — Jack Ruch, [My Home Recording Setup for Guitar](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KYTGFneEJ2A)
- **A wide performance dynamic range as a HARD REQUIREMENT for two separate downstream effects, not just good mixing hygiene.** Called out as "hugely important" on a Mk.gee-inspired guitar/bass chain: some notes need to be played really quiet, some really loud, because (1) a driven preamp is set to crunch only the loudest notes, and (2) a noise-gated distorted reverb send is threshold-set to trigger only on the loudest notes. A flat, over-compressed performance defeats both effects simultaneously — the dynamic range has to survive from the performance all the way through the chain, not get flattened before it reaches these gates/thresholds. → [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[Gate]], [[Mk.gee Baritone Guitar Chain]] — loner, [the secrets behind Mk.gee's HYPNOTIC production!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=969p5Q4JSZU)
- **"Play light, then push it hard" — playing/programming SOFT first for tone, boosting level second, as two separate deliberate moves.** Mk.gee's own description of his drum sound: "just saturation... play light and then push it hard." A softly-played hit has a genuinely different tone from a hard one, not just lower volume — so play light to get the tone, then boost gain/level afterward to get the punch. Only audible on a velocity-sensitive multi-sampled instrument, since a static one-shot's tone doesn't change with velocity. → [[Kontakt 7]], [[Saturation and Distortion]] — loner, [the secrets behind Mk.gee's HYPNOTIC production!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=969p5Q4JSZU)
- **Size headroom in dB true peak (dBTP), not RMS — the two answer different questions.** dBTP accounts for inter-sample peaks (the reconstructed waveform's amplitude *between* digital samples, invisible to a plain sample-peak meter) so it's the accurate read on how much headroom actually exists; RMS measures average loudness instead, reads much lower than dBTP, and doesn't represent peaking at all — useful for judging dynamic range against the peak, not for sizing headroom. Ties into this note's own VU/peak meter-sandwich convention above: watch both, but size headroom decisions off the peak (true-peak) reading specifically. → [[Mix Handoff]], [[Clipping and Loudness]] — Sage Audio, [What is Headroom for Mastering?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mYtL4V01Unw)
- **Keep 3–6 dB of headroom while mastering, peaks landing between −3 and −6 dBFS — with the arithmetic reason spelled out, not just the number.** Worked example: start with only 1 dB of headroom and a saturator/EQ stage that adds 2 dB of gain and the signal clips; start with 6 dB of headroom and the same 2 dB gain still leaves 4 dB in hand, room to keep processing without introducing clipping distortion. A wider range than [[Master Chain]]'s own "~5 dB of clip-gain headroom" Session Setup target from a different Sage Audio source — same house habit, stated as a range with the underlying math rather than a single fixed number. → [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [How to Gain Stage Your Master](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WW4bnNi4DAA)
- **Most plugins run 64-bit internal processing, so a signal can swing above 0 dB *inside* the plugin without clipping — it only clips if it's still above 0 dB at the plugin's actual output.** That headroom cushion doesn't excuse sloppy gain staging: get in the habit of watching both input and output meters on every mastering plugin (favor plugins that show both, reinforcing this note's own meter-sandwich convention above) so an over-hot handoff to the next stage gets caught immediately, rather than discovered several stages later as accumulated clipping. → [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [How to Gain Stage Your Master](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WW4bnNi4DAA)
- **Level everything with NO EQ first, add EQ only once the raw balance is right.** Rule of thumb: if two sounds already sound good level-wise with zero EQ, adding EQ afterward makes them sound great — skipping straight to EQ before levels are settled undermines the whole pass. → [[EQ]] — Jay Eskar (Sol State), [Get UNSTUCK Using A "Song Theme"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HJSK_EIGNP8)
- **Plan bus levels by number before mixing, so the master bus lands with predictable headroom.** A concrete per-bus target set: drum bus ≈ -8 dB, vocals ≈ -8 dB, bass ≈ -10 dB, pads/synths ≈ -10 dB, summing to roughly -6 to -4 dB going into the master compressor/limiter — a numbers-first habit that lets the master input level be predicted before actually mixing into it, instead of pushing buses up "until it feels loud enough." → [[Master Chain]] — Disclosure, [Loudness The SMART Way](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xfqCnOYjRNY)
- **Program every new MIDI part at maximum velocity, then only ever reduce it from there** — a simple session-wide discipline: since a part will only ever get quieter relative to its max, starting at 127 removes the need to track/match previous parts' relative velocities as new ones get added — one less thing to think about across a whole session. → [[House Music Tutorial in Studio One in 15 Minutes - Weekly Challenge No. 04|House Music Tutorial in Studio One in 15 Minutes]] — Max Konyi, [House Music Tutorial in Studio One in 15 Minutes | Weekly Challenge #04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CIO3Z_a0gaA)
- **Do the first volume-balance pass at low listening volume, since loud monitoring distorts perceived frequency balance** — turning monitors up changes how the ear perceives balance between elements: something that reads fine loud can be genuinely wrong quiet, and vice versa. Do the initial balance pass at a fairly low volume, then periodically turn up and back down to spot-check that the balance still holds at louder levels — don't build the balance at a loud, flattering monitoring level in the first place. → [[Referencing and Translation]] (the same low-volume-first principle applied at final QA, not just the first pass) — Max Konyi, [Creating a Solid First Mix (Specific Process)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dXBNtKHt2r0)
- **Balance in strict order of importance against one fixed reference point, not by eyeballing every fader** — before touching a fader, mentally rank every element in the song by importance (a lead melody/vocal outranks supporting parts), then work outward one element at a time, always checking the newest addition against the single most-important element rather than against the whole mix at once. Pick one instrument as a fixed reference point (drums, here) that the ranking is built around, and re-check every new element against it. A variant of the same "primary element first" idea already documented above, applied with an explicit importance-ranking + fixed-anchor discipline rather than a strict −∞-up rebuild. — Max Konyi, [Creating a Solid First Mix (Specific Process)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dXBNtKHt2r0)
- **A track with volume automation ignores fader moves — insert a gain-only device instead of fighting the fader** — moving a track's fader has no lasting effect once that track already carries volume automation: on playback, the automation snaps the level right back to whatever it says, because the automation curve (not the fader position) drives the track's level. To adjust the overall level of an already-automated track without erasing the automation, insert a gain-only utility device ahead of the fader instead — the Ableton stock equivalent of Studio One's "Mix Tool" is [[Utility]]'s Gain knob — and use that as the new volume control, leaving the drawn curve untouched underneath it. → [[Automation]], [[Utility]] — Max Konyi, [Creating a Solid First Mix (Specific Process)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dXBNtKHt2r0)
- **Prep every track before bouncing to a mixdown project — strip what's inessential, keep what defines the sound, extend the render tail** — before exporting individual audio stems, comb through each track's effects and only leave what's actually integral to the character of the sound (a camel-crusher distortion that gave a congas part its whole identity stayed on; unfinished EQ/reverb and a sidechain compressor still being tuned got turned off so they can be rebuilt properly in the mixdown project instead of getting baked into the audio); also strip unnecessary automation and any unused tracks. For a drum rack, right-click each drum sound and Extract Chains to split it onto its own track — with its own MIDI and any audio effects on that hit — before bouncing, rather than exporting the whole rack as one baked-together drum track. When exporting, set the render length a little past the end of the track to catch reverb/delay tails, and leave Normalize on even though it wipes out the relative volume balance — that forces a second, more careful gain-staging pass and surfaces anything you'd buried in the mix. Leave dither off (save it for the final master only) and convert stereo content to mono where appropriate. Once bounced, drag every file into a blank Live set (hold Cmd/Ctrl so each gets its own track) and organize before touching a single fader: color-code by instrument group, then within each group order the elements top-to-bottom by when they occur in the track — a "downward staircase" layout that makes the next step, rebuilding relative volumes, much faster. → [[Committing and Printing]] — You Suck at Producing, [You Suck at Mixing No. 1: Preparing Your Track for a Mixdown](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YP1dSPX8doc)
- **Rebuild relative volumes from silence, working primary-instrument-first and group-by-group — trust ears over eyes** — select every track and pull all faders to −∞, then bring the most primary element of the whole song up first (usually the kick, ~−12 dB is a safe non-clipping starting point — the exact number doesn't matter much since everything else gets set relative to it, not to an absolute target); within that same instrument group, bring up secondary and tertiary elements next (a second/layered snare, then hi-hats) before moving on to the next group's primary element. While dialing any fader in, look away from the screen and judge purely by what's coming through the speakers, and loop a specific section of the track instead of playing from the top so elements don't drop in and out mid-adjustment. When a problem surfaces (a bass that clearly needs EQ/compression), don't stop to fix it — note it for later and keep moving; this pass is about relative levels only. Session View's faders can be dragged out taller than the Arrangement-view mixer for finer moves. — You Suck at Producing, [You Suck at Mixing No. 2: Relative Volumes and Gainstaging](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Pmn_ZJ1pdfg)
- **Build the mix up from silence, in priority order** — select every track, pull all faders to −∞, then bring elements in one at a time starting with the kick (~−12 dB as a first guess), then the next-most-important (clap), then hi-hats/loops — go by ear for anything without an obvious reference point. Related: exporting stems with Normalize on maximizes each stem's individual level (makes clicks/pops easier to spot) but destroys the mix's relative volumes in the process, so a "bounce to a fresh project" mixdown always means rebuilding levels from scratch on the other side, not carrying them over. — You Suck at Producing, [You Suck at Mixing: The Ultimate Guide](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YSR6P2G5GvY)
- **Drums own the limiter — turn everything ELSE down** — "the number one thing" wrong when drums don't come through: pads/keys/808s too loud. Drums should be the loudest elements hitting the master limiter; everything else sits secondary. Mix by pulling the rest down, not pushing drums up — Lophiile, [Make Your Drums Slap](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v4ZhwlhF5yE)

- **Select-all, push to just under the red** — raise every fader together until the master almost clips, then back off: at full level the balance problems announce themselves (snare too loud, hat louder than you thought). Quiet mixes hide bad balance — Kenny Beats, [Beat Mix & Creation Process](https://youtu.be/FC__hbvnETY)
- **Never send/record over a limited beat** — nothing on the master of a beat you'll track vocals on (or send to artists): a smashed 2-track leaves no room, vocals fight the snare, artists push or pass. Loudness comes from staging hot early in the chain, not a master limiter — Kenny Beats, [Vocal Template](https://youtu.be/X_iDENABLmk)
- **Turn OFF the "Analog"/"Lo-Fi" buttons Waves ships enabled** — "it's just noise"; audition-off any character switch a plugin ships on before trusting it — Kenny Beats, [Vocal Template](https://youtu.be/X_iDENABLmk)
General tips pulled from production breakdowns (via `/music-enrich`), newest-first.

- **Gain-stage *from the drums*: get them out at ~−8 dB (lightly limited), then build everything around that** — bass, pads, and vocals fall into place and you reach the master with healthy headroom (keep the master fader at 0, peaks ~−8 to −10). Also: **sit where the bass is** — pull the desk to the center of the room and walk around to find the neutral spot before treating. → [[Referencing and Translation]] — Disclosure, [Ultimatum](https://youtu.be/uWe3UvI4KlU)
- **Every fader should sit near 0** — if you're riding faders way up or down, your gain staging is wrong; set levels upstream (in the box + at the input trims) so faders only need tiny moves. On a hybrid analog rig, **back off the interface output and gain up on the desk** so nothing soft-distorts on the way into the monitors. → [[Master Chain]] — Disclosure, [SSL SIX Mixing Al Green](https://youtu.be/ZCNX8U6oZmE)
- **Match makeup gain honestly** — use each plugin's I/O gain to hit the same level after processing; too much makeup "sounds awesome," but level-matched it may be peaking higher on transients while sounding quieter — Disclosure, [Looking For Love](https://youtu.be/NlJt0pCptGk)
- **Prefer auto-gain plugins** ([[Decapitator]] auto, [[Saturn 2]], [[Pro-L 2]] 1-for-1) so you judge the *processing*, not the level jump; a wet/dry knob helps A/B — Disclosure, [Looking For Love](https://youtu.be/NlJt0pCptGk)
- **Target bus levels for headroom** — drum bus ~−8, bass ~−10, rest to taste; a drum bus hitting 0 is already trouble when elements sum; keep a big lead vocal clean or the mix pumps distortion into it — Disclosure, [Looking For Love](https://youtu.be/NlJt0pCptGk)
