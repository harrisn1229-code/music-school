# Pedal Point

A pedal point (pedal tone) is a sustained or repeated note — almost always the tonic, sometimes the dominant — held underneath a moving chord progression above it. The name comes from pipe organ playing: the pedalboard at the performer's feet plays sustained low bass notes while the hands play changing chords on the manuals, so "keeping a note going with the pedal" while harmony moves above it became "pedal point."

## Why it works

Holding one bass note constant while the chords above it change reframes every one of those chords as a slash chord over that fixed bass — which can turn an otherwise plain progression into something much richer without changing a single note in the upper voicings. Example: a G triad played over a held C bass isn't heard as "G over C," it's heard as **C major 9 (missing the 3rd)** — the pedal note recontextualizes the chord above it into an extended harmony the player never had to consciously voice.

## Where the pedal sits

- **Tonic pedal (most common)**: the tonic note held throughout, chords of the key moving above it. "Jump" (Van Halen) — the intro is built entirely on a held C bass under G/C, C, F/C, G/C, and a few suspended chords; reharmonizing the bass to follow each chord root instead "completely loses the character" of the riff. "Eye of the Tiger" (Survivor) — verse 1 pedals the tonic under moving chords, creating sustained tension; verse 2 switches the bass to follow each chord root instead, and that switch itself reads as a release — the song was "being held in place" and then let go.
- **Dominant pedal**: same idea, built on the 5th degree instead of the tonic — creates a different, more actively unresolved kind of tension since the pedal note itself wants to resolve.
- **Inverted pedal**: the held note sits in a **treble/upper voice** instead of the bass — same device, different register. "Eye of the Tiger" actually layers two pedal points at once: the tonic-pedal bass described above, plus an electric guitar chugging on the same tonic note up in the treble range simultaneously — the treble instance is the inverted pedal.

## Film-score use

John Williams uses pedal point to marry chords that wouldn't otherwise relate — in his Indiana Jones score, a sequence of chords that would sound unconnected on their own are all played over one consistent pedal tone, which "forcibly marries them all together." Useful technique for underscore/cue writing: a static pedal note lets you move through harmonically distant chords without the progression sounding random, because the constant bass note keeps supplying a shared reference point.
