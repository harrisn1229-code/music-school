# Zach Scholz - This One Knob Completely Changed My Music

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OeHzepmklOo)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OeHzepmklOo)

## Synopsis

Zach Scholz frames the pitch/varispeed knob on his Tascam 34 reel-to-reel as one of the most emotional tricks in music — not just a transport-speed control but a creative and emotional tool he leans on deliberately across mixing and mastering, on the idea that small, tape-style tempo/pitch shifts change how a track feels even when nothing about the notes or arrangement has changed. He grounds it historically with two examples: Tears for Fears' "Everybody Wants to Rule the World," tracked at a slower BPM and then sped up on tape by about 4% "to give a little bit of life to the track," and his own channel's first video, a recreation of Led Zeppelin III's "That's the Way," where he recorded the acoustic guitars fast and then slowed the recording down (digitally, standing in for what a tape machine would have done) to get the same lift. The video closes by testing the trick directly against its digital equivalent — re-recording a previously-mastered Fleetwood Mac track back through the Tascam and trying to match, on tape, a pitch/tempo shift he'd originally done in the box.

## Techniques

- **Core mechanism — tape speed moves tempo and pitch together, in either direction.** Slow the tape down and tempo + pitch drop together, which softens transients; speed it up and tempo + pitch rise together, which "tightens the groove"; either way the harmonics also shift — exactly what Nate's own [[Varispeed 3.0]] recreates in the box, via its central Speed knob linking pitch+time and a Tempo_on toggle to lock the shift to project tempo. → [[Varispeed 3.0]], [[Pitch and Formant]]
- **Historical practical use: slow the tape to make a hard vocal part easier to sing.** A vocal line that was difficult to hit could be tracked with the tape slowed down (the singer performs at the lower, easier pitch/speed), then the tape is brought back to normal speed afterward so the voice pitches back up with it — turning a hard take into an easy one.
- **Keep the move subtle — a few percent, not a speed-halving.** Real varispeed use isn't dramatic: dropping from 15 in/s to 7½ in/s would cut the speed in half and drop the source a full octave; the actual adjustment is usually just a few percent, and his Tascam 34 tops out at 12% — even that small a shift is enough to change how a track feels emotionally.
- **Cited example — Tears for Fears, "Everybody Wants to Rule the World":** tracked at a slower BPM, then the tape was sped up about 4% afterward "to give a little bit of life to the track."
- **His own example — recreating Led Zeppelin III's "That's the Way":** recorded the acoustic guitars at a faster BPM, then slowed the recording down (digitally, in that earlier video — not on an actual tape machine) to simulate the tempo/pitch drop a tape deck would have produced back in the day.
- **Mixing-stage use: bounce, then speed-shift for fresh ears on a fatigued mix.** At the point in a session where his ears are fatigued and he feels indifferent toward the track — even mid-mix, unfinished — he bounces it into a new session and speeds it up or slows it down just to hear it differently; it plays back like hearing the mix for the first time.
- **Slowing down specifically tends to surface buried melodic ideas.** He most often runs this trick by slowing the track down — possibly because it opens more perceived space between elements — and it regularly reveals melodies/ideas he couldn't hear after working the track too long; he notes them, goes back into the real mix, and adds them, sometimes generating entire new sections this way.
- **Also a mastering-stage decision, not just a mixing trick.** Right before finishing a track he'll try shifting its overall speed/pitch, and sometimes a few BPM slower or faster at the resulting pitch just works better than the original and he keeps it — the same move tape engineers were effectively making when they'd nudge tape speed and let the song "sit" wherever that landed.
- **Tape-vs-computer varispeed comparison — left open to the audience, with one firm technical caveat.** He re-recorded a previously-mastered Fleetwood Mac mix (slowed a few BPM digitally the first time) back through the Tascam, tried to match that same shift by ear with the pitch knob — by his own account it was tough to nail exactly — then asked viewers in the comments which version they preferred rather than declaring a winner himself; his one firm takeaway is that slowing something down (more than speeding it up) is where artifacts are most likely to show up, especially with digital time-stretching, which is specifically why he chose a slow-down for the test.
- **General reel-to-reel mechanics apply beyond this specific Tascam.** The core explanation — tempo and pitch locked together, subtle percentage-level moves, printing through the machine and back into the DAW — isn't Tascam-34-specific; the same tape mechanics are available on Nate's own [[Sony TC-630]] if it's run at an adjustable speed for varispeed coloring rather than only its usual fixed-speed tape-warmth pass. → [[Sony TC-630]]
