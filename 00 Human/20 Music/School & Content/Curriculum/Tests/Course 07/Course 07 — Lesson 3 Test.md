> **Q1.** **The ♭3, ♭5 and ♭7**
> Played against a major or dominant background — that contrast is what makes them blue.
>
> **Q2.** A blues scale: A C D E♭ E G
> *Also accepted:* A C D Eb E G
>
> **Q3.** **Minor pentatonic plus the ♭5**
> One added note to a five-note scale.
>
> **Q4.** **As a passing tone — never as a resting note**
> Landing on it and staying there sounds wrong; moving through it is the whole point.
>
> **Q5.** **The friction is the sound · blues isn't functional major/minor · the note is a region, not a pitch**
> Three independent reasons, and each would be sufficient on its own.
>
> **Q6.** **They bend the ♭3 up toward the ♮3 and sit between them**
> The blues has microtonality built in, and notation can't represent where the note actually lands.
>
> **Q7.** **The natural 4th — and no, the scale does not avoid it**
> In A that's the D in A C D E G, a semitone above the chord's C♯. Minor pentatonic contains it. What the scale does instead is fill three of its five slots with the chord's own tones — root, 5th and ♭7 — so the ♭3 is the only deliberate clash in it, and the 4th is passed through rather than landed on, the same way the ♭5 is. **Major** pentatonic (A B C♯ E F♯) is the scale that leaves the 4th out.
>
> **Q8.** **The scale is imposed over the chords rather than derived from them**
> *Rubric (5):* says functional harmony derives usable scales from the key and chord (2) · says blues does the reverse, laying one scale over all three chords (2) · connects it to the dominant-7th tonic from Lesson 1 (1)
> In functional harmony the chord and key generate the scale — you look at the chord, work out what key it's in, and the available notes follow. Blues inverts that. One scale sits over the entire form regardless of which of the three chords is sounding, and the chords are all dominant 7ths that don't resolve to each other in the first place. So a clash between the scale and a chord tone isn't a derivation error, because the scale was never derived from that chord. It's the same fact as the dominant-7th tonic in Lesson 1, seen from the melodic side.
>
> **Q9.** **Mixolydian is smooth and inside; the blues scale grinds — the ♭3 and ♭5 do it**
> *Rubric (5):* says A Mixolydian contains all four chord tones of A7, so almost everything in it agrees (2) · says the blues scale swaps the major 3rd for a ♭3 that clashes with the chord's 3rd (2) · names the ♭5 as the other colour (1)
> A Mixolydian (A B C♯ D E F♯ G) carries all four chord tones of A7, so it sounds correct and slightly bland — almost everything agrees. The blues scale (A C D E♭ E G) swaps the major 3rd for the ♭3, which sits a semitone under the chord's C♯ and produces the characteristic grind, and adds the ♭5 as a passing colour. What it drops is Mixolydian's 2nd (B) and 6th (F♯) along with that major 3rd, which is why it sounds narrower and more vocal. Both scales keep the natural 4th (D) — the note that genuinely fights the chord's 3rd — and blues passes through it rather than resting on it. The trade is agreeableness for friction, and the friction is what reads as blues.
