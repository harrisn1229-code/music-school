# Layering Rhythm Guitar Parts with Triads

Part of [[Guitar Positions]], the arranging-side counterpart to [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]] (which applies the same triad-shape/position-relocation idea to soloing). This page is about **stacking multiple rhythm-guitar parts on the same chord progression** without them turning into mud — the core move is reusing one small set of closed-position triad shapes across a handful of string groupings and neck positions, then separating the resulting layers by **tone/pickup selection**, not by writing genuinely different material for each layer.

## The core idea: one triad vocabulary, four string groupings, three positions

A four-chord progression — worked in **A major – E major – F♯ minor – D major** (I–V–vi–IV in A) — gets played as **three different guitar parts**, and all three are built from the *same* triad shapes, just relocated:

| Part | String group | Position | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Bed | D, G, B strings | One position, jumps to a second higher position for the verse | Arpeggiated harmonic floor |
| 2 — Backbeat | Top three strings | Two positions (a "close-by" pair), used across sections for a flavor change | R&B accent, rhythmic punctuation |
| 3 — Melody | Top three / top two strings | Built from Parts 1–2's own shapes plus two double-stop devices | Melodic double-stop line |

The stated general lesson: the same handful of chords lives in **four different string groupings** and **three different neck positions**, and once those shapes are under your fingers you can freely recombine them — this is the arranging equivalent of [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]]'s "one triad, three zones" soloing method.

## Why triads specifically: they're small on purpose

Closed triad shapes (root-3rd-5th, no doubled notes, no full six-string chords) are deliberately lean — "these little morsels... don't take up too much space, don't fill up too much sound." That leanness is *why* three of them can stack on top of each other in a mix without collapsing into noise — the same "which notes to leave out" economy [[Chord Voicings]] argues for on the jazz-comping side, applied here for a production reason (headroom to layer) rather than a voice-leading one.

## Voicing sources for each part

- **Part 1 (bed):** plain closed triads on the D-G-B string set, arpeggiated. 🎬 *From video:* a companion lesson on the same channel maps this exact bed layer in full over the same I-V-vi-IV-in-A progression (A-E-F♯m-D): on the D, G, B strings, each of the three usable neck positions uses **one consistent triad-inversion shape family carried across all four chords** — position 1 = 5th-root-3rd, position 2 = root-3rd-5th, position 3 = 3rd-5th-root — which is *why* the four chords fall right next to each other in any given position (learn one hand-shape per position, not four). Glue technique for turning the bare triads into a moving part: since all four chords are diatonic to A major, connect any two adjacent triads with a short 2-4 note passing-tone run pulled from the A-major-scale fingering that sits in that same position — no separate lick vocabulary needed per chord change. Practice order: drill one position's four triads bare, then again with scale-tone fills between changes, before moving to the next position; only string positions together once each is solid alone. Full position-by-position voicings and the drill sequence → [[Learn These Simple Triads to Improve Your Rhythm Guitar Skills!]] — Jack Ruch, [Learn These Simple Triads to Improve Your Rhythm Guitar Skills!](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oZWZ81aHoQU)
- **Part 2 (backbeat accents), all derived from full chord shapes you already know, sliced to the top three strings:**
  - A major = an open D-major-chord shape moved up the neck, root on the **10th fret of the B string**.
  - E major = the top three strings of the standard E-shape bar chord.
  - F♯ minor = the top three strings of the F♯m bar chord.
  - D major = the top three strings of the D-shape bar chord.
  - The trick generalizes: instead of inventing new triad grips, take the **top slice of a bar chord you already play**.
- **Part 3 (melody), built from the same shapes plus two double-stop devices off a root-3rd-5th triad:**
  1. **Add the 6th above the 5th** — from a held triad, move the 5th up to the 6th/13th (worked as **fret 5 → fret 7 on the B string**) — a melodic extension layered on top of the triad, not a new shape.
  2. **Drop the 3rd to the 9th** — from the same root-3rd-5th grip, drop the 3rd **down a whole step** to the 9th (2nd) — a contrasting color move off the identical hand position. Both are named directly as common R&B double-stop devices in this style (cited lineage: **Curtis Mayfield, Jimi Hendrix**).
  - A leaner top-two-strings variation reuses the same triads with **hammer-ons/slides inside the shape itself** (e.g. hammering within the A-major triad's top two notes) rather than fretting a new pitch — one more textural pass through the same four chords.

## The separation move: pickup/tone, not different material

The part that makes this a genuine **production/arranging** technique rather than just a guitar lesson: each of the three layers gets a **deliberately different pickup + effect setting**, stated explicitly as the reason each part gets "its own sonic space":

- **Part 1 (bed):** middle pickup position (both pickups blended) + a light **tremolo** from the pedalboard — suits the arpeggiated feel.
- **Part 2 (backbeat):** switch to the **bridge pickup**, tremolo off — brighter/more percussive for accents.
- **Part 3 (melody):** switch to the **neck pickup** — warmer/rounder for the lead-ish double-stop line.

This is the guitar-specific version of the same rule the [[Sound Palette]] states generally — give each simultaneous occupant its own space rather than stacking identical timbres and fixing the collision with EQ afterward ([[Masking and Carving]] is the repair shop for when this isn't done up front). Here the separation tool is pickup selection + a modulation effect, decided **before** tracking, not carved in afterward.
