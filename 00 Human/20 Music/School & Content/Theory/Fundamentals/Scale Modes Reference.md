# Scale Modes Reference

Mode descriptions use the "anchor + alteration" format: each mode is named by the familiar diatonic mode it most resembles, plus what's different. This matches the logic of the major/minor chart — Natural Minor is just Major starting on the 6th degree, so the mode order inherits naturally. These non-diatonic scales don't have that shortcut, so each degree is described independently.

This page is the mode-by-mode **catalog** — looked up, not derived. For the reusable *technique* behind it (deriving any mode's formula by hand, finding its parent scale by chart, building a polychord substitute for a big chord), see [[Mode Derivation and Polychords]].

**Chord symbol key:** Δ = major 7th · mΔ = minor-major 7th · m7 = minor 7th · ø = half-diminished · ° = full diminished · 7 = dominant 7th

## Melodic Minor

| Degree | Chord | Mode |
|--------|-------|------|
| i | mΔ | Aeolian nat.6, nat.7 |
| ii | m7 | Dorian b2 |
| bIII | Δ#5 | Ionian #5 |
| IV | 7 | Lydian b7 |
| V | 7 | Mixolydian b6 |
| vi | ø | Locrian nat.2 |
| VII | 7 | Locrian b4, bb7 |

> Modes don't cycle in the same order as the diatonic modes — each degree must be worked out independently.
> The VII is uppercase (dominant 7th) even though it's the 7th degree — the actual intervals stack to a major 3rd above the chord root.

## Whole Tone / Diminished

These are symmetrical scales — every rotation is the same scale transposed, so they don't have "modes" in the traditional sense.

**Whole Tone** (2 unique transpositions — all dominant):

| Every degree | 7#5 | Lydian Dominant #5 |

**Diminished** (alternating half/whole, 3 unique transpositions):

| Odd degrees | ° | Locrian b4, bb7 |
| Even degrees | 7b9 | Phrygian nat.3, b5 |

## Major Pentatonic Modes

The 5 modes of Major Pentatonic — degrees skip IV and VII since pentatonic omits those:

| Degree | Scale Tones | Mode |
|--------|-------------|------|
| I | 1, 2, 3, 5, 6 | Ionian (no 4, no 7) |
| II | 1, 2, 4, 5, b7 | Dorian (no b3, no 6) |
| III | 1, 2, 4, b6, b7 | Phrygian (no b3, no b5) |
| V | 1, 2, 3, 5, b7 | Mixolydian (no 4, no 6) |
| VI | 1, b3, 4, 5, b7 | Aeolian (no 2, no b6) |

> The VI mode is the Minor Pentatonic — same relationship as Aeolian being the relative minor of Ionian.

## From videos

- **Whole tone scale construction and its melodic (not harmonic) application, plus why there are only two of them.** Symmetrical T-T-T-T-T-T construction means every note carries equal weight — no leading-tone pull, hence the "dreamy, rootless" sound — and means there are only **two unique whole tone scales** total (any starting note just restates one of the two collections). Harmonizing it in thirds only yields augmented and major-♭5 triads, so it's used almost exclusively as a brief melodic color, not a harmonic foundation for a whole passage. **Practical match: over an altered dominant chord** (e.g. F7♯5/"Falt"), a whole tone scale from the same root shares its note content directly — demonstrated on Bacharach's "Alfie" and Stevie Wonder's "You Are the Sunshine of My Life" (climb harmonized in parallel thirds = two whole tone scales moving together). Guitar examples: Robert Fripp's "Fracture" (D♭ whole tone, 5/8 eighth-note run), David Gilmour's brief fragment in "Dogs." Historical: Debussy's *Voiles* is built almost entirely from it; Mozart used the same scale in "A Musical Joke" a century earlier specifically to sound like wrong notes. → [[Altered Chords]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use the Whole Tone scale]]
- **Lydian ♭2 and harmonic major used deliberately for "unsettling" video-game area music, each named and mechanism-explained.** **Lydian ♭2** (raised 4th + flattened 2nd together, an unusually unnatural combination) — Pokémon's Lavender Town theme, cited as the actual (non-supernatural) explanation for the "Lavender Town Syndrome" urban myth. **Harmonic major** (major scale, lowered 6th degree) — Pokémon's Mt. Moon theme, the lowered-6th motif restated at a shifted pitch center followed by a chromatic descending line ("spiraling"); composer Junichi Masuda names Stravinsky and Shostakovich as the direct influence behind this more chromatic material. → David Bennett Music Theory, [How Pokemon 8-bit music was inspired by classical music](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WyvfKFVRqVs)
- **Why harmonic minor exists — the functional-harmony rationale, not just the note formula — plus its "exotic" byproduct and real-song examples (a direct setup for the melodic-minor entry below, which fixes the problem THIS scale creates).** Only the 7th degree is raised vs. natural minor, specifically to turn the diatonic v (minor) into a proper V (major) — restoring a real perfect cadence (V→i) in the minor key, which natural minor can't produce on its own. Byproduct: raising the 7th widens the 6th-7th gap to a minor 3rd/augmented 2nd, giving the upper scale an "exotic, Arabic-like" sound. Worked functional-harmony example: "bury a friend" (Billy Eilish, G harmonic minor) — i(tonic, no tension)→iv(subdominant, some tension)→V(dominant, maximum tension)→i, the cadence only possible because of the raised 7th; same device reused in "bad guy," "idontwannabeyouanymore," "when the party's over," "my future." **Blending rather than committing**: "Girl" (Beatles) alternates harmonic and natural minor within one song rather than picking one. Historical association: reads as "classical/Baroque" — a common pre-Romantic default (Bach's Toccata and Fugue in D minor). → [[Cadences]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[What is the Harmonic Minor scale?]]
- **The ascending/descending melodic-minor convention explained from first principles — why it exists, not just what it is — plus real-song examples of composers actually following (or ignoring) it.** Three-minor-scale lineage: natural minor (default) → harmonic minor (raises the 7th for a strong leading-tone pull, but widens the 6th-7th gap to an awkward minor 3rd/augmented 2nd) → melodic minor (also raises the 6th ascending, closing that gap for smoother melodic motion; reverts to natural minor descending, since the leading tone only wants to resolve upward). Character: ascending form = major scale with a ♭3, giving a "whimsical, curious" minor color, less mournful than natural/harmonic minor. **Bach's own inconsistency** (cited as proof this is a convention, not a rule): his Bourrée in E minor follows it cleanly; his Fugue in A minor (WTC Book 2) follows it in one passage then abandons it in the very next. **"Yesterday" (Beatles)** — full worked example: modulates to D minor at bar 3, ascends D melodic minor, descends D natural minor, both melodically AND in the accompanying chords — likely a subconscious jazz/classical influence per McCartney's own dream-origin account of the tune. Also: Green sleeves, "Carol of the Bells," "Autumn Leaves" (A section shifts to relative minor, ascends melodic minor), "Lullaby of Birdland" (opens in melodic minor, shifts to natural minor 2 bars later), "Possibly Maybe" (Björk) — C melodic minor outro, distinct raised-6th/7th spots in the arpeggio vs. vocal. **Why full pieces rarely stay in melodic minor throughout**: chords built purely from its own note collection tend to sound "limited and odd" — real minor-key writing usually blends natural/harmonic/melodic freely rather than committing to one. → [[Harmonic Minor Modes]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use the Melodic Minor scale]]
- **Two ear-training identification cues for telling minor-scale variants apart quickly.** Harmonic minor's audible giveaway: the augmented 2nd between ♭6 and 7 (the raised leading tone) — an "Arabic"-sounding wide gap right before the top of the scale, distinct from natural minor's plain whole step there. Ascending vs. descending melodic minor, distinguished by the 3rd alone: both use the natural (major-scale) 6th and 7th, so the natural minor 3rd is the only tell separating it from a plain major scale by ear. → [[Harmonic Minor Modes]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[The Ear Training Olympics]]

Mode ♭III (Lydian augmented / this table's Ionian ♯5): essentially unused as a whole-song scale — real use is the maj7♯5 chord scale (paired with harmonic minor's Ionian ♯5, see [[Harmonic Minor Modes]]); heard in Herbie Hancock's solo on Miles Davis's *Prince of Darkness* (G♭ Lydian augmented over G♭maj7♯11). - **A practical one-note-change learning chain: natural minor → harmonic minor → Phrygian dominant → Hungarian minor.** Raise natural minor's 7th for harmonic minor; **Phrygian dominant is NOT a separate scale to learn — it's harmonic minor's own 5th mode**, same pitch collection just a different root (A harmonic minor started on its 5th degree, E, IS E Phrygian dominant), so a player who already knows A harmonic minor across the neck can solo in E Phrygian dominant over an E-rooted backing with zero new fingering, just by centering phrases on E inside the same shape. **Hungarian minor is a genuinely NEW 4th color** (not a mode-relabeling like Phrygian dominant) — raise harmonic minor's 4th degree as well (1 2 ♭3 ♯4 5 ♭6 7), described as harmonic minor's "darker, more dissonant cousin" via the added ♯4-against-♭3 tension. → [[Harmonic Minor Modes]] — BERNTH, [[3 Best Shred Scales - Use These To Sound AMAZING!]]

- **A major-scale-first build for this page's own Melodic Minor table row IV (Lydian ♭7 / Lydian dominant / "acoustic scale" / overtone scale), plus a concrete avoid-note warning.** Rather than deriving it from melodic minor, build it as major scale with two changes — raise the 4th (Lydian) AND lower the 7th (Mixolydian) — and confirm the identity via tetrachord split (C-D-E-F♯ = Lydian's whole-whole-whole tetrachord; G-A-B♭-C = a minor-scale-shaped whole-half-whole tetrachord). **Avoid-note warning:** defaulting to the natural (major) 7th over a dominant-tonic chord clashes audibly against the chord's own minor 7th — named directly as a common source of "sometimes it sounds right, sometimes it doesn't" confusion for players who haven't isolated which scale degree is the actual culprit. — BERNTH, [[The BEST Exotic Guitar Scale - Acoustic-Lydian b7 Scale Lesson]]

- **Pick Lydian dominant over a IV-chord-turned-dominant-7 by reasoning from its diatonic role, not just its chord quality.** A chord that's diatonically the IV of the key but altered into a dominant 7th (e.g. G♭7 as the IV of D♭ major) doesn't default to Mixolydian — the 4th degree of a major scale, taken as its own root, produces the Lydian mode (♯4/♯11); turn that major-quality chord into a dominant 7 and the matching scale is Lydian dominant (Mixolydian with the 4th raised).
