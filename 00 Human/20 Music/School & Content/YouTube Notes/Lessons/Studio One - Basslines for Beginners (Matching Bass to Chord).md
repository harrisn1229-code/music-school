## Synopsis

An absolute-fundamentals lesson from Max Konyi (2020, Studio One) on writing a bassline that follows a chord progression — the beginner-tier version of the same "note choice reflects the chords" idea his later [[How to Write Advanced MIDI Basslines (Part 1) - Essential Knowledge|Advanced MIDI Bass series]] (Parts 1–3, enriched 2026-08-05) builds into a full Tone/Note Choice/Groove method. Worked example: a C–Am–F–G progression, root notes only at first, then simple rhythmic and octave variations layered on top, and a note on the value of being able to play basslines live from a keyboard rather than only drawing them in. **DAW note:** recorded in **Studio One**, not Ableton — the one DAW-specific move (a keyboard shortcut to loop-select an event) doesn't carry over 1:1; see the cross-DAW note below for Ableton's equivalent workflow. Everything else (root-note matching, octave register, rhythmic variation, real-time playing) is DAW-agnostic and directly usable.

## What's taught

- **Play the chord progression in first, then write the bass to match it — root note per chord, one note per bar to start.** With a C–Am–F–G progression, the simplest possible bassline is just the root of each chord (C, A, F, G) landing once per bar in time with the chord changes — establish this before adding any rhythm or decoration. Note: it doesn't matter that the Am chord is minor when only playing the root — the *chord quality* only starts to matter once you add other chord tones. → [[Bassline Writing]] ("Roots first, by ear")
- **Register matters — too low sounds "terrible," not "cool."** Demonstrated live: the same root-note bassline dropped an octave lower than the workable register goes from fine (C) to bad (A) to outright broken/muddy (F) — low bass notes have a register floor below which they stop reading as a pitched note and just sound wrong. Rule of thumb given: if it's sounding bad/undefined like that, you're simply too low — move it up an octave rather than trying to EQ or process your way out of it.
- **Layer in rhythmic variation once the root-per-bar version works** — instead of one note per bar, subdivide (demoed: playing the first note of every chord change, then duplicating/offsetting it) so the bassline has its own rhythmic identity instead of just mirroring the chord track's rhythm.
- **Add the octave of the root as a second note, not just more roots.** Once a rhythm exists, jumping some notes up an octave (rather than only repeating the same root pitch) is presented as the natural next layer of interest — the line still reads as "root note" harmonically, but the register motion gives it shape. Direct beginner precursor to the "octaves are the default vocabulary" and "low melody / high melody split" ideas developed much further in the Advanced MIDI Bass series (Parts 1–2).
- **Know the chords you're playing before writing the bass under them.** If you don't know what chords you played (e.g. an improvised/random progression), you're guessing at the bass note too — sometimes that guess works out interestingly, but more often it lands "confusing" or merely okay when it could have been better with intent. Know the chord, then place the matching root.
- **Bass rhythm choices are provisional until the drums exist.** The rhythms chosen here were picked in isolation (no drum loop yet); the video flags that a real drum loop playing underneath would likely dictate different rhythmic choices for the bass — matches [[Bassline Writing]]'s "groove first, bass second" principle and [[Rhythm and Groove]]'s bass/kick-interlock content from the more advanced series.
- **Practice playing basslines live on a MIDI keyboard, not just drawing them in the piano roll.** Real-time keyboard performance (once you know the chords and have practiced the target notes) builds keyboard fluency that compounds — faster composing, and access to ideas you wouldn't stumble onto by clicking in notes one at a time. Explicitly not a "you must" — drawing everything in by hand is a completely valid, widely-used workflow too — but flagged as worth practicing specifically because of the speed and idea-generation payoff.

## Cross-DAW note (Studio One → Ableton)

The video uses a Studio One-specific shortcut (**Shift+P** to instantly loop-select around any selected event/clip, then **/** to toggle the loop on) to bracket the chord clip for looped writing. Ableton's closest native equivalent: select the clip in Arrangement View and either drag the loop brace to match the clip's start/end, or select the clip's full time range and use the Loop/Braces controls (View → Loop) to set the arrangement loop to that span — same outcome (a tight practice loop around the chord idea) via a different mechanism. No Ableton-specific gap here otherwise; the actual bassline-writing content is DAW-agnostic.
