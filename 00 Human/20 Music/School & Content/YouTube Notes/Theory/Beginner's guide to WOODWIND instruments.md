# Beginner's guide to WOODWIND instruments

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EleBNmzpgn4)

## Synopsis

Third installment of the orchestra crash-course series, after [[How to compose for Strings]] and [[How do Brass instruments actually work]] — and the one where Bennett opens by pointing out that woodwinds are the *diverse* section: unlike strings and brass, which each share a single sound-production mechanism, woodwinds differ significantly from each other in how they actually make sound. Covers the edge-vibration definition and the four edge types that split the family, the four orchestral player categories plus the doubling/auxiliary system, ranges, the woodwind-vs-brass dividing line, the transposing-instrument convention and why it exists, and closes with a timbre cheat sheet feeding an eight-clip guess-the-instrument quiz. Worth flagging that Bennett explicitly declines to teach *how to write* for the section — he says he has no personal experience doing it and hands that off to the sponsor's courses — so this is an orientation/identification video, not an orchestration method. Dense enough to warrant a dedicated reference note: see [[Woodwind Section Arranging]] for the full organized extraction.

## Techniques

See [[Woodwind Section Arranging]] for the complete breakdown — summary here: every woodwind sounds by splitting or vibrating blown air against an **edge**, then a pipe whose length sets the pitch (keys/holes change the effective length). Four edge types divide the family — **single reed** (clarinet, sax), **double reed** (oboe, cor anglais, bassoon), **blown-across open hole** (flute, piccolo), and **fipple** (recorder, ocarina, tin whistle). The orchestral section is four player types — flutes, oboes, clarinets, bassoons — two of each in a smaller orchestra, up to three at full size, but more instruments appear via **doubling**: a player also covers an auxiliary variant and may switch mid-piece (flute→**piccolo**, an extra octave at the top; oboe→**cor anglais**, effectively an alto oboe, lower and richer; bassoon→**contrabassoon**, an extra octave at the bottom), worked through Bartók's *Dance Suite* where a bassoonist switches to contrabassoon for the second movement. Naming trap: **cor anglais** = "English horn" in French and is a woodwind; the **French horn** is named in English and is brass. **Range: the bottom note is fixed** by the pipework, **the top is flexible** — the higher you push the less usable the tone, so the ceiling is practical rather than hard. **Woodwind vs. brass** comes down to what vibrates: brass players buzz their *lips*, woodwind players blow breath over a *sharp edge* — which is why the **saxophone is a woodwind despite being brass-bodied**, the family being defined by sound production, not material. "Woodwind" itself is a historical hangover from when they were made almost entirely of wood. **Transposition is the big compositional point:** many woodwinds sound a different pitch than is written (alto sax reads C, sounds E♭; tenor sax reads C, sounds B♭), and it exists so the **same fingering yields the same written note across every instrument in a family** despite their differing sizes — the player gets music "in the wrong key" to cancel the offset. Reason about it by treating non-transposing instruments as being *in C*: an E♭ clarinet is three semitones sharp of C, so to sound in **D major** you write its part in **B major**. Some transpose by a plain octave (contrabassoon = one octave below written), which is the easy case. Closing **timbre cheat sheet** for identifying each by ear — flute light/breathy, oboe richer but still pure, clarinet rounded and warm with less top end, bassoon rounded and deep and sometimes honky (confusable with clarinet in its upper register), cor anglais oboe-like but deeper and melancholic, sax reedy and audibly jazzy/poppy rather than orchestral, with the two easy extremes being *particularly high* → piccolo and *particularly low* → contrabassoon.
