# Line Cliché

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Chord Progressions]]. A **line cliché** is a chromatic (semitone-by-semitone) line running through the top or bottom voice of a repeated/held chord while every other note stays fixed — the "line" is the melodic element, the "cliché" is that it's a well-worn, instantly-recognizable device. Strictly, a line cliché keeps the underlying chord identity unchanged and moves only the one voice; once other notes also change (as often happens once the line reaches its target chord), it's no longer a pure line cliché in the strict sense, even though the melodic device is still clearly present.

## The classic shape

Descending version — the "Something" progression: **C – Cmaj7 – C7 – F**, where the top note walks C→B→B♭ over a held C-major-family chord, before the harmony resolves to F. The [[Secondary Dominants|secondary dominant]] mechanics: Cmaj7 is just the diatonic 7th on the tonic (mild color/tension, nothing unusual); C7 is NOT diatonic to C major (its B♭ doesn't belong to the key) — it's the **V of IV** (the dominant of F), which is why the progression naturally resolves to F next: dominant chords want to resolve a 5th away, and C7 is F's dominant. Only the first three chords (C–Cmaj7–C7) are a line cliché in the strictest sense; the move to F changes another chord tone (E→F) alongside the line.

Ascending version — the "augmented climb": **A – Aaug – A6 – A7 – D**, the mirror image, where the top note climbs A→A♯→B→C (through an augmented, then a 6th, then a dominant 7th chord) before resolving to IV. Same underlying mechanism as the descending version — the climbing A7 is the V of D, same secondary-dominant resolution logic, just approached from below instead of above.

**The line can sit in the bass instead of the top voice** — same chords, same function, inverted register; the character changes (a different quality of tension) but the harmonic logic is identical.

## General shape (not just the "Something"/climb pair above)

A line cliché can be built on ANY chord (major or minor), moving either the highest or lowest note, and can descend, ascend, or do both:
- **Descending, most common** — "Michelle" (The Beatles): Fm, lowest note steps down a semitone at a time to D♭. "Butterflies and Hurricanes" (Muse): Dm, top note steps down through six chords to a re-voiced Dm. "Try Some, Buy Some" (George Harrison) doubles it: a line cliché on the i chord (G minor) followed immediately by a second one on the iv chord (Cm) — Harrison used the device repeatedly (see also the descending "Something" progression above).
- **Down then back up** — "I Just Called to Say I Love You" (Stevie Wonder): E♭m → E♭m(maj7) → E♭m7, then climbs back the way it came to E♭m(maj7) and home to E♭m.
- **Ascending, usually built off the 5th of the chord** — a descending line from the 5th tends to collide with the chord's other notes and sound unsatisfying, so ascending is the natural direction there. The James Bond theme: Em, the 5th (B) climbs B→C→C♯ then back down to C. "Hey Bulldog" (The Beatles): Bm, top note (F♯) climbs a semitone at a time, then the same climb repeats on the IV chord (Em) — stacking tension.
- **Jazz/American Songbook standards** made the device a genuine cliché in the 30s–50s: "My Funny Valentine" (Rodgers & Hart); "In a Sentimental Mood" (Duke Ellington) — two line clichés back to back, one on i (Dm) immediately echoed by another on iv (Gm).
- **The signature "tell" chord**: a **minor-major 7th** (minor triad + natural 7 — e.g. Am with G♯ on top) is a harsh, rarely-used chord on its own, but it's a very natural mid-point in a descending minor line cliché (Am → Am[maj7] → Am7 → Am6...) — spotting a minor-major-7 chord in a song is a strong tell that a line cliché is happening.
- **Chord-chart labeling convention**: line clichés are often written as slash chords for readability (Am, Am/G♯, Am/G, Am/F♯...) even though this is technically imprecise — "Am/G♯" as written would mean an A-minor triad with G♯ added in the bass, but the actual chord sounding is a G♯ augmented triad (A and E reinterpreted as the augmented chord's other tones). The slash-chord shorthand is kept anyway because it makes the descending-bass-line intent obvious to a performer at a glance, at the cost of literal accuracy.
- **Practical length limit**: most line clichés run 4–6 chords before the ear "runs out of notes" and the effect stops being satisfying — pushing past 6–8 steps starts sounding aimless rather than purposeful.

## Extending past the initial line

Once the line cliché resolves to IV, songs diverge in how they continue:
- **IV → iv (minor four) → I** — a common extension of the descending version: continuing the semitone-line logic one step further (F→F, but the chord quality drops to minor) before resolving home. A melancholic color change.
- **Doubling the climb** — for the ascending version, some songs repeat the whole climb starting from the IV chord (IV → IVaug → IV6 → IV7 → ♭VII or similar), stacking two augmented-climb passes back to back.
- **A "left turn" variant** — substituting the dominant-7 landing chord for something else entirely (e.g. a ♭9 chord resolving to a different target than the plain IV), keeping the line-cliché feel while changing the harmonic destination.

## Songs

**Descending (the "Something" progression):** "Something" (The Beatles) — the original; also notable for Paul McCartney's bassline treating the 2nd chord as a 2nd-inversion (C/G) rather than root position. "Something More" (Train) — same progression, same key, likely a direct homage. "Give Me Love (Give Me Peace on Earth)" (George Harrison) — same songwriter, IV chord given a dominant 7th. "Kiss Me" (Sixpence None the Richer) — teases the line repeatedly before finally releasing to IV just before the chorus. "Raindrops Keep Fallin' on My Head" (B.J. Thomas). "Can't Take My Eyes Off You" (Frankie Valli). "Groovin'" and other late-60s pop.

**Ascending (the "augmented climb"):** "Last Night on Earth" (Green Day) — resolves through IV to the minor iv before landing home; same iv-extension also heard in "505" (Arctic Monkeys) and "I Don't Feel Like Dancing" (Scissors Sisters). "Greatest Love of All" (Whitney Houston), "Mis-Shapes" (Pulp), and "Life on Mars?" (David Bowie) all double the climb — the second pass starting from the IV chord itself. "For Once in My Life" (Stevie Wonder) is a close cousin that takes a left turn: climbs identically through the augmented and 6th chords, but resolves the 7th chord to a ♭9 leading into a minor chord instead of the plain IV the other examples use.

## From videos

- **Invert a borrowed line cliché's direction to build a genuinely new song from an existing one's opening.** "Life on Mars?" (Bowie) and "My Way" (Sinatra/Anka) share the identical opening line cliché note-for-note (descending by semitone from the same starting chord) — "My Way" continues descending into a conventional ballad; "Life on Mars?" reverses to ASCENDING (top note of each chord rising instead of the bottom falling), climbing until it resolves into an entirely new key for the chorus. The shared opening makes the divergence read as a deliberate reference rather than coincidence — reportedly written by Bowie specifically as a harmonic answer song after losing a lyric-writing competition that became "My Way." → David Bennett Music Theory, [['Life On Mars' is revenge on Frank Sinatra]]

- **The James Bond theme's ascending line cliché, given its own dedicated deep-dive.** The mechanism spelled out in full: Em → C/E (1st inversion, top note climbs Em's 5th, B, up a semitone to C) → Cdim/E (top note climbs again to C♯) → back down to Em — the climbing top line effectively IS the James Bond motif, a melody built directly into the harmony. So distinctive that any song using this exact progression reads as evoking "James Bond" regardless of intent: "Everybody's Been Burned" (The Byrds, 1967); "Shades of Cool" (Lana Del Rey); "Secret Agent Man" (Johnny Rivers, an explicit tongue-in-cheek nod used as the main guitar hook). **The Bond-theme-song trend, dated precisely**: "Thunderball" (Tom Jones, 1965) nods to the progression only in passing. The deliberate practice of weaving the ORIGINAL Bond-theme progression into a brand-new theme song's own harmony starts with **"You Know My Name"** (Chris Cornell, *Casino Royale*, 2006) — co-written with franchise composer David Arnold, whose own idea it was, per Cornell's account, to "write a song that echoed the film score." Chorus: Bm–G–E (transposed to Em for direct comparison) keeps the signature semitone climb (B→C→C♯) but only reaches C♯ without falling back to C, and voices the chords in root position rather than the original's inversions. The trend continues, each film's theme independently confirmed to keep the semitone climb while losing the inversions: "Another Way to Die" (Jack White & Alicia Keys, *Quantum of Solace* — power chords throughout, so the melodic 3rd needed to complete the climb is simply absent even though the harmonic skeleton matches); "Skyfall" (Adele) — full climb up and down preserved. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use the James Bond chord progression]]
