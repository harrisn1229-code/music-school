# 7 levels of reharmonising Eleanor Rigby

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tMAM8kPn0rY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tMAM8kPn0rY)

## Synopsis

David Bennett takes the Beatles' "Eleanor Rigby" melody through seven progressively more distant reharmonizations — from committing fully to one parallel mode up to blending three parallel modes with jazz extensions in a wholly unrelated key — demonstrating a single core rule (match the bar's melody note to a chord tone) stretched further and further from safe to genuinely difficult.

## Techniques

- **Core rule**: the main melody note of a bar should ideally appear in the chord set under it — pick a containing chord first, then decide whether the fit should read as safe or as deliberate tension.
- **Level 1**: commit fully to E Dorian (diatonic chords only), including a deliberately mismatched v chord (B minor) chosen specifically to manufacture a stronger resolution back to the tonic.
- **Level 2**: switch to E harmonic minor — work its two distinctive tones (raised 7th, lowered 6th) into the chords.
- **Level 3**: full blues reharmonization — keep the minor-key melody but set it against MAJOR chords plus dominant 7ths.
- **Level 4**: modal interchange across multiple parallel modes at once (Phrygian + Aeolian), closing on a Picardy third (resolving to the major tonic).
- **Level 5**: recontextualize the key without changing a single melody note — shift the tonal center to G Mixolydian (the relative major, sharing every note — the easiest possible key change) purely via which chord the harmony returns to.
- **Level 6**: recontextualize into a genuinely unrelated key (A major) — harder, since most melody notes no longer belong to the new key's diatonic chords.
- **Level 7**: recontextualize into D major, blend multiple parallel D modes, and add 9th extensions simultaneously — some melody notes now genuinely clash with their chord, reframed as deliberate "bluesy ornament" tension rather than error, provided it's brief. The hardest sub-problem: the melody's own final note (G) can't resolve in D major, solved with a Dsus4→D major cadence — G is literally the suspended 4th, so the suspension absorbs the unresolvable note before the sus4→major move supplies the arrival.
