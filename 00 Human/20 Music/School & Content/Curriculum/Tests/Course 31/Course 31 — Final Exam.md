> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **Cmaj7 or C6 — every rule downstream assumes four notes**
> Guide tones need a 7th to exist and a tritone sub has nothing to preserve without one.
>
> **Q2.** **Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7 · Dm7♭5 – G7♭9 – Cm7**
> *Also accepted:* Cm6 or CmMaj7 in place of Cm7
>
> **Q3.** **Any m7 followed by a dominant 7th a fourth above — deceptive, or unresolved**
> Deceptive lands on vi or ♭VI; unresolved moves straight to the next ii–V.
>
> **Q4.** **The 3rd and the 7th — the exception is m7♭5, which also needs its ♭5**
> Cm7♭5 and Cm7 share E♭ and B♭, so only the ♭5 separates them.
>
> **Q5.** **C → B → B and F → F → E**
> *Rubric (5):* gives both lines correctly (2) · states that each 3rd is held as the next chord's 7th and each 7th falls a half step to the next chord's 3rd (2) · says a substitute keeping the lines stepwise is a good one (1)
> Dm7's guide tones are F and C, G7's are B and F, Cmaj7's are E and B. One voice runs C → B → B while the other runs F → F → E: one holds, the other moves a half step, then they swap. That is not a coincidence in this progression, it is what root motion by fifths does — each chord's 3rd is literally the next chord's 7th (F is Dm7's 3rd and G7's 7th; B is G7's 3rd and Cmaj7's 7th), and each chord's 7th falls a half step onto the next chord's 3rd. The test follows directly: write the two lines, try the substitute, and keep it if the lines are still stepwise. The tritone sub passes because it swaps the two notes' roles rather than replacing them.
>
> **Q6.** **Root, 3rd and 7th, no 5th — right whenever something else needs the register**
> Under a busy vocal, or with a second chordal instrument, a full five-note chord is the mistake.
>
> **Q7.** **Shared tones and shared function — and function comes first**
> A substitute sharing three notes that resolves wrong is worse than one sharing two that resolves right.
>
> **Q8.** **They share the same tritone — B and F — with the 3rd and 7th swapping roles**
> *Rubric (5):* names the shared tritone and the 3rd/7th role swap (2) · says only dominant 7ths qualify, because only they contain a tritone (1) · says the substitute sits a semitone above the target and produces chromatic bass motion (2)
> G7 is G B D F and D♭7 is D♭ F A♭ C♭, and C♭ is B — so both chords contain the pair B/F that makes a dominant a dominant, with G7's 3rd serving as D♭7's 7th and vice versa. Same tension, same resolution, different root. Only dominant 7ths can be substituted this way, since the tritone is the thing being preserved, and the substitute always lands a semitone above the target: resolving to C, the sub is D♭7. What you buy is chromatic bass — Dm7 D♭7 Cmaj7 walks D – D♭ – C, which is most of the sound of neo-soul.
>
> **Q9.** **♭II-of-ii — and any major triad a semitone above the target will do the job**
> It is the commonest precisely because it is undramatic, reading as a chromatic step from iii down to ii.
>
> **Q10.** **Chromatic arrival makes it a tritone sub; arrival from ♭VI by fifths makes it a Phrygian cadence**
> *Rubric (5):* gives the arrival test and both readings (2) · says every note of the ♭II triad falls a semitone into a tonic chord tone (2) · notes that ♭II going to V is a third device, the Neapolitan (1)
> Approached chromatically, with the roots stepping down a semitone at a time into it (`Dm7 → D♭7 → Cmaj7`), the ♭II is standing in for a dominant that never appears. Approached from ♭VI as part of a flat-side run of descending fifths, it is a borrowed chord arriving diatonically — a Phrygian cadence. Same chord, same destination, different device, and the roots on the way in are the tell. It sounds complete either way because ♭2̂ falls to 1̂, 4̂ falls to 3̂ and ♭6̂ falls to 5̂: three simultaneous semitone resolutions with no tritone and no leading tone anywhere. And ♭II going to V rather than to I is a third thing again, the Neapolitan.
>
> **Q11.** **Fm7 – B♭7 – Cmaj7 — when V7 sounds too final or too bright**
> Both chords are borrowed from C minor; there is no leading tone, so it settles rather than resolves.
>
> **Q12.** **I – vi – ii – V, to point back at the top — write it in four chords, then compress**
> Compressing a good turnaround gives a good short one; expanding a bare ii–V gives nothing.
>
> **Q13.** **1–4 are a looped I–vi–ii–V; 5–8 go to the subdominant and back through a borrowed minor iv**
> The loop does not get boring because real harmonic movement answers it.
>
> **Q14.** **Harmonic rhythm drops 4× — two chords per bar becomes one per two bars**
> *Rubric (5):* gives the bridge as four dominants each falling a fifth, three of them non-diatonic (1) · says harmonic rhythm drops from two chords a bar to one per two bars (2) · says contrast does not require new material (2)
> The bridge is D7 G7 C7 F7 in B♭, two bars each, a pure circle-of-fifths run that leaves the key and walks back in. But what makes it feel like a new section is not the chromaticism — it is the pace. The A section moves two chords per bar; the bridge moves one chord per two bars, a fourfold slowdown, with no key change, no tempo change and no new instrumentation. The lesson for production is that a bridge can be manufactured by halving the harmonic rhythm of what you already have, which is cheaper than writing new parts and works for the same reason.
>
> **Q15.** **AABA and 16+16 — the first half must not resolve to I, it has to reach V**
> The second half's ending is the tune's real cadence.
>
> **Q16.** **A passing chord connects, a substitution replaces — passing chord on the weak beat**
> The bass can keep walking the original changes under a passing chord and it still works.
>
> **Q17.** **The melody note must be a usable tone of the new chord — so you work melody-first**
> *Rubric (5):* states the constraint (2) · defines usable as a chord tone or a functioning extension, and names something excluded (1) · says work melody-first, because choosing chords first is what produces the clashes (2)
> Usable means a chord tone — 1, 3, 5 or 7 — or a functioning extension: 9, 11, 13 or an altered version of one. What it must not be is a note the new chord actively contradicts, like a natural 9 over a ♭9 chord or a natural 4 sitting on the 3rd. Everything else about the chord is negotiable: function, bass motion, density. Because the melody is the only fixed thing, it is what you plan around — write the tune out, ask what each note could be relative to a candidate chord, and choose from there. Choosing chords first and hoping the tune survives is exactly how the clashes get in.
>
> **Q18.** **Cmaj7 root · Am7 ♭3 · Fmaj7 5th · Dm7 ♭7**
> *Also accepted:* any four of Cmaj7 (root) · Am7 (♭3) · Fmaj7 (5th) · Dm7 (♭7) · A♭maj7 (3rd) · F♯m7♭5 (♭5)
>
> **Q19.** **When the tune is the point — so reharmonize the repeats, not the first statement**
> Let the listener learn the plain version, then change what is underneath it.
>
> **Q20.** **The mode, quartal voicings, pedals, and rhythm and texture — interchange is a different thing**
> Modal jazz holds one mode for many bars; interchange borrows a chord from a parallel mode and returns.
>
> **Q21.** **Natural 6 · ♯4 · ♭7 · ♭2 — avoid it and the music collapses into plain major or minor**
> A loop that never plays the ♯4 is not Lydian, it is major with a Lydian label.
>
> **Q22.** **E A D G B — a stack of thirds names a chord and implies a function; fourths do not**
> Fourths sound open and unassigned, which is where the hollow, floating sound comes from.
>
> **Q23.** **A chromatic passing tone added to a seven-note scale — it keeps chord tones on the beat**
> The reason is rhythmic, not harmonic.
>
> **Q24.** **Notes surrounding the target before you play it — D♭ – B – C — and not on every note**
> They belong on the note you want heard, usually a chord tone on a downbeat.
>
> **Q25.** **A chromatic line walked under static harmony — sustained, not a single arrival**
> It generates forward motion without changing the chord.
>
> **Q26.** **Chunk, analyse against the chord, name one concept, then improvise from the concepts**
> *Rubric (5):* gives the chunk, analyse, name, improvise sequence (2) · identifies naming the concept as the skipped step (1) · names a transfer and says density does not transfer (2)
> Learn the solo by ear in full, break it into phrase-sized chunks, work out what device each chunk uses against the chord underneath it, reduce each one to a single named concept, then improvise your own choruses over the same changes from the concept list rather than the licks. The fourth step is the one people skip, and skipping it leaves you with a memorised part instead of vocabulary. What transfers out of the lesson is small and cheap: an enclosure on the second note of a synth hook, a chromatic approach into the downbeat of a chorus, the bebop scale's added eighth in a bassline. What does not transfer is the note rate, which was a genre convention rather than a principle.
