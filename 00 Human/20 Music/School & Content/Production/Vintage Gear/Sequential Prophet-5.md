# Sequential Prophet-5

## What it is
Dave Smith's masterpiece — the **first fully programmable polyphonic synth**, the instrument that defined what "a polysynth" is. Five voices, two oscillators each, patch memory that actually worked, and a sound so complete that studios have never stopped booking it. **The first microcontroller-based synth**, full stop — Smith repurposed a Z80 chip (the same class used in home computers of the era) to scan the keyboard/panel, assign voices, and generate control voltages, replacing the CS-80's room of discrete analog logic with a single digital "brain." One genuinely novel capability that came with it: **the Prophet-5 could tune its own oscillators** (hold two specific keys simultaneously to trigger auto-calibration) — a first; the CS-80 and other pre-microcontroller synths required a tech to manually calibrate drift by hand. Tradeoff for going microcontroller-first this early: **no velocity, no aftertouch** — keys are on/off only, dynamics have to come from elsewhere in the signal chain.

## What makes the sound
- **Two VCOs per voice** (saw/square/tri with sync) through the **SSM (Rev 1/2) or Curtis (Rev 3) filter** — the Rev 2/3 debate is audio theology: Rev 2 = wilder, creamier; Rev 3 = tighter, punchier. Either way: warm, present, *dense*. **Chip specifics on the SSM (Rev 1/2) boards**: SSM2050 envelope generators (5 per voice-pair, one each for filter and amp), SSM2030 oscillators, SSM2040 filter (described by a synth tech as "very revered... very hot resonance"), SSM2020-based VCAs. **"Better" is the wrong frame** — SSM and Curtis chips just sound different, not worse/better than each other; **most of the canonical "classic Prophet-5 sound" studio records used Rev 3 (Curtis)**, with Yellow Magic Orchestra as a notable Rev 1/2 (SSM) exception.
- **A hidden "Prophet-10" expansion path existed on early units** (nylon standoffs and a second ribbon connector on the analog board, present on units as early as serial #14) meant to double voice count — discontinued almost immediately because the doubled circuitry ran too hot and baked the lower board; the hardware provisions were left in unused units anyway.
- **Poly-Mod** — routing filter envelope/osc B to frequency/PW/filter *polyphonically* gave it growls, bell-metal FM-ish tones, and sync sweeps no rival poly had; the P5's "character patches" are Poly-Mod patches.
- Slight voice-to-voice variance (five analog voice cards) animates chords — the "expensive analog chorus" effect with no chorus circuit.
- Committed, mid-forward voicing that sits in a track without EQ — the definition of "mix-ready analog."

## Used on
- Tame Impala — *Deadbeat* (2025): Kevin Parker's main synth for the album, ~70% of the album's synth sounds by his own estimate (named specifically for "Dracula") — a deliberate single-synth-palette choice ("only a handful of synths… that one just got, it was the one next to me the whole time") reinforcing the album's overall narrow-sonic-palette strategy. → [[Sound Selection Guide]] — Kevin Parker (Tape Notes), [[TN 188 Tame Impala - Full Video Episode (Part 1)]]
- Radiohead — "Everything in Its Right Place" (2000): the P5 as the whole harmonic world of the song (widely documented).
- John Carpenter — the classic score run (*The Fog*, *Escape from New York*): P5 drones and stabs as horror's house sound.
- Genre/era: 1978–85 everything — new wave, R&B, film scores, pop; alongside the [[Minimoog Model D]] the most-recorded analog synth in history.

## From videos

- **The full engineering-history detail behind "What it is" and "What makes the sound" above** — the Z80 microcontroller repurposed from home-computer tech, the self-calibration mechanism (hold two keys to auto-tune all oscillators), the SSM chip specifics on Rev 1/2 boards (2050/2030/2040/2020), the hidden Prophet-10 expansion hardware present on early serial numbers and its heat-related discontinuation, and the Yellow Magic Orchestra Rev 1/2 exception to the general Rev-3-is-the-classic-sound rule — sourced from a synth tech's guided tour of a signed, 14th-ever-built Rev 1 unit. Also makes a broader production-philosophy point: deliberately-imperfect per-voice analog variance (a modern synth's factory calibration tunes every voice/note-range identically; real vintage analog never does) is itself part of what makes the instrument "alive" — same point and same source interview as the entry already on [[Voice Architecture]]. — Anthony Marinelli & Rob Rosen, [[Prophet 5 Under the Hood w Rob Rosen-Clip]]
