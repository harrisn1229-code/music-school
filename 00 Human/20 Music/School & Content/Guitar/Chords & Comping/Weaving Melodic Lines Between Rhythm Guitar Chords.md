# Weaving Melodic Lines Between Rhythm Guitar Chords

Part of [[Guitar Positions]]. A beginner-friendly method for turning a plain open-position chord progression into something that moves melodically, by (1) breaking each big open chord down into the small **triad shapes hiding inside it**, and (2) connecting one chord's triad fragment to the next chord's triad fragment with a few **passing notes from the progression's one parent major scale**. The same system is then relocated up the neck with the **CAGE system**, so the whole open-position vocabulary — chords, triads, and scale — transposes as one block rather than needing to be relearned. Distinct from [[Layering Rhythm Guitar Parts with Triads]] (same channel): that page stacks **multiple separate guitar parts** on one progression, separated by pickup/tone; this page is about **one guitar part** that alternates between full chords, triad fragments, and short connecting melody lines — a single-guitar arranging tool, not a multi-track layering one. Also the mechanical inverse of [[Adding Chords to Single-Note Lines]] (different author): that page starts from a solo line and drops chords under it; this page starts from chords and threads a melody line between them.

## Step 1 — find the three triad shapes hiding inside any open chord

A triad is just the **root, 3rd, and 5th** of the chord — the first, third, and fifth notes of the parent major scale. Any full open-position chord voicing already contains **three separate triad shapes**, stacked at different heights within the same chord grip, one per inversion:

- **Root position** (lowest triad in the grip): root–3rd–5th.
- **First inversion** (next triad up): 3rd–5th–root.
- **Second inversion** (highest triad in the grip): 5th–root–3rd.

Worked in the key of **G major**, open position:
- **G major chord:** the three stacked triads are G-B-D (root position), B-D-G (1st inversion), D-G-B (2nd inversion) — literally slices of the same open-G chord shape you already play, isolated rather than strummed as a full six-string chord.
- **C major chord:** the same three-inversion stack (C-E-G root position → E-G-C 1st inversion → G-C-E 2nd inversion) sits inside the open C chord, plus a lower inversion available below it.
- **D major chord:** played with the **F♯ (3rd) in the bass** — fret with the thumb over the top of the neck, or with a fretting finger — giving a **1st-inversion** D-triad grip (3rd-5th-root); the alternative D-major shape available in the same position is 5th-root-3rd (2nd inversion).

The generalizable move: don't think of an open chord as one indivisible grip — it's **three small triads stacked on top of each other**, and any one of them can be played alone as a lighter, more "musical" substitute for the full chord, especially useful right before or after a single-note line (playing the full six-string chord and a fast passing line back to back sounds cluttered; the triad fragment doesn't).

## Step 2 — connect adjacent chords' triads with notes of the parent scale

Once a chord is reduced to a 3-note triad fragment, connect it to the *next* chord's triad fragment using only notes drawn from the **one major scale all the chords in the progression belong to** — no separate vocabulary is needed per chord change, because every chord in a diatonic progression already shares the same scale.

**Worked progression (open position, key of G major):** verse — G, C, G, D; bridge — Em, C, Em, D, Em, C, Am, D. All eight chords are diatonic to G major, so the **entire connecting vocabulary is the G major scale fingered in this one open-position area of the neck** — nothing else is needed to link any chord in the progression to any other.

- **G → C:** play the G-major triad fragment, then a short line of G-major-scale notes, landing on the C-major triad fragment.
- **C → D** (and elsewhere): drop from the C triad down through the scale to the 3rd of the D chord (the F♯, played in the bass per the D voicing above).
- **Scale-run connector:** a connecting line can also just be a straight run up the parent scale — worked example: 3rd, 4th, up to the 5th of the current chord — landing back on the big open-position chord shape before dropping to a triad fragment again.
- **Any diatonic chord's own triad** doubles as connecting material too — e.g. moving into the B section's E minor chord, the line targets notes straight out of the **E-minor triad shape** (still just the G-major scale, reframed around a different diatonic chord).

**The core principle, stated directly:** the triads live *inside* the larger chord voicings, and both the triads and the chords belong to the same parent scale — so all of it is "just elements of one larger picture." Practically: once you know a progression is diatonic to one key, you only need **one scale pattern, fingered in one position**, to freely connect any chord in it to any other — pick and choose whichever scale notes sound good between two chord tones rather than following one fixed connector lick.

## Step 3 — ornament a landing note with a half-step trill

A small, optional flourish for extra nuance when a connecting line resolves onto a triad or chord tone: a quick **half-step trill** (a fast hammer-on/pull-off between the target note and the note a half step below or above it) played right as the line lands — described directly as adding "a nice little inflection," not a structural change to the line, just a decoration on the landing note.

## Step 4 — relocate the whole system with the CAGE shape

The open-position chord/triad/scale system above isn't tied to open position — it can be **moved up the neck as one block** using the CAGE system (see [[CAGE System Scale Shapes]], [[CAGED Chord Shapes by String-Shift]]), because the triads, the chords, and the scale pattern are all physically anchored to each other in the same shape.

**Worked relocation:** the open-G-position example above (G-C-G-D verse, Em-C-Em-D-Em-C-Am-D bridge) is moved wholesale up the neck by taking the **G-shape voicing and barring/fretting it at the 8th fret**, which reads as a **C chord** (root on the 8th fret of the low E string) instead of open G. The same relative chord relationships transpose along with it: **G→C→G→D becomes C→F→C→G**, and **Em→Am→D→G** (the bridge's relative-minor motion) becomes the equivalent progression a 4th up. The **same three triad shapes** (root position / 1st inversion / 2nd inversion) and the **same scale-pattern fingering**, physically identical to the open-position versions, are used unchanged — only their fretboard location changes, "almost as if you just put a capo up here and played the exact same thing," except done with the fretting hand instead of a literal capo.

**Why this matters generally:** this is presented explicitly as "dipping your toe in the water" of the wider CAGE system — the same open-chord-plus-triad-plus-scale unit exists in **all five CAGE root positions** (C, A, G, E, D shapes), not just the two demonstrated here, so the same connecting-line vocabulary is available anywhere on the neck once the CAGE shape at that position is known.

## Drill — the video's own build order

1. **Play the plain open-position progression** (G-C-G-D / Em-C-Em-D-Em-C-Am-D in G major, or any diatonic progression you're working on) as full chords first, no melody, to lock in the harmony.
2. **Isolate the three triad shapes inside each chord** (root position, 1st inversion, 2nd inversion) one at a time — for each chord in the progression, find and play just its root-position triad, then just its 1st inversion, then just its 2nd inversion, before combining any of them with a connecting line.
3. **Connect two adjacent chords using only their triad fragments plus scale passing tones** — pick one transition at a time (G→C first), play the outgoing chord's triad, a short 2-4 note run from the parent major scale, landing on the incoming chord's triad. Repeat for every chord change in the progression.
4. **Add the half-step trill** on one or two landing notes once the connecting lines are comfortable, for phrasing/nuance — don't force it onto every note.
5. **Relocate the entire drill to a second CAGE position** (e.g., the G-shape-barred-as-C example at the 8th fret) and repeat steps 1-4 there, using the same triad shapes and same scale fingering, just transposed. This is the actual test that the vocabulary has been internalized as a *shape*, not memorized per key.

*(General starting-point structuring of the 5-step drill order — the video demonstrates each layer in sequence but the explicit "drill it this way" framing is the standard way to internalize a chord-plus-connecting-line system like this.)*
