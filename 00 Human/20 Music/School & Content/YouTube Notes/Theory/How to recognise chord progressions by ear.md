# How to recognise chord progressions by ear

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H9e_1DTm-VQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H9e_1DTm-VQ)

## Synopsis

A deeper, "how it feels" companion to the presenter's song-anchor videos — rather than just pairing each scale-degree function with a memorable song, this one describes the qualitative FEELING of each function (how resolved, how tense, where it pulls) so a listener can identify a chord's function even without a matching anchor song in memory. Closes with a graded 5-progression ear-training quiz. Major key only — a minor-key follow-up is announced (companion: [[Songs to help you recognise Minor Key chord progressions]] covers the minor-key song-anchor version; this presenter also separately references a planned minor-key "how it feels" follow-up).

## Techniques

- **Core method restated**: train relative pitch (hearing "that's the I chord") rather than absolute pitch (hearing "that's a C chord") — the same progression transposed to any key produces the identical functional relationships and feeling.
- **I (tonic)** — completely resolved, "could it go anywhere else?" test: if a chord sounds fully at rest with nowhere left to go, it's probably I. Example: the ending of "Imagine" (John Lennon).
- **V (dominant)** — maximum tension in the key, strongly wants to resolve back to I. Example: the tension-building section of "Twist and Shout."
- **IV (subdominant)** — a "subdued version of V" — some sense of wanting to move eventually, but in no hurry, more relief-on-arrival than urgency. Example: "Walk on the Wild Side" (Lou Reed), largely built on I↔IV.
- **vi** — an alternative resting point to I, but darker/minor in color — "the song could end here, just a darker ending." Example: "Stand By Me."
- **ii** — the "minor-feeling version of IV" — similarly patient/unhurried resolution. Example: "Time After Time" (Cyndi Lauper) verse, oscillates ii↔I.
- **iii** — a genuine tension/rest midpoint: not fully at rest but not tense either, "balancing on the edge in the middle" — could resolve back to I for a nice payoff, or the song could just end on it for a less satisfying but still valid ending. Example: "Space Oddity" (Bowie) verse, I→iii.
- **♭VII (chromatic/borrowed)** — described as "epic," like the horizon suddenly opening up. Example: "Don't You Forget About Me" (Simple Minds), both intro and chorus use ♭VII↔I.
- **♭III (chromatic/borrowed)** — a "bluesy edge," explained as the major/minor mixing that defines blues harmony (a minor-3rd-built chord inside a major key). Example: "Love Shack" (The B-52's) chorus climb, I→♭III (continuing to IV→♭VI, though only the first move is the focus).
- **♭VI (chromatic/borrowed)** — an "unusual left turn" out of the key. Example: "Poison" (Alice Cooper) opening, I→♭VI.
- **♭II (chromatic/borrowed)** — "exotic," "Arabesque" — rare but distinctive. Example: "Pyramid Song" (Radiohead), I→♭II (technically ♭IImaj7).
- **♯4/♭5 (rarest diatonic-adjacent chord)** — "cinematic," unusual but does show up. Example: "Just" (Radiohead) chorus, I→♭V→IV.
- **iv (minor, borrowed)** — "melancholic, bittersweet," common in a I→IV→iv→I turnaround shape. Example: "Creep" (Radiohead) — G→B(major III)→C(IV)→Cm(iv)→G(I).
- **III (major, borrowed — a secondary dominant)** — briefly explained as a secondary dominant (full mechanism deferred to a linked separate video — see [[Secondary Dominants]]); tends to resolve to IV or vi. To IV: "Creep" (same example as iv above, I→III→IV). To vi: "Happier Than Ever" (Billie Eilish), I→III→vi.
- **II (major, borrowed — a secondary dominant)** — "a ray of sunshine," typically resolves up a 5th to V, which then resolves to I (I→II→V→I). Example: "Love Me Tender" (Elvis Presley).
- **VI (major, borrowed — a secondary dominant)** — resolves up a 5th, typically to ii. Example: "I Get Around" (Beach Boys), I→VI→ii.
- **VII (major, borrowed)** — rare, "a little bit jarring." Example: "Karma Police" (Radiohead) chorus, IV→V→I→VII.
- **v (minor, borrowed)** — a "reflective, wistful" alternate take on the usual major V, often followed by IV→I. Example: "Clocks" (Coldplay), I→v→IV→I.
- **Closing ear-training quiz (5 graded progressions in C major, presenter's own answers given)**: (1) I–ii–IV–I. (2) I–iii–vi–ii. (3) I–♭VII–vi–IV–I. (4) IV–iv–I (doesn't start on I). (5) I–♭II–♭VI–♭VII (hardest — enough non-diatonic chords in a row that the key center itself gets genuinely hard to track).
