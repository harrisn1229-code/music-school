# These songs have the same chords... let's swap them! (Numb)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PNrWsXW3IlI)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PNrWsXW3IlI)

## Synopsis

Third and longest RipX mashup video in the series, this time explicitly built around named/well-known progression families (minor Axis, Latin i-VI-V-family, doo-wop, and rhythm changes) rather than one-off coincidences, using each pair to demonstrate how little the underlying chords actually determine a song's perceived style.

## Techniques

- **Pair 1 — "Otherside" (Red Hot Chili Peppers) / "Numb" (Linkin Park)**: both use the **minor Axis progression** — the minor-key relative of the famous I–V–vi–IV Axis Progression (Axis of Awesome's namesake sketch), i.e. the same four-chord relationship rooted in the minor key instead.
- **Pair 2 — "Havana" (Camila Cabello) / "Smooth" (Rob Thomas & Santana)**: both use a Latin-styled progression moving from the minor i chord down to the major V via the vi chord (i–VI–V family) — a very common Latin harmonic move.
- **Pair 3 — "I Will Always Love You" (Dolly Parton, more famously Whitney Houston) / "Earth Angel" (The Penguins)**: both are the classic **doo-wop/"50s" I–vi–IV–V progression**. Explicit style-independence demonstration: putting Whitney Houston's vocal over Earth Angel's 1950s doo-wop instrumental makes the song "sound like it could have genuinely come out in the 1950s"; the reverse (Earth Angel's vocal over Whitney's 1980s/90s-style instrumental) makes it sound like an '80s/'90s ballad — Bennett's explicit takeaway: chords "only contribute so much" to a song's perceived era/style; rhythm, instrumentation, arrangement, and timbre carry most of that signal, not harmony.
- **Rhythm changes variant, named and dated**: swapping the doo-wop progression's IV chord for ii produces "rhythm changes" — named for the old jazz standard "I Got Rhythm" (Gershwin) that popularized this exact variant. **Pair 4 — "Birds of a Feather" (Billie Eilish) / "Last Christmas" (Wham!)** — both use rhythm changes, in nearly the same key and with matching chord extensions (not just matching root progression); noted as a widely-recognized similarity once Birds of a Feather was released. **Tuning trivia**: the original "Last Christmas" recording sits a quarter-tone flat of concert pitch D — a side effect of the tape being slowed down (VarSpeed) during production to hit the desired tempo, which also drags the pitch down; Bennett digitally corrected this back to concert D for the mashup, something not possible with 1980s tape technology (modern pitch-shift software can change pitch independent of tempo; slowing analog tape cannot).
- **Pair 5 — "Staying Alive" (Bee Gees) / "Another Brick in the Wall, Pt. 2" (Pink Floyd)**: not identical progressions, but both largely vamp on i before eventually moving to the major IV — the **Dorian vamp** (i–IV), already documented on [[Chord Progressions]] from an earlier video in this batch citing this exact Pink Floyd track. New context added here: Bennett ties the device to disco/funk's fondness for Dorian specifically, and notes the disco undercurrent in "Another Brick in the Wall" is what makes the two tracks blend so well despite Pink Floyd not usually being thought of as disco-adjacent. Close BPM match meant minimal tempo correction was needed — the only real adjustment compensated for Pink Floyd's original recording drifting slightly off a click track.
- **Coldplay reusing their own progression a second time**: following on from the earlier Chili Peppers self-reuse example (Can't Stop/Zephyr Song, covered in a prior mashup video), Coldplay's "Speed of Sound" reuses the same unusual progression as "Clocks" from the preceding album — similar piano-riff arrangement too, read as a deliberate "Clocks 2" attempt to repeat that song's success. The two songs' keys are a tritone apart (about as far as two keys can be), which is why this particular mashup sounds the roughest of the video's pairs — extreme digital pitch-shifting introduces audible artifacts.
- **"Hey Jude" (The Beatles, outro coda) / "One Day Like This" (Elbow, outro coda)**: same key, same progression — I–♭VII–IV–I (F–E♭–B♭–F) — already documented on [[Chord Progressions]] as the "Mixolydian vamp" from an earlier video in this batch. Elbow's outro is judged "almost definitely" a direct homage to the Hey Jude coda — both are simple, uplifting, singalong-melody outros built on the identical chord loop.
