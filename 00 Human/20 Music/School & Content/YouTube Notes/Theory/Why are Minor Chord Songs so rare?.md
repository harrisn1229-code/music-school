# Why are Minor Chord Songs so rare?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xnoxNEgOw-s)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xnoxNEgOw-s)

## Synopsis

An entirely-minor-chord song is rare because the three most common cadences (perfect, deceptive, plagal) all lean on a MAJOR chord somewhere in the resolution — so an all-minor harmony can't use functional cadences at all, which pushes it toward static, one-or-two-chord songs. Surveys the handful of real all-minor examples, then explains the genres (funk, rap, modal jazz) that embrace that harmonic stasis on purpose because the musical interest is meant to live somewhere other than the chord progression.

## Techniques

- **Core claim, with the mechanism named directly**: nearly every common cadence — **perfect** (V→I), **deceptive/interrupted** (V→vi), **plagal** (IV→I) — requires a MAJOR chord (the dominant V, or a major IV) to do its resolving work. An all-minor-chord song therefore can't use functional cadences at all, which is why all-minor songs tend to collapse to one or two chords with minimal harmonic movement rather than a full functional progression.
- **All-minor-chord song examples (rare, hence the video)**: "505" (Arctic Monkeys, Em/Dm throughout). "I'm Going Back a 555 (Call Out My Name)" (The Weeknd, A♭m/E♭m throughout). "Get the Party Started" (Pink) — stays on a single chord (Bm) the whole song. "Run Through the Jungle" (Creedence Clearwater Revival) — sticks on Dm throughout. "Political World" (Bob Dylan) — stays on Fm throughout.
- **Extending the search to minor 7th chords finds a few more**: "Sing" (Ed Sheeran, A♭m–D♭m7). "Moves Like Jagger" (Maroon 5, Bm7–E7sus — note the sus7, not a plain major). "Falling" (Alicia Keys, Em–Bm7). "Family Affair" (Mary J. Blige, Cm–G7sus). "Chain of Fools" (Aretha Franklin) — almost entirely Cm7, but does include a brief C7 at the start.
- **The near-miss case that clarifies the rule**: "Ain't No Sunshine" (Bill Withers) is largely minor-chord, but its brief G major chords are explicitly flagged as important, not incidental — they're what provides the song's actual cadential motion back home.
- **Genres that avoid functional harmony ON PURPOSE, making all-minor/static harmony a genuine stylistic choice rather than a limitation**: **funk** — famously sits on one chord for minutes, because the musical interest is meant to live in the groove/rhythm, not the harmony. **Rap** — often uses a sample/loop built from just one or two minor chords, deliberately keeping harmonic simplicity so focus stays on lyrics and flow rather than chord movement. **Modal jazz** — built explicitly on AVOIDING functional harmony; late-1950s artists (Miles Davis, Bill Evans, John Coltrane) began vamping on one or two chords treated as **key centers** rather than functional chords, with musical development instead coming from exploring different modes/scales over that fixed center. "So What" (Miles Davis) vamps almost the whole 9 minutes on Dm7→E♭m7 (each a key center, not a "chord" in the functional sense) — though a 30-second intro with major chords means it's not a pure all-minor example. "Impressions" (John Coltrane) is harmonically identical to "So What" minus that intro, making it a genuinely pure all-minor-chord piece. → [[Modal Jazz]].
- **Even a BRIEF major chord meaningfully changes an otherwise-minor piece**: "The Imperial March" (John Williams, *Star Wars*) comes very close to all-minor, and that near-constant minor harmony is cited as central to its unnerving, disconcerting quality — but it briefly resolves to an E♭ MAJOR chord, giving a moment of "opening up"/epic quality; Bennett floats a programmatic reading that this brief major moment represents the small amount of good left in Darth Vader.
