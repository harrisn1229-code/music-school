> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **The melody's overall shape over time — independent of the actual pitches**
> Rising, falling or sitting still. You can draw it as a single line without knowing what key it's in.
>
> **Q2.** **Balance — what goes up comes back down, producing an arc with one clear peak**
> The failure modes it rules out are a flat line and a line that drifts in one direction indefinitely. A melody that keeps revisiting its own top note reads as weaker than one with a single peak.
>
> **Q3.** **After a stretch of stepwise movement, take a leap — and the reverse**
> Steps read as smooth and connected; leaps read as dramatic. All of one or all of the other is one-dimensional.
>
> **Q4.** **Fix an explicit top and bottom note before writing — about an octave and a third apart**
> *Also accepted:* constrain the range · roughly an octave to an octave and a half
>
> **Q5.** **No range constraint and no balance — the line has direction but never returns**
> *Rubric (5):* identifies the missing range constraint as the cause of the wandering (2) · says the contour isn't balanced, so nothing comes back down and no peak forms (1) · gives two concrete fixes, such as setting an explicit top and bottom note before rewriting, and forcing a return after each ascent so a single peak emerges (2)
> Wandering isn't a shortage of ideas, it's a shortage of boundaries. With no upper and lower bound the line has nowhere it has to stay, so each phrase starts wherever the last one left off and the shape never resolves into anything the ear can hold. Fix the band first — pick a top note and a bottom note and refuse to leave them — and most of the shape appears by itself. Then check the balance: if the line travels up for four bars, it owes you a descent, and once it has one you'll find the peak has placed itself.
>
> **Q6.** **A short fragment used as raw material to generate much more music — two or three bars is plenty**
> The value isn't in the fragment. It's in what you do with it afterward.
>
> **Q7.** **Repetition · sequence · inversion · contraction · expansion · diminution · augmentation**
> The full set is repetition, sequence, inversion, interval contraction, interval expansion, diminution, augmentation, rhythmic metamorphosis and imitation. Sequence restates the motive at a new pitch level; inversion flips it upside down; diminution plays it faster and augmentation slower; imitation echoes it between voices or registers.
>
> **Q8.** **Diminution, augmentation and rhythmic metamorphosis — and they still read as new material**
> That's the same principle Lesson 3 leans on when it puts "change the rhythm" at the top of the boring-melody triage. Rhythm alone is enough to make a fragment sound like something else.
>
> **Q9.** **Most strong melodies are one short idea developed, not a long line invented**
>
> **Q10.** **Transform the four bars — one technique per variation — rather than writing anything new**
> *Rubric (5):* says to generate the rest by applying development techniques to the existing four bars (2) · names at least two techniques by name (1) · says the result stays cohesive because no new idea is introduced, where an invented longer line tends to wander (2)
> Take the four bars and run them through the table one technique at a time: repeat it, sequence it up a step, invert it, contract the intervals, double the note values, change the rhythm without changing the pitches. Each pass gives you material that is audibly related to the original without being the original, and sixteen bars arrive faster than they would from a blank clip. The reason it beats inventing is cohesion. A long line written straight through has no internal reason to hang together, and the usual result is the wandering contour from Q5. Development guarantees the relationship — every bar is the same idea seen from a different angle, which is what makes a listener feel they recognise a melody the second time they hear it.
