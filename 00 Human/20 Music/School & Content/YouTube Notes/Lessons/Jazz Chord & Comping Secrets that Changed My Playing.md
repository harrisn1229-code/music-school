# Jazz Chord & Comping Secrets that Changed My Playing

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mT9vTUbR91o)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mT9vTUbR91o)

## Synopsis
An 11-minute jazz-guitar comping lesson from Nathan Borton laying out three named "comping secrets" he credits with changing both his comping and his soloing: **breaking up voicings** (treating a struck chord as separable notes rather than one fixed event), **counterpoint within chords** (a loose, non-species two-voice contrary-motion exercise for generating lines, credited to Steve Kabal), and **multifunctional chords** (one memorized voicing that reads as several different chords depending purely on the bass note underneath it — demonstrated here with a melodic-minor-derived shape he calls "the magic chord"). The video opens with a short comping étude that combines all three, then unpacks each one individually with worked examples over a C major 7 drop-2 voicing, a G7, and a full 2-5-1. Borton closes with practical advice on how to actually absorb material like this: pick one narrow fragment (one break-up pattern, one area of the neck) and hyperfocus on it for months rather than trying to internalize everything at once — his own current example being applying the breaking-up idea to diminished-chord inversions. Most valuable here is less any single voicing and more the three *reusable comping mechanisms* themselves — they generalize past the specific worked examples to any voicing family Nate already knows.

## What's taught

**1. Breaking up voicings** — take a known chord voicing (worked on a C major 7 drop-2, root on the A string) and instead of always striking all four notes together, recombine them: outside notes then inside notes (or reversed), every other note, or hold the top note as a pedal and play the rest as a line underneath. Repeat across every inversion of the voicing — more notes (bigger/denser chords) means more combinations, so it's called especially effective on larger chords. Apply over a 2-5-1: first pass, break up every chord; second pass, break up only a couple of chords for contrast, which increases the impact versus a first-pass "break everything" approach. Generalizes to any voicing, not just drop-2. Full method and cross-links → [[Breaking Up Voicings]].

**2. Counterpoint within chords** — a loose (non-species) two-voice exercise: pick a key center (worked in C), start on any note in the key (A on the G string), play a diatonic 2nd below it (G) as the bottom voice, then move the top note up 3 diatonic steps while the bottom note moves down 3 diatonic steps — contrary motion within the fixed key. Repeat starting from every scale degree. Drop a chord underneath (worked on G7, but any diatonic chord works) and walk the top note up until it lands on a usable extension — in this case landing on a G13 color. Run the resulting two-voice lines through a 2-5-1 as real comping *and* soloing vocabulary. Credited to Steve Kabal (name as captioned). Full method and cross-links → [[Counterpoint Line Generation]].

**3. Multifunctional chords — the "magic chord"** — a single voicing built from C melodic minor reads as five different chords purely by changing the bass note played underneath it: **C** → Cm(maj7)6/9, **F** → F13(9,♯11), **B** → B altered, **A** → Am7♭5(♮9), **E♭** → E♭maj7♯5(♯11). Worked as a complete 2-5-1 off the one shape, and explicitly combinable with the other two secrets — fragment the magic-chord voicing with the breaking-up technique, or reach it via a counterpoint-generated line. Full derivation and cross-links → [[Multifunctional Voicings]] (added as a new device on the existing note, alongside Barry Galbraith's related but separately-derived multifunctional-shape system).

**Practice philosophy (closing advice):** don't try to absorb all three secrets at once — pick a small fragment (e.g. one specific break-up pattern, or one neck area for the counterpoint exercise) and hyperfocus on it exclusively for an extended stretch (a couple of months, sometimes closer to a year) until it surfaces naturally in real playing, then move to the next fragment. Borton's own current focus: applying the breaking-up-voicings idea to diminished chord inversions rather than always striking the same diminished shape (→ [[Diminished Chords]]).

**Gear aside (sponsor mention, not a technique):** Borton names Stringjoy as a sponsor and states he personally uses their Broadway Pure Nickels with a custom gauge — top two strings 13, bottom four 12 — for what he calls a balanced, warm tone.
