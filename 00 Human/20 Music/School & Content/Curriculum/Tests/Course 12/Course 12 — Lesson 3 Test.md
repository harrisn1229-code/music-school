> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **Most of them weren't leaning on risers, swooshes or reverse cymbals at all**
> The cohesion was coming from somewhere else, which is the finding worth internalising because it contradicts what a transition looks like from the outside.
>
> **Q2.** **Because it papers over a join that doesn't work**
> The two sections still don't connect; there's now something loud on top of the seam. Risers are legitimate as deliberate icing once the parts already flow into each other.
>
> **Q3.** **Automate existing devices across the section — filters, detune, sends, wet/dry**
> It relocates the transition from an object you insert to a property of the two sections either side of it.
>
> **Q4.** **Micro drop · macro drop · wet/dry drop**
> A micro drop is an instant filter wet/dry snap plus a gradual percussion sweep. A macro drop goes for maximum contrast through kick filtering, bass removal, slight detune and a timbre shift. A wet/dry drop uses send automation to contrast wet against dry and wide against mono rather than contrasting tone.
>
> **Q5.** **Mine your own elements for one — a bassline repurposed as a sweep**
> It keeps the transition made of the song's own material, which is most of why it reads as connected rather than as something laid over the top.
>
> **Q6.** **Keep one recognisable element running unchanged straight through the boundary**
> A melodic motif or a signature sound. It gives the listener something to hold onto while everything else changes underneath.
>
> **Q7.** **Restate the theme later in a new key — it reads as development rather than repetition**
> A returning idea can feel like it has moved without needing a full modulation plan.
>
> **Q8.** **Because contrast and connective tissue are two jobs, and moderate contrast does neither well**
> *Rubric (5):* names contrast and connective tissue as separate jobs that are easy to confuse (2) · says moderate contrast everywhere is a compromise that fails at both — the boundary isn't felt and nothing links the sections (2) · says one carried element supplies the continuity, which frees everything else to change as much as it likes (1)
> The mistake is treating cohesion as a dial on the same axis as contrast, so that making a transition smoother means making the two sections more alike. It doesn't work, because a listener needs both a reason to notice the boundary and something that survives it. Split the jobs and both get done properly. Pick one element — the motif, the signature sound, whatever the section is recognisable by — and let it run through untouched. That single thread is now carrying all of the continuity, which means every other element is free to change completely. What you get is a boundary that is unmistakably a boundary and still doesn't feel like the track cut to a different song.
>
> **Q9.** **Cut all of them — only the most essential element carries into the boundary**
> The last gesture before maximum is minimum.
>
> **Q10.** **Because the sudden reduction reads as dramatic tension release, and it reinforces whatever hits next**
> It's the counterintuitive half of building: the emptiness is what gives the payoff something to land against.
>
> **Q11.** **A listener can't track many simultaneous parts, so less makes what remains read as more**
> *Rubric (5):* says attention is limited, so removing parts concentrates it on what's left (2) · names a big reverb wash selling an emptied section as intentional (1) · names scheduled silence as legitimate material in its own right (2)
> Suspense is an attention effect before it's a musical one. With eight things playing, no single one of them is being listened to closely; the ear is sampling a texture. Take six away and the two that remain are suddenly under a magnifying glass, and everything about them — their timing, their tail, the space around them — becomes information. That's why an emptied bar feels like something is about to happen: the listener has been given nothing to do except anticipate. The two related devices work the same way. A big reverb over a stripped section tells the ear the emptiness was a decision rather than a dropout, and scheduled silence goes further still, treating an absence as a written event rather than as the gap between events.
