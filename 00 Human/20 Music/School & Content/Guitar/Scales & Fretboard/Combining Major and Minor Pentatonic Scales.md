# Combining Major and Minor Pentatonic Scales

**Three escalating ways to use the major and minor pentatonic scales of the same root together, as a deliberate practice ladder: free alternation with no harmonic rule, whole-scale switching tied to the chord, and blending the two scales into one hybrid scale via a single note swap per chord.** Both scales share the same tonic throughout (E major pentatonic and E minor pentatonic, not a relative-major/relative-minor pair) — a **parallel**, not relative, pentatonic relationship. Sits alongside [[Minor 6 Pentatonic Scale]] and [[Diagonal Pentatonic Blues Soloing]] in the vault's "one-note-edit unlocks the chord tones" family of pentatonic hacks, but this page's contribution is the **practice progression that gets you there** — a concrete three-stage drill order, not just the final trick.

## Level 1 — Free alternation over a static vamp (no harmonic commitment)

**The physical trick: play a lick in major pentatonic, then move the identical fingering shape up the fretboard by 3 frets to get the "same" lick in minor pentatonic (or down 3 frets, minor → major).** No new fingering to learn — the two scales' movable box shapes interlock across the neck 3 frets apart for the same root, so relocating a known shape is the whole mechanic.

**When to reach for this level:** material with **no clearly defined harmony** — power-chord rock/funk riffs (no 3rd in the chord, so no major/minor commitment is implied), and a lot of blues. Since nothing in the harmony forces a choice, alternating major and minor pentatonic phrases both "work," and the contrast becomes a color/expression choice rather than a correctness question.

**Drill:** take a short pentatonic lick already under your fingers, play it as-is (major), then transpose the identical shape up 3 frets and play it again (now minor) — repeat back and forth until the 3-fret relocation is automatic and instant, then apply it freely over a one-chord vamp.

## Level 2 — Whole-scale switching tied to the chord

**Rule: major pentatonic on the I chord, minor pentatonic on the IV and V chords**, over a 12-bar blues. Still whole-scale switching (no note blending yet), but now the switch point is dictated by the chord itself rather than chosen freely — the explicit stepping stone toward chord-tone targeting that Level 3 completes.

**Drill:** loop a 12-bar blues shuffle backing track in any key. Drill switching pentatonic scale — major on I, minor on IV and V — exactly on the downbeat of each chord change, first with plain scale runs, then with real phrases, until the switch lands cleanly in time without hunting for the new scale.

## Level 3 — Blend the scales: swap one note per chord

Instead of switching whole scales, **edit a single note in each scale so the resulting 5-note set covers every chord tone of the dominant-7 chord it's played over:**

- **Over the I7 chord:** start from **major pentatonic** and **drop the 6th down to the ♭7** (borrowed from the minor pentatonic). Worked example in E: E major pentatonic (E F♯ G♯ B C♯) → drop C♯ to D → **E F♯ G♯ B D**. This modified scale now contains the root, major 3rd, 5th, and ♭7 of the I7 chord — every chord tone — plus one extra color note (the 2nd/9th).
- **Over the IV7 chord:** start from **minor pentatonic** and **raise the ♭7 up to the major 6** (borrowed from the major pentatonic). Worked example in A (the IV of E): A minor pentatonic (A C D E G) → raise G to F♯ → **A C D E F♯**. Root and 5th are direct chord tones; the retained minor 3rd (C) functions as a blue note against the chord's major 3rd (standard blues bend-toward-major-3rd practice — see [[Blues Lead Phrasing and Touch]]); the added major 6 is the borrowed color note.
- **The general rule, in one line:** major pentatonic's 6th and minor pentatonic's ♭7 are the *same scale degree slot*, a half step apart — swap whichever direction is missing the chord's ♭7 (major pentatonic → drop 6th to ♭7) or missing a strong upper color tone (minor pentatonic → raise ♭7 to major 6), one note at a time, per chord.

**Drill:** over a static I7 vamp, play major pentatonic normally, then replay identical phrases with every 6th swapped to the ♭7. Over a static IV7 vamp, play minor pentatonic normally, then replay with every ♭7 swapped to the major 6 — the same paired-lick, ear-then-hand comparison method [[Minor 6 Pentatonic Scale]] uses for its own five worked licks, applied here to both scale directions instead of just one.

## The practice ladder, as a session structure

Reuse the *same* backing track across stages so only the vocabulary changes, not the loop:
1. Drill the 3-fret major↔minor shape relocation in isolation (no backing track).
2. Loop a static one-chord vamp; freely alternate major/minor licks (Level 1).
3. Loop a 12-bar blues; switch scale by chord — major on I, minor on IV/V (Level 2).
4. Loop the same 12-bar blues; play the note-swapped hybrid scales instead of switching whole scales (Level 3).
