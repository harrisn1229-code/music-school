# Delayed Resolution - A Gorgeous Reharmonization Technique (10 Examples)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VS2JauxhBBI)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VS2JauxhBBI)

## Synopsis

Jared Borowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared) gives **delayed resolution** its own dedicated, systematic treatment — the exact device his [[Georgia on My Mind Chords (Guitar Lesson) - Easiest way to play the REAL chords|Georgia on My Mind chords lesson]] pointed to but didn't teach ("references a separate video of his on the theory of delayed resolution — not yet in the vault" — this is that video). Defined directly: instead of resolving to the expected tonic chord at the end of a progression, substitute an unexpected chord that still contains the melody's tonic note, leaving the tension hanging longer before finally arriving home. The whole lesson works one fixed setup — a ii–V–I in C major with the melody landing on the tonic note **C** — and reharmonizes that final C ten different ways by treating it as a different chord tone (root, 3rd, 5th, 7th, ♭7, 9th) each time, both as single substitute chords and as full delaying progressions, closing with a bonus tip on applying the same idea to single-note lead lines rather than full harmony. Highly concrete and example-driven — exact chord names and voicing logic throughout, not vague theory-speak — a strong direct feed for [[Reharmonization]] technique #1 (Change the chord's function under a held note), which this video expands into a fully worked-out, named device.

## What's taught

**Setup (0:00–1:19):** ii–V–I in C major (Dm7–G7–Cmaj7), melody's final note is the tonic **C**, landing on the I chord. That C is the note reharmonized every time below — same melody note, different chord, always eventually resolving back to plain C major (or Cmaj7). Example 1 is the plain, non-reharmonized baseline for comparison.

**The 10 examples — each harmonizes the held tonic note C as a different chord tone, delaying arrival at plain C major/Cmaj7:**

1. **Baseline (no delay).** Plain ii–V–I in C: Dm7 → G7 → Cmaj7. The control version — what all the following examples deviate from.
2. **C as the root — C diminished 7.** Play **Cdim7** on the tonic instead of Cmaj7, then resolve to whatever major chord/quality you want (major triad or maj7). Called out as "a go-to delayed resolution chord" historically common in early popular music, classical music, and jazz arrangements — less common in pop today but still a strong, immediately recognizable move. → [[Diminished Chords]].
3. **C as the 3rd — A♭ major.** C is the major 3rd of A♭, so harmonize with **A♭maj7** (any A♭ major or minor-quality chord that contains C works). Named variant given: **A♭ dominant 7♯11** for a jazzier color on the same root.
4. **C as the 5th — F major.** C is the 5th of F, so **Fmaj7** (any F chord containing a 5th). Jared notes this reads as a version of the **plagal (IV–I) cadence**. A jazzier upgrade on the same idea: make the IV chord minor — **F minor 6** instead of F major — described as "very jazz harmony but beautiful."
5. **C as the 7th — D♭ major7.** C is the major 7th of D♭, so **D♭maj7**, resolving a half step up onto the D♭ chord and then a half step back down to C — called "a very common delayed resolution... especially in jazz music," specifically the *major-seven-a-half-step-up-then-down* motion. **Guitaristic voicing variant:** play the same D♭maj7 in **3rd inversion with C in the bass** (C as the bass note, D♭maj7's upper tones above it) — the top three notes then move down in a smooth line as it resolves. **Related move (same note, different chord):** C is also the **♭7 of D minor**, so harmonize with **D half-diminished (Dø7 / Dm7♭5)** instead — called his favorite of all ten. Side-tip: Dø7 shares the exact same notes as **F minor 6**, just a different inversion (minor 6 and half-diminished chords are the same intervallic structure built from different roots) — so this is the same color as example 4's Fm6 upgrade, re-voiced. His single favorite voicing of the whole video: the **3rd inversion of the half-diminished chord — Dø7/C** (D half-diminished over a C bass) — praised specifically because the bass note (C) stays exactly where it lands when it resolves to C major, so the root doesn't move, only the chord above it changes. → [[Slash Chords]], [[Diminished Chords]].
6. **C as the 9th — B♭ dominant 9.** C is the 9th of B♭7, so harmonize with **B♭9** (B♭ dominant 7 with a 9 added), played as one physical chord shape.
7. **Progression: A♭maj7 → B♭9.** Chaining examples 3 and 6 together as a two-chord delaying move before the final resolution — played slowly, letting the bass motion (A♭ → B♭) and the sustained tonic note sit before resolving to C.
8. **Progression: A♭maj7 → G7sus4.** A♭maj7 (example 3) moving down to **G7sus4** — the sus4 is chosen deliberately to keep the harmonized tonic note held inside the dominant chord rather than resolving the suspension immediately, adding a second layer of delay before the actual resolution to C. → [[Suspended Chords]].
9. **Progression: E♭m6 → A♭7 → D♭major7.** A full **ii–V–I in D♭ major** (a half step up from the tonic key of C) substituted in ahead of the real resolution, so the ear briefly reads a key a half step up before the whole thing drops back down a half step onto C — described as revealing "where you are in the key" once it resolves.
10. **Chromatic descending chain: F half-diminished → F minor 6 → E7♯5 → E♭13 → D minor 7 → D♭major7 → (resolve half step down to C).** The biggest, most elaborate example — every chord in the chain still carries the tonic note, and the **bass line descends chromatically** step by step all the way down to the root before the final half-step resolution onto C. Combines examples 5's Fm6/Dø7 color, example 5's D♭maj7 half-step-up device, and altered-dominant color (E7♯5, E♭13) into one continuous chromatic-bass delaying gesture. → [[Chromatic Bass Lines]].

**General takeaway (9:09–9:28):** any chord that contains the tonic note being harmonized is fair game to try — experiment by landing on it first, worrying about labeling the theory afterward rather than up front; find your own way back to the true tonic chord.

**Resource plug (9:28–10:03):** Jared's own free chord chart "Chords with Color" (open-position shapes, multiple keys, major and minor) — a companion resource for finding extended-chord shapes to try this technique with. Free via `soundguitarlessons.com/chordswithcolor` — not a plugin/owned-tool match, a free external chart resource, not routed to the wishlist.

**Bonus tip — applying delayed resolution to single-note lines, not just chords (10:03–11:00):** the same idea works without the rhythm section changing anything — over a plain ii–V (Dm7–G7) heading to C, **superimpose/outline the arpeggio of a substitute chord in your lead line** before landing on the real target. Worked example: over the ii–V, outline **D♭major7** chord tones in the lead line, then resolve the line to the real target (Cmaj7) — same "hold the tension, avoid the expected note, then land" logic as the full-harmony examples, but applied purely melodically/in improvisation, so it works even when the harmony underneath stays plain.
