## Synopsis

Jens Larsen walks through the exact process he runs in the roughly ten seconds before playing a jazz standard he's handed cold at a jam or a gig — using a tune he already knows ("I Remember You," key of F) specifically so the worked example carries real, checkable detail rather than guesswork. The video is really a case study in **triage under time pressure**: identify the form before anything else, reduce the chord chart to a small set of recurring, nameable devices (rather than 32 bars of unrelated chords), read the melody just well enough to comp and add chords without clashing, and solo from harmonic *function* rather than chord-by-chord decoding. It's one of the clearest single-source statements in the vault of the "form first, then function, then note-by-note only as a last resort" reading philosophy, and it's explicitly framed as applying equally to slow woodshedding a new tune, not just live sight-reading. Most valuable parts: the concrete bar-by-bar analysis of "I Remember You" (including where Larsen deliberately overrides what's printed in the real book because it doesn't fit the melody), the melody-vs-voicing clash examples, and the reduce-to-functions soloing method.

## What's taught

**Form-first triage (the core method)**
- The first thing to establish on *any* lead sheet is the **form**, before looking at a single chord — most standards played as the chorus-only (no verse) run 32 bars in one of two shapes: **AABA** (one main 8-bar idea repeated three times with a contrasting 8-bar bridge) or **ABAC** (effectively two 16-bar halves). Knowing which one you're in tells you how much material you actually need to analyze, since large chunks repeat.
- Read the form off the page mechanically first: a repeat sign after 8 bars with two different endings (1st/2nd ending) signals AABA even when the book doesn't print "AABA" outright.
- **A part with two different endings + a distinct middle section that returns = AABA; a chorus that only ever splits into two big halves = ABAC.** Real books sometimes label the form explicitly — use that when it's there.
- The last A of an AABA form is often extended with a **tag** (extra bars tacked onto the end so the tune can properly close) — in "I Remember You" the last A grows from 8 bars to **12 bars** (a 4-bar tag), so effectively there are 6 bars' worth of *distinct* material to actually learn (A, B, and the tag), not 32.
- **Why this matters practically:** the whole point of theory and form-recognition here is **prediction** — being able to expect what a chord/passage will sound like *before* you play it, so you're not just reacting to a chord symbol you don't have an internalized sound for ("it says B♭m7, I wonder what that sounds like" is the failure mode to avoid). A solid read of the form gets you to ~95%+ confidence on what's coming, even accounting for the fact that a lead sheet always requires interpretation from whoever's actually playing it.
- **A specific, reusable prediction rule:** if a tune is AABA and the very first chord is the tonic (the root), there's a very high probability (Larsen estimates ~90%) that the last two bars of each A section are some kind of **turnaround** back to the top — meaning you already know your turnaround vocabulary in every key covers that spot before you've even analyzed it.
- Larsen treats music theory explicitly as **a description of what you hear**, not a rulebook — the value of functional harmony/classical theory here is that it's a reasonably accurate description *and* it's practical: it lets you group differently-notated progressions together once you recognize they act (and sound) the same way.
- **Generalize across tunes, not just within one.** The ♯4-diminished device that opens bar 2 of "I Remember You" (see below) also shows up in "Embraceable You" (resolving to the ii chord) and "You Do Something to Me" (same spot, same resolution) — recognizing a device from one tune inside a *different* tune is presented as the actual payoff of learning theory: once you've internalized what one instance sounds like, a new tune using the same device is already halfway analyzed.

**Worked analysis — "I Remember You" (key of F, AABA, last A has a 4-bar tag)**
- **Bar 1** — plain tonic, F major 7. Straightforward, nothing to watch for except normal voice-leading motion.
- **Bar 2** — printed in the real book as **Bm7 → E7**, but Larsen says it's rarely actually played that way; the far more common reading is **B half-diminished7 → E7♭9 → F**, i.e. a **♯4-diminished-family cadence** (the "sharp-4" sound) resolving to the tonic. → [[Diminished Chords]]
- Bars 3–4 — a **secondary cadence to the IV chord** (B♭), which in the tempo Larsen plays reduces in practice to just **F7 → B♭**. Common, expected move.
- Following that — **iv minor (B♭ minor) used as the cadence BACK to the tonic**, which Larsen flags as noticeably less common than the usual "IV → iv → I" (heartbreak-cadence) placement, since here it's doing double duty as the actual resolution device.
- End of each A — a **3-6-2-5 turnaround** back to the top (the "already known, throw it out the window" prediction described above). → [[Turnarounds]]
- **The 2nd A doesn't repeat the ending** — instead of the turnaround, it needs a cadence *into the bridge*, and the bridge's first move is (again) to the **IV chord** — so structurally, the tail of the 2nd A is best understood as "a cadence to the bridge" rather than analyzed chord-by-chord.
- **Bridge** — moves to B♭, then treats that B♭ as a **stepping-stone modulation to D major** (read as the mediant of B♭) via its own cadence; that pattern repeats, and then **D major flips to D minor** to pull the tune toward C major — the exact same "turn the major chord into its parallel minor to pivot key" device used in "How High the Moon," "Afternoon in Paris," and "Solar." C major is the V of the home key (F), so landing there sets up the cadence back into the last A.
- **Last A differs from the first two A's** — same opening ♯4-diminished move, but where the earlier A's cadence to B♭, this one cadences to **G minor (ii)** instead, purely because of what the melody is doing: cadencing to B♭ here would put the melody uncomfortably against the harmony, so the composer (or the standard arrangement) routes around it.
- The real book's printed harmonization of the tag (an **A minor with a G in the melody**) is common as a stock way to harmonize a tag in a lot of standards generally, **but Larsen doesn't play it here** — it doesn't fit this tune's melody well. His substitution: back to **F major7**, then in place of the book's clashing **D7** he plays **B♭ major7 → B♭ minor6** (a minor-iv sound), then a **3-6-2-5-shaped move** (A minor → C → D minor → G minor7 → C7) leading into the final turnaround back to the top of the form. **Takeaway modeled here, not just the specific substitution:** a printed real-book harmonization is a starting point, not gospel — when it visibly fights the melody, professional practice is to swap in something that serves the tune, and that's expected/normal among players who actually know the song, not a liberty you need permission for.

**Reading the melody well enough to comp (not full sight-singing)**
- Lead sheets are written in **concert pitch** — guitar is a transposing instrument that sounds an octave below what's written, so factor that in when judging where the melody actually sits before choosing voicings.
- If you're sight-reading behind someone else who's stating the head, you almost never need to play the melody note-for-note yourself — but you **do** need a working sense of where it sits so your comping voicings don't clash with it.
- **Concrete clash examples given:**
  - Over the **B half-diminished → E7** bar, if the melody is sitting on the **11th**, avoid stacking a chord tone (an F) directly next to it — the half-step-adjacent dissonance reads as a mistake, not color, unless it's a deliberate effect.
  - Over the **secondary dominant cadence to IV**, be careful with the **♭9** relative to the 3rd sitting on the downbeat; if the melody carries the **9th** (D) above an E♭, that's fine (the interval opens upward), but the same two notes with the 9th sitting *underneath* the E♭ is a half-step clash that needs to be intentional, not accidental.
  - General rule extracted: **check which chord tone/extension the melody is actually voicing, and don't place your own voicing a half-step away from it unless you mean the clash as a deliberate effect.**
- If you can't voice a full chord safely in time, it's not a disaster to lay out — as long as the bass note and the melody note are both covered (e.g., by a bassist), the harmony reads fine even with a comping instrument dropping voicings here and there. Add a chord whenever you safely can; don't force one.
- The practical payoff of learning many tunes this way: over time you build a bank of "this ♯4-diminished always wants the 7th of the scale in the melody," "this kind of minor 11 voicing is the one that recurs here" — so a brand-new-to-you standard increasingly resolves to *combinations you already have covered*, rather than needing fresh analysis every time.

**Comping behind a soloist**
- Play *less*, not more, when the soloist is busy; get more harmonically active in the gaps where they're laying out — comping is in service of the soloist, and constant full-density voicings crowd them out.
- You can strip comping down to almost nothing (root motion only, or chords only on beat 1) and it can still sound complete and musical — density is a choice, not an obligation.
- Overriding rule: **you're there to support**, so listen and adapt to what's actually happening rather than executing a fixed comping plan regardless of the soloist.

**Soloing by reducing to function, not decoding chord-by-chord**
- The same reduction used for reading (collapsing a whole bar to "this is a ♯4-diminished cadence") is reused for soloing: instead of tracking every individual chord symbol, reduce a passage to **which note you're moving from and which note you're targeting**, and let that (rather than a fresh scale choice per chord) drive the line.
- **Chunk multi-chord units into one concept**, the same way turnarounds get chunked: a full ii–V into IV reduces mentally to "the IV chord," a ii–V–I reduces to a single melodic arc, rather than three separate harmonic events to negotiate individually. Example given: the A section reduces to **F, ♯4-dim, F, F7, B♭, B♭m, [turnaround]** — a much shorter list to actually think in real time than the full chart.
- **Know which chord in a reduced unit is actually the important one** — in a 3-6-2-5 turnaround, the dominant chords are doing less harmonic work than the tonic-function chord they're passing through (e.g., in a iii-VI-ii-V, the vi/tonic-substitute chord, not the V7 at the end, is the chord actually worth targeting); collapsing everything down to "just play off the V7" loses that.
- **Reharmonizing on the fly is legitimate**, once the underlying function is understood — worked example: substituting a plain **Bm7 → E7** for the expected **B half-diminished → E7♭9** mid-solo, deliberately choosing the "further from home"/more surprising color as a contrast to the more expected sound played the first time through.
- **Chord-scale theory (matching one scale to one chord symbol) is called out explicitly as an approximation, not a rule** — it gets you "in the ballpark" (e.g., "play Dorian on a minor 7 chord") but a minor chord isn't always the same *kind* of minor chord in context, and ignoring the rest of the song's harmonic direction when picking a scale loses real information you need. Treat it as a fast first guess to refine with the functional/contextual read, not the final answer.
