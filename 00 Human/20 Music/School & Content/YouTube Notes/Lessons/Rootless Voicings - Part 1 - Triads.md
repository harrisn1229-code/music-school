# Rootless Voicings - Part 1 - Triads

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nVDy0zwMMJU)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nVDy0zwMMJU)

## Synopsis

Jens Larsen's practical, non-theoretical take on rootless voicings: instead of deriving them from scratch, take chord shapes you already know, strip the root, and you're left with a **triad** — and that triad is what you actually practice, move around, and decorate. The whole lesson is one system built from a single 2-5-1 in C (Dm7 → G7 → Cmaj7): drop the root from each of the three voicings and you get an F major triad (2nd inversion, for Dm7), a B diminished triad (root position, for G7), and an E minor triad (1st inversion, for Cmaj7). Larsen then shows the same triad shape covers **every inversion** of the underlying chord (root position, 1st inversion, 2nd inversion are all "the same trick" applied to different starting voicings), walks each triad diatonically through the C major scale as a fretboard-mapping drill, adds a melodic "suspend the top note" ornament that turns the exercise into a mini-melody generator, and closes with three worked example progressions (major 2-5-1 in C, minor 2-5-1 in A minor with harmonic-minor extensions ♭9/♭13 on the V, and a 3-6-2-5-1 in C). Deliberately framed as the practical companion to his earlier, more theoretical "Jazz Chord Essentials — Triads" lesson: this one assumes you already know some jazz voicings and shows what to actually *do* with them once you drop the root, rather than deriving triad-substitution theory from first principles. Directly overlaps with — and is a guitar-fingering-first companion to — the vault's existing [[Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords]] note (same 3-5-7-of-a-seventh-chord derivation, worked from a different starting angle: known voicing → strip root, rather than seventh-chord quality → triad-quality rule) and reinforces [[Chord Voicings]]'s existing "rootless = 3rd+7th+extensions, root droppable" material with concrete drop-2/drop-3-derived fretboard shapes.

## What's taught

- **The core move: any jazz 7th-chord voicing you already know, minus its root, is a triad — and that triad substitutes for the full chord.** Worked on a plain major 2-5-1 in C (Dm7–G7–Cmaj7): drop the root from each voicing and you get **F major triad (Dm7, 2nd inversion)**, **B diminished triad (G7, root position)**, **E minor triad (Cmaj7, 1st inversion)**. Matches the four-rule pattern already documented on [[Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords]] (minor 7 → major triad a minor 3rd above the root; dominant 7 → diminished triad a major 3rd above; major 7 → minor triad a major 3rd above) — this video arrives at the same three answers from the opposite direction (strip a known voicing) rather than deriving from the seventh-chord-quality rule table.
- **Why drop the root at all (stated reasons, both practical, not just theoretical):** (1) avoid clashing with the bass player, who already has the root and may not even be playing straight roots; (2) frees a finger, which matters specifically because jazz comping means reharmonizing on the fly while playing — fewer fixed notes per grip means faster chord-to-chord changes.
- **A single triad's three inversions cover the same seventh chord's three most commonly-played voicing shapes** — Larsen explicitly frames 1st-inversion and 2nd-inversion triads as the ones guitarists already know without realizing it ("we already know them, because it's the top of this one and the top of that one"), while the **root-position triad is the one nobody plays as a bare chord shape** and is worth deliberately learning because it unlocks a voicing shape you can't otherwise reach in root position. *(general note: root-position/1st-inversion/2nd-inversion terminology here refers to the triad's own inversion, not the underlying 7th chord's bass note — the whole chord is rootless regardless of which triad inversion is used.)*
- **Fretboard-mapping drill: walk each triad inversion diatonically through the C major scale, thinking the implied 7th-chord root the whole time.** Two full worked cycles given: (1) **2nd-inversion triad**, derived from a drop-3 Fmaj7 voicing with the root removed → A minor triad (2nd inversion) → moved through the scale = Fmaj7-G7-Am7-Bm7♭5-Cmaj7-Dm7-Em7-Fmaj7; (2) **1st-inversion triad**, derived from the Cmaj7 voicing with the root removed → E minor triad (1st inversion) → moved through the scale = Bm7♭5-Cmaj7-Dm7-Em7-Fmaj7-G7-Am7-Bm7♭5. The point of the drill isn't the triad shape alone — it's holding the mental map of "which 7th chord this triad currently stands in for" while the shape slides. → [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]] for the general positional-triad method this drill is an instance of.
- **The root-position triad gets the same treatment as a third, separately-drilled case**, e.g. Dm7-Em7-Fmaj7-G7-Am7-Bm7♭5-Cmaj7-Dm7 — explicitly called the least-familiar of the three inversions and the one worth deliberately practicing precisely because it opens a voicing you couldn't otherwise grab.
- **Melodic ornament: "suspend the top note" — hold the diatonic note one scale-step above the target melody note, then resolve down into the triad.** Demonstrated across all three triad inversions (2nd inversion is easiest; 1st inversion works; root position is "a bit trickier but possible"). The point: once you're only playing three notes, the freed-up attention lets you treat the top note as a real melody rather than a static chord tone — a direct, small-scale instance of [[Chord Voicings]]'s existing "the top note is a melody" voice-leading rule.
- **Minor 2-5-1 in A minor, with harmonic-minor extensions on the V — the concrete instance of "if a dominant resolves to a minor chord, use harmonic minor on it."** Bm7♭5 (as its rootless triad) → E7 (using harmonic minor's raised leading tone, giving the dominant its ♭9 and ♭13 as the two extensions demonstrated) → A minor (as a C major triad, i.e. Am7's rootless upper structure). Two worked variants shown back to back: one voicing the E7 triad with the ♭9 as the color note, one with the ♭13, "just so you have an idea how that sounds." Matches [[The ii-V-I]]'s existing minor-ii-V-i entry (Dm7♭5→G7♭9→Cm7 in C minor) transposed to A minor — this is the same device (minor V7 pulls its color tones from harmonic minor) worked in a new key with the rootless-triad voicings attached.
- **3-6-2-5-1 in C, full worked example chaining the whole system:** iii (Em7, as a G major triad) → suspend the top note → VI (A7, using a 2nd-inversion diminished-triad shape, adding the ♭13 as the color extension — the same harmonic-minor-on-the-dominant logic as the A minor example, since A7 here is functioning as a secondary dominant resolving toward D minor) → ii (Dm7, as an F major triad, again suspending the top note) → V (G7, as a B diminished triad) → I (Cmaj7, as an E minor triad).
- **Practice-order takeaway (implicit but stated plainly as advice):** get comfortable with the 1st- and 2nd-inversion triads first (they're already familiar shapes), *then* deliberately add the root-position triad once the concept clicks — it's harder to recall but pays off with a voicing you can't get any other way.
- **Explicitly positioned relative to his other lesson** ("Jazz Chord Essentials — Triads," referenced but not linked/findable in this vault) as the practical, apply-it-now companion — that older lesson is framed as the more systematic/theoretical derivation of the same underlying triad-substitution idea.
