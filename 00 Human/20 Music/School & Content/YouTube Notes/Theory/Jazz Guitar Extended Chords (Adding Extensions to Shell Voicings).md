# Jazz Guitar Extended Chords (Adding Extensions to Shell Voicings)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=T6o4gzWWIQQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=T6o4gzWWIQQ)

## Synopsis

A practical, root-position "go-to shape" system for adding one extension at a time to [[Guitar Shell Voicings - The Ultimate Guide|shell voicings]] — the third video in a chord-mastery series (built on shell voicings and extension theory covered in the two prior entries). The core organizing rule, stated up front: **shell voicings with the root on the 5th string get 9th-family extensions added; shell voicings with the root on the 6th string get everything else (11, ♯11, 13, and altered/natural 5ths) added.** The video then works through every common jazz chord quality (maj7, 6, dom7, m7, m7♭5, dom7sus4, m6, mMaj7, dim7), stating which extensions are idiomatic/usable for each and which aren't (and why), closing with two worked musical examples ("You've Got a Friend in Me," a Miles Davis and a Bill Evans tune via iReal Pro) and an explicit philosophy: know ONE reliable go-to voicing per extension type before worrying about the full universe of substitutions/rootless voicings (reserved for a stated future video).

## What's taught

- **The core organizing rule**: 5th-string-rooted shell voicing + a 9th-family note on top (♭9, 9, ♯9) = add-9 chords; 6th-string-rooted shell voicing + anything else (11, ♯11, 13, ♭13, natural/altered 5) = the rest. One consistent physical logic across all chord types, rather than memorizing a separate shape per chord/extension combination.
- **Per-chord-type extension usability map (the video's real payload — which extensions are idiomatic and which don't exist/aren't used):**
  - **Major 7**: + 9 → maj9 (very common, "the most ubiquitous useful major seven shape"). NOT ♭9 or ♯9 (not in the common tonal vocabulary — would require an unused synthetic scale). NOT natural 11 (doesn't function musically over maj7 — as a rule of thumb, a "wrong" extension either doesn't get used or reclassifies the chord as something else entirely, e.g. m6 = 1st-inversion maj7, illustrated as a worked aside). + ♯11 → maj7♯11 ("beautiful chord," common). + 13 → called maj13 (same physical note as the 6, renamed because a 7th is already present). + 5 (natural, plain) is available; maj7♯5 exists as its own named chord type — the iii chord of both harmonic and melodic minor.
  - **Major 6**: + 9 → 6/9 chord ("gorgeous"). + ♯11 possible but rare/uncommon on a lead sheet. + 5 available. NOT + 13 (redundant — the 6 already occupies that scale degree).
  - **Dominant 7 ("a free-for-all — we get almost everything")**: + ♭9, 9, or ♯9 all usable (C9, C7♭9, C7♯9). NOT natural 11. + ♯11 → same physical voicing as dominant 7♭5 (enharmonically identical shape, different implied harmony/scale depending on which name is used — ♯11 implies the natural 5 is also present, ♭5 implies it's absent). + natural 5, or ♯5/♭13 (same shape, same enharmonic-equivalence caveat), or 13.
  - **Minor 7**: + ♭9 is a "modern" sound, not traditionally considered a functional chord but usable, especially in modal contexts. + 9 → very common (m9). NOT ♯9 (duplicates the chord's own ♭3). + 11 → m11, described as always available/"gorgeous." m7♭5 (half-diminished) is its own distinct chord type, not derived by adding a ♭5 to m7. + 5 available generally; NOT ♭13. + 13 → an m13-type sound, described as functionally close to a minor 6 chord with the 7th also present.
  - **Half-diminished (m7♭5)**: no further extensions added — the video's only addition here is the ♭5 itself, since the shell-voicing version doesn't include the 5th at all and the ♭5 is what makes the chord's identity audible.
  - **Dominant 7sus4**: + ♭9 usable ("beautiful chord"). NOT ♯11. + natural 5. + ♭13 or 13 both usable.
  - **Minor 6**: + 9 is essentially the only common extension ("just a gorgeous chord, very rich"). + 5 available.
  - **Minor(major7)**: + 9 usable. + 5 available.
  - **Diminished 7**: no extensions added when rooted on the 5th or 6th string — just add the ♭5 to complete/color the chord if desired; a ♭13 (via pinky, barring the root) is common as a melodic passing tone over this chord specifically.
- **Multiple simultaneous extensions** (e.g. C7♭9♭13): the video's stated recommendation is to keep bouncing between the single-extension go-to shapes covered here rather than reaching for combined-extension voicings yet — full multi-extension/rootless voicings explicitly deferred to a future video in the series.
- **Two worked musical examples, confirming the theory against real material:**
  - "You've Got a Friend in Me" — cited for melodic use of extensions: E♭ major triad → B7♯5 (= B7♭13, same shape) → E♭9 (E♭7 with a 9).
  - iReal Pro run-throughs of Miles Davis's "Blue in Green" (Gm6/9, A7♯9, Dm7 with an added 11, D♭7, C7 with an added 11, F7 with an added 9, B♭maj7♯11) and an unnamed Bill Evans tune (mostly played as written on the chart, occasionally adding a 5 or extension by ear).
- **"Dominant 7♯9" name-check**: nicknamed "the Jimi Hendrix chord" for its use in "Purple Haze."
