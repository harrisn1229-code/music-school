# Functional Harmony - Chord substitutions for songwriters

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aPMe6_hrowg)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aPMe6_hrowg)

## Synopsis

A songwriter-facing (not jazz-facing) explainer of **functional harmony** from Jared Borkowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared), pitched squarely at using T/SD/D grouping to find **chord substitutions that keep an existing melody intact**. Unlike the vault's jazz-analysis sources on this topic (Jens Larsen, Nathan Borton, Jack Ruch — all cataloged on [[Chord Progressions]] and [[Chord Substitution]]), this video works the *proof* end of the mechanism explicitly and numerically: it names the exact scale-degree makeup of every tonic/subdominant/dominant chord and shows, degree by degree, that each function-mate pair shares exactly two notes with its "root" chord — the concrete arithmetic behind a rule the vault's other sources mostly assert. It then runs a full worked demo: writes a chord progression + melody + lyric, then substitutes chords one at a time (staying inside each function group) while proving the melody still sits comfortably on top, including a bar substituted with the rarely-used vii°7 (fully diminished 7th on the leading tone) as a deliberate "works better than you'd expect" example. Closes with two secondary payoffs: (1) a wider philosophical point — function-thinking generalizes past chords to any element of a song (a lyric, an instrument's role, a whole section), and the T/SD/D labels themselves are just one person's theorizing, not fixed law; (2) a pointer to a second, unrelated substitution method (adding extensions to an existing chord) via his free "Chords with Color" chart. ## What's taught

- **Roman-numeral basics as a lead-in.** Seven scale degrees, seven diatonic chords, labeled I–vii; uppercase = major, lowercase = minor, ° = diminished — standard notation, same convention as [[Chord Progressions]] § *Roman numeral analysis*.
- **The three functions, defined by feel/role, not just by label.** **Tonic** = home base, the main arrival point, a stable area. **Dominant** = unstable, points toward tonic, creates tension/momentum that wants to resolve home. **Subdominant** = simply comes *before* the dominant (hence its other name, **predominant**). Matches [[Chord Progressions]] § *Harmonic function (T / SD / D)* exactly.
- **Function groupings, major key:** Tonic = **I, iii, vi**. Dominant = **V, vii°**. Subdominant = **ii, IV**. Flags vi as dual-function — usable as either tonic or subdominant depending on context — and notes explicitly that even "official" functional-harmony labels tolerate exceptions: *"you can break the rules, but this is the basic foundation."*
- **The governing substitution rule, stated plainly:** *"you can replace any chord with another chord that has the same function."*
- **Why it works — the shared-tone proof, worked with actual scale-degree numbers for all three function groups (the vault's most concrete version of this mechanism to date).** Any triad is the upper 3 notes of a 7th chord, any 7th chord the upper 4 notes of a 9th chord — chords "pile" on shared notes. Worked degree-by-degree in a generic major key:
  - **I** = scale degrees {1, 3, 5}. **iii** = {3, 5, 7} → shares **3 and 5** with I.
  - **vi** = {6, 1, 3} → shares **1 and 3** with I.
  - **V** = {5, 7, 2}. **vii°** = {7, 2, 4} → shares **7 and 2** with V.
  - **IV** = {4, 6, 1}. **ii** = {2, 4, 6} → shares **4 and 6** with IV.
  Every function-mate pair shares exactly **two** scale-degree tones with the chord whose function it's substituting for — this is the general form of the rule [[Chord Progressions]] states only for the tonic group ("vi & iii substitute for I because they share 2 of its 3 notes") and [[Chord Substitution]] §1 states for diatonic thirds-apart pairs generally; this video derives it explicitly for **all three** function groups rather than just the tonic one, filling in the dominant (V/vii°) and subdominant (IV/ii) cases with the same tone-count logic.
- **The core songwriter workflow this whole video demonstrates: write melody + lyric over a chord progression FIRST, then substitute chords while keeping every substitute inside its original function group — the melody survives untouched because function (not exact chord identity) is what the melody was actually built against.** Stated directly as the constraint: *"we can't mess with them too much because we don't want to also change the melody, and that melody works because we wrote it over those chords."*
- **Worked demo, full chord letters (key of C major).** Started progression: **I–vi–ii–V** (C–Am–Dm–G), repeated once, with an original melody and lyric ("I hate being tired... I hate being tired but I love sleeping") written over it. Substituted, function-group-by-function-group:
  - **iii for vi** (Em for Am) — both tonic-functioning.
  - **IV for ii** (F for Dm) — both subdominant-functioning.
  - **V unchanged** (G) — still the dominant anchor.
  - On the repeat: **I and vi swapped with each other** (C↔Am, both tonic) rather than each independently subbed.
  - **IV revoiced as a slash chord** — F/C ("four with C in the bass") — a subdominant substitution that's really a re-voicing (inversion) of the same chord rather than a different scale degree; ties directly to [[Chord Inversions]]' bass-note-reinterpretation techniques already logged from this same channel.
  - **vii°7 for V** (B°7 for G) — the fully diminished 7th built on the leading tone, substituting for the dominant. Flagged explicitly on-camera as *"very rare in a songwriting context, but it's gonna work quite well"* — a concrete, deliberately-unusual instance of §*dominant-functioning chords* (V, vii°) from the T/SD/D table, worth cross-linking to [[Diminished Chords]] as a real (if rare) songwriting use of a fully-diminished 7th as a straight dominant substitute rather than a passing/approach chord.
  - Result played back with the original melody and lyric plus an added closing phrase — confirmed on camera to still sit comfortably, proving the substitution-within-function-group approach.
- **The stated payoff, direct quote:** *"the function that each chord was serving for the song doesn't change, really, even after swapping them out — the song itself still behaves like it did before... we haven't really done anything to actually change the underlying harmonic quality of the functions of the chords and the chord progression."* Framed as *freshness without disruption* — the explicit goal of this whole technique (there are separate techniques, not covered here, for actually changing a progression's underlying harmonic quality).
- **Scope/honesty caveat on functional harmony itself:** it's a specific tonal-Western-music dialect that "isn't actually used in most popular music today" — but the underlying skill (seeing how chords overlap, and using that to find substitutions) is useful regardless of genre or whether the strict T/SD/D framework applies.
- **A broader creative-process point, generalizing past chords entirely:** function-thinking applies to *any* element of a piece of music — an instrument's role coming in, a lyric, a whole section of a song — always worth asking *"what is the function of this, and how is it serving the ultimate goal of what I'm trying to express."* Paired with a permission-giving note that the T/SD/D labels aren't handed-down law: *"these terms didn't always exist, someone theorized them into existence, so don't hesitate to be your own theorist."*
- **A second, unrelated substitution method, named but not demonstrated in this video:** take an existing chord and add extensions to it for a different flavor of alternative — pointed at his free downloadable resource **"Chords with Color"** (soundguitarlessons.com/chordswithcolor), which charts every diatonic chord in 5 keys plus every extension alternative per chord. Not extracted further here since it's an external resource, not a technique taught on-screen — flagged for reference only.
- **Series pointer:** next lesson in his series covers quartal harmony (chords built in 4ths) — not otherwise documented in this vault yet; worth watching for when it lands in the queue.
