# The SIMPLE Chord Shapes That Every PRO Guitarist LOVES

## Synopsis

Daniel Seriff teaches a rhythm-guitar system for playing pro-sounding dominant-7th comping over a classic 12-bar blues in A, explicitly framed against the CAGED system's full barre-chord shapes, which he argues carry too much doubled information and eat up sonic space in a real band mix. The lesson (inspired by Tom Bukovac's idea that a guitarist's job is to "play in the spaces the rest of the musicians have left") walks a I–IV–V (A7–D7–E7) blues through three layers: smaller top-4-string dominant-7th voicings with the root left for the bass, the Charleston comping rhythm, chromatic approach-chord decorations, and finally dominant 9/13 color extensions built by shifting one note of the same shape a whole step. Also covers a practical practice method (isolate one chord at a time against a drum loop rather than a metronome). ## What's taught

- **Framing: play in the space left by the rest of the band.** Lesson opens by crediting Tom Bukovac's idea that a guitarist's job is to play in the spaces the other musicians have left — both tonally (register/voicing choice) and sonically (not occupying frequency space others need). Motivates the whole "smaller voicings" approach that follows.
- **Why full CAGED barre shapes are the wrong tool here.** The classic CAGED system is good for learning big shapes and connecting them to scales/arpeggios across the neck, but a full 6-string barre chord is mostly duplicate notes (a triad played 2–3× over), eats a ton of sonic space, and can make a real band mix feel worse — there's no room left for the rest of the band.
- **The progression: 12-bar blues in A, dominant blues (not major blues).** I7–IV7–V7 = A7, D7, E7 — each a 1-3-5-♭7 dominant seventh, not a major triad with an add-on.
- **Building the dominant 7 from the E-shape barre.** Start from the classic E-shape A barre chord at the 5th fret (root on the low E string): E-A-C♯-E-A, essentially a triad with heavy note doubling. To turn it into A7, take the root note on the D string (7th fret) and lower it a whole step to the ♭7 (G) — result reads 1-5-♭7-3-1 bottom to top. Full shape works but is still too dense for most situations.
- **The core move: drop the low E and A strings, play only the top 4 strings, let the bass carry the root.** Cutting the two lowest strings from that shape leaves ♭7 as the lowest note played — technically a chord inversion (♭7 in the "bass") — but since the actual bass player has the real root, it doesn't read as an inversion at all. Explicitly warns against the common trap of thinking your guitar part alone determines what the listener hears — the perceived chord is the sum of everything the band plays (bass, drums, keys, vocal), so don't overthink a guitar-only "inversion."
- **A second, more flexible top-4-string system: dominant 7th shapes built off the D-shape.** Rather than the classic A-shape dominant 7 barre next door (solid but not very colorable), Seriff uses D-shape-derived 7th-chord voicings, all living on the top 4 strings — the same string set as the reduced E-shape voicing above, so everything stays high in pitch, up where "rhythm guitar sounds so good." Four such shapes exist (all included in the video's download); the lesson uses two of them directly:
  - **A7** — top 4 strings (D-G-B-e), frets **X X 5 6 5 5**.
  - **D7** — same string set, frets **X X 7 7 7 8**.
  - **E7** — the same D7 shape moved up a whole step (2 frets): **X X 9 9 9 10**.
- **The Charleston rhythm — the single most important comping rhythm in blues/early jazz.** Named for its origin in early blues/jazz dance music. Play the chord stab on **beat 1** and again on the **"and" of beat 2** (i.e., 1 . . . & . . .) — leave the rest of the bar open. Recommended as a default comping rhythm to lean on any time you don't know what else to play; drilled across the entire 12-bar form against a backing track.
- **Chromatic approach decorations, three variants — makes any chord change sound "10× better."**
  - **One fret below, played as a grace note just before the downbeat** (a quick slide up into the target chord right before beat 1, or before any target beat).
  - **The same one-fret-below approach played squarely ON the beat instead of as a pre-beat grace note** (e.g. landing the approach chord on beat 4, then resolving to the target on beat 1) — a different rhythmic placement of the same interval move.
  - **Two frets below**, giving more of a "walk-up" feel with real momentum into the target chord — demonstrated moving into both the A and the D shapes this way.
  - Also mentions approaching **from above** (a half step higher, sliding down into the target) as a tension-creating variant of the same idea. Recommendation: mix approach directions rather than using only one.
- **Extending to dominant 9: raise the shape's "root" note a whole step.** Since the bass has the actual root, the guitar is free to move its own root note up a whole step to the 2nd scale degree, which — an octave up — reads as the **9**. On the A7 shape (X X 5 6 5 5), the root lives on the high E string (5th fret); raising it a whole step to the 7th fret turns A7 into **A9 (X X 5 6 5 7)**. Same move applies to the D7 shape (raise the note that functions as its root — on the G string, 7th fret — up a whole step to 9) and to the E7 shape identically, giving **D9** and **E9** as whole-step-up variants of the same fingerings. Rule generalizes: "take the root you were playing inside the shape, move it up a whole step, you've got the dominant 9."
- **Extending to dominant 13: raise the shape's 5th a whole step instead.** On the A7 shape, the 5th lives on the B string (5th fret); raising it a whole step gives the **6th**, which in extension language is the **13** — **A13 (X X 5 6 7 5)**. Same move applies analogously to the D7 and E7 shapes to get **D13** and **E13**.
- **Extensions are for motion, not for parking.** The point of the 9 and 13 variants isn't to replace the plain 7th chord outright — moving between plain-7, 9, and 13 versions of the *same shape* while comping (adding and removing the extension note as you play) creates harmonic motion within a static chord, which reads as more musical than holding one fixed voicing through the whole bar.
- **Origin note on the extension concept:** credits Barry Green for the underlying idea — that you can spice up dominant chords without cramming your fingers into bigger, harder shapes, just by substituting one note within a shape you already know.
- **Practice method: isolate one chord at a time against a drum loop, not a metronome.** If the full 12-bar form (with rhythm + approach notes) is too much at once, drop the metronome and instead loop a single bar/chord against a real drum loop — a drum loop "beats a metronome every day of the week." Named loop sources: **Jake Reed**, **Yurt Rock** (a company), **AJ Hall**. Using a loop instead of playing through the whole form lets you isolate and drill each chord individually rather than needing the full progression to practice. General principle stated explicitly: breaking a part down into simpler chunks is one of the best ways to master something slightly beyond your current technical ability.
