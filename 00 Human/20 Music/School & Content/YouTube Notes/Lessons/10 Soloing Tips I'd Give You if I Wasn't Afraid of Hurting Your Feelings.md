# 10 Soloing Tips I'd Give You if I Wasn't Afraid of Hurting Your Feelings

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nRfrWXMjwSc)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nRfrWXMjwSc)

## Synopsis

Brandon D'Eon lists 10 "hidden" intermediate-level soloing mistakes — deliberately skipping the commonly-repeated ones (staying stuck in the box, sounding too scalar) in favor of subtler habits players usually can't hear in their own playing. Several tie back to his other videos in this batch (fretboard-anchoring, rhythmic-subdivision limits, constraint-based practice), forming a coherent picture of his overall pedagogy across the channel.

## Techniques

- **Mistake 1 — Leaving the scale before it's actually learned.** The stated diagnosis for a shaky, unconfident-sounding solo usually isn't "too scalar" — it's the opposite: the player doesn't know the scale shape well enough to even be guilty of over-scalar playing. Fix: don't move to variety/deviation until the underlying scale shape is genuinely solid.
- **Mistake 2 — Escaping the box before mastering it.** Most players know only one scale shape (the minor pentatonic "box") and still can't reliably target chord tones from within it. Worked example: over Em–G–D–C, target the chord tones of each chord using notes available inside that single box shape before ever moving to a second position. Get good at chord-tone targeting in one familiar shape before adding new positions.
- **Mistake 3 — Starting every line on beat 1.** Most players default to starting phrases on the downbeat because it's the most instinctive entry point. Fix: deliberately start lines on other beats/offbeats — named as one of the things separating beginner improvisers from professional ones, since always-beat-1 phrasing limits rhythmic variety.
- **Mistake 4 — Always starting/targeting the root.** A byproduct of how scales are usually practiced (always starting from the root note) — "the way you practice is the way you play." Fix: practice starting scale runs from other scale degrees, not just the root, so soloing lines stop being magnetically drawn back to it.
- **Mistake 5 — Rhythmic sloppiness in single-note lines (not chords).** Diagnosed as the single worst intermediate mistake — described as players who are technically capable of the notes but stumble because they're unsure of themselves in real time. The mechanism: improvising requires anticipating a chord change a few beats before it lands, like timing a jump onto a moving train car — jump too early or too late and the landing is wrong. **Fix: know what you're going to play before you play it** — playing with intention (pre-decided) rather than uncertainty (searching for notes as you go) is what produces rhythmic precision.
- **Mistake 6 — Playing faster than your actual limit.** Concrete self-diagnostic: find your personal **16th-note-limit tempo** (the fastest BPM you can play clean unbroken 16th notes at). From that one number, every other subdivision's safe tempo is derivable by simple ratio: 16th-note limit of 100 BPM → clean 8th notes up to 200 BPM, triplets up to ~133 BPM, sextuplets up to ~67 BPM (same physical motion rate, different subdivision labels). Push past your derived ceiling for a given subdivision and lines get sloppy. *(General starting-point framework — the specific BPM numbers are worked examples, not universal targets; find your own 16th-note limit and derive the rest.)*
- **Mistake 7 — Abandoning lines/motifs too early.** Most improvised solos never repeat a motif — a line gets played once and dropped rather than developed. Root cause named: not knowing the fretboard/key well enough to safely continue an idea in a new direction without hitting wrong notes, so players unconsciously bail rather than risk it. Fix requires deeper fretboard/key knowledge, not just willpower.
- **Mistake 8 — No silence.** Simplest fix of the ten: deliberately leave space — stop playing periodically rather than filling every beat. Framed as low-effort, high-payoff — "silence is music too."
- **Mistake 9 — Restarting/going silent at every chord change (advanced mistake).** Named as showing up specifically over harmonically complex, multi-key progressions (not simple one-key progressions, which are more forgiving). When a bar's chord changes cross a key boundary mid-bar, players who can't elegantly connect the two often just stop playing through that transition ("no man's land") rather than finding a connecting line — this repeated stop-start pattern reads as amateurish. Fix: practice specifically targeting the transition beat/chord rather than treating it as a gap to survive.
- **Mistake 10 — Never practicing with constraints.** The meta-fix for all nine mistakes above: assign yourself a **specific constraint designed to attack one specific weakness** — e.g. force yourself to stay in one fretboard position, restrict yourself to only eighth notes, or force yourself to leave more space — rather than practicing "generally." Framed as the highest-leverage practice habit on the list because it turns a diagnosed weakness directly into a targeted drill.
