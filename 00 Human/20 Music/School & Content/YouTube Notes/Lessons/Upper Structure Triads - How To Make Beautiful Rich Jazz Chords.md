# Upper Structure Triads - How To Make Beautiful Rich Jazz Chords

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PRimvO-dvPE)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PRimvO-dvPE)

## Synopsis

Jens Larsen builds four full-neck, four-or-five-note ii-V-I chord voicings out of upper structure triads, and spends most of the video on the *derivation method* rather than just handing over shapes. The core idea: an upper structure triad voicing is a small triad (usually major or minor) stacked on top of just enough of the underlying chord's guide tones (typically the 3rd and/or 7th, sometimes the root) to imply the full harmony — the opposite process from shell/drop-2 voicings, where you learn one fixed shape and run it through inversions. Because there's no fixed shape to invert, Jens frames finding upper structure triads as an active *search*: take every diatonic triad available in the chord's scale, relate each one back to the chord's root in scale-degree terms, then keep only the ones that avoid an unwanted tension (he specifically avoids stray 13ths on the minor 7 chord and the avoid-note 11/♮4 on the major 7 chord in his worked examples) — the same search extended to the diminished (octatonic) scale for the altered dominant, which yields eight usable triads (four major, four minor) a minor 3rd apart instead of a diatonic set, since a whole-tone/diminished scale doesn't stack into tertian triads the normal way. All four sets are built over the same three-chord frame (Dm7-G7(alt)-Cmaj7, i.e. a plain major ii-V-I in C), so the four sets function as four different "answers" to the same harmonic question, letting the derivation method and the resulting shapes be compared directly. The video also demonstrates a second, purely mechanical way to find these voicings: take a triad shape you already know from one set, invert it up the neck into a new fretboard position, and find the nearest available guide tone (root/3rd/7th) to complete the chord — useful once the theory-driven search gets slow. ## What's taught

**Framing (~0:00-1:00):** Upper structure triad voicings are typically 4-5 notes and sound noticeably richer/more colorful than shell voicings or plain drop-2 grips. The key mental shift: shell/drop-2 voicings are one fixed shape worked through inversions and scale positions; upper structure voicings have no single fixed shape — each one is found by scanning what triads are actually *available* inside the chord's scale, so the skill is search-and-select, not memorize-and-transpose. Four ii-V-I sets are built over the same progression (effectively Dm7-G7-Cmaj7) to compare four different derivations side by side.

**The two derivation methods (~1:00-2:00):** (1) Stack the chord in thirds and extend the stack all the way up through the available scale (root-3rd-5th-7th-9th-11th-13th), then look at which three-note windows in that stack form usable triads. (2) **The more generally useful method, especially on non-tertian scales** (diminished, altered): take every diatonic triad in the chord's parent scale and relate each one's three notes back to the chord's root in scale-degree terms (e.g. "this triad reads as the 3rd, 5th, and 7th"), then filter by which scale degrees you do/don't want in the voicing. Method 2 is what carries the rest of the video, including the diminished-scale example where stacking thirds isn't meaningful.

**Set 1 — full diatonic-triad-relative-to-root derivation (~2:00-6:30):**
- **Dm7 (drop-2-style voicing):** a **C major triad** (relates to D as 7-9-11) is played over an added **F** (the 3rd) to complete a **Dm11(9)** sound — the 7th (C) is already inside the triad so only the 3rd needs adding underneath. *Full derivation, all seven diatonic triads of the D Dorian/C major scale related to D as root:* C major = 7-9-11 · D minor = root-♭3-5 (the chord itself, not useful as an "upper structure") · E minor = 9-11-13 · **F major = ♭3-5-7** · G major = 11-13-root · **A minor = 5-7-9** · B diminished = 13-root-♭3. Jens doesn't want a 13th in this voicing, so every triad containing B (E minor, G major, B diminished) is discarded, leaving three usable upper structures for Dm7: **C major (7-9-11)**, **F major (♭3-5-7)** — the latter matching the fixed minor-7→major-triad rule on [[Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords]] — and **A minor (5-7-9)**.
- **G7 (altered dominant), diminished-scale derivation:** the chosen voicing reads as an **E major triad over G7**, sourced from the (half-whole) diminished scale rather than a diatonic major scale — the diminished scale carries a ♭9 and (as transcribed) a 13-type tension, and being symmetrical rather than tertian it doesn't stack into thirds the normal way, so method 2 (relate triads to root) is the only practical approach. Because the diminished scale repeats every minor 3rd, its available major/minor triad pairs also repeat every minor 3rd: **E major/E minor, G major/G minor, B♭ major/B♭ minor, D♭ major/D♭ minor** — eight usable triads total. The triad built on the chord's own root (G major/G minor) is skipped because it doesn't add any color beyond the plain chord tone.
- **Cmaj7, diatonic-triad derivation with a scale-choice twist:** the voicing is an **A minor triad, 1st inversion** (reads as 13-root-3rd) with a **B added** to complete a **Cmaj7add13** sound. *Full derivation relative to C as root:* C major = root-3-5 (the chord itself) · D minor = 9-11-13 · **E minor = 3-5-7** · F major = 11-13-root · **G major = 5-7-9** · **A minor = 13-root-3** · B diminished = 7-9-11. The 11th (F) is an avoid-note against a major 7 chord, so every triad containing F (D minor, F major) is discarded, leaving C major (not much color), E minor, G major, and A minor. **Scale-choice note:** switching the parent scale from C major (Ionian) to C Lydian (♯4/♯11 instead of natural F) removes the F avoid-note constraint entirely, which reopens D minor and F major as usable upper structures too — a direct illustration of how the *scale choice*, not just the chord quality, determines which triads are available.

**Set 2 — fretboard-construction method, same triad choices as Set 1 (~6:30-9:00):** rather than re-deriving triads theoretically, take a triad shape you already know (the C major triad from Set 1's Dm7 voicing) and **invert it up the neck into a new fretboard position**, then find the nearest available guide tone to complete the chord — for the Dm7 voicing that's locating the closest F to add under each inversion of the C major triad. Repeated across multiple neck positions to build a full fretboard map of the same Dm11(9) sound. For G7, the voicing shown uses (as best reconstructed from a badly garbled caption passage) an **E♭-family triad** implying a half-diminished/altered color, again completed by adding the missing F; Jens flags this section explicitly as "figure out what notes are missing in the chord, then find a way to play them under the triad" — the fretboard-construction method in one sentence. For Cmaj7, the set closes on what's transcribed as a **G major triad** upper structure (5-7-9) with the missing 3rd (E) added underneath, plus an optional extra A woven in between voices — Jens calls out that *this specific choice of which extra note to double* is itself a deliberate voice-leading decision, not a default.

**Set 3 — deliberately harder/sparser voicings (~9:00-12:30) *(lower confidence — this section's caption track is the most garbled in the video, note names below are approximate)*:** Dm7 again uses the C major triad, this time in a different inversion, with F added underneath and a **deliberate gap where the 7th would sit** — Jens explicitly says he tried to fill it but the fingering became impractical, so he leaves the gap rather than forcing an unplayable stretch, framing "some notes just don't fit under the thumb" as an acceptable real-world tradeoff. The G7 voicing here is called out as the hardest in the video, built from the **G altered scale (G7alt = A♭ melodic minor)**'s diatonic triads related to root — B♭ major (♭9-3-5), B♭ minor (♯9-♭5-♭9... as transcribed), A♭ diminished and G diminished among the options — landing on an **E♭ major triad**, described as giving a **♭13/♯9** color, which Jens singles out as the *less common but genuinely nice* choice for a tension-rich ballad dominant sound versus the more commonly used B♭-minor-based version of the same G7alt sound. Resolves to a **Cmaj7** voicing built from stacked-in-thirds E minor territory, called difficult to play but a "nice, different" sound worth having in the vocabulary occasionally.

**Set 4 — voice-leading-driven, chosen for smooth motion between chords rather than color alone (~12:30-16:00):** Dm7 uses an **A minor triad** upper structure (5-7-9) with F added, doubling the melody note A. G7 moves to a **B♭ minor triad** upper structure with F retained/doubled — the half-step triad shift from A minor to B♭ minor is explicitly the point: smooth chromatic voice-leading between the two upper structures rather than a fresh derivation. Resolves to Cmaj7 using an **E major triad** upper structure with E added in the bass; where the Set-3/earlier Cmaj7 voicings added an A as the extra doubled note, this one adds a **C** instead specifically because the resulting bass-voice motion sounds better in context — reiterated as a genuine taste call, not a rule.

**Closing framing (~16:00-17:27):** the choice of *which* extra note to double (the A vs. the C on the closing Cmaj7, for example) is presented as pure ear-driven taste, not a formula — different doublings carry different colors and different voice-leading consequences, and working that out by ear is framed as real ear training in its own right, alongside knowing triad inversions and scales. These five-note upper structure voicings are called out as best suited to **sustained, low-rhythmic moments** where color matters more than comping density, rather than fast rhythmic comping. Briefly mentioned but not elaborated: upper structure triads are also usable in **incomplete** voicings, deliberately dropping the 3rd or 7th, when the harmonic context makes the omission safe — flagged as a related idea for a future video, not developed here.
