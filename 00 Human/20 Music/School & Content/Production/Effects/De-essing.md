# De-essing

General best practice for taming sibilance and keeping air — the single richest topic in the Disclosure breakdowns. Specific tools: [[Pro-DS]], [[Gem Dopamine]] (boost + de-ess), [[Soothe2]] (harshness). See also [[EQ]], [[Masking and Carving]].

## What it is
Controlling harsh 's'/'sh'/'t' energy without killing the air that makes a vocal sound expensive — a constant push-pull between brightness and harshness.

## Owned (first-call)
[[Pro-DS]] · [[Gem Dopamine]] (boost-while-de-essing) · [[Soothe2]] (dynamic harshness/resonance) · [[Pro-Q 4]] (dynamic band)

## From videos
- **Earbuds amplify sibilance two independent ways — frequency response AND bypassing the outer ear's natural diffusion — de-ess harder for earbud translation, or edit with an FFT tool instead.** (1) Earbud frequency response (measured on 3rd-gen AirPods) significantly amplifies the high-mid/presence range around and above the vocal's clarity formant — good for intelligibility, but the same boost extends into the sibilant range and reads harsh unchecked. (2) Earbuds physically bypass the pinna, so the natural shaping/diffusion a room or car cabin would normally apply to sibilant energy — plus all room reflections — never happens; sound is delivered straight into the ear canal, reading as more "internal"/harsh than through speakers. Fix: de-ess harder specifically for tracks headed to earbuds, or for finer control edit sibilance directly with an FFT editor ([[RX Spectral Editor]]) rather than a threshold-based dynamics processor. → [[RX Spectral Editor]], [[Referencing and Translation]] — Sage Audio, [Perfecting Vocals on Modern Playback Systems](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pzEG3HxI3WE)
- **Why doubles/BGVs need heavier de-essing than the lead — the consonant-TIMING mechanism, not just "the ear can't tell how many S's."** Doubling works because small timing/pitch/amplitude/frequency variations blend into one bigger, more complex sound — but sibilants and hard consonants are short and percussive, so if they land even slightly out of time between takes they read as two distinct performances instead of one thick one, breaking the illusion. Fix: de-ess doubles/BGVs MUCH harder than the lead so they blend rather than announce their own separate timing; alternatively/additionally attenuate highs with EQ or dynamic EQ, or boost the mids slightly (accentuating the pitch differential between takes produces a subtle chorus effect that helps fusion). Complements — doesn't replace — the existing "de-ess doubles more aggressively, the ear can't discern multiple S's unless they're out of time" entry below: this is the mechanism explaining WHY out-of-time S's specifically give the double away. → [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]], [[Artificial Double Tracking (ADT)]] — Sage Audio, [The 5 MOST IMPORTANT Vocal Mixing Tips](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rXk1lvT6AhU)
- **Dynamic EQ vs. a dedicated de-esser: roughly the same CPU cost, but different control tradeoffs — plus a dual-filter dynamic-EQ de-essing recipe if you go that route.** Dynamic EQ makes it easier to pinpoint the exact offending frequency; a dedicated de-esser or multiband compressor typically gives more control over the attack/release of the reaction. **Recipe if de-essing with dynamic EQ specifically: run two bands** — one narrow (~200–300 Hz wide) attenuating aggressively, plus a second, more gradual bell in the general sibilant area for gentler broad control; fine-tune both center frequencies until they land exactly on the sibilance — better positioning needs less total attenuation. → [[EQ]], [[Pro-Q 4]] — Sage Audio, [This Makes Equalizing Vocals A Lot Easier](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dM8TyAYzpxs)
- **De-ess background vocal doubles more aggressively than the lead — the ear can't discern multiple simultaneous S sounds unless they're out of time with each other.** Tony Maserati: "We don't need more than one S — your ear doesn't really discern between how many S's are there unless they're out of time... we want to hear a little bit of that double S but much lower level, that'll keep the lead vocal in the center." → [[RX De-ess]], [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]] — Tony Maserati (Mix with the Masters), [[Background Vocals  - Tony Maserati]]
- **De-essing folded into a broader subtractive multiband EQ stage rather than run as a standalone plugin — one tool doing problem-frequency notches, low/high shelving, AND de-essing together.** "So after the LA-2A I like using the C6 to shape my vocal EQ-wise... I'll do some light de-essing work with this right here, and then so on and so forth." Read as McDSP C6 (unowned — see [[Plugin Wishlist]]); owned-lane stand-in [[Pro-MB]]. Separately, a FabFilter de-esser gets a plain strong endorsement elsewhere in the same chain — "a fantastic de-esser, I love these guys" — with no settings given. → [[Pro-MB]], [[Pro-DS]], [[Multiband Compression]], [[Plugin Wishlist]], [[Dua Lipa Vocal Chain]] — Mix with the Masters (Josh Gudwin), [[Mixing Dua Lipa's vocals with Josh Gudwin]]
- **Don't chase minor sibilance at the mic — fix it afterward with a surgical dip, and save mic/windscreen changes for genuinely bad cases.** Al Schmitt's tracking-stage policy: "if they're really, really bad, then we'll try to work something — maybe use a different microphone or try different windscreens. But if we do a song and there's a couple of sibilant sounds in it, we can fix it in Pro Tools — we just dip those things out... it's like a P-pop." Explicitly framed as a tape-era-vs-DAW-era mindset shift — "it's not like years ago when you're directly to tape and you couldn't fix those things, today you can" — a veteran engineer naming what changed for him once fixing things after the fact became cheap and precise. → [[Vocal Mic Technique]] — Al Schmitt (Mix with the Masters), [[Recording techniques with Al Schmitt]]
- **A layered two-de-esser discipline on a busy lead vocal chain: one plugin does the heavy lifting, a second, lighter one runs later purely as touch-up.** Illangelo (The Weeknd): the primary stage (an unowned Eiosis E2 Deesser — see [[Plugin Wishlist]]) gets the video's strongest endorsement ("nothing does what this de-esser does... it's my first choice"); several stages later, a second de-esser catches "a couple of peaks that I felt needed to be tamed," explicitly NOT the heavy lifter — "all these plug-ins are just doing a little bit by little bit at different moments." Worth adopting as a pattern: a strong primary de-esser early, a light mop-up pass after later stages (limiting, multiband) have had a chance to reintroduce sibilance. → [[Pro-DS]], [[Plugin Wishlist]], [[The Weeknd Vocal Chain]] — Illangelo (Mix with the Masters), [[Producing The Weeknd's vocals with Illangelo]]
- **A tape machine can BE the de-esser — sibilance tamed as a side effect of tape saturation/EQ character via a named preset, not a threshold-based de-esser plugin.** "A little de-essing, again I'm using the Studer A800 and trying to get that analog sound. In this case, I'm using the Pop setting because I wanted him to be a little thinner." Worth remembering as a legitimate lane when the goal is character + gentle taming together on a part that doesn't need surgical control — not every de-ess stage has to be a dedicated de-esser. Owned lane: [[Kramer Tape]] dialed toward thin/bright. → [[Kramer Tape]], [[Plugin Wishlist]], [[Tony Maserati Vocal Chain]] — Tony Maserati (Mix with the Masters), [[Vocal chain with Tony Maserati]]
- **Diagnose sibilance by naming the actual consonants involved, not just "sibilance" — 's', 'ch', and 'ts' were called out as three distinct trigger sounds on the same lead vocal.** "I noticed that the 'esses' and the 'ch' and the 'ts', all those sorts of sibilant sounds were the main concern" — a reminder that affricates (ch, ts) sit in the same harsh-consonant problem zone as pure sibilants and get chased the same way, listening against the artist's rough mix to confirm what's actually bothersome. Also confirms de-essing runs **twice** in a busy chain — early to tame the raw take, again at the very end to catch anything later drive/saturation stages reintroduced. → [[Pro-DS]], [[James Blake Vocal Chain]] — Mix with the Masters, [[MSM Mixing James Blake's Vocals]]
- **A subtle de-essing pass folded into a staged, many-small-plugins chain, plus a harsh-resonance notch just below the sibilant range aimed at loud-playback safety.** "Some nice, subtle DS" — no product, frequency, or amount given, just a light touch as one of several gentle stages. In the same session, a separate but adjacent move: repeated narrow-Q notches by ear on a harsh/spiky frequency near the sibilant zone (garbled auto-caption number, read as roughly **~2.2–2.8 kHz**), justified specifically so the mix can be played loud without a frequency "spiking in your ear" — the same reasoning behind de-essing generally, applied here as manual EQ rather than a dedicated de-esser. → [[EQ]], [[Pro-DS]], [[Pro-Q 4]] — Mix with the Masters (Stuart White), [[Mixing vocals in the box with Stuart White]]
- **The "why" for mastering-stage de-essing, and where it belongs in the chain.** A mixing engineer won't always account for how much more abrasive sibilance reads once a master's overall level gets raised — a vocal that was properly de-essed during mixing can still read sibilant once mastering-stage gain and loudness processing push everything up, which is the actual reason a mastering chain sometimes needs its own de-essing pass even after the mix already had one. Placement: right after the first-insert subtractive EQ, before any dynamics/loudness stages. Prefer a dynamic de-esser over a static EQ cut at the sibilant frequency — a static cut attenuates that frequency *at all times*, not just during the sibilant moment, where a de-esser only engages when it's actually present. → [[Pro-DS]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [How to Make a Mastering Chain](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WNkkwWh7DJg)
- **Match a dynamic band's timing to an analyzer's spikes to confirm sibilance before touching the band, rather than ear-sweeping alone.** Watch a spectrum analyzer for where the signal spikes in the high-frequency range, then match that spike's *timing* against when an S is actually sung or spoken — that confirmation step is what separates real sibilance from other high-frequency content sharing the same band. Center a dynamic bell on the confirmed frequency (automatic threshold, or set threshold/range manually) — cymbals and other high-frequency instrumentation, not sharing the sibilant timing, stay relatively unaffected even though they overlap the same frequency range. → [[Pro-Q 4]], [[EQ]] — Sage Audio, [How to Use Dynamic Eq During Mastering](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tvuBplNrYMs)
- **A full-master mid-side de-esser, run FIRST in the chain, has an incidental stereo-widening side effect worth knowing.** Shelf + Bell filters, subtle attenuation only (no more than a few dB); the mid-side angle is the point — since the mid image usually carries more sibilance, M/S processing means the mid gets attenuated more often than the side, giving both more accurate de-essing on the centered content *and* a subtle, incidental expansion of the side image's highs as a byproduct (not the goal — a real effect to expect, not chase). → [[Pro-DS]], [[Width and Stereo]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [How to Master a Song Start to Finish](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aUygMNRx2yE)
- **Mastering-stage de-essing — narrower and more careful than a mix-stage pass, and not always needed.** Sibilant energy at the mastering stage usually sits **5–8 kHz** (confirm by ear/spectrum — it varies by track). Set the threshold carefully: too loose and the de-esser attenuates unrelated high-frequency content (cymbals, air) whenever it triggers, not just the sibilant itself. The correctness check is listening specifically for whether attenuation fires *with* the sibilant and nothing else. Multiband compression is a valid substitute for a dedicated de-esser here. → [[Pro-DS]], [[Multiband Compression]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [Mastering Compression Settings](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YUn31aE9X_8)
- **The simplest possible de-esser: a second compressor devoted only to the vocal's sibilant highs.** Rather than reaching for a labeled de-esser plugin, insert a second compressor on the vocal and let it react specifically to S/T energy — it ducks only during those sounds, leaving the rest of the performance untouched. The beginner on-ramp to the more surgical Multiband-Dynamics build below. — You Suck at Producing, [You Suck At Producing: Rap Vocals](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DIuHKtdDK8Y)
- **Build a de-esser from scratch with a multiband dynamics device when a static EQ cut sounds unnatural** — a static EQ cut on the sibilant band removes that frequency for the *whole* recording, not just during the S sounds, and starts sounding thin/unnatural; a stock de-esser plugin can also read as continuously turning the sound down rather than only ducking during S's. The DIY fix: on [[Multiband Dynamics]], turn off the top and bottom bands, keep only the middle band, narrow its frequency range to the sibilant zone found by ear-sweeping an EQ point up the spectrum (commonly ~3-7 kHz, centered near 4-5 kHz), then crank that band's downward-compressor ratio to something extreme like 1:∞ so it behaves like a limiter on just that narrow band — clamps hard when the S hits, leaves everything else untouched. Generalizes past vocals to any problem frequency on any instrument that's only loud sometimes. → [[EQ]], [[Multiband Dynamics]] — You Suck at Producing, [You Suck at Mixing No. 11: How to Make Your Own De-esser in Ableton](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y36rFBUYhEc)
- **De-ess the *specific problem resonance*, not "sibilance" generally** — Ian knocked out the "e-of-fever" ring at ~**2.1–2.6 kHz** (near the ear's harshest band) with a dynamic EQ node, not a broad de-ess. → [[EQ]], [[Pro-Q 4]] — Ian Kirkpatrick, [Vocal Production for Dua Lipa](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0cG4_vxe1tY)
- **De-ess *backgrounds* individually and hard for center definition** — remove the doubles' sibilance (super dark) so the **stereo image stays wide but the "s" definition lives in the mono center** (the lead). Lets you push doubles louder without S's competing. → [[2 Backup Vocals Group]], [[Stereo Field]] — Ian Kirkpatrick, [Vocal Production for Dua Lipa](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0cG4_vxe1tY) & [Bad Liar](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WrJWYpehZLY)
- **Add air *back* after de-essing (Gem Dopamine / 16k Pultec)** — heavy de-ess, then boost the highs so you get "sibilance back without the sibilance" — loud, bright, non-annoying vocals. Placement matters: after the de-esser. → [[EQ]], [[AIR Slot]] — Ian Kirkpatrick, [Move Me Full Breakdown](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=22odAiV5I5w)
- **Separate the breaths onto their own high-passed track** — pull breaths below the lead bus, unprocessed + high-passed, so the OTT/C2 smashing the lead doesn't inflate them ("preserves the realness"). → [[1 Main Vocal Track]] — Ian Kirkpatrick, [Vocal Production for Dua Lipa](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0cG4_vxe1tY)
- **Air vs de-essing is a balance, not a single move.** Record with too much treble (S's hurt), then ride up and down: boost highs ([[Gem Dopamine]]) → immediately de-ess → [[Soothe2]] for harshness → boost again (a character preamp/EQ). Like mastering, it's the *stages leading up*, not one hard squash — Disclosure, [Higher Than Ever Before](https://youtu.be/9XkHSronZck)
- **Manual de-essing beats any de-esser.** Chop each too-loud S and pull it down a couple dB by hand — a de-esser always adds a bit of lisp when pushed, and manual editing avoids the phasing. Worth it once you've committed to a track (album stage), not while demoing. (Ed Sheeran records word-by-word and turns every S down manually.) — Disclosure, [Higher Than Ever Before](https://youtu.be/9XkHSronZck) & [NO CAP](https://youtu.be/kz5dmb3QdI8)
- **The whisper trick for airy singers:** record a subtle whisper layer, line it up carefully so it doesn't read as a double, then de-ess the *main* vocal hard and let the whisper carry the air/S's. Great for very airy voices (Mariah, Little Dragon) — Disclosure, [Higher Than Ever Before](https://youtu.be/9XkHSronZck)
- **Whisper track for *clarity/intelligibility*, not just air** — Guy + Howard whisper the lyrics along with the lead (or a lo-fi sample), high-passed to the air + [[Gem Dopamine]], blended under it — it makes an old crackly recording (or any vocal) intelligible. Done on *most* songs; they even get big singers (Mary J. Blige) to record a whisper take — Disclosure, [Where Angels Fear To Tread](https://youtu.be/8nMXSU_K_IM)
- **Boost-type de-essing adds air while taming S's** — [[Gem Dopamine]] lifts the highs and de-esses at once, giving breathiness without the harsh sibilance; follow with a subtractive de-esser that removes what the booster doesn't — Disclosure, [You've Got To Let Go](https://youtu.be/1iRPvK1gaOI) & [Waterfall](https://youtu.be/sL5CG444qwQ)
- **A powerful sustained vocal through analog hardware needs *resonance-taming*, not just de-essing** — a big note through "nice uneven hardware" throws a strong harsh resonance alongside the richness; hunt and notch it (a single dynamic node) — the difference is night-and-day loud in a club. Learn resonances, not only sibilance. → [[Masking and Carving]], [[Soothe2]] — Disclosure, [Holding On](https://youtu.be/8H8U-WjvdVc)
- **In a stack, only the loudest/most-forward vocal needs the sibilant** — the upfront center vocal carries the T/S/P sounds; tame or mute the esses on the doubles instead of de-essing every layer — Disclosure, [Holding On](https://youtu.be/8H8U-WjvdVc)
- **De-ess in stereo for group vocals** — a stereo de-esser's link knob is the control: **100% = ducks L+R together** (mono-ish, safe on a centered lead), **0% = two independent de-essers** (L and R move separately) so a hard-panned stack stays natural and doesn't collapse. De-ess individually *and* lightly on the bus — [[Pro-DS]] — Disclosure, [You've Got To Let Go](https://youtu.be/1iRPvK1gaOI) & [Birthday](https://youtu.be/b-6x_4XRwOQ)
- **De-ess non-vocal sources too** — a touch of de-esser on the *drum bus* controls harsh hi-hats — Disclosure, [Waterfall](https://youtu.be/sL5CG444qwQ)
- **Dry saturation on the high end** adds air with fewer phasing problems than a pure EQ boost — put a saturator ([[Saturn 2]]) on the vocal, distort the top, then shape — Disclosure, [Higher Than Ever Before](https://youtu.be/9XkHSronZck)
- **The George Michael reverb trick:** *invert* a de-esser and use it to drive a reverb *send* — every S triggers the reverb, using sibilance like a crash cymbal (the "Older" sound) — Disclosure, [Higher Than Ever Before](https://youtu.be/9XkHSronZck)
- **One [[Soothe2]] on the vocal bus, not per track** — it's CPU-heavy, and a single instance on the group tames harshness across everything — Disclosure, [Waterfall](https://youtu.be/sL5CG444qwQ)
- **Use a multiband compressor as a manual de-esser on the vocal bus, narrowed to the S/T sibilant band.** After a plain compressor for level control and OTT for presence, run a multiband compressor purely to catch and flatten every S/T/harsh consonant so it doesn't spike, rather than reaching for a dedicated de-esser plugin. → [[OTT]] — Laxcity (Sol State), [Laxcity Breaks Down "Focus" (19 GREAT Music Production Tips) [part 1]](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NseRLW5SN3I)
