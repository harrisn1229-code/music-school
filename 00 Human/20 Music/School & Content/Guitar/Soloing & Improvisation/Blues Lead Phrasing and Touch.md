# Blues Lead Phrasing and Touch

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]] (the note-choice half) and [[Blues Form and Harmony]] (the form the phrasing sits inside). This note covers the **guitar-specific delivery** side of blues soloing — the physical/phrasing moves that turn "the right scale" into "the right sound": where a phrase lands relative to the beat, how vibrato and pick position shape touch, and the chord-tone-targeting/chromatic-connector vocabulary that gets you from box to box. A close cousin, covered in its own section below: **lead-fill accompaniment** — phrasing that answers a separate vocal/melody line rather than carrying one, the B.B. King / Albert King call-and-response half of the same skill set.

## Phrasing

- **Build one 2-bar melodic motif and repeat it literally over the next 2 bars before touching the harmony's chord tones — a structural memory device, not just a phrase you improvise once.** Demonstrated over a 12-bar shuffle in E: a motif built from the E minor pentatonic (B on the 4th fret G string, hammer up to D on the 3rd fret B string, then E on the 5th fret B string, back to B, slide down to A, half-step slide up to B♭ then pull off to open G, hammer on to G♯ — the ♭3→3 blues signature move) is played once over bars 1–2, then repeated **note-for-note** over bars 3–4, before the IV chord arrives and the motif is set aside for chord-tone material. On a second full solo pass over the same form, the same two-bar-repeat structure is kept but the **second repetition is deliberately varied rather than identical** — same opening shape, then a different descent that ends by targeting the upcoming IV chord's 3rd (C♯) instead of resolving back to the tonic — turning the repeat into a set-up for the next chord change rather than a pure echo. General takeaway: build a 12-bar solo in 2-bar chunks, repeat (exactly, then with variation) as a landmark device, and let the repeat's ending pivot into whatever's coming next. → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]], [[Blues Form and Harmony]] — Jack Ruch, [[Jack Ruch - Tricks and Tips for Soloing Over the Most Common Blues Progression]]
- **Turnaround lick: root-position double stop (5th+♭7) rung under an open string, then the flat-5-passing-tone move resolving into a chromatic walk-up to the V chord.** Over the E7 turnaround bar: play the double stop **B (5th) with D (♭7) on top**, letting the open high E string ring underneath; then repeat the flat-5 passing move used earlier in the solo — slide up a half step from the 4th (A) to the ♭5 (B♭), hammer on from the minor 3rd (G) to the major 3rd (G♯) — then **walk up chromatically from A to B** to land squarely on the incoming B7 (V) chord at the top of the next chorus. A compact, reusable closing shape: double-stop → flat-5 passing move → chromatic walk-up into the next chord. → [[Blues Form and Harmony]] — Jack Ruch, [[Jack Ruch - Tricks and Tips for Soloing Over the Most Common Blues Progression]]
- **The pickup phrase — start the lick *before* the downbeat, so it anticipates the top of the form.** A common blues phrasing device: a short run (e.g. 5th degree → major 6th → root) played as a rhythmic pickup in the last beat(s) of the previous bar, landing its resolution (often a double-stop) exactly on beat 1 of the new section. The anticipation is what makes the entrance feel alive rather than four-square. *(general blues vocabulary, demonstrated at the top of each 4-bar section)*
- **Reuse the same pickup shape to mark every structural boundary.** In a worked 12-bar solo example, the same 5th→6th→root pickup idea opens bar 1 (into the I chord) and reopens bar 5 (into the IV chord) — the repetition itself is a phrasing device, giving the ear a landmark it recognizes each time the form turns over. Ties directly into [[Blues Form and Harmony]]'s AAB call-and-response structure — restating a phrase at each 4-bar boundary is the melodic version of that repeat.
- **Double-stops mark structural downbeats.** Sliding into a double-stop (e.g. the 3rd + 5th of the I chord) is used as the resolution note of a pickup phrase, landing exactly on the downbeat that starts a new 4-bar section — a small but effective way to make the "arrival" register as a distinct event rather than just another single note.

## Touch

- **Bend execution mechanics (finger support, starting-pitch accuracy, stopping clean at the peak, intonation, string-hooking) are covered on their own dedicated note** — this page's material below is about bend *placement/role* (delayed vibrato, chord-tracking bend distance, bend as color tone); the physical how-to-execute-a-bend layer underneath all of it lives on [[String Bending Technique]]. → [[String Bending Technique]] — Jared, [[Guitar Bending Tips - Bend like the pros]]
- **Hybrid pick-and-finger technique to sustain an open string as a drone underneath fretted lines, exploiting an open-position key (E).** Grab the high E string with the **middle finger** (holding/plucking it to ring out) while picking the fretted notes on the B/G strings with the pick — the open E rings through much of the phrase as a pedal tone underneath the moving line. The same open-string-as-pivot logic recurs lower down the form: the **open B string** used as a drone/pivot point over the V chord (B7), and the **open low E** ringing under a turnaround double-stop. General takeaway: in open-position blues keys (E, A, D, G), look for a sympathetic open string that matches or complements the current chord and let a spare picking-hand finger hold it while the pick handles the fretted line. → [[Blues Form and Harmony]] — Jack Ruch, [[Jack Ruch - Tricks and Tips for Soloing Over the Most Common Blues Progression]]
- **Tone control through picking-hand position, cross-confirmed on a real Scofield recording.** A second, independent sourcing of the same device documented below: Jens Larsen singles out John Scofield's playing on "Alone Together" (with Chris Minh Doky, no drums) as being as much about *sound* as note choice — Scofield gets a range of different tones "just by picking in different places," using different techniques at each position, treated as a first-class expressive choice rather than an amp/pedal decision. Matches the "picking down by the bridge = twangier/brighter/more aggressive" mechanism below exactly; now attested in both a taught lesson and a real performance. — Jens Larsen, [[My Favourite YouTube Jazz Guitar Videos! - John Scofield, Jonathan Kreisberg & Gilad Hekselman]]
- **Foundational ornament layer, underneath everything else in this section: slide into a note from one fret below, hammer-on from a lower fretted note to a higher one two frets up, and pull-off ("pluck-off") from a higher fretted note back down to a lower one already held.** Beginner-tier versions of the same three ornament types this note's more advanced licks build on. Slide: don't linger on the approach note, move through it quickly or it reads as a wrong/out-of-tune note rather than an ornament. Pull-off: physically more of a small pluck/pull by the fretting hand than a passive lift-off, but gentle enough that the note doesn't go sharp or get pulled off the fretboard. **Isolated-technique drilling:** practice one ornament type at a time against a backing track (a full pass of only slides, then only hammer-ons, then only pull-offs) before combining them — speed isn't the goal on the isolation pass, getting the motion under the fingers is. → [[Dorian from the Minor Pentatonic Box]] — Daniel Seriff, [[Guitar Soloing Tips We Don't Talk Anymore - Charlie Puth Style Lesson|\[Guitar Soloing Tips\] We Don't Talk Anymore - Charlie Puth Style Lesson]]
- **Don't always start a phrase at the bottom of the scale/box — starting from the middle is a deliberate habit-break.** Named directly as a common beginner tendency (spending too much time down at the root); varying the starting point within the box is a low-effort way to vary rhythm and contour without learning new vocabulary. — Daniel Seriff, [[Guitar Soloing Tips We Don't Talk Anymore - Charlie Puth Style Lesson|\[Guitar Soloing Tips\] We Don't Talk Anymore - Charlie Puth Style Lesson]]
- **Default ("half-cocked") vibrato is the habit to break — land clean, then vibrato with intention.** Flagged as one of the most common, hardest-to-unlearn habits in guitar playing: bending the landed note slightly out of tune *before* starting vibrato, rather than landing the note clean and then intentionally choosing to bend/vibrato it. Especially costly on a phrase's anchor note (the root of the box position) — bending the most stable note out of tune first undercuts the sense of "landing" the phrase was building toward. The fix is sequencing: clean landing first, intention (bend/vibrato or not) second, never a reflexive default. → [[Solo Dynamics and Arc]]
- **Vibrato-free phrase-ending drill.** Play a short line, land on the final note, and hold it completely still — no vibrato at all, even without a backing track. Deceptively hard: the hand reaches for vibrato automatically even when told explicitly not to, which is the point — it isolates the habit above so it can be practiced in reverse. Pair with "swing the pendulum hard the other way": drop vibrato to zero for a few minutes a day, then reintroduce it deliberately rather than by default. *(general starting point: a few focused minutes daily, not a wholesale ban on vibrato)*
- **Two-flat-three-two slide/pull-off lick, one pick attack.** A specific embellishment fingering: 2 → ♭3 → 2, executed as slide up, slide down, pull off, all from a single pick attack — the descent and return are articulated by the fretting hand alone. Practice it starting from more than one scale degree (e.g. also from the 6th) so it isn't tied to one fixed starting point. — Daniel Seriff, [I Fixed His Soloing in 27 Minutes (Here's Exactly What We Did)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zbvK3imq00g)
- **Delayed vibrato on a bend — bend to pitch first, then bring vibrato in gradually, rather than vibrato-ing immediately.** Executed on a big bend from the ♭7 up to the root: hit the target pitch clean, hold it briefly without vibrato, then slowly widen into vibrato. Described as giving the note "more of a vocal" quality — the delay mimics how a singer's sustained note blooms into vibrato rather than starting with it. *(general starting point: hold ~a beat before easing vibrato in, then widen gradually rather than snapping straight to full width)*
- **Pick-hand position changes the character of a phrase, independent of note choice.** Picking down by the bridge (rather than over the neck pickup/further from the bridge) gives a **twangier, brighter, more aggressive** tone — used deliberately on the more minor-sounding, chromatically-dense phrase in bars 5–8 to match the harder edge of that lick. A touch move, not an amp/pedal move — repositioning the picking hand is treated as a first-class tone control alongside note choice.
- **Bending, pull-offs and hammer-ons stacked together are the "signature flourish" vocabulary of this style**, not a single ornament used sparingly — most of the phrases in this solo chain at least two of the three (e.g. a bend into a pull-off, or a hammer-on that immediately resolves into the next chromatic target). Isolating one ornament type at a time is a reasonable practice entry point, but the real vocabulary is the combination.

## Note choice / connecting vocabulary

- **Mix major and minor 3rd in the same phrase, don't pick one.** Directly demonstrates [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]]'s "the blend" — moving from ♭3 up to the major 3rd (via a hammer-on) within a single lick, more than once in the solo. The hammer-on ♭3→3 is the concrete physical gesture for what that theory note describes as "bending the ♭3 up toward the natural 3rd."
- **Trill/alternate between the ♭3 and the major 6th as a held color, not just a passing tone.** Named as a recurring device across Mike Bloomfield, BB King, and Albert Collins: rapidly toggling between the flat 3rd and the major 6th scale degree over the I chord, used as a way to sit in the ambiguity between major and minor rather than resolve it. *(the source audio renders the describing word ambiguously — likely "trill" — treated qualitatively rather than as an exact transcript quote; see the transcript note)*
- **Target chord tones chromatically when the chord changes underneath you.** Over the IV7 chord (F7 = root–3rd–5th–♭7), walk up to each chord tone from a half-step below in sequence: chromatic approach to the 3rd, then the 5th, then the ♭7 — i.e., play the arpeggio but connect each tone with its own chromatic approach note rather than leaping directly. A reusable pattern for any dominant-7th chord: approach-tone-into-3rd, approach-tone-into-5th, approach-tone-into-♭7.
- **Chromatic connector: ♭3 – major 3 – 4th, then bend.** A specific three-note chromatic run (flat 3rd, up to major 3rd, up to the 4th) used as a connector into a bend, then resolved back down through the 4th/5th to the ♭3 and root. Works as a self-contained lick for moving out of a minor pentatonic box position and back.
- **Switch scale/box position mid-phrase rather than staying in one shape for the whole 4-bar section.** The solo starts in a major-pentatonic-based position for the pickup/double-stop material, then shifts up the neck into a minor pentatonic box for the chromatic ♭3–3–4th connector — box-switching mid-lick, not just chord-to-chord. Complements [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]]'s note on "one scale over all three chords" vs. "following the changes" — this solo does a hybrid: broadly one tonal center (C), but the *box position* still moves to serve a specific lick.
- **A recognizable "signature lick" is often just a small interval toggle over a static chord.** The solo's closing turnaround phrase — described as a genuine Mike Bloomfield signature — toggles between the 5th and the ♭5 over the I chord. The lesson's framing: a simple two-note alternation, played with conviction and the right touch, reads as a defining lick precisely because it's compact and repeatable, not because it's technically dense. Portable to other chords/keys as a general device: pick a chord tone and its chromatic neighbor a half-step below, alternate.
- **Bending to a "color" scale degree above the chord, not just the root.** Over the V7 (G7) chord, a bend targets the 6th of that chord (from the root) rather than a more expected chord tone — a reminder that a bend's destination doesn't have to be the 3rd/5th/root; a 6th, 9th, or other extension can be the payoff note as long as the ear is led there deliberately.

## Worked example: the 12-bar structure this solo follows

A concrete case study for how the above vocabulary maps onto [[Blues Form and Harmony]]'s three 4-bar phrases, in the key of C:
- **Bars 1–4 (I chord, C7):** pickup phrase (5th→6th→root) into a double-stop downbeat; major pentatonic with ♭3 incorporated (hammer-on ♭3→3); big bend ♭7→root with delayed vibrato; ♭3/6th trill; repeat of the pickup material heading into bar 5; minor pentatonic box shift with the ♭3–3–4th chromatic connector to close the section.
- **Bars 5–8 (IV chord, F7):** chromatic chord-tone targeting (3rd, 5th, ♭7 of F7) via approach notes; big bend up to C; repeat of the ♭3/6th trill; a more minor-sounding, chromatically dense phrase played with the pick near the bridge for a twangier tone, closing with a hammer-on into the major 3rd.
- **Bars 9–12 (turnaround: V–IV–I–V, i.e. G7–F–C7/F7/C7–G7):** bend from the root of G7 up to its 6th; repeat of the minor pentatonic chromatic connector; repeat of the IV-chord targeting phrase as the progression passes back through F; closing signature lick toggling the 5th/♭5 over the I chord.

This is a good default template for building a first slow-blues solo: one recognizable phrase shape per structural boundary, reused rather than invented fresh each time, with the vocabulary above providing the connective tissue between them.

## The B.B. box, built from the ground up

**How the position is actually built, not just where to put your hand.** Root on the **8th fret of the B string** — in the key of G, that's the note G. That root is the top note of a **D-major-shape triad** slid up to the 8th fret (giving G major); that triad in turn sits inside a larger **C-major-shape barre chord** slid up and barred behind it (also a G major voicing). Stacking those two chord shapes on the same root is what the "B.B. box" hand shape actually is — a memorable fingering because it's two familiar chord grips nested together, not an arbitrary scale diagram.

**One hand position, four scales.** Without shifting the hand, all of the following are available: the G major scale, G major pentatonic, G minor pentatonic, and the G minor blues scale (minor pentatonic + ♭5). This is the practical payoff of the triad-stacking above — one physical shape, four melodic options layered on top, matching [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]]'s catalog of what's available over a dominant I chord.

**The real B.B. King vocabulary is the blend, stated directly**: combining the **major 6th** (from the major pentatonic) with the **minor 3rd** (from the minor pentatonic) inside the same phrase, rather than committing to one scale — the concrete version of [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]]'s "the blend is what most players actually do."

### Three foundational licks (in G, over a 12-bar I–IV–V)

- **Lick 1 — 6th → root → double bend → back to 6th.** Major 6th (E) → root (G) → bend from the 9th (A) up a whole step to the 3rd (B), described as "a double little pump bend" → resolve back to the 6th. **Rake as articulation**: rake the pick across the muted 6th note along with the fretted root to add attitude, palm-muting the unused strings so the rake reads as one aggressive attack rather than noise.
- **Chord-tracking bend distance — bend the interval the chord actually contains, not a fixed distance.** Over the I chord this lick bends a full whole step (A→B); over the IV chord (C7, which contains B♭) the *same* lick is played with the bend shortened to a **half step (A→B♭)**, because a full bend to the natural B would rub against C7's ♭7. General rule: recheck a bend's target note against the chord underneath it before assuming last bar's distance still applies. *(general starting point — adjust by ear against the current chord tone)*
- **Lick 2 — the iconic B.B. King whole-and-a-half bend.** 5th degree (D) bent up a **minor 3rd** to the ♭7 (F) — described as needing real strength to execute cleanly. Release back to D, then a smaller first-finger half-step bend on the 4th (C). Resolve down through minor 3rd → 9th → major 6th, landing on the same major-6th/minor-3rd tritone that defines the style.
- **Lick 3 — half-step hammer-on/pull-off trill (♭5 minor blues scale).** Run up the top of the minor pentatonic (♭3-4-5), then trill between the **4th and ♭5** via hammer-on/pull-off rather than a straight bend. Two resolutions: pull off straight down to the 4th (stays fully minor) or resolve up to the **major 6th** (sits in a deliberate major/minor "in-between zone"). Fully expandable/repeatable, and its phrasing should flex with the groove — read differently over a medium shuffle vs. a slower or funkier feel.

### Arpeggios inside the same box — outlining the IV and V chords

- **C7 and D7 arpeggio shapes, fingered without leaving the B.B.-box hand position.** C7 arpeggio = root–3rd–5th–♭7 of C7; D7 arpeggio = the same shape relative to D7. The point is staying in one hand shape and shifting *which notes* get targeted as the chord changes underneath, rather than shifting position for every chord.
- **Target the C-major triad the instant the IV chord (C7) arrives**, rather than continuing the I-chord scale material — the simplest version of chord-tone targeting: just hit the chord's keynotes to outline the change.
- **D7 targeting phrase: 3rd → 5th → root → ♭7**, then the identical shape relocated back onto the IV chord when the form returns to it — one arpeggio motif reused across two chords by moving it, not reinvented per chord.
- **Fuse arpeggio and scale-lick vocabulary by entering a scale lick from the top of an arpeggio run.** Run the C7 arpeggio root-up (3rd-5th-♭7-root-3rd-5th-♭7), then flow directly from that landing point into one of the scale-based licks above — arpeggio and scale-lick vocabulary treated as one continuous well, not separate toolkits.
- **Beginner sequencing, stated directly**: learn the three foundational licks first inside the box, layer the two arpeggio shapes on top to start tracking chord changes, then recombine/vary freely rather than playing anything verbatim.

## Five chromatic connector licks over a 12-bar blues

**Five movable shapes for using chromaticism as connective tissue, taught individually then combined into a full solo — key of G, 12-bar shuffle.** Distinct from this note's other chromatic-connector material above in being narrower and more mechanical: each is a specific fingering meant to be relocated around the neck and recombined, not a worked phrase tied to one spot.

- **Lick 1 — ♭5-passing-tone connector, one shape in two positions.** Position A (3rd fret, over the I chord): starting on **D (the 5th of G)**, play D→F→G→A, pull off **A♭→G** (the chromatic passing tone between the 4th and 5th), then back down F→D. Position B (8th fret, identical fingering, moved up): starting on the **root (G)** instead, the same shape yields root–♭3–4–5–♭5 — "that nice kind of bluesy sound with the flat fifth." One building block, attachable to other material before/after rather than a complete phrase.
- **Lick 2 — three-notes-per-string chromatic run from root to the 5th of the current chord shape.** Starting on G: **G→A♭→A→B♭→B→C→D♭→D**, fingered three notes on the G string then crossing to the B string for the last two — chosen to stay in one comfortable position rather than running up a single string. Because it's a movable shape (root of a chord's own position up to that chord's 5th), it relocates cleanly through the I, IV and V chord positions as the form moves.
- **Lick 3 — chromatic I→IV voice-leading connector, placed specifically in bar 4.** Over the I chord: walk chromatically **G→F**, jump to the **9th (A)**, come down to the **6th (E)**, half-step hammer-on **E→F**, slide down to **D** — landing exactly as the IV chord arrives. Once on the IV chord (C7), walk chromatically up to its **3rd (E)**. A voice-leading device rather than scale-based improvising — placed precisely at the I→IV turn so the phrasing lands "properly" against the change. → [[Blues Form and Harmony]]
- **Lick 4 — E-minor-triad line (relative minor of G) with two chromatic-approach variants, built from CAGED triad groupings.** Base: slide up an E minor triad, then walk **D→C** into G major. Variant: walk up chromatically **B→D** (the 3rd of G major) instead of the triad slide. All reorderable notes from one CAGED triad grouping (3rd–5th–root, optionally the ♭7) — the same shape retargets the IV or V chord by recognizing that chord's own CAGED triad shape, including jumping down a string set specifically for the IV chord. → [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]]
- **Lick 5 (most advanced) — long chromatic descent into a D-altered-scale phrase over the V chord, resolving to I.** Placed at the very end of the 12-bar form. Slide into the **3rd of the I chord (B)**, up to **D**, chromatic descent **E→E♭→D**, jump down a string, continue chromatically **D♭→C→B→B♭** — one continuous chromatic line. Then, over the V chord (D7), move through altered-scale territory: **♯9→♭9→root→♭7**, resolving to the I chord by landing on **B (its 3rd)**. Explicitly credited as the kind of phrase "guys like Joe Pass" play over a dominant V in a blues — jazz vocabulary grafted onto a straight blues form. → [[Altered Chords]], [[Blues Form and Harmony]]

**All five are pitched as movable shapes, meant to be relocated and recombined** — the closing solo example strings phrases from all five together over one continuous 12-bar shuffle, rather than leaving them as isolated exercises.

## Call-and-response accompaniment (B.B. King vs. Albert King)

**A different job from the solo above: fills that *answer* a vocal/melody line rather than carry one.** Demonstrated over a slow 12-bar in C with an organ playing a stand-in melody — the guitar's whole task is fitting phrases into the gaps that melody leaves, "where it doesn't step on any toes," rather than phrasing in line with it the way a full solo (worked example above) does. Same underlying blue-note vocabulary as the rest of this note, applied under a tighter space constraint.

**Core discipline, both styles:**
- **Leave the space** — simple, short phrases beat busy ones; the melody/vocal owns its bars, the fill answers in the gap. Directly enacts [[Blues Form and Harmony]] §Call and response's "the space in the form is where the response lives."
- **Target chord tones at the V chord specifically**, on top of playing in the gaps — both styles below use a distinct move right at the I→V transition rather than just continuing whatever scale/box was already running.
- **The major/minor 3rd juxtaposition — bend to the major 3rd, then grab the minor 3rd right after — is the one move common to both styles**, just approached from different positions. Same friction as [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]] §Why the ♭3 isn't just wrong, played as a deliberate two-note gesture rather than this note's hammer-on ♭3→3 above.

**B.B. King style — the B.B. box:**
- **The B.B. box**: a position centered on the root note on the B string (13th fret of the B string for a C root) — blends major and minor pentatonic *around that root* rather than one fixed shape; the home position for everything below.
- **Major 6th accent**: 14th fret of the G string (major 6th in C) — repeatedly hit the root, then fall back onto this note.
- **Drop to the standard minor pentatonic box** from the same area, blending major/minor there too — not treated as separate vocabulary from the B.B. box.
- **I→V chord-tone line (from B.B.'s live "Three O'Clock Blues")**: moving C (I) to G7/G9 (V), outline the change by playing into the **♭7 of the V chord** right at the transition — a real chord tone, not just a scale note. Described as T-Bone Walker–ish, jazzy. General lesson: use note choice to mark the chord change itself, and track where you are in the form, not just which scale you're in.
- **Signature bend**: 5th up a minor 3rd to the ♭7 (G → B♭ in C).
- **Signature closer**: root up an octave, let it ring, slide off it.

**Albert King style — upper/lower minor pentatonic:**
- **Position, not scale, is the difference**: stays mostly in minor pentatonic (vs. the B.B. box's major/minor blend), lower on the neck, still bending minor 3rd ↔ major 3rd.
- **Why it's an approximation**: Albert King played right-handed guitar strung upside down, open-tuned, tuned down — genuinely impossible to replicate on a standard-tuned, standard-strung guitar. Everything here is the closest practical stand-in.
- **Position**: the **upper minor pentatonic box** (one position up from the "home" box, starting on the ♭3) for the top part of a phrase, dropping to the **lower/standard minor pentatonic box** underneath for the rest.
- **Short-gap lick**: bend 4th → 5th, hit the minor 3rd, fall to root/5th.
- **Long-gap phrase tail — the recurring Albert King texture**: pick once, then pull-off/slide the rest — root → ♭7 (pull off, slide down), or minor 3rd → root → ♭7 the same way. One pick attack, the rest articulated by the fretting hand; this is what ties separate pentatonic-box phrases together across a longer gap and reads as "Albert King" more than any single lick.
- **I→V chord-tone line (Albert version)**: minor pentatonic line sliding up into a **double stop pulled directly from the G9 chord** — its ♭7 and 5th together — same "mark the chord change" idea as B.B.'s version, landing on a double stop instead of a single note.
- **Multi-pitch bend emulation (from Albert's "The Sky Is Crying")**: Albert's real technique bends one fretted note through multiple pitches, possible only because of his tuned-down/upside-down open tuning. Approximate on a standard-tuned guitar by **switching fingers/frets mid-bend**: fret the 13th fret of the high E string, bend up a whole step, then shift down to a finger a half-step lower to fake the same rising/falling contour.
- **Stated practice priority**: "the best way to get that stuff happening in your playing is to just listen, listen, and listen" — pointed at Albert's actual recordings over further drilling of the approximation.

**Reference recordings named directly**: B.B. King's live **"Three O'Clock Blues"** (the I→V line above) and Albert King's **"The Sky Is Crying"** (the multi-pitch bends above) — both cited as the literal source of a technique, not general inspiration. Also named as players doing this same skill: Eric Clapton, Stevie Ray Vaughan.

**Stated use case**: explicitly an ensemble skill — "playing blues with a singer, or singing and playing guitar at the same time" — practice it against something else carrying the melody, not in isolation.

### The rhythmic phrasing template — where a fill lands, not just what it plays

**Same skill, rhythm-first framing: figure out *where* a lead-fill phrase lands relative to the vocal before worrying about *what notes* fill it.** Demonstrated over a slow 12/8 blues in G with a recorded sung verse, using a straight Albert King transcription and then an original phrase built on the identical rhythmic skeleton.

- **Count a slow 12/8 blues as "1-2-3-4," each count covering a triplet (three eighth notes), rather than counting 12 discrete eighth notes.** This collapses the groove into the same 4-beat frame the vocal phrases against — the actual unit for planning where a fill goes.
- **Lead-fill phrases land on one of two spots almost exclusively: the "4" count or the "2" count.** A binary rhythmic decision — land on 4, or land on 2 — rather than an open-ended choice, and a concrete generalization of §Core discipline's "leave the space" above: the two landing spots *are* the gaps the vocal leaves.
- **A full verse's landing-point sequence is a reusable template independent of note choice.** One worked example: four-count, four-count, four-count, two-count, two-count, four-count, two-count — seven phrases total. Reused verbatim with a different, more major-leaning note palette (minor pentatonic swapped for major pentatonic blended with the major 6th) to prove the rhythmic skeleton and the melodic content are separable — and that the skeleton is the more portable half to practice.
- **The template shifts with the tune and the groove** — different vocal phrasing/lyrics move the landing points even within slow 12/8 blues, and shuffle or funk blues changes both the phrasing and the note choice further. Treat 4-count/2-count as a starting method (count through the form, notice where the gaps open), not a fixed rule to paste onto every song.
- **Practice method, stated directly: transcribe verses from real recordings and count through them as you learn them, tracking where each phrase lands relative to the beat**, not just which notes it uses. Match the source recording's groove (slow blues vs. shuffle vs. funk) to whatever rhythmic vocabulary is being studied.
- **Reference recordings for this specific rhythmic study**: Albert King's live **"Live Wire/Blues Power"** and **"Thursday Night in San Francisco"**; B.B. King's live **"Live at the Regal"** — named directly as go-to source material for transcribing lead-accompaniment phrasing and counting through it.

**Stated use case**: explicitly an ensemble skill — "playing blues with a singer, or singing and playing guitar at the same time" — practice it against something else carrying the melody, not in isolation.
