# 7 Days to Learning Bass Scales (Beginner Lesson)

## Synopsis

A beginner-focused BassBuzz lesson that packages five essential bass scale shapes — major, major pentatonic, minor, minor pentatonic, and blues — into a single 7-day practice routine, one shape (or one improv session) per day. The framing device runs the whole video: the 12-note chromatic alphabet is a spice cabinet, and a scale is a pre-mixed subset of spices guaranteed to taste coherent together, so learning a scale means learning both *what notes it contains* and *a fretboard shape that finds them*. Rather than teaching five isolated topics, the lesson deliberately builds each new shape as a small modification of the previous one (pentatonic = full scale minus two notes; blues = minor pentatonic plus one note) so viewers are never really learning from scratch after day one. Every shape gets the same three-step treatment: (1) the bare scale shape with exact fingering, (2) a short, well-known riff or melody built from that scale to internalize it as music rather than a pattern, and (3) on two of the seven days, a freeform improvisation session against a sustained drone track instead of a written riff — explicitly framed as the deepest of the three internalization methods, because it forces a genuine ear-to-finger connection rather than shape recall or pattern-copying. The riffs chosen are all recognizable classic-rock/soul cuts (Lord of the Rings' Shire theme, My Girl, Crazy Train, Knock on Wood, Sunshine of Your Love), each picked specifically because it's simple enough to teach in a few minutes while still using most of the notes in that day's scale. A paid course plug (BassBuzz's "Beginner to Badass") appears twice but doesn't gate any of the free content — every shape, fingering, and riff taught in the video is complete on its own.

## What's taught

- **[~0:00–1:30] What a scale actually is.** The 12-note chromatic alphabet is framed as a spice cabinet — too many notes to use all of them in one song, so a scale is a pre-selected subset of those 12 notes "guaranteed to work together," giving a song a specific, coherent flavor. This framing (spice metaphor) recurs for every scale in the video, assigning each one a specific taste (major = all-purpose seasoning, major pentatonic = sugar/butter/vanilla, minor = black pepper/cocoa, minor pentatonic = salt, blues = salt plus a hot spice).
- **[~1:30–2:00] What a fretboard shape is, and why it's transposable.** A shape is the starting point that locates a scale on the neck without getting lost. The same shape works from any root note — starting it on C names it "C major scale," starting the identical finger pattern on F names it "F major scale." This transposability rule is stated once and then assumed for every scale that follows.
- **[Day 1, ~2:00–4:30] Major scale — shape, fingering, and internalization riff.** C major scale played from the **8th fret of the E string** (chosen over open position specifically to avoid a bigger stretch). Fingering: middle–pinky–index–middle–pinky–index–ring–pinky. Internalization riff: Howard Shore's Shire theme ("Concerning Hobbits") from *The Lord of the Rings*, played low in the same position and fingering pattern as the scale (can also be played an octave up using the identical shape). Explicit teaching principle stated here and reused for every later scale: **melodies/riffs use more notes of a scale than a typical bassline does**, so they're a better internalization tool than writing your own bassline would be at this stage. Day-1 homework: 5–10 min reviewing the shape + playing along with the riff; free practice PDF offered in the description for all 7 days.
- **[Day 2, ~4:30–6:15] Major pentatonic scale — derivation, shape, and internalization riff.** Derived on-camera by **removing the 4th and 7th degrees from the major scale** (7 notes → 5, "penta" = five) — explicitly taught as a modification of day 1's shape, not a new pattern. Root example C, moved to the **3rd fret of the A string** (a different position than the major scale's C, same pitch); frets are further apart there, so the lesson calls out **micro-shifting** the fretting hand rather than holding one big stretch. Fingering copies the scale's pattern: middle-pinky-index-pinky-index-pinky. Internalization riff: the intro guitar riff from "My Girl" (The Temptations, 1964; originally played by Robert White of the Motown Funk Brothers). Named songs using the scale's vibe: Ramble On (Led Zeppelin), Jump, Jive an' Wail (Louis Prima), Crazy Little Thing Called Love (Queen). **Pacing note stated directly:** learning a fully different scale every single day for 7 days "would be kind of counterproductive" because a scale only sticks with repeated use across sessions — which is why days 2 and 5 (pentatonics) lean on shapes already mostly known from days 1 and 4.
- **[Day 3, ~6:15–8:45] First improv-with-drone session (major / major pentatonic).** The pitch: shape-drilling and riff-copying are good starts, but the deepest internalization tool is improvising your own riffs/melodies using the scale. Setup: a **drone track** — any sustained note matching the scale's starting note. Two internalization methods given: (1) play the scale in order, then reorder it by ear, following curiosity note to note; (2) a "math nerdy" alternative — play sequences of scale-degree *numbers* rather than by ear, e.g. 1st note, 3rd note, or longer runs like 2-4-3-5-4-6-5-7-6-8. Rhythm is called out as fully flexible since the drone doesn't impose a groove — loose flowing melody or your own stuck-in groove both count. Stated payoff: doing any version of this trains the scale "at a much deeper level than most shape regurgitators," plus doubles as ear training and finger-coordination work. Homework: warm up on major + major pentatonic shapes, then 5–10 min of drone improv. (First of two course plugs for BassBuzz's "Beginner to Badass" course here — improv formula method, technique, theory, full songs.)
- **[Day 4, ~8:45–11:00] Minor scale — shape, fingering, and internalization riff.** F♯ minor scale (same shape transposes to any root). Fingering: index–ring–pinky–index–ring–pinky–index–ring; explicit caution to **micro-shift rather than force a stretch** on the low frets to avoid hand/wrist strain. Stated general benefit of learning multiple scale shapes: it lets you read a song as recognizable patterns instead of memorizing "chains of meaningless tab notes," so your brain digests new material faster. Internalization riff: the guitar riff from "Crazy Train" (Ozzy Osbourne; originally Randy Rhoads) — same scale fingering (index-ring-pinky-ring-index-pinky-ring-pinky-index-pinky-ring), plus an **open low E** that looks outside the fretted shape but is actually just the lower octave of the scale's 7th degree — i.e. it's still in the scale, just played open. Named songs: Feel Good Inc. (Gorillaz), The Chain (Fleetwood Mac), and Criminal World (David Bowie) is called out specifically as playing the full minor scale in order. Homework: 5 min reviewing minor + a review pass on major/major pentatonic, plus 5 min more Crazy Train or any other riff enjoyed so far.
- **[Day 5, ~11:00–12:45] Minor pentatonic scale — derivation, shape, and internalization riff.** Derived by **removing the 2nd and 6th degrees from the minor scale**. Root example E minor pentatonic. Fingering: index-pinky-index-ring-index-ring. Named songs that use this scale: Moby Dick (Led Zeppelin), I Shot the Sheriff (Bob Marley), Paranoid (Black Sabbath), Black Night (Deep Purple) — all explicitly flagged as harder teaching choices (tricky rhythm or too-fast note runs) than the riff actually used: the intro to "Knock on Wood" (Eddie Floyd; bass by Duck Dunn), which walks the scale in order and is called out as easy to teach because of that. Fingering: index-pinky-index-ring(or pinky) on the 9th fret, back to index, back to ring/pinky; listen for the offbeat notes specifically. Homework: 5 min reviewing all four scale shapes learned so far, prioritizing the weakest one, plus 5 min reviewing favorite riffs.
- **[Day 6, ~12:45–13:45] Second improv-with-drone session (minor / minor pentatonic).** Same two methods as day 3 (free reordering by ear vs. scale-degree number sequences), applied to the "dark side" scales. Explicit reframe: improv isn't just for soloists/jazz players — it's the mechanism that connects "I hear the sound in my head" to "my fingers move like this," which is what turns a bassline or fill into something that comes from the player rather than random finger-wiggling. (Second, self-aware course plug here.) Homework: 5 min warming up on shapes/riffs, 5 min minor-focused drone improv.
- **[Day 7, ~13:45–15:15] Blues scale — derivation, shape, fingering, internalization riff, and a naming caveat.** Built from minor pentatonic **plus one added chromatic passing note between the 4th and ♭5th degrees** — named on-camera as both "the blue note" and "the tritone." Root example D blues scale. Fingering: index-pinky-index-middle-ring-index-ring, or a stretch-friendlier alternative index-pinky-index-middle-pinky-index-pinky. **Explicit naming caveat, stated as the single most important thing to know about this scale:** you can play it over blues changes but don't have to, and you can play it outside a blues context without it sounding "bluesy" — the name doesn't gate when it's usable. Internalization riff: "Sunshine of Your Love" (Cream; Jack Bruce on fretless bass) — two fingering options given, a straight scale-fingering version (ring-index start) and a compressed alternative (pinky-index-pinky-pinky-middle-index, then stretch index-pinky-index) that delays the pinky stretch to the very end of the phrase. Named songs: Heartbreaker (Led Zeppelin), I Want to Take You Higher (Sly and the Family Stone), Bombtrack (Rage Against the Machine). Homework: brief review of the blues shape plus any shape still fuzzy, 5 min on any riff from the lesson, 5 min improvising with the blues scale (or any favorite) over a drone.
- **[~15:15–end] Closing takeaway.** To actually retain these scales long-term, keep learning more music that uses them — that's the signal that tells your brain the information is worth keeping. Points to a companion free resource: "the first 50 songs you should learn as a beginner."
