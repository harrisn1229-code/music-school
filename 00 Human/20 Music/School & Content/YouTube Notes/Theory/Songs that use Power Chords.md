# Songs that use Power Chords

## Synopsis

Power chords (root + 5th, no 3rd) explained via their real reason for existing — distortion turns a full major/minor triad muddy, so removing the 3rd is what makes an overdriven guitar sound clean and powerful — plus how a "major/minor-less" power-chord guitar part still often reads as major or minor overall once the vocal or another instrument supplies the missing 3rd, a chord-vs-interval labeling debate, and non-guitar/non-distorted uses where "power chord" stops being the most natural name.

## Techniques

- **Definition**: root + 5th only, no 3rd — technically a "5 chord" (C5 = C + G, no E/E♭). Standard 3-note voicing on guitar adds the root doubled an octave up.
- **Why it exists — the real mechanism, not just a stylistic choice**: playing a full major or minor triad through distortion produces a muddy, unpleasant sound (the distorted harmonics of the 3rd clash badly); removing the 3rd and keeping just root+5th cleans the sound up dramatically. This is presented as THE reason power chords became standard as distortion use grew, not an arbitrary genre convention.
- **Mostly-power-chord songs**: "Smells Like Teen Spirit" (Nirvana), "You Really Got Me" (The Kinks), "I Bet You Look Good on the Dancefloor" (Arctic Monkeys) — all built almost exclusively from power chords.
- **Inverted voicing (5th on the bottom, root on top)**: "Smoke on the Water" (Deep Purple) — one of the most iconic riffs of all time uses this simpler two-note voicing; since the LOWER note isn't the root but the 5th, this is technically an inverted power chord (a perfect 4th rather than a perfect 5th), and could pedantically be labeled as a slash chord.
- **A power-chord guitar part can still read as major or minor overall, once another instrument fills in the missing 3rd**: "American Idiot" (Green Day) — the guitar in isolation plays only bare power chords (D♭5-A♭5-E♭5) through the chorus, but the vocal melody adds the major 3rd on top of each chord, so the OVERALL sound is major even though the guitar part itself contains no 3rd at all — "best of both worlds": distorted guitar stays clean/powerful, vocal supplies the major-third sweetness.
- **Chord-vs-interval labeling debate**: a strict definition requires 3+ notes to count as a "chord," which would disqualify a 2-note power chord as an interval rather than a true chord — Bennett argues context should decide the label instead of a rigid rule. Where "chord" fits naturally: "Yesterday" (The Beatles, opening) — widely heard/described as a chord, even though it's technically an F5 power chord on acoustic guitar. Where "interval"/"harmony" fits better: "Seven Nation Army" (The White Stripes) — the riff starts as a single-note melody, then later the SAME melody is harmonized in 5ths/octaves using power chords — functioning more like a harmonized melodic line (the way backing vocalists harmonize a tune) than a genuine chord progression, since it's the same melody restated, not a sequence of different chords.
- **On piano/non-distorted contexts, "power chord" stops being the natural name — "bare fifth" fits better**: "All of Me" (John Legend) — the piano accompaniment uses many root+5th voicings without a distortion context to justify the "power" framing. "Defying Gravity" (*Wicked*) — its main motif also uses a bare D♭5 (viewer-suggested example, credited to a channel fan named Raphael).
