# How to Play Bass with a Drummer (Foolproof Beginner Blueprint)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PSw5uqkTPzs)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PSw5uqkTPzs)

## Synopsis

A short, beginner-targeted BassBuzz lesson with a two-part structure: first a plain-English "crash course on drum kit basics" (what each piece of the kit is called and what it does), then a single foolproof method — "stick with the kick" — for turning that drum knowledge into a usable bass-line strategy, taught through a graduated play-along drill rather than just described. Josh's framing is explicitly beginner-first: this isn't presented as the only or best way to build a bass line, just a reliable starting point that produces music that feels good while a player builds confidence. The video opens with an A/B comparison (two versions of the same bass line, different rhythms, one obviously locking better with the drums than the other) to motivate *why* rhythm choice against the drums matters before explaining the method, then closes the loop by having the viewer actually play along to isolated kick patterns and a full rock groove — an ear-training/muscle-memory approach rather than a lecture. Much shorter and more foundational than BassBuzz's other drum-lock video already in the vault ([[Why Beginner - Intermediate Bassists Sound “Karaoke-y”]], which assumes lock-in basics and moves on to beyond-beat-1 nuance) — this one is the prerequisite layer underneath that one.

## What's taught

### 1. Drum kit basics crash course

Most pro musicians know the basic parts of a drum kit even without playing drums — framed as baseline ensemble literacy, "enough drum knowledge to get you through most conversations with other musicians without saying anything embarrassing":
- **Kick drum (bass drum)** — "the foundation of the drum set," very important to a lot of styles of music.
- **Snare drum** — together with the kick, "a lot of drum beats are all about the kick and snare."
- **Hi-hat** — often keeps time through the bar with a consistent rhythm (typically every quarter note).
- **Ride cymbal** — if the drummer isn't playing hi-hat, they're probably riding the ride to do the same timekeeping job.
- **Toms and crash cymbals** — usually mark accents in the music rather than the steady pulse.
- Kit sizes vary — "some kits have less pieces than this, some have a lot more" — this is the common baseline, not a fixed spec.

### 2. The "stick with the kick" blueprint

The video's central thesis, illustrated first with three short unnamed song clips (song titles/artists aren't verbalized in the captions and aren't otherwise identifiable from the transcript — the audio comparison plays over on-screen text not carried into the captions) that Josh says all share the same trait: **the bass follows the kick drum's rhythm.**
- **Why it works:** the kick drum is the lowest-sounding part of the kit and the bass is the lowest-sounding instrument in the band — together they form a low-end foundation the audience physically feels, while higher frequencies (vocals, guitars, keyboards) sit on top and get heard rather than felt.
- **It generalizes beyond literal kick-matching:** even bass lines that don't 100% follow the kick drum rhythm are usually still strongly linked to it — which is why the video frames listening to the kick consistently as valuable even for more independent/syncopated bass rhythms, not just literal kick-doubling.
- **Framed explicitly as a starting point, not a rule:** "there's no one right way to play bass on any given song in relation to the kick or anything else," but a beginner needs a confident, reliable starting point, and sticking with the kick reliably makes the music feel good — which in turn "makes people want to play with you even if you're not the most advanced player."
- Full method (plus the graduated play-along drill below) routed to [[Locking In With the Drummer]] — woven into that note's existing "How to build the skill" section as the concrete drill underneath its more abstract "keep your ears open to the drums" guidance.

### 3. Play-along drill: isolating the kick, then applying it musically

The video's practical teaching mechanism — play (or clap/slap, if bass isn't handy) an open E string exactly matching just the kick drum's rhythm, across patterns of rising difficulty:
1. **Plain quarter notes** — "1 2 3 4, 1 2 3 4" — the simplest possible kick pattern.
2. **An unnamed intermediate pattern** — introduced only as "an easy start for a bass line"; the specific rhythm isn't verbalized in the transcript (it's demonstrated in audio only).
3. **A syncopated pattern** — "1, 2-&, 3, 1, &, 3, 1, &, 3" — described as usable for "perfectly good bass lines depending on context."
4. **Applied musically** — a full rock-groove play-along built on the syncopated pattern #3, so the drill moves from isolated pattern-matching to sticking with the kick "in musical context."
- **The listening tip for when it feels shaky:** listen to the drums, not yourself — specifically, the hi-hat gives every single quarter note (a steady timing anchor) while the kick gives the actual rhythm being played; "between the hi-hat and the kick, you're getting all the info you need to play along."
