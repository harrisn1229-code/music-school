# Pivot Tone Lines

Part of [[Bebop Vocabulary]] (§3 Approach notes) and [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] — a fourth device in the enclosure/approach family, distinct from the three already documented ([[Enclosures Through the Scale]]'s scale-mechanical sizing, [[Downbeat and Upbeat Enclosures]]'s beat-driven sizing, and §2's four static enclosure shapes). Where those all frame or approach a single target note, a **pivot tone** repeats ("pedals") one note and uses it as a **launch point** to chromatically approach a second note — a two-note unit, not a single-note ornament. Also covers two spin-off devices from the same lesson: half-step approaches walked through an **arpeggio**, and the same approach vocabulary applied to **chord shapes** when comping.

## The core device: pedal a tone, chromatically approach the next
1. Pick a **pivot tone** (a chord tone you return to / repeat).
2. From the pivot, move by **half step** to a second, target tone.
That's the whole mechanism — a repeated note doing double duty as both a stable anchor and the approach note's launchpad.

## On minor chords — 5th pivots to the 6th
Worked example: pedal the **5th** of a minor chord, chromatically approach the **6th** from below. Set up as a **2-5-1**: that chromatically-approached 6th of the ii chord becomes the **3rd of the V7 chord** a beat later, so the same pivot-tone shape that started as decoration on the ii chord resolves directly into V7's most important guide tone. Finish the phrase into the I chord using scales + enclosures ([[Enclosures Through the Scale]] / [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §2) for a complete worked 2-5-1 line. *(general starting point: over Dm7 G7 Cmaj7, pedal A (5th of Dm7), chromatically approach B from below — B then reads as the 3rd of G7 the instant the chord changes underneath it.)*

## On major and dominant chords — root pivots to the 5th (both directions)
For major/dominant chords the pivot tone is usually the **root**, not the 5th:
- **Root → 5th, approached from the 3rd.** Pivot on the root, then chromatically walk up from the chord's 3rd to its 5th. Worked in a blues line context — described as "quintessential" for the sound of jazz language.
- **The reverse: start on the 5th, walk down to the root** (through the same territory the first shape covers ascending). Works over both blues (dominant) and major 2-5-1 contexts.

Both directions land the same core sound — treat them as one device played two ways, choose whichever direction fits the phrase's melodic contour in the moment.

## Spin-off 1 — Half-step approaches on arpeggio tones (bebop vocabulary)
A simpler expansion: instead of pivot-toning, just **precede every note of an arpeggio with a half-step approach from below**, then resolve the last approached note into the target chord's 3rd. Common over dominant chords, a bebop-era staple. *(general starting point: approaching every tone of a G major arpeggio by a half step, then resolving similarly into the 3rd of the I chord — "super simple," per the lesson, but reads as authentic bebop vocabulary immediately.)*
- **Donald Byrd device (solo on "Lex")** — walk up a **dominant 9th arpeggio**, use a **five-note enclosure** to land on the dominant chord's **7th**, then apply the half-step-approach-per-arpeggio-tone device above on the same dominant chord. → cross-referenced worked lick on [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]].
- **Wes Montgomery device (solo on "D Natural Blues")** — same core idea, but over an **altered dominant** (♯5, ♯9 added). Opens with a classic Wes minor-9 shape (a device Wes leans on often over minor 9 chords), adds an **enclosure of the 3rd**, then outlines the altered chord largely as an **E♭ major triad over the dominant root** (the source of the altered color), finishing with the half-step-approach-per-arpeggio-tone device applied to each note of that triad.

## Spin-off 2 — Approaches applied to chord shapes (comping)
The same enclosure/approach vocabulary doesn't have to stay single-note — apply it to **comping voicings**:
1. Treat a chord *shape* like a single note in a line (its "identity," not its individual voices).
2. Add approach notes / enclosures **into** the chord shape the same way you'd approach a single target tone — e.g. approach a 2-5-1's basic guitar voicings chromatically the same way Donald Byrd's half-step-per-arpeggio-tone device works on single notes.
3. Layer in **more voicing color** once the basic version is comfortable — a minor 9 shape, a major 6, a major 6/9 — each one is just another "target" to approach into.
4. **Use inversions**, not just root-position grips — knowing a chord's inversions is what makes the approach-into-a-shape device usable across the full range of a progression rather than jumping around.
Framed as the payoff of the whole video: once chord shapes are treated as single-note-line targets, comping opens up the same way melodic soloing does. → full write-up of comping application on [[Comping Fills]].
