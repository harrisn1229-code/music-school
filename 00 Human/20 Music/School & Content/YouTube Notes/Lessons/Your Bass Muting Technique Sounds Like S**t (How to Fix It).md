## What's taught

**The problem, framed up front.** Ringing strings you don't mean to be playing smother the notes you do mean to play — subtle on a small practice amp or phone speaker, not subtle through a loud amp or a PA. Muting means deliberately keeping the three strings you're *not* playing silent so the one you are playing reads clean.

**Sympathetic vibration — why muting matters even when you're not touching anything wrong.** Fret the 2nd fret of the D string (an E) without touching the low E string at all; pluck it; then mute the A, D, and G strings with both hands, still without touching the low E. The low E rings anyway — visibly moving and audible — purely from the vibration of the other strings transferring through the bridge/body. Demonstrates that "I'm not knocking any strings" isn't enough; ringing strings need active, deliberate muting regardless of contact.

**The two-hand map.** Plucking hand mostly covers the **lower** strings; fretting hand is most helpful for the **higher** strings. The five fixes below split cleanly across that map — fixes 1–3 are plucking-hand tools, fixes 4–5 are fretting-hand tools — and are taught in that order because plucking-hand muting is achievable for most beginners with practice, while fretting-hand muting takes noticeably longer to become reflexive (well into intermediate).

**Fix 1 — The thumb mute (plucking hand, easiest).** Move the plucking thumb between three resting spots depending on what string is being played: the pickup, the E string, or the A string. If the thumb rests on the A string, angle it so it also covers the E string underneath. Never move the thumb all the way down to the D string — fix 2 makes that unnecessary.

**Fix 2 — The pull across / rest stroke (plucking hand).** A common beginner assumption is that the thumb has to sit on the string immediately next to whatever's being plucked — not true, because of the pull-across: the plucking finger should pull *across* the horizontal plane of the bass on every pluck, coming to rest on the next string over, which mutes it as a side effect of the pluck itself. That "coming to rest on the next string" is specifically named here as a **rest stroke**. Because each pluck already mutes the string right next to it, the thumb can sit **two strings away** rather than one. The alternative failure mode — plucking up and away from the bass instead of across it — loses this free muting tool entirely and also produces a thinner, less "fat and bassy" tone; pulling across is presented as strictly better on both counts, not a stylistic tradeoff. *(This mechanic is the fuller-named version of the pull-across basics already logged on [[String Crossing and Plucking Patterns]] and [[Bass Note Muting Technique]] from BassBuzz's very-first-lesson video — this video adds the "rest stroke" term and the two-strings-away consequence.)*
- Fixes 1 and 2 together are called "about as much muting as I would teach to an absolute beginner" — the floor, not the ceiling.

**Fix 3 — The sneaky finger (plucking hand).** The ring and pinky fingers go unused in standard two-finger (index/middle) plucking — sneak one of them onto a string as an extra, dedicated mute so the thumb doesn't have to do all the muting work alone. Concrete use case: rather than moving the thumb all the way to the A string while playing on higher strings, leave the thumb further back on the E string and cover the A string with the ring or pinky finger instead.

**Worked low-string example — "You Give Love a Bad Name" (Bon Jovi), fixes 1–3 only.** Fretting fingering for the main riff: pinky on the 3rd fret of the A string, ring on the 3rd fret of the E string, index on the 1st fret of the A string, back to pinky on the 3rd fret, back to index on the 1st fret *(the caption track garbles the string name at this exact point — most likely the low E string, matching the E/A alternation the rest of the phrase follows; flagged low-confidence rather than guessed as fact)*, pluck that note twice, then pinky on the 3rd fret again, index on the 1st fret; bar two repeats the shape: pinky, ring, index, pinky, index, index, pinky. Because the riff alternates mostly between the A and E strings, **pull-across (fix 2) does most of the muting work automatically** — A-string plucks mute the E string, D-string plucks mute the E string — leaving only the E string uncovered when playing on the A string, which gets closed with either the thumb mute (fix 1) or a sneaky ring finger (fix 3). Played with only fixes 1–3 applied, the low strings come out clean but the D and G strings ring — the stated reason the fretting hand has to join in next.

**Fix 4 — The string sitter (fretting hand, harder — takes practice to become reflexive).** Beginners tend to let a fretting finger fly straight off the string the instant a note ends, which leaves that string free to ring open. Instead: when a note ends, release the *fretting pressure* but stay in *physical contact* with the string — the contact itself keeps it silent. Framed with a "strings are like babies, you can't leave them home alone" analogy. Advanced refinement once the basic reflex is solid: keep **multiple fingers** touching the string rather than just one, which kills ringing overtones that a single point of contact can miss.

**Fix 5 — The flat hand (fretting hand, "subject of some controversy").** Curved, piano-style fretting-hand fingers is a legitimate way to play (some pros do it, and it can give very slightly cleaner articulation) — but for most bass playing, a **flatter** hand works better for muting: fretting more on the pads of the fingers instead of the tips leaves the rest of each finger's length lying across the adjacent strings, muting them — especially useful for the higher strings. The **index finger** is called out as the best starting point for this tool specifically because it can rest behind the other fingers on the lower-pitched strings while they're busy fretting notes, covering ground the other fingers can't reach.

**Worked high-string example — same Bon Jovi riff, fixes 4–5 added.** String-sitter usage: leave the pinky resting on the A string when moving to the ring finger's note on the E string; on the D-string 1st-fret notes (marked staccato — the little dot in notation, meaning a deliberate short gap between two plucks of the same note), string-sit in the gap between the two plucks rather than lifting off cleanly, then re-fret; string-sit again with the index finger on the 1st fret when moving back up to the pinky's 3rd-fret A-string note. Flat-hand usage: keep the index finger flat enough, while fretting the E/A-string notes (most of the riff), to lie across and mute both the D and G strings simultaneously; when the line moves up onto the D string itself, the flat index finger's far end covers the G string.

**The big muting test — a self-diagnostic drill (new to this vault).** Play a bass line in slow motion, but pluck **all four strings on every note**, not just the intended one. Done correctly, only the target note should ring clearly — the other three strings should each produce a dull, muffled *thump* rather than an open ring. Any audible open-string ringing exposes a gap in the muting technique on that specific note. Caveat: this drill is deliberately harder than normal playing — the big four-string plucks disrupt the pull-across (fix 2) and make thumb anchoring (fix 1) awkward — so it's a diagnostic stress-test to build awareness of what every string is doing, not a technique to be played smoothly or a replacement for normal playing.

**Closing performance note.** In the full-riff play-along, the repeated riff holds a note over into beat 1 of the next repetition and ties it — called out so the demonstrated muting choices are read against the actual phrasing rather than assumed to reset cleanly at the barline.
