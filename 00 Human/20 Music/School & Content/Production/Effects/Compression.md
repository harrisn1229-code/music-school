# Compression

General, tool-agnostic best practice for compression — the *why* behind the moves, independent of any one plugin. Specific-plugin settings live on the plugin notes ([[Solid Bus Comp]], [[Glue Compressor]], [[Pro-C 2]], [[OTT]], [[API-2500]]); reusable lessons live here. See also [[Compression Staging]], [[Limiting]], [[Multiband Compression]].

## What it is
Turning down the loud bits so the quiet bits can come up — control, glue, and (used as an effect) color. The art is matching gain so you judge the *processing*, not just the level jump.

## From videos

- **"EQ into the compressor/limiter" — boost specific resonant frequencies aggressively BEFORE the compressor stage, letting its gain reduction tame the resulting peaks instead of fighting an equally hot EQ move on its own.** Chris Lord-Alge, mixing a lead vocal: boosts ~2 kHz ("telephone" presence), ~4.5 kHz, and a high shelf around 8-10 kHz for "shine" ahead of a limiter, so the limiter reacts to and controls what would otherwise be a harsh boost — producing controlled excitement rather than raw brightness. Stated as a general principle, not vocal-specific: "EQ into the plugin, and let the plugin's character pull back some of the things you don't like about the EQ" — treat the downstream compressor as part of the EQ decision, not a separate later stage. Cut low end alongside the boosts to counter proximity-effect buildup that gets worse once compression is applied ("woofing"). → [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]] — Chris Lord-Alge, [[Mixing Carrie Underwood's Vocals - Chris Lord-Alge]]
- **A compressor-type taxonomy, with each type's real detection/timing behavior: FET, tube, VCA, optical, digital, upward.** FET (1176-style): fixed ratio steps, 20-800 μs attack — fast enough to almost always color transients by design, good for drum overheads/parallel/aggressive character. Tube (STA/Fairchild-style): nonlinear ~3:1 ratio, 2-8 s release even at its fastest mode — smooths rather than punches, the inverse tradeoff from FET. VCA: often RMS (average-loudness) detection rather than peak — closer to how the ear perceives loudness. Optical (LA-2A-style): fixed 10 ms attack, but a two-part program-dependent release — first half releases at a fixed 60 ms, second half anywhere from 1-15 s later depending on the signal's own frequency/amplitude — the mechanism behind its "detail + glue" reputation on vocals/bass. Digital: best understood as a combinable toolkit of all the above (detection method, attack/release range, distortion amount) plus look-ahead and flexible sidechain. Upward: compresses from the noise floor up rather than peaks down — no transient reduction, no gain-reduction harmonic distortion, but raises the noise floor along with quiet detail. Exact distortion-threshold math: **1000 ÷ frequency (Hz) = oscillation period in ms**; an attack/release shorter than that period distorts that frequency. Also: a compressor plugin without oversampling can generate aliasing distortion at high frequencies that folds back down into the audible spectrum, sometimes disharmonious with the song's key — prefer compressors that explicitly use oversampling/filtering. → [[UAD UA 1176LN]], [[UAD Teletronix LA-2A]], [[Pro-C 2]] — Sage Audio, [Compression Masterclass](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P2_uC93HshU)
- **Stack three compressors on a vocal, each doing a DIFFERENT job in this order: peak-down → RMS-down → upward.** Peak-down attenuates the loudest individual transients. RMS-down measures overall program loudness (not just instantaneous peaks) and compresses from the loudest points down — smoother and more program-aware than peak detection alone. Upward compression measures from the noise floor up and amplifies quiet content, adding detail. Combined attenuation from the two downward stages stays to just a few dB; the upward stage runs MORE aggressively, since upward compression reads as less obvious/audible than downward. Quick attack on both downward stages, ~50 ms release to retain detail without release-side transient distortion. → [[1 Main Vocal Track]] — Sage Audio, [Vocal Masterclass](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dq-vQAGsd8w)
- **The real purpose of compression is creating headroom so makeup gain can reveal whatever's sitting in the quiet range — good detail or unwanted noise, indiscriminately.** Attenuating peaks creates distance from the noise floor to 0 dB; makeup gain then raises everything, including previously-masked content — reads as "upfront" if it's genuine detail, "farther away" if it's mostly reverb tail, or louder noise/hum if that's what's there. Recording problems get emphasized by mixing, mixing problems get emphasized by mastering — compression amplifies what's already there rather than fixing it. Also: **6 dB is the real "noticeable" threshold** for attenuation, since dB FS is logarithmic — 6 dB actually halves perceived loudness and amplitude, not a small tweak. Every compressor also introduces harmonic/intermodulation distortion when triggered (not just saturators) — controllable via knee shape (soft knee reduces THD vs. hard knee at equal gain reduction), attack/release speed (faster cuts into the waveform before a full oscillation, same mechanism as EQ/filter ringing), source frequency (higher = less THD, more oscillations per cut window), and look-ahead (removes most of it by avoiding cutting into the waveform at all). → [[Gain Staging]], [[Pro-C 2]] — Sage Audio, [The Science of Perfect Compression](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L_MSH8_HCek)
- **Attack/release ramp continuously to their max rather than acting like a switch — and a concrete 5-part recipe for an upfront vocal.** A 20 ms attack doesn't mean "wait then snap"; the compressor starts attenuating immediately at threshold-crossing and reaches max attenuation at the 20 ms mark (ramp shape varies by unit/algorithm) — release works the same way in reverse, still compressing throughout the release window after the signal drops below threshold. Upfront-vocal recipe: **soft knee + 4:1 ratio + quick attack with 1–4 ms look-ahead + 5–8 dB gain reduction + automatic makeup gain.** When compression alone still leaves a vocal weak or lifeless, don't reach for more/serial compression — reach for a wave shaper instead (conservative settings, amplify only the quiet-detail range with a linear 1:1 relationship above it) as a tonally gentler way to bring detail forward. → [[Oxford Inflator]], [[EQ]] — Sage Audio, [Vocal Compression: Save Years of Trial & Error](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2RWijLWuW7I)
- **The core distortion rule: attack/release times shorter than a frequency's own oscillation period will distort that frequency — a wave's period in seconds = 1 ÷ frequency.** An 80 Hz wave completes one oscillation every ~12.5 ms; attenuating faster than that cuts into the wave mid-cycle and generates new harmonic content. For a genuinely transparent compress, keep attack and release **longer than the target frequency's own period** — attack >10 ms / release >50 ms is a clean-sounding full-mix starting point. The accuracy behind an unusually low-THD compressor (ToneBoosters' Punch algorithm) traces to a Hilbert transform for more precise level detection, vs. standard detectors whose measurement error becomes distortion. → [[Pro-C 2]] — Sage Audio, [The CLEANEST Compression Possible (not clickbait)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xA2J2TpfoD4)
- **EQ moves can quietly change an element's dynamic range and peak level — recheck levels after EQ, right before a compressor sees the signal.** Boosting a fundamental raises its peak more than the quiet parts of the same range, widening dynamic range; with multiple bands touching overlapping harmonics, whether the net peak rises or falls after EQ depends on which harmonic actually dominates — a compressor set before that recheck may be responding to a peak level that's already shifted. → [[EQ]] — Sage Audio, [RETHINK EQ - It'll Change How You Mix Music](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JCjHSd10rGI)
- **Compress with a real character unit at effectively zero gain reduction, purely for the harmonic/timbral shift — an analog-mastering habit most digital compressors can't replicate.** The Omnipressor (Eventide, unowned) at a **1:1 ratio with no measured compression or expansion** still generates a strong **third-order harmonic** and noticeably maximizes the signal via its input/output stages. Molot (Tokyo Dawn Labs, free, unowned) at no measured attenuation introduces something like a **6 dB/octave high-pass** subtle enough for mastering, plus aggressive even+odd harmonics at high amplitude. The reusable idea beyond either plugin: reach for a great-sounding compressor purely for its color even when the track doesn't need gain reduction. → [[Compression Staging]], [[Plugin Wishlist]] — Sage Audio, [5 Advanced Compression Techniques (Simple to Complex)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V1lZaf8NWd0)
- **Upward processing as the alternative to peak-down compression for an "upfront" vocal, without the overprocessed artifacts.** The classic recipe (peak-down compression + makeup gain) frequently reads overprocessed from ADSR/transient distortion. Instead, amplify the vocal's quiet details WITHOUT touching peaks: [[Oxford Inflator]] boosts lower-amplitude content while leaving peaks essentially untouched until they near 0 dB; the Omnipressor (unowned) and plain parallel compression do something similar. Best result blends both — subtle-to-moderate peak-down compression alongside upward processing/maximization — for an upfront vocal that doesn't announce itself as processed. → [[Oxford Inflator]], [[Parallel Processing]] — Sage Audio, [The 5 MOST IMPORTANT Vocal Mixing Tips](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rXk1lvT6AhU)
- **A compressor's attack parameter is NOT "time before compression starts" — it's "time to maximum attenuation," and the release setting substantially changes when that maximum is reached, which is why compression timing can't be solved with a formula.** Demonstrated on a sine wave slammed low→high→low: attenuation begins the instant the signal crosses threshold, regardless of attack setting. With 20 ms attack / 200 ms release, the *majority* of gain reduction happens within ~20 ms, but *maximum* attenuation isn't reached until roughly 100 ms in — driven by the release time, not the attack figure. Dropping release to 10 ms shrinks both the total attenuation amount and the time to reach it, pulling peak attenuation back down closer to the nominal attack figure. Compressor algorithm changes this further: a "classic" mode (FabFilter [[Pro-C 2]]-style) speeds up attenuation; look-ahead gives near-immediate attenuation, removing the delay almost entirely. **Practical takeaway: there's no reliable BPM-to-attack/release formula — tune by ear, watching the gain-reduction meter, and try to land the return-to-unity right before the next transient** (harder on sporadic-transient sources like vocals). Same advice applies to dynamic EQ. → [[Pro-C 2]], [[Compression Staging]], [[EQ]] — Sage Audio, [How to Time Every Audio Effect to the BPM](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MXz2hPL36PQ)

- **Reframe "Release" as "Weight" — the fix for restoring a cut/ducked band without touching gain.** Once a frequency has been reduced — a static EQ notch, or a band actively being compressed/ducked — the way to bring presence back into that band isn't raising the gain, it's **increasing the release** on whatever's compressing/ducking it: a longer release lets more of the pre-cut content back through on the decay/tail side of each hit, restoring weight with more control than a level move. Practical habit: mentally rename any band-specific compressor's Release knob "Weight" — "if you want it to be heavier in that band, add the release." Paired in the same session with a related sustain trick: when a kick needs to hit harder but is already driving the compressor as hard as it safely can, **extend its sustain/tail instead of pushing more gain** — the ear judges perceived size by *duration*, not just amplitude, so a longer-decaying hit at the same peak level reads as bigger. → [[Compression Staging]], [[Kick Design]] — Jaycen Joshua (Mix with the Masters), [Mixing Summits with Jaycen Joshua](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L6xx7DWKzng)

- **Compression vs. saturation as the choice for controlling a master's dynamics from the peaks down — both do the job, with different character trade-offs.** Compression is more flexible — compress from the average loudness, shape ratio/attack/release/behavior precisely. Saturation is more program-dependent and reads more natural, adding harmonics that fill the sound out rather than just attenuating it. Stated personal habit: skip compression on a full mix and reach for saturation instead — but combining both is valid, in a specific order: **compression first**, for accurate/specific dynamics control, **then saturation**, for the harmonics and analog-feeling character on top. → [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [How to Make a Mastering Chain](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WNkkwWh7DJg)
- **Saturation is, mechanically, soft-knee compression plus harmonic generation — a saturated device overloads past the point of linear input-to-output representation, exactly like a compressor above a 1:1 ratio, except the gain reduction happens gradually (soft-knee) rather than instantly.** That gradualness is part of why saturation reads as smooth rather than "processed" — the transients/loudest parts get reduced in amplitude gradually, and turning the whole signal back up afterward lifts the quietest, most-masked detail the most, which is the real mechanism behind saturation making a mix sound "more detailed and complex." → [[Saturation and Distortion]] — Sage Audio, [What is Saturation for Mixing and Mastering?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KkzZbDg73Zg)
- **Low-level (upward) compression vs. downward compression for loudness, and how it sets up a transient-shaping pass afterward.** Mastering engineers commonly reach for downward compression first — control dynamics so overall level can be pushed up without clipping — but low-level/upward compression captures the *quieter* parts of the signal and amplifies them while leaving peaks at the same level; detail gets pushed forward, so the signal reads louder without downward compression's artifacts. The result is a less dynamic mix/master, but — unlike heavy downward compression — one whose transients are **not truncated**. From there, transient shaping ("a special form of expansion" that captures and expands only the peaks) can make those transients even more distinct — called out specifically for **snares, kicks, and other quick-transient sources**. Sequence: low-level compression first for loudness/detail, [[Transient Master|transient shaping]] after to keep or boost the punch. → [[Transient Master]], [[Oxford Inflator]], [[OTT]] — Sage Audio, [How to Master Loud and Clear Without Sounding Harsh](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cy-S6YmVe2U)
- **Compress only if the material actually needs it — and let optical vs. digital character pick the flavor.** Whether to compress at all during mastering is genuinely debated; the discipline worth keeping regardless of genre is not reaching for a compressor by default. When it is warranted, optical compressors read smooth and gentle (a subtle glue pass), digital compressors read more aggressive (better suited to louder/harder genres) — choose the type for the genre before dialing settings. → [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [How to Setup Your Mastering Chain](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y-5B4kOXUoM)
- **A compressor is functionally an EQ too, via attack and release — worked example on a strummed acoustic guitar.** Quick attack + long release: the quick attack attenuates the initial transient (which carries its own frequency content, so shaping it changes the instrument's overall frequency response); the long release also affects other transients/content across the signal, further reshaping frequency balance. Separately, a short attack distorts the signal, generating new harmonics that add yet another frequency-response change on top. - **Compression mistakes for a loud master: no look-ahead, no long release, and mostly skip compression entirely — except for a specific mid-side exception.** For a mastering pass aimed at loudness, avoid look-ahead and avoid a long release time on any compressor stage, and don't heavily compress the signal at all — outside of a corrective role (de-essing), compression "doesn't help too much when mastering." The one deliberate exception: **subtle mid-side compression for dynamic stereo expansion** — compressing the mid (center) image more than the side reads wider, the same mechanism already logged on [[Width and Stereo]]'s mid-image-compression entries. Consistent with this note's own "transparent settings" bullet below (attack ≥10 ms, release ≥50 ms) but framed here as a stronger, loudness-specific "mostly skip it" stance rather than a settings recipe. → [[Width and Stereo]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [Mastering Loud But Not Squashed](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YJVE4_CQ9Ts)
- **Mid-image-more-than-side compression as a dynamic stereo-width move, applicable beyond mastering — drums, vocals, and full mixes/masters (subtly on the latter two).** Whenever the mid (center) image is compressed more than the side, the mix reads wider, because attenuating the usually-louder center makes the side read louder by comparison — often the natural outcome anyway, since the mid image typically triggers a compressor more than the side does. → [[Pro-MB]], [[Width and Stereo]] — Sage Audio, [Mid Side Masterclass](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SHxih1xc5yM)
- **BPM-time the release for glue compression — the actual formula.** A long release compresses multiple transients collectively (the "glue" cohesion), but if that release isn't in time with the track, the signal's return-to-unity lands like a rogue transient and can mess up perceived timing. Formula: `60,000 ÷ BPM = one quarter note in ms`, ×4 for a whole note, minus the attack time = the release. Pairs well with a **vari-mu ("very-mu")** character compressor for tone (harmonics + subtle EQ on top of gain reduction) — see [[Glue Compressor]] for the owned mapping. → [[Glue Compressor]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [Mastering Compression Settings](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YUn31aE9X_8)
- **Transparent compression settings: attack ≥10 ms, release ≥50 ms (16th-note-timed), stereo sensitivity biased to the mid image, capped at 1.5 dB total.** For compression meant to be inaudible as processing rather than felt: attack no quicker than 10 ms and release no quicker than 50 ms (faster cuts into the transient → harmonic distortion), release additionally timed to a **16th note** (`60,000 ÷ BPM ÷ 4`), stereo sensitivity toward the **center/mid image** (where the master's energy actually concentrates), and threshold/ratio dialed so total gain reduction stays under ~1.5 dB. - **A compressor's attack/release settings are, mechanically, an ADSR-domain EQ move — not just a dynamics move.** Worked example: fast attack + fast release + some lookahead + moderate knee on an acoustic guitar clamps the transient snap; since the sustain/release content sits lower in amplitude and gets relatively lifted by that same compression, the tonal (sustain/release) frequencies rise relative to the transient (attack/decay) frequencies — an EQ shift nobody dialed in on purpose, because transient and tonal content usually carry different frequency content to begin with (ADSR framing). → [[EQ]], [[Compression Staging]] — Sage Audio, [All Processing is Connected - Crucial Knowledge for Pro Mixes & Masters](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XV-0rdUrgHs)
- **Waveshaping: a compressor doesn't just attenuate a waveform, it generates genuinely new, higher-frequency harmonic content — worst on low frequencies with long oscillations, and attack time is the biggest single variable.** Reshaping a waveform via gain reduction isn't pure subtraction; the altered portion of the wave — occupying less time than the original oscillation — reads as new, higher-frequency content layered on top (demonstrated directly on a sine wave: attenuating a 50 Hz wave can generate a 150 Hz component, an EQ move in two directions at once from one compression decision). Attack time visibly reshapes the waveform's curvature more than ratio, release, or knee — easiest to see/hear when compressing low frequencies hard. **Lookahead + oversampling largely solve it**, letting the oscillation pass through before gain reduction is applied so the compressor retroactively attenuates without cutting into the wave's shape — unlike saturators/clippers, where waveshaping is the intended effect rather than an artifact. → [[SUB Slot]], [[BASS Slot]], [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[Limiting]] — Sage Audio, [All Processing is Connected - Crucial Knowledge for Pro Mixes & Masters](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XV-0rdUrgHs)
