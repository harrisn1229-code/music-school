### The song

- **Became a jazz standard after the Modern Jazz Quartet's 1954 swing recording.** Widely covered since — John Coltrane, Sonny Rollins, Chet Baker, Stan Getz, George Benson among the artists named.
- **Framed as a minor 2-5-1 study** — the A sections are essentially nothing but `i – ii°(iv7) – V7` chained over and over in C minor; learning this tune well transfers directly to "thousands of other songs" built on the same shape. → [[🎵 SIGMUND ROMBERG - Softly As in a Morning Sunrise]]

### Melody — scale-first teaching method
- **Before learning any melody, scan it for its scale and any accidentals against the key signature.** The song reads as C natural minor throughout, with exactly one outside note: an **E natural** where the key signature calls for E♭. That single altered note is what pulls one chord's melody line out of **F harmonic minor** instead (see Scales below) — spotting it up front makes the melody far easier to learn and retain, because you're not memorizing 32 arbitrary notes, you're memorizing "C natural minor, except one spot."
- **Root-to-root practice method:** when drilling a scale, start and end on the root, repeat the root note every time it recurs, and don't repeat or linger on any other note — this grounds the ear in that scale's *tonic*, which matters because several of this tune's scales are literally the same seven notes as another scale (C natural minor = E♭ major; F harmonic minor's C-rooted 5th mode = "the same collection of notes," just heard from a different root) and the only thing that distinguishes them audibly is which note you keep returning to. → [[Diatonic Modes]], [[Scale Modes Reference]]
- **Guitar notation reads an octave below concert pitch** — the small **8 printed below the treble clef** ("8vb") on guitar/classical-guitar sheet music means "everything here sounds an octave lower than a piano/violin/vocal reader would assume." Already documented in depth (including the `8va`/position-mark table) at [[Reading Notation on Guitar]] §1 — this video is a live corroboration from a viewer-comment aside, no new fact beyond what's already there.

### Scales
- **C natural minor** — the melody's home scale; the melody itself never plays the ♭7 (no B♭ anywhere), so it can be practiced as "avoid B♭ and you're golden" relative to C harmonic minor, whose only difference from natural minor *is* that one note (B♭ vs. B♮).
- **F harmonic minor, rooted C-to-C** — covers the tune's C7♭9/♭13 chord (the V7 of the minor 2-5-1, spelled as a dominant built on F harmonic minor's 5th degree). **The 5th mode of harmonic minor is Phrygian dominant** — practicing this scale root-to-root off C means you're practicing **C Phrygian dominant** directly, the exact scale to improvise over that C dominant 7 chord. → [[Scale Modes Reference]] (Harmonic Minor mode table, "V — Phrygian nat.3")
- **Two chord-scale-map "levels," explicitly presented as equally valid, not beginner-vs-advanced:**
  - **Complete scale sheet** — every harmonic zone gets its own precise scale: C natural minor (i) → C harmonic minor (the minor-2-5-1's dominant-tension zone) → E♭ major (the relative-major bridge's ii-V) → F Dorian (a single passing measure, same 7 notes as E♭ major/C natural minor, just re-rooted) → F♯ diminished (the passing diminished chord between the ii and V) → back to harmonic minor/2-5-1. Switching scale that literally every measure is called "a little trite" if overdone but still worth being able to do.
  - **Simplified scale sheet** — collapse most of that into **three bigger regions**: "basically C natural minor" for most of the A section (deliberately including — not avoiding — the chromatic major-7 tone that harmonic minor adds, added "by ear, by intuition" once the ear knows it's there), a genuine **F harmonic minor** region for the 2-bar zone that can't be faked as anything else, and **E♭ major** for the bridge. "Big broad strokes" — how advanced players actually think once they know a tune, versus a beginner's precise per-chord scale-switching.
- **Prolongation, explicitly named:** the whole A section can be heard as one prolonged C-minor sonority, with the ii-V motion just "causing a little tension and coming back" rather than a real key change — the same device jazz soloists and classical composers both use to keep a static harmony interesting (go to the five and come back). Directly extends [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]'s prolongation section — same concept, applied here to a *whole tune's* harmonic rhythm rather than a single 4-chord turnaround. → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] §Prolongation

### Chords / harmony
- **Minor 2-5-1 chain, the whole A section:** `Cm(i) – Dm7♭5(ii°) – G7♭9(V7)` repeated. The **G7♭9** voicing used carries an implied ♭13 as well — "a flat 9 always has, unless it says otherwise, a flat 13 as well," because both are borrowed from harmonic minor. → [[The ii-V-I]] (minor version) · [[Extended Chords]] · [[Scale Modes Reference]]
- **A diminished passing chord** sits a half step above the ii and a half step below the V, stepwise-connecting D-half-diminished to G7 — functional-harmony tension resolving to a chord that isn't the tonic ("tonicizing the two"). → [[Diminished Chords]] · [[Secondary Dominants]]
- **Bridge modulates to the relative major (E♭).** Relative major/minor share a key signature (three flats here) and are literally the same 7-note collection, just rooted differently — exactly the C-natural-minor/E♭-major identity used for the root-to-root scale drill above. **Practical fretboard shortcut: the relative major's root sits three frets above the minor tonic's root** (E♭ is the ♭3 of C minor). The bridge is `iv(Fm) – V7(B♭7) – I(E♭maj)`(a 2-5-1 into the relative major, Fm read alternatively as "the 5-of-2" via the circle of fourths) then `VI7(C7) – iv° – V7(G7)` walking back into the top of the form. → [[Modulation (Key Change)]] · [[Circle of Fifths]] · [[The ii-V-I]]
- **Bridges as harmonic contrast, general songwriting/analysis principle:** a bridge's job is to visit a *fresh* tonal area that reads as a real contrast to the verse — "we're so minor, we're so dark, let's go to the happy place" — and in a lot of standards the lyric does the same pivot in parallel with the harmony, then both return to the A section's material. *(General compositional observation, not a specific technique with settings — noted for [[Musical Form]]/[[Chord Progressions]] awareness.)*
- **"Practice your chords through the key" as the fix for not knowing where the ii/iii/IV/V naturally fall** — drill the diatonic 7th-chord quality sequence (I–ii–iii–IV–V–vi–vii°, "major7 minor7 minor7 major7...") in multiple keys until it's automatic. → [[Chords by Key]]

### Chord voicings (guitar)
- **Two full passes through the chart at 140 BPM, one chord per change, at two voicing densities:**
  - **Shell voicings** — root + 3rd + 7th only, "the simplest chord shapes possible... widely used in jazz." → [[Chord Voicings]] §"Which notes actually matter"
  - **Fuller jazz voicings** — the same shapes with one extra color note added to fill out the sound.
- **G7♭9 spelled/voiced as G-B-F-A♭ (1-3-♭7-♭9)**, with the more commonly-played guitar voicing adding the **implied ♭13 on the pinky** even when the lead sheet only shows "♭9" — because a ♭9 dominant chord (coming from harmonic minor) carries a ♭13 by convention unless stated otherwise. Concrete, playable-as-written guidance: if you see `G7♭9` on a chart and play this shape, "you're playing the correct chord." → [[Chord Voicings]] · [[Extended Chords]]

### Comping tip (applies to any song)
🎬 See full writeup at [[Jazz Comping — Rhythmic Motif and Walking Bassline]].
- **Primary tip: pick one simple, repeatable rhythmic motif and stick to it** rather than reaching for fancy/varied comping rhythms before that's locked in — repetition reads as cohesive, "reinventing the wheel" every bar reads as scattered. Demonstrated rhythm: hit on the **"and" of beat 1, and on beat 3.** Save rhythmic variation and departures from the motif for later, once the motif itself is reliably locked in — and returning to the motif after a departure reads as "grounded," not just repetitive.
- **Bonus tip: walking bassline comping.** Play the chord root on beats 1 and 3; on beats 2 and 4, pass toward the *next* chord's root using a half-step-below, half-step-above, or whole-step-above approach note (a chromatic or scale-step lead-in). Works especially cleanly on any progression where chords change every two beats — the C-to-D and D-to-G motions in this tune's A section are given as the worked examples. On a chord that lasts a full bar (the tonic Cm), the same beats-1-and-3 root logic is used "passing to itself." Once basslining is solid, the chord voicing itself can be repositioned within the bar (e.g. beat 1 + the "and" of 3, instead of a fixed grid) for a less mechanical feel — same "vary it once the base pattern is locked" logic as the primary motif tip. Jared references a separate 4-part walking-bassline series (not in the vault) for more depth.

### Internalization tip — think in relationships/distances, not chord names
The series' signature closing segment, unusually fully worked here:
- **Core instruction: replace "this is C minor, then D half-diminished, then G7" with function + interval-distance thinking** — "minor 2-5-1 leading back to the tonic," a whole step up to the ii, then movement through the **circle of fourths** (up a 4th / down a 5th, functionally equivalent — "the 5-1 relationship") to the V, then again to resolve to i. The payoff stated explicitly: thinking in root-shapes-and-distances rather than named chords is what lets the same mental map transfer to *any* key, because "up a fourth" is the same physical/aural move everywhere a specific chord name is not.
- **Worked all the way through the form, including the bridge:** the bridge's key center is "a minor third above the [previous] key, three frets up" (i.e. the relative major, per the harmony section above) — reachable either by thinking distance-to-the-bridge's-own-ii-chord (C minor → up a 4th/down a 5th to F minor, the bridge's ii) then a 2-5 from there, or by jumping straight to the new key center and doing a 2-5 *into* it mentally. From E♭ (the bridge's I), the next move (to C7) is framed as **"off the major 6"** — in a `I-VII-VImaj` degree count, playing a dominant seventh chord built on the 6th scale degree — then resolving that VI7 back through the circle of fourths to F minor, up a half step to the passing diminished, up another half step to G7, and back to the top of the form. **Every one of these moves is described as a distance/relationship/quality query — "what distance does it go to, what quality does it go to, what relationship physically on the guitar am I looking for" — never as a chord name.** → [[The ii-V-I]] §Chains · [[Circle of Fifths]] · [[Secondary Dominants]]
