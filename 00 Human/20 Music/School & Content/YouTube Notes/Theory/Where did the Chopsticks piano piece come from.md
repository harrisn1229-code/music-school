# Where did the Chopsticks piano piece come from?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KCqdAV-yDsY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KCqdAV-yDsY)

## Synopsis

A history-plus-harmony look at "Chopsticks" — originally "The Celebrated Chop Waltz" (1877), published under a male pen name by its actual 16-year-old female composer — including a genuine analytical point about how the piece implies full chords using only one note per hand.

## Techniques

- **Implied harmony from single-note voicing — a genuine minimal-voicing technique, not just a beginner's simplification.** Each hand plays only ONE note at a time, yet the progression reads as a clear, satisfying G7→C (V7→I) resolution: F+G alone stands in for a full G7 (with the 3rd and 7th present, the root and 5th omitted) resolving to E+G (standing in for C major, missing the root), then D+B (another partial G voicing, different notes omitted this time) resolving again to the same C pair — real, purposeful voice leading achieved with the absolute minimum number of notes.
- **Attribution history**: originally "The Celebrated Chop Waltz" (1877), credited to pen name "Arthur de Lulli" — actually composed by Euphemia Allen, 16 at the time, sister of the Scottish publisher who first released it. Originally published BOTH as a solo piece and as a piano duet (a fuller left-hand part exists but is now almost never played — even the famous *Big* movie duet scene has both performers playing the same right-hand part).
- **A genuinely different piece shares the "Chopsticks" name in the UK**: "The Flea Waltz" — not actually a waltz (it's in 4/4, not 3/4) — is a separate, author-unknown beginner piece sometimes mislabeled "Ferdinand Loh" as a pun on the German word for "flea." Worth knowing the two are distinct compositions that happen to share informal naming.
