# Free As A Bird — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

A slow, serious anthem assembled by **creative derivation** — the two-bridge model bent, the I–vi–IV–V cliché bent, and a key scheme playing A major against both its parallel minor and C major. Pollack reads that key scheme as narrative: the early verses reach toward C and get yanked back through A minor, the guitar solo finally arrives in C for real, and the ending returns to A major **by a different route**, so the same tonic doesn't mean what it meant at the start ([[POP-C08 — Relative-key switching]]).

**Form.** The verses are **doubled up three separate times**, which Pollack doesn't find in any earlier Beatles song. The guitar solo is placed so late that most songs would be winding down, and is shortened by two bars to make the pivot home work. The bridge's repeat is halved by cutting its most uncomfortable bars rather than trimming from the end, so the shortening means something.

**Harmony.** The engine is a single substitution: in I–vi–IV–V the third chord is consistently replaced by **iv or ♭VI**, both of which cross-relate with the vi chord immediately before them ([[HAR-C08 — Borrowed chords and modal interchange]]). That one swap buys the pathos *and* an easy pivot into C. Bridge 1 is entered by a **deceptive cadence** ([[HAR-C04 — Deceptive cadence]]) and is more honestly a rising chromatic bassline harmonised toward a key not yet decided — the proof being that the tonic chord ending its first phrase doesn't sound like a tonic at all. Bridge 2 is set up so it can be entered directly instead: same section, two emotional temperatures. The solo lands in C properly and then re-reads its own dominant as **♭VII of A** to pivot straight home ([[POP-C09 — Mixolydian ♭VII]]), and that substitution replaces the old reach-and-fall gesture for the rest of the track — which is why the ending reads as acceptance rather than sadness.

**Melody.** Narrow, remembered by an opening phrase that occupies a tiny space and sustains its first note into the second bar. Re-harmonised in C for the guitar solo, the identical phrase feels like a different melody entirely — the late arrival is a reharmonisation, not new material.
