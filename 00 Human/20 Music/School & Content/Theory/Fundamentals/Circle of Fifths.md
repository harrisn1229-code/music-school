# Circle of Fifths

Part of [[Music Theory]]. The 12 keys arranged so each clockwise step is a perfect 5th up (see [[Intervals]]). Interactive version: [all-guitar-chords.com/circle-of-fifths](https://www.all-guitar-chords.com/circle-of-fifths).

```
            C
        F       G
    B♭             D
   E♭               A
    A♭             E
        D♭      B
           G♭/F♯
```

- Clockwise (5ths): C → G → D → A → E → B → F♯/G♭ → D♭ → A♭ → E♭ → B♭ → F → C
- Each clockwise step adds one sharp; counterclockwise adds one flat ([[Key Signatures]])
- Inner ring = relative minors: Am under C, Em under G, etc. ([[Keys]])

## What it's for
- **Key relatedness** — neighbors share 6 of 7 notes; the closer on the circle, the smoother a key change or setlist transition
- **Diatonic chords at a glance** — for any major key, its I, IV, V are itself + both neighbors; the relative minors of those three are its VIm, IIm, IIIm. That's all six common chords of [[Chords by Key]] in one wedge
- **Progressions** — root motion by 5ths (V→I) is the strongest pull in [[Chord Progressions]]; the jazz ii–V–I walks the circle
- **Transposition** — shift every chord the same number of steps around the circle
- **Pentatonics** — the 5 notes of a major pentatonic are 5 consecutive circle positions ([[Scales]])

## Reverse-lookup: finding the key from a bare progression
The same window-rotation works backwards. Given only a few chords and no other context, scan for every key whose diatonic window contains all of them, rather than assuming the key from the first chord you hear — a short fragment usually fits more than one key. Full worked example (Am–C–G turning out to fit inside three different keys, plus how to disambiguate between them with a 7th chord) → [[Chord Progressions]] § *Reverse-lookup: finding the key from a bare progression*. — Jim Fleser, [[The Chord Wheel - Enrichment|The Chord Wheel]], p.6

## Ear training: harmonic vs. melodic distance
Scale degrees, not just keys, can be arranged around this same circle (each step a 5th apart) — and it predicts which degrees get confused by ear. **Degrees a 5th apart on the circle sound/feel similar** (1 and 5, 5 and 2…), even though they're far apart melodically. **Degrees a half-step apart melodically (1 and ♭2) sit almost opposite each other on the circle** and feel completely distinct despite being pitch-adjacent — melodic closeness and harmonic closeness are two different axes entirely. Full drone-training method: [[Scale Degree Ear Training]]. — Max Konyi, [[Essential Ear Training - Feeling the Minor Scale]]

## Sharp/flat order mnemonic
The accidental order that accrues going around the circle spells a string: sharps add in the order **F C G D A E B**, flats add in the reverse, **B E A D G C F**. Same order already documented in [[Key Signatures]] — this is just the memorable one-string version of it. — Toby W. Rush, [[Music Theory for Musicians and Normal People - Enrichment|Music Theory for Musicians and Normal People]], p.9

## From videos

- **A follow-up clarification: describing chord-to-chord root motion around the circle as "a fifth" or "a fourth" are BOTH correct, and the disagreement usually comes from conflating an interval with a chord function.** For a single melodic note-to-note move (A up to D), direction settles it unambiguously — ascending is a 4th, descending is a 5th. But a *chord*-to-chord move (A minor → D minor) rarely has every voice moving the same direction at once — some notes ascend, some descend, some hold — so labeling the move as purely "ascending" or "descending" is usually inaccurate to begin with, and a 4th/5th are inversions of each other regardless (interchangeable by definition), so either label is valid. **A tempting but wrong fix**: labeling it by chord function instead (i→iv in A minor = "a fourth," v→i in D minor = "a fifth") — this breaks the moment either chord ISN'T the tonic, because it's quietly substituting chord-function vocabulary for interval vocabulary, which use overlapping words ("fourth," "fifth") for genuinely different concepts. **A guitarist-specific bias worth naming**: open strings (except one pair) are tuned in ascending 4ths, which primes guitarists to hear/default to the ascending-4th framing over the descending-5th one for the exact same two notes. → [[Chord Progressions]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Is this a Perfect 5th or a Perfect 4th?]]
- **Why root motion around the circle sounds so satisfying, and both directions demonstrated.** The perfect fifth is (beyond the octave) the most consonant, universally satisfying interval — true for melodic intervals, key relationships, or chord-to-chord root motion alike, which is why chords a fifth apart read as "meant to be." Circle neighbors (e.g. C next to F and G) share all but one note and modulate/progress smoothly; opposite sides (e.g. C and F♯) are maximally unrelated and a jump between them sounds stark (not necessarily bad — sometimes that's the goal). **Anticlockwise root motion** is "the" circular-fifths progression people usually mean. **Clockwise root motion** works just as well and bridges otherwise-distant chords smoothly: "Here Comes the Sun" (The Beatles) and "Hey Joe" (Jimi Hendrix) both travel clockwise — in "Hey Joe" specifically, C major and E major are harmonically distant, so a direct jump would sound abrupt, but walking clockwise glides between them "frictionlessly." **Deliberate compositional use, in the composer's own words**: Ray Manzarek (The Doors) has described consciously building the "Light My Fire" keyboard intro to travel around the circle in imitation of Bach — clockwise for the first two changes, then anticlockwise from the F chord onward, before a deliberately stark final jump to A minor: *"it was a box circle of fifths as it's called... I knew I was going to end up on an A minor but we had to get to that A minor, so I went from A, G, D-flat, A, and then modulated to the A minor... it just came out of 15, 20 years of music... of practice."* **General writing tool**: from any chord, moving up or down a fifth is close to "foolproof" — you can chain several fifth-moves in a row even while straying outside the starting key, each step still sounding consonant; the further you wander this way, the more deliberate you need to be about the fifth-move back home to resolve cleanly. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use the Circle of Fifths progression]]
- **A local fifth-relationship can override a chord's total foreignness to the wider key**: in Blur's "Coffee & TV," a B♭ chord (the ♭5, entirely foreign to the song's home key of E major) is reached directly from an F chord — a fifth-step per the circle — and reads as locally consonant purely from that fifth relationship, even though B♭ has no other business being in the key at all. Full progression: [[Chord Progressions]] § *Blur's "Coffee & TV"*. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Blur's unique chord progression]]
- **Four progressively advanced uses of the circle, in one pass.** (1) **Key signatures (the basic use)** — count sharps/flats, step that many places clockwise (sharps) or counterclockwise (flats) from C to find the major key; the circle also gives the SPECIFIC sharps/flats in order (e.g. counting to A major = F♯, C♯, G♯, in that order). (2) **Modulation smoothness** — adjacent keys on the circle share all but one note, so modulating between them is subtle (G major → C major in "I Want to Hold Your Hand," differing only by F♯ vs. F natural); keys far apart on the circle share few notes, so modulating between them is bold/disconnected (D major → F major in the bridge of "Summer of '69") — useful as a deliberate dial for how dramatic a key change should feel. (3) **Chord-progression smoothness** — root motion that steps around the circle (a fifth at a time) sounds cohesive even across chords outside a single key, e.g. "Hey Joe"'s string of major chords each a 5th apart; root motion that JUMPS across the circle (a tritone, the maximum distance) sounds jarring/acrobatic, e.g. Radiohead's "Subterranean Homesick Alien" intro (Dm→A♭→Dm) — deliberately unnatural-sounding, used there to underscore the song's alien-abduction theme. (4) **Mode brightness ordering** — the circle doubles as a brightness ladder for the modes: moving one step clockwise from a key adds that key's sharp and yields the next-brighter mode (C major → add F♯ → C Lydian, one step brighter); one step counterclockwise adds a flat for the next-darker mode (C major → add B♭ → C Mixolydian); four steps counterclockwise (the four flats of A♭ major: B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭) gives C Phrygian, four degrees darker than C major. → [[Modulation (Key Change)]], [[Diatonic Modes]], [[Scale Modes Reference]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[4 levels of the Circle of Fifths]]
- **In-place key modulation for a lick: edit its accidentals to match a new key instead of relocating it to a different fretboard position.** The default guitarist habit when a song changes key is finding a new scale box/arpeggio shape whose root sits somewhere convenient and moving the whole lick there — often landing in an area the player didn't actually want to play in. Instead, keep the lick in its EXISTING physical position and just raise/lower the specific note(s) the new key's accidentals require (a C major lick moved to G major needs only F changed to F♯, same fretboard position) — the shape stays put, only the pitch content updates. Stated payoff for improvising specifically: reads as less predictable than relocating a pattern, since the physical motion doesn't telegraph the key change. Requires solid fretboard note visualization first. → [[Notes & Pitches]] — BERNTH, [[BEST Music Theory Trick - Use The Circle Of Fifths For SOLOS!]]

- **A fretboard practice system: play the SAME fingering pattern (built on the C major scale shape, fixed neck position) at every step around the circle of fifths, changing only the one note each new key's signature actually adds — rather than relocating a scale box to start on each new root.** C major → G major: raise only F to F♯, everywhere it occurs in the fixed fingering; G major → D major: raise C to C♯ next, same fingering again; and so on around the circle — each key becomes "the previous key's fingering, one note edited," a continuous chain instead of 12 separate memorization tasks or a relocated box that might land in an inconvenient neck position. Only the C major scale in the exercise starts on its own root; every other key's root sits elsewhere within the shared fingering, which is what makes the single-note differences directly audible/visible. Stated payoff beyond fretboard fluency: this connected way of learning keys pays off for sight-reading and composition, more than the box-sliding shortcut most guitarists default to. → [[Keys]] — BERNTH, [[Circle Of Fifths - Why Guitarists Need It In Their Life]]

- **A working-pro prioritization call: the circle is useful for one specific thing (memorizing key signatures) and not the all-purpose master key it's often sold as.** Explicitly contrasted against "theory book authors" who call it "the Rosetta Stone of music theory": *"for the modern musician, the circle of fifths is like, kind of useful for memorizing key signatures, but it's not the frigging Rosetta Stone. It's just a map of how different keys are related."* Direct advice, credentialed by the speaker's own background (undergrad theory + 15 years teaching): **"if you want to be a working pro, skip the circle and work on your groove"** — i.e. past a certain skill tier, marginal practice time is better spent on rhythm-section feel than drilling a reference tool. → [[The Five-Tier Bass Theory Curriculum]] § *Level 4 — The Working Pro* — BassBuzz, [[Music theory you actually need, none of the sh*t you don't]]
- **A circle-of-fourths (descending-fifths) fretboard drill for seeing "crossing strings" chord options that a straight up/down-one-string ladder misses.** Take a key's full diatonic chord set, start from the highest playable voicing, and move root to root via 4th-up/5th-down motion instead of stepping through scale degrees in order — worked in G minor as **iv → ♭VII → ♭III → ♭VI → ii° → i** (Cm → F → B♭ → E♭ → A° → Gm), named explicitly as the same chord sequence as the jazz standard **"Autumn Leaves"** ("and a million other songs"), then repeated switching strings at every chord change (instead of walking a straight line) and again as full 7th chords. Root motion by a 4th up/5th down = a **"5-1"/dominant-tonic relationship** — creates a felt "gravity"/sense of arrival independent of the actual chord qualities involved, which is why so much functional harmony (especially jazz) leans on circle-of-fourths root motion generally. → [[Chords by Key]], [[The ii-V-I]], [[Chord Progressions]] — Jared Borowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared), [[Writing a Complete Song in a Minor Key (Part 1) - Chord Progression]]
