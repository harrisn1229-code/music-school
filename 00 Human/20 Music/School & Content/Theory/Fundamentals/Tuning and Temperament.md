# Tuning and Temperament

Part of [[Music Theory]]. Why Western music settled on 12 notes per octave, and why the way those 12 notes are actually tuned (12-tone equal temperament) is a deliberate compromise, not a physical law. Ranked **F tier** — "nice to know" — on [[Music Theory Concepts Tier List]]; this note exists because a video landed on it, not because it's load-bearing for everyday playing.

## Why 12 notes, not some other number

- **Frequency isn't what matters — intervals are.** A melody is a sequence of intervals, not a sequence of fixed frequencies; transpose every note by the same amount and the melody still reads as itself. Tuning systems are built around which INTERVALS are useful, not which absolute pitches.
- **The octave (2:1 frequency ratio) is the anchor.** Notes an octave apart are perceived as different registers of the same note (octave equivalence, hence the shared letter name) — the simplest possible frequency relationship, and the near-universal starting point for dividing pitch space in tuning systems worldwide.
- **Consonance tracks ratio simplicity.** After the octave, the next-most-consonant intervals are the perfect 5th (3:2) and perfect 4th (4:3) — followed by the major 3rd, minor 3rd, major 6th, minor 6th, each with progressively less simple ratios. The remaining intervals (major 2nd, minor 7th, tritone, major 7th, minor 2nd) are the traditionally dissonant ones — still useful, since a piece with no dissonance at all lacks tension/drama.
- **12 notes is the practical sweet spot** for playing all of the above intervals AND keeping the notes roughly evenly spaced (needed for an instrument to be playable in every key). Fewer notes loses intervals; a few extra microtonal notes squeezed in unevenly destroys the spacing.
- **Alternatives exist but trade playability for access**: 19 notes/octave and 24 notes/octave (quarter-tone) both preserve the standard intervals while adding microtonal ones, but make instruments significantly harder to play — Ivan Wyschnegradsky built a 24-note quarter-tone piano as one real example. Most instrument design (including non-keyboard instruments like violin or trombone, which technically *could* play between the 12 notes) still optimizes around 12 per octave as the best playability/access compromise.

## Just intonation vs. equal temperament

- **Just intonation** tunes every interval to its pure, simple-ratio frequency relationship relative to the root — the purest-sounding tuning, but the intervals are NOT perfectly evenly spaced (each was derived by a different ratio). Result: a piece sounds gorgeous in the key it was tuned for, and progressively more out-of-tune in other keys, because the pure ratios don't transfer when the root changes.
- **12-tone equal temperament (12-TET)** — the modern standard — instead tunes the OCTAVE as the one pure ratio, then divides the space between octaves into 12 perfectly equal steps. Every key now sounds equally (im)pure, which is what makes an instrument playable across all 12 keys without retuning.
- **The tradeoff, in cents**: equal-tempered 4ths and 5ths are only ~2 cents off their pure-ratio counterparts (essentially inaudible). The equal-tempered major 3rd is over 13 cents sharp of its pure counterpart — audible as a "beating"/wah-wah effect when a major triad is played in equal temperament, which disappears if the same chord is retuned to just intonation. Almost all recorded music is 12-TET, so the ear has effectively normalized to it.

## From videos

- **Why a fixed-tuning instrument (piano, guitar) can never be perfectly in tune, and how non-fixed instruments work around it in real time.** Tune every interval relative to one root purely (harmonic-series ratios: 5th = 3:2, major 3rd = 5:4) and OTHER intervals not built directly off that root (e.g. B–F♯ on an A-rooted piano) come out measurably impure — there's no single fixed tuning where every interval is simultaneously pure, which is the actual mechanical reason 12-TET exists as a compromise (only the octave stays pure; the rest is divided into 12 equal steps so every key is equally, not selectively, impure). Unfixed-pitch instruments (violin, voice) sidestep this by retuning each note in real time based on harmonic context — the same fingered B♭ is pitched differently depending what it's stacked against. **"Beating"** (two close frequencies producing an audible pulsing at their difference rate — 400+402 Hz beats at 2 Hz) is both the audible symptom of an impure interval and a practical real-time tuning target: a John Williams scoring-session clip shows him telling a choir to "listen for the beating" and adjust until it vanishes; Jacob Collier describes doing the same by ear when stacking vocal harmonies. **Concrete studio example**: John Frusciante tuned his guitar's A string 14 cents flat for the "Scar Tissue" intro specifically to cancel 12-TET's ~14-cent-sharp major 3rd/10th and get a genuinely just major tenth on the opening interval — retuned back to standard before the rest of the song, only possible because it's a recording, not a live performance. → [[Sound Selection Guide]] (detuning a single sustained interval for extra purity is a reusable production move, not just historical trivia) — David Bennett Music Theory, [Why pianos and guitars aren't really in tune (just intonation vs 12TET)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7JhVcGtT8z4)
- **Microtonality — pitches smaller than a standard semitone — a technique survey plus a common myth-check.** **24-TET** (quarter-tone tuning, doubling 12-TET) achieved historically via two pianos tuned a quarter-tone apart (Wyschnegradsky, Ives), or in modern rock via purpose-built extra-fret guitars (King Gizzard & the Lizard Wizard's *Flying Microtonal Banana*, 2017 — genuinely composed in 24-TET, harmonically static/melody-driven as a direct consequence since quarter-tone chords are hard to stack functionally). **Glissando/slide instruments** pass through microtonal pitches inherently (Gershwin's *Rhapsody in Blue* clarinet glide, an EDM pre-chorus "riser"). **Quarter-tone string bends as blues-derived blue notes** (Jet, "Are You Gonna Be My Girl" — D bent to D-half-sharp, not full D♯). **Vocal glissando, ornamental vs. structural** — Paramore ("Misery Business") passes through a microtonal pitch without resting on it; contrast a **microtonal modulation** (Jacob Collier's "In the Bleak Midwinter," E major → G-half-sharp-major over 4 chords, demonstrated by a held reference pitch audibly shifting from "in tune" to "out of tune" as the passage progresses — proof the listener's whole harmonic reference point moved, not just one note). **The myth-check**: a recording not at standard concert pitch (A=440Hz) is NOT microtonal — pitch-shifting the whole recording preserves all its original semitone-sized intervals; only intervals SMALLER than a semitone count as microtonal. → [[Songs that use Microtonality]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use Microtonality]]
