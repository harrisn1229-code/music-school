## Synopsis

A BassBuzz breakdown of Chris Squire's bass part on Yes's "Roundabout" (*Fragile*, 1971) — framed explicitly as decoding *why* Squire's playing influenced Geddy Lee, John Entwistle (named as "john," the reference is The Who's Entwistle rather than a typo), Steve Harris, and Les Claypool, not just a note-for-note tab walkthrough. Six distinct devices get pulled out of one riff/song, each with a real, transferable lesson rather than trivia: ghost notes as a contrast/texture device (with real cross-genre examples beyond rock), a 16th-note syncopation the video says most players get wrong, a rhythmic "push"/anticipation device, a genuinely rule-breaking drum-and-bass conversational rhythm, whole-song use of space as an arrangement tool, and Squire doubling a buried organ melody on bass in the bridge. The tone/gear section is the most concrete "steal this" segment: a specific string, a specific pick, a specific plucking position, and — notably — an explicit fallback path for anyone without a Rickenbacker or without pick technique (Geddy Lee's own fingerstyle approach to the same riff). The whole video reads as a "here's the toolkit, here's how to use it even if you're not Chris Squire" lesson, closing the gap between "listen to how cool this is" and "here's what to actually practice."

## What's taught

**Ghost notes (contrast device, not just texture).** The main riff is full of ghost notes — cover the string(s) with the fretting hand, then pick or pluck for a thumpy, percussive (unpitched) sound, marked `X` in tab/notation. The stated payoff is contrast, not just "cool texture": played every-note-clean instead, the riff loses contrast and the notes fill up more sonic space, leaving less room for other instruments to cut through. Ghost notes break the riff into a mix of pitched notes and percussive thumps on purpose. Cross-genre examples given, deliberately not all rock: Chuck Rainey's bass line on "Rocksteady" (Aretha Franklin), Kim Deal's bass line on "Hey" (Pixies), and slap examples like Mark King on Level 42's "Love Games." Practice suggestion: drill switching clean-note ↔ ghost-note back and forth, e.g. through a scale.

**16th-note syncopation in the main riff (the video's flagged "people get this wrong" moment).** The main riff mostly lands notes on the beat, but there's a rhythmic emphasis off the beat later in the riff that's commonly missed. 16th-note primer given directly: divide each beat into four equal parts, counted "1 e and a, 2 e and a, 3 e and a, 4 e and a." The riff goes "1 e and, 2 e and a, 3 e and—" and then a note starts the *next* group early, on the "and" of 3 — that's the syncopation. The common mistake: players (BassBuzz's own former self included) miss it and start that note right on beat 4 instead, landing it a 16th note late. The end-of-riff note pattern itself is common and, if you know the minor pentatonic scale, is just the 4th–5th–7th scale degrees — but the syncopation is what makes it read differently than an unsyncopated cousin using the same notes, e.g. "Paranoid" (Black Sabbath, same notes, straight quarters) or the Tenacious D "Tribute" guitar riff. Cross-referenced against BassBuzz's own catalog: syncopation at the 8th-note level is covered in the Beginner to Badass course; this is the 16th-note-level version; applying it to your own playing is covered in a "beginner bass lines people play wrong" video; and it's also named as present in "Another One Bites the Dust" (Queen).

**Anticipation ("the push") — the riff's first note arrives early.** Normally the riff starts squarely on beat 1. But the first time it's heard (and a few times after), the first note actually lands early, on the "and" of the previous beat 4 — i.e. it anticipates the downbeat by an 8th note. Named directly as the musical device **anticipation**, also called a **push**. Effect: adds excitement, pulls the listener into the next section. Important caveat given directly: pushes are usually played by *multiple instruments at once* (bass + drums together here) — a push played by only one instrument just sounds like you can't play in time. A second worked example later in the track pairs the push with a drum fill leading into it. Demo contrast: the same simple riff played squarely on the beat every repeat feels unmotivated; adding the coordinated push with the drummer gives it real momentum.

**Drum-and-bass "conversation" — deliberately breaking the "stick with the kick" starting rule.** BassBuzz's own baseline advice (from a separate "how to play bass with a drummer" video): as a safe starting point, play the bass in the same rhythm as the kick drum. Roundabout's chorus-ish section (called "the chorus" loosely in the video) deliberately breaks that rule: bass and drums mostly share the same 8th-note grid but bounce/ping-pong off each other rather than locking together, and drummer Bill Bruford is swapping the **snare** in for places the **kick** would normally sit. Looking at snare, kick, and bass together: sometimes they line up, sometimes they don't — that disagreement is what makes the listener pay more attention, and there's room for it in the arrangement because the hi-hat is playing simple quarter notes and there are big gaps in the vocal in this section. Demonstrated directly: if the bass lined up 100% with the drums here, the section would sound noticeably less cool (a "bummer" A/B demo is played). **Explicit guardrail:** this doesn't mean rule-breaking bass/drum conversation is always the right call for a song — test it with a real drummer first, and don't default to a busy counter-kick-drum bass part on a simple pop song where it isn't earned.

**Use of space across the whole song, as an arrangement-level tool.** Zoomed all the way out to the full song's bass track: the first thing that stands out is the big blank stretches where the bass isn't playing at all — that absence-then-presence is itself a contrast device. Even within the sections where Chris IS playing, density varies hard: the main riff is dense (a lot of notes even with ghost notes thinning it) → the chorus riff is much sparser ("way more space") → the space then inverts completely in the closing guitar solos, where the bass hits every 16th note. Practical takeaway given directly: think about a song from a bird's-eye view — verse, chorus, bridge — and ask whether there's contrast between them. Not every song needs it (some deliberately stay the same throughout), but if the song's felt energy is changing section to section and the bass part isn't changing with it, the two simplest levers are (1) fewer notes ↔ more notes, or (2) shorter notes ↔ longer, sustained notes — either one changes the bass line's felt sense of space.

**Melody doubling in the bridge — Chris plays two bass parts' worth of material in the same register.** The bridge's "obvious" bass part is the one you'd play live. But on the record, buried under the main bass line, Chris is *also* doubling the keyboard/organ melody on bass — in the same register as the other bass material, simultaneously — described as "freaking mayhem" and demonstrated by playing both bass parts together against the drums so the listener can hear how intensely they bounce off each other. BassBuzz reads this melody-doubling instinct as evidence of Squire's debt to earlier melodic bassists — Paul McCartney, Jack Bruce, and John Entwistle are named directly — and points to more of the same across Yes's catalog and especially Squire's 1975 solo album *Fish Out of Water* ("so much bass melody").

**Tone and gear — concrete recipe plus an explicit no-Rickenbacker fallback.** Squire's tone is described as clicky, growly, with the notes buzzing audibly as they sustain. Real gear: a **1964 Rickenbacker**, both pickups engaged, run into **two separate amplifiers**. Stated directly: you cannot get this sound out of a P-bass with flatwounds, there's no way to exactly replicate the original gear, and — pointedly — even the official Chris Squire signature reissue basses reportedly didn't sound like his original instrument despite carrying his name. Four concrete, achievable steps toward the tone on any bass:
1. **Strings** — bright stainless-steel round wounds, specifically the **Roto Sound Swing 66** (what Chris actually used).
2. **Pick** — the **gray Herco heavy** pick, described as fairly plasticky and bright-sounding.
3. **Plucking/picking position** — near the bridge, for more edge/bite.
4. **Attack** — play hard and nasty; digging in is part of the tone, not optional.

**The no-pick / no-Rickenbacker fallback, worked through explicitly rather than left as a gap:** if you don't own a Rickenbacker or don't play with a pick, Geddy Lee's own version of this riff (played fingerstyle on a Jazz bass) is offered as proof the tone is reachable another way — on a two-pickup bass, engage **both pickups**, turn the bass's tone knob up, and crank treble/high-mids on the bass or amp for extra nastiness. Two named plucking-hand options: **clacky downward plucks** (per BassBuzz's separate Steve Harris video — Geddy also uses this a lot), or, specifically for this riff, **Geddy's actual approach**: a **back-and-forth strum with the index finger** rather than string-by-string alternating plucks.
