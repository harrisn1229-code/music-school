# Key Signatures

Part of [[Music Theory]]. A key signature = the set of sharps or flats written at the start of each staff line — those notes are **automatically** sharped/flatted everywhere in the piece (all octaves). It tells you the song's [[Keys|key]]: one major key or its relative minor (the notes/chords decide which).

## The pattern
- Sharps are always added in the same order: **F C G D A E B** (each new sharp key adds one)
- Flats are added in the order: **B E A D G C F** (sharps order reversed)
- Each step around the [[Circle of Fifths]] adds exactly one sharp (clockwise) or flat (counterclockwise)
- Memory hook: last sharp = one half-step below the major tonic; second-to-last flat = the major key name

## From videos

- **A 4-rule "legality test" for whether a set of letter names forms a valid major key, plus his own "15 logical major scales" framing of why 12 pitch-classes yield 15 named keys** — (1) a key can never mix sharps and flats, all accidentals are the same type; (2) all 7 letter names (some type of A B C D E F G) must be present exactly once; (3) no letter can appear twice with two different accidental states (can't have both A and A♯ in the same key); (4) the next scale degree always uses the next letter of the musical alphabet (from G the next note is always some flavor of A). Full 15-key list as given: C · F · B♭ · E♭ · A♭ · D♭/C♯ · G♭/F♯ · B/C♭ · E · A · D · G — the 3 overlapping enharmonic pairs (D♭/C♯, G♭/F♯, B/C♭) match this table's D♭, G♭, and B/C♭ rows exactly (the "15" = 12 pitch-classes + 3 enharmonic duplicates). Also notes string/guitar players default to sharp spellings while horn players default to flat spellings — comfort naming a key either way makes it easier to read charts or jam across sections. — Daniel Seriff, [Master Basic Music Theory in Just 12 Minutes: A Beginner's Guide](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W8WIEgavHc0)
