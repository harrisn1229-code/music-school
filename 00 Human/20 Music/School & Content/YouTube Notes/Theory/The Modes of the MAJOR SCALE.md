# The Modes of the MAJOR SCALE

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nI7-2EGDRRk)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nI7-2EGDRRk)

## Synopsis

A walk through all seven modes of the major (diatonic) scale — not just the major/minor pair everyone knows, but Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian and Locrian too — framed entirely around **character note + emotional quality + a real song example**, so each mode gets an identifiable "sound" instead of just a formula. Ends with a seven-piece ear-training quiz, all on the same tonic (C), asking the listener to identify each mode by ear using its character note.

## What's taught

- **Ionian (major)** — bright, happy, stable, "prim and proper." Example: *You Can Call Me Al* by Paul Simon, entirely in F major.
- **Dorian** — a brighter, "whimsical/mystical" minor; character note = **raised 6th** relative to natural minor. Examples: *Riders on the Storm* by The Doors (E Dorian); *Mad World* (originally Tears for Fears, popularized by Gary Jules & Michael Andrews), F Dorian — the character note (D natural) lands right on "the dreams in which I'm dying."
- **Phrygian** — a darker, "spooky/intimidating" minor; character note = **lowered 2nd degree** relative to natural minor. Examples: the guitar riff of *Wherever I May Roam* by Metallica (E Phrygian); *The Sound of Music* by Porcupine Tree; *A Place for My Head* by Linkin Park. Phrygian's dark color is called out as a common metal-genre choice.
- **Lydian** — bright but "curious," a sense of "wonder/magic/brilliance"; character note = **raised 4th degree**. Examples: Yoda's theme from Star Wars (a common film-score choice specifically for its mystical quality); *Waltz No. 2* by Elliott Smith, D♭ Lydian, with the raised 4th audible from the first melody note.
- **Mixolydian** — major but "laid-back, less directional, cooler"; character note = **lowered 7th degree**, which removes the leading-tone pull back to the tonic that plain major relies on for a cadence. Called out as more common in rock than the plain major scale, even though most players think of it as "a version of major." Examples: *Clocks* by Coldplay; *Bittersweet Symphony* by The Verve, 100% in E Mixolydian, giving it an "open, epic" quality.
- **Aeolian (natural minor)** — darker than Dorian but not as dark as Phrygian; "pensive, meditative." Examples: *Californication* by Red Hot Chili Peppers; *Street Spirit* by Radiohead; *Losing My Religion* by R.E.M.
- **Locrian** — the "unusable" mode: its tonic triad is **diminished**, so the chord that should resolve the key never actually resolves, making the whole mode inherently unstable. Character note = **lowered 5th degree**. Practical takeaway for writing in it: **avoid the 6th degree**, since that note is a perfect 5th away from the relative major and easily pulls the ear back into that far more stable key — this is explicitly how *Dust to Dust* by John Kirkpatrick (B Locrian) stays locked into Locrian instead of resolving to C major. Examples: *Army of Me* by Björk (C Locrian, flat-5 audible in the bass riff); *I'm Looking Forward to Joining You, Finally* by Nine Inch Nails (D Locrian, flat-5 in both bassline and vocal); *Gamma Knife* by King Gizzard & the Lizard Wizard (A Locrian — deliberately written in Locrian as one track of a 7-track album where each track uses a different mode). **A workaround for the diminished-tonic problem**: 3 of the 4 Locrian examples given are vocal-plus-bassline only, with no chords at all — sidestepping the awkwardness of ever having to voice that diminished tonic chord.
- Framing note: calling this set "the modes of the major scale" implicitly treats major as the default. A more neutral term for the same seven-note interval pattern, regardless of which degree is treated as tonic, is the **diatonic scale**.
- Ear-training close: seven short piano pieces, all on the same tonic (C) but each in a different mode, played out of order for the listener to identify by ear — reinforces "listen for the character note" as the practical identification method, not just scale-formula memorization.
