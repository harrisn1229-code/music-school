# The Bass Inner Game — Comparison, Your Why, and Embracing the Suck

BassBuzz's Josh, reflecting on what he'd change if he restarted learning bass, names four **inner-game** mechanisms — not scales, fretting technique, gear, or metronome work (the video opens by explicitly ruling those "outer game" answers out) — as the actual difference between the players who quietly quit and the ones who are still playing decades in. His framing for why this matters at all: technique and theory "will only get you halfway there" — if the inner game is neglected the outcome is years of plateau, frustration, and stagnation rather than steady visible progress, and it's offered as the explanation for why **so many more people used to play an instrument than currently play one, and so few people are still playing into their 50s and 60s.** The four keys, in the order given (the video's own numbering skips from "key number one" to "key number two" to an unlabeled third movement to an explicitly-flagged "final key," so the third isn't verbally numbered in the source — treated here as key 3 for continuity): **(1)** don't let comparison run your life, **(2)** know your why, **(3)** don't wait for inspiration — just show up, **(4)** embrace the suck. This is the direct mindset/motivation counterpart to [[Goal-Tiered Practice and the Self-Taught Bass Commandments]]'s Commandment 6 (patience as the #1 self-taught failure cause) and [[Noodling as a Scheduled Reward (The Peak-End Rule)]]'s motivation-as-a-resource framing — all three are BassBuzz videos converging on the same underlying claim, that *staying* a bassist is a psychology problem at least as much as a technique problem.

## Key 1 — Don't let comparison run your life

**The trap, named concretely:** you're feeling good about your playing, you had a good week, then you see a nine-year-old shredding harder than you ever have and want to quit — one bad comparison erases real progress you'd actually made. Personal case study offered as the extreme version: moving to LA "overconfident," having his ego crushed comparing himself to the city's musicians, and — rather than connecting with the new players, being shown his weaknesses, and working through them — **fleeing to Phoenix** instead of meeting the challenge. Framed as the textbook bad outcome of unmanaged comparison: discouragement, deterioration, giving up.

**The reframe — comparison itself isn't the problem, the *kind* of comparison is:**
- **Helpful comparison** — *"I love how tastefully she chose her bass fills, I'm inspired to improve"* — comparison used as a specific, actionable model to move toward.
- **Unhelpful comparison** — *"she's so much better than me, I quit"* — comparison collapsed into a global, hopeless verdict on yourself.
- Named cause of why the unhelpful version wins by default: **brains are wired to focus on the negative** — the helpful framing takes deliberate redirection, not just "not comparing."

**The mechanism underneath both outcomes — fixed vs. growth mindset** (drawn from mindset research, not named on-camera but described directly): a **fixed mindset** treats talent/ability as a static trait you either have or don't, which makes comparison *inherently* discouraging — if you don't believe you can improve, you don't act to improve, which means you don't improve, a self-fulfilling prophecy. A **growth mindset** treats talent/ability as something built through hard work, strategy, and mentoring, which makes comparison *inspiring* instead — believing you can improve makes you want to practice, which makes you actually get better, which reinforces the belief. **Everyone runs a mix of both beliefs across different areas of life** — this isn't a switch you flip once, and nobody has a clean growth mindset everywhere.

**The concrete tool: the "question-markify" technique.** Take a limiting belief stated as flat fact and turn it into a question — *"I can't get better"* → *"Can I get better?"*; *"I'm too old to learn bass"* → *"Am I too old to learn bass?"* The question form leaves an open blank where the statement had a closed verdict, which prompts you to actually go check the belief against reality instead of accepting it unexamined. *(General starting point — apply it to any fixed-mindset belief that shows up around comparison, not just the two named examples.)*

## Key 2 — Know your why

**Core claim: no clear why → aimless drift.** Named directly as his own 20s — "stumble aimlessly, forget to practice for years at a time, and just generally suck." **Your why is simply what you actually want out of learning bass** — examples given run from grand to trivial on purpose (be famous, play with friends, play along with favorite songs) specifically to establish that a small, personal why counts exactly as much as an ambitious one.

**Two named ways people get this wrong:**
1. **Thinking the why has to be grandiose.** A modest why — *"I just want to play music and have fun"* — is a completely legitimate why if it's actually true for you. A clear why, however simple, does two concrete things: it keeps you connected to whatever originally inspired you to start, and it gives you a practice agenda — you can now deliberately work on whatever boosts *your* enjoyment, rather than a generic curriculum.
2. **Forgetting that your why changes over time.** Treated as natural, and possibly a sign of maturing rather than a failure to stay consistent. **Worked personal example:** at 14 his why was "become a world-renowned virtuoso like Victor Wooten" — and he did the matching work (heavy practice, music study, gigging). Over time the why quietly shifted — he cared more about groove and musical communication, less about shredding, and no longer wanted to spend five hours a day practicing — but **he didn't notice the shift and kept running the old virtuoso-practice regimen against a why that no longer existed**, which led to abandoning practice altogether for a long stretch. **The actionable lesson: periodically check whether your why has moved, and update what you practice to match the *current* why, not the one you started with** — a stalled-out practice habit can be a why mismatch rather than a discipline failure.

**Finding your current why:** no universal answer given — "hunt for the one that lights you up the most" among the range of reasons players actually have (the transcript doesn't itemize BassBuzz's specific list on-screen, so treat this as an open self-check rather than a fixed catalog).

## Key 3 — Don't wait for inspiration; just show up

**Names the failure mode directly: "the inspiration myth"** — the belief that you'll consistently *feel* like practicing, and that feeling is what should trigger picking up the bass. Reality, per the video: it doesn't work like that, and believing it does keeps you "stuck passively waiting for lightning to strike before you actually take any action."

**Two outside references used to back this up:**
- **Stephen King** (65+ novels) — "amateurs sit and wait for inspiration, the rest of us just get up and go to work."
- **Flea** (Red Hot Chili Peppers) — "it's not always fun, but if you want to be good at something, you have to do it when it's not fun."

**Self-honesty beat:** Josh admits he still loses to this himself — money spent on pedals and a looper for a "cool bass looping idea" he'd made zero progress on in six months at time of filming. The point isn't that this gets solved once; it's an ongoing practice.

**The actual fix, stated plainly:** show up and do the work regardless of whether you feel like it or feel inspired — inspiration is not a precondition for practicing. **This is where Key 2 pays off directly**: a clear why is what carries you through the moments resistance shows up ("I don't feel like it, I'm tired, the economy...") — without a why, that resistance wins.

**Practical lever named for making "just show up" easier: remove the daily decision-making overhead.** A structured course (his own "Beginner to Badass" is the named example, but the mechanism generalizes) works specifically because you don't have to invent a practice routine from scratch each session — you just execute the next bite-size lesson. **This is the same lever as [[Goal-Tiered Practice and the Self-Taught Bass Commandments]]'s Commandment 1 short-term-goal ladder** — both are ways of pre-deciding what to practice so "should I practice today" never has to become "what would I even practice," which is where a lot of no-shows actually originate.

## Key 4 (the final key) — Embrace the suck

**Reframe: skill acquisition isn't a smooth climb — there's suck woven through the whole process**, and the fix isn't tolerating it passively, it's leaning into it intentionally. Named instances of "the suck": feeling discouraged after a bad comparison, feeling uninspired during scale practice, not feeling like practicing at all next week — Keys 1–3 are, in effect, three specific flavors of the suck this key is the general answer to.

**Worked case study — "Forget Me Not" (the Patrice Rushen bassline later sampled in Will Smith's "Men in Black"):** after 15 years of playing, Josh found himself embarrassed he couldn't play a fast passage in it cleanly. He was tempted to permanently fake it with hammer-ons (an easier substitute technique that dodges the actual skill gap) rather than build the real thing. Instead he **spent weeks practicing the one specific weak-point movement (a thumb technique) every day, slow, at his own actual speed** until he could genuinely play it — and demonstrated the result on camera in a later video. **The reframe that made this sustainable: the fact that something sucks that much is itself a signpost you're moving in the right direction, not a signal to quit or route around it.**

**Failure-based learning, backed by Michael Jordan:** *"I've missed more than 9,000 shots in my career... I have failed over and over and over again in my life, and that is why I succeed."* Named claim: people who fail and learn from the failure improve faster than people who avoid failing/risking failure in the first place — avoidance isn't actually the safer path for progress, it's the slower one.

**A concrete drill for training the "grit muscle" itself** (not a substitute for embracing the suck generally — a specific exercise to build the capacity for it):
1. **Flip the bass over and play it "opposite-handed"** — mirror the way you normally hold it; the actual string order doesn't matter, the point is guaranteed incompetence on your own instrument.
2. **Try something simple in that reversed configuration** (his own example: "some Pink Floyd").
3. **The target isn't the music — it's your relationship to the mistakes.** Normal playing + a mistake tends to produce *"I shouldn't have made that mistake"* (resisting the suck). This drill is deliberately unplayable enough that the mistakes become funny instead of shameful — *"these are funny horrible noises we're making, this is how I sounded on day one."*
4. **Repeat this kind of exposure regularly** — one session of the reversed-bass drill doesn't rewire the mindset by itself; the grit-building effect comes from doing it, or things like it, "day after day."

**Closing pointer:** names a "10-minute daily practice exercise" as a way to both embrace the suck and build real technique/groove gains through repetition — this is BassBuzz's [[Practice THIS Daily (10 Min Technique Fix)]], already in the vault with the actual exercise (minimum-viable-pressure fretting, flying-finger control, octave technique) worked out in full.
