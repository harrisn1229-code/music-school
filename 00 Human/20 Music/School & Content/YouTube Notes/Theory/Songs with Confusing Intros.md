# Songs with Confusing Intros

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XrXSupjkhWw)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XrXSupjkhWw)

## Synopsis

Explains a deliberate (or sometimes accidental) arrangement device: at the very start of a song, the ear has no rhythmic context yet, so a solo instrument's opening pulse can be misheard as landing on the downbeat when it's actually on the offbeat — corrected with a jolt once the full band enters and reveals where "one" really is. Works through a spectrum of examples from clean/deliberate to genuinely ambiguous even among trained listeners.

## Techniques

- **The core mechanism: exploit the ear's lack of rhythmic context at a song's very start to misdirect where the downbeat is perceived to be.** With no other instruments to confirm a meter, a solo repeating pulse can be heard as landing "on the beat" when the underlying part is actually offset — the misdirection resolves the moment a second instrument enters and supplies the real metric context, producing an audible "jolt" as the listener's internal pulse snaps into the corrected position. "In the Shadows" (The Rasmus) — opening quarter-note pulse is heard as on-beat until the guitar enters and reveals it was on the offbeat the whole time, an 8th-note perceptual shift.
- **The same device via a doubled/withheld riff**: "Everybody's Got Something to Hide Except Me and My Monkey" (Beatles) — the opening guitar riff seems to land on the downbeat until the rest of the band enters and reveals it was on the offbeat, same "reveal" mechanism as the Rasmus example.
- **A more complex version: inserting an extra beat/8th-note into a rhythmic intro just before the full band enters, so the listener's already-locked-in pulse gets thrown out of sync at the transition rather than corrected cleanly.** "Rock and Roll" (Led Zeppelin) — the drum intro (itself lifted from Little Richard's "Keep A-Knockin'") adds an extra 8th note right before the guitars land, so a pulse the listener DID correctly lock onto during the drum-only section still gets displaced at the exact moment the band enters — genuinely ambiguous even in transcription (the video offers two valid ways to notate it: shifting time signatures, or a fixed meter with the drums starting on the "and" of beat 3).
- **A case where the disorienting beat insertion looks unintentional rather than composed**: Jimi Hendrix's version of "All Along the Watchtower" — an extra 8th note appears somewhere around where the lead guitar enters (its exact placement is genuinely ambiguous even to the analyst), producing a couple of seconds of real rhythmic confusion, compounded by a simultaneous ~5 BPM tempo acceleration at the same spot. Evidence for "unintentional": Bob Dylan's original version has no such added beat — reads as Hendrix's guitar part being played the way he wanted, with the band forced to accommodate the resulting displaced beat rather than the shift being a planned device.
- **A genuinely bistable rhythmic illusion — different trained listeners hear the SAME recording's downbeat in different places, and even the "provably correct" answer doesn't always overwrite a listener's initial impression.** "Bodysnatchers" (Radiohead) — the analyst hears the opening riff's main notes landing on the beat; other listeners hear it landing on the offbeat instead (the offbeat reading is more "logically" correct — it lines up with where the band enters AND with the later vocal melody, which is the identical rhythm) — described explicitly as "the musical equivalent of an optical illusion," where knowing the objectively-supported reading doesn't change what the ear insists on hearing. "Sex on Fire" (Kings of Leon) — similar effect: for the guitar riff to line up correctly with the drums' entrance, a specific note has to be heard as the downbeat, but different listeners' ears latch onto different notes, and a soloed guitar riff genuinely has no way to disambiguate this until the drums arrive and force a reading.
- **A practical observation about when these tricks actually get noticed**: casual listening rarely registers these sections as "wrong" or jarring — the effect surfaces specifically when a musician tries to LEARN/play along with the recording and discovers their count doesn't match the original, meaning the device works precisely because it doesn't feel odd on a passive first listen, only under active rhythmic scrutiny.
