# Rootless 9th Chords and Chromatic Movement in Blues Rhythm Guitar

Part of [[Guitar Positions]] and [[Jazz Harmony]] — a **blues rhythm-guitar comping vocabulary**, the T-Bone Walker/Robin Ford lineage, built almost entirely from one small rootless dominant-9 shape moved chromatically and reharmonized with inserted ii–V–I's. Where [[L-Shape Root Map for Blues Chords]] answers "where does my hand go for I/IV/V" and [[Chord Voicings]] documents shell-voicing shapes generally, this page is about **what to do with your hand once it's there** — the fills, chromatic slides, and functional substitutions that turn a static I-IV-V into sophisticated rhythm playing. This is the **comping-side counterpart** to [[The ii-V-I]]'s existing "insert a 2-5-1 into the blues's I→IV pivot" soloing material (worked there as single-note lines in [[If Your Blues Solos Feel Stale, Try This!]]) — same device, played here as chords instead of lines.

## The base shape: rootless dominant 9

Four notes: **major 3rd, ♭7, 9th, 5th** — no root. This is the vocabulary's entire raw material; every move below is either a relocation, a chromatic slide, or a reharmonization of this one grip.

## Move 1 — the major-6 fill (T-Bone Walker's signature move)

From the rootless-9 shape, slide the two inner notes (the ♭7 and 9th) **up a whole step** — the grip stops sounding like a dominant 9 and starts sounding like a **major 6 chord** (root-3rd-6th once the root is added back in with the 1st finger). Named directly as a T-Bone Walker rhythm-guitar signature. **Use case: a passing fill that sits neatly between solo lines or vocal phrases** — not a chord to hold, a decoration that moves the harmony forward for a beat.

**Drill:** take the rootless-9 shape on the I chord of a 12-bar blues, do the whole-step-slide-to-major-6 fill, then relocate the identical fill (same shape, same fingering) to the IV and V chords — the video works it moving G9 → C9 → D9, i.e. play the fill on every chord of the form as it goes by, not just the I chord. Vary the rhythmic placement of the fill (on the "and" of 2, right on beat 4, etc.) once the shape itself is comfortable — the video notes there are "all kinds of variations" possible in the phrasing.

## Move 2 — half-step-up slide = implied altered V ("Stormy Monday" move)

Slide the **whole rootless-9 shape up one half step**. Reinterpreted at the new fret, the same four notes now voice an **altered dominant a half step above the original root** — e.g. a G9 shape moved up a half step reads as a **D-altered** voicing (♭7, major 3rd, ♭13, ♭9 of D, all present in the unchanged fingering). Named as a classic T-Bone Walker device, heard on tunes like "Stormy Monday."

**Why it works:** in a lot of blues arrangements the bass is walking **I → V → I** underneath a static rhythm-guitar chord, so sliding the guitar's shape up a half step lines up with — and colors — that walk-up, implying the altered five chord the bass is heading toward. **Soloing pairing, stated directly:** if the rhythm player makes this move, a soloist should reach for the **altered scale rooted a half step above the original chord** (in the worked example, D altered over that moved G-shaped voicing) — "works perfectly."

**Drill:** hold the rootless-9 shape for two beats, slide it up a half step for the beat before the chord change, resolve. Do this on the I chord walking toward V and try it on the IV chord walking toward I — the move generalizes to any spot in the form where the bass is approaching a target chromatically from a half step below.

## Move 3 — down-up chromatic neighbor + root stab (T-Bone Walker "classic" move)

From the base shape (G9 in the worked example): **move down a half step, then up a half step back to the original**, then **aggressively stab the root note on the high E string.** A three-part chromatic ornament — neighbor tone below, return, accented root — named directly as "a classic T-Bone Walker thing."

## Move 4 — inserting a 2-5-1 into the blues rhythm part (functioning dominants)

The comping-side application of the same device already documented in [[The ii-V-I]] and [[Blues Form and Harmony]]'s "insert a ii-V ahead of any landing chord" family — here run as **rhythm-guitar chords**, not soloed lines.

**Worked drill (blues in G, resolving to the IV chord):** in the bar(s) before the IV chord (C7/C9) arrives, play **Dm7 → G7(altered) → C7/C9** — a full major ii-V-I in the key of C, landing exactly where the plain blues changes would put the IV chord anyway.

**Generalizes to any target chord in the form** — back to the I chord, up to the V chord, anywhere — the rule given directly: "you just have to look at the chord you're going to" and build the matching 2-5-1 in front of it. Worked second example: to get to G7 (the I chord, in this progression's context), play **D7 or D-altered → G7**.

**Altered-V voicing options for the "5" of the inserted 2-5-1** (both derive from the same scale, ♯9/♭9 are interchangeable colors on the same chord):
- ♭7, major 3rd, ♭13, **♯9** — the "rootless" altered shape used earlier in the lesson.
- ♭7, major 3rd, ♭13, **♭9** — same shape, swap the color tone. Both sets of notes come from that dominant's own **altered scale** (equivalently, the melodic minor a half step above the chord's root).

**A live-band caution stated directly:** this is a **soloist's/rhythm-guitar-player's private reharmonization** — it works "even if the band is not doing it with you," but a 2-5-1 inserted where the bass/other comping instrument isn't expecting it can clash. The I→IV insertion in particular "works great" in most contexts; be more careful inserting one elsewhere in the form if you're not the only harmony instrument. **Also usable directly in soloing** — build lines out of the same inserted 2-5-1 (see [[If Your Blues Solos Feel Stale, Try This!]] for a fully worked single-note-line version of exactly this insertion point).

## Move 5 — Robin Ford voice-leading movements (borrowed, explicitly credited)

Jack Ruch states directly he lifted these from a Robin Ford performance video (linked in his own video's description) and worked a few "snippets" into this lesson — **explicitly credited borrowed vocabulary**, not original to this channel.

- **Pickup into the IV chord:** a short descending run of voicings leading into the IV chord — a **13-type chord (♭7, 3rd, 13th, 9th on top)** → an **augmented-7th/♭13-type chord** → down to the plain rootless-9 voicing on the IV itself. **Ensemble caution stated directly:** these voicings "clash" easily with another chordal instrument (guitar or keys) playing simultaneously — use a lighter touch, don't overplay them, when comping alongside another harmony instrument.
- **Over the IV chord itself, a three-chord color sequence:** a **sus-type chord (readable as a triad a whole step below the bass note, e.g. a B♭ triad over a C bass)** → a chord Ruch names as a **"minor-major7"-quality color chord** (alternatively readable as a dominant chord stacked with ♭7/9/♯11/13 tensions — same notes, different label) → up to the **top part of a 9-type chord a 4th above**. Stated purpose: this sequence deliberately **builds tension that needs somewhere to go** — the chords voice-lead directly into one another rather than being independent grips. **Same ensemble caution repeats here** — play it with a lighter touch around other chordal instruments.
- **Turnaround-section voicings over the V chord:** a **6/9-type chord with no 3rd and no ♭7** (an intentionally "vague," open-sounding grip, usable moving the IV chord up to the V, e.g. C-shape walking to D-shape) and its equivalent built off the V's own root (readable either as a ♭13-type chord or as a **7♯9 chord**, same notes). **Chromatic tension move:** slide that V-chord voicing **up a half step and back down** — the same "chromatic-slide-implies-alteration" logic as Move 2, deployed specifically inside a turnaround for extra pull before resolution.

## Move 6 — high-position 9th-chord "cluster" voicing (up-the-neck, works over any feel)

A distinct rootless voicing higher up the neck: **♭7, 9th, major 3rd, root (on top).** Named as one of the source's own favorite voicings for a dominant chord — the **9th and major 3rd sit only a whole step apart** inside the grip, which gives it a deliberate "cluster" sound (a dissonant second interval, used on purpose for color).

**Chromatic move from this shape:** slide the **3rd up a half step to grab the 4th** — this produces a short chord sequence (roughly: 13-chord color → 9-chord → a ♯9-type "altered" color) that functions the same way as Move 2's half-step-up trick: an **altered-five-chord move resolving into the IV chord**, just fingered from this higher position instead of the lower rootless-9 shape. Soloing pairing, same rule as Move 2: outline that altered five chord with the **altered scale** if soloing over it.

**Feel-agnostic, stated directly:** the source video demos this over a slow 12/8 (slow-blues) feel, but states explicitly the same voicing/movement vocabulary "works great" over a shuffle or swing feel too — don't treat any of these moves as tied to one groove.

## The stated practice philosophy

Ruch is explicit about *how* he wants this internalized, and it's a deliberate, stated choice, not incidental: he skips naming individual chord tones by note name while teaching, because when he's actually playing this material he's "really thinking about interval relationships" — the shapes need to transfer to any key by ear/feel, not by note-name lookup. Stated practice method: **listen to real recorded performances carrying this vocabulary** (not just read a chart) — "you've got to listen to it, you've got to kind of hear it and get into the feel of it." Reference listening named directly: **T-Bone Walker** (Moves 1–3, and named specifically for "Stormy Monday"), **Robin Ford** (Move 5).
