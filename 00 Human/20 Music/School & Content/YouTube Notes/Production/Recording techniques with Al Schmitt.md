# Recording techniques with Al Schmitt

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_9d6jopvF6Q)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_9d6jopvF6Q)

## Synopsis

A short, dense **Mix with the Masters** two-part masterclass with the late Al Schmitt (26x Grammy-winning engineer — Sinatra, Steely Dan, Diana Krall, Ray Charles) walking through his actual tracking setups for upright bass and lead vocal, filmed live in session. Part one covers double-micing an upright bass (two Neumann M149s, one at the f-hole and one near the plucking hand) into a Neve preamp and a Summit tube compressor tapped for barely a dB — used purely for color, never for control — plus how his isolation strategy scales with the ensemble (tight and close for a jazz trio, a full iso booth for a big band). Part two covers his vocal chain, built around a Neumann U-47 (Sinatra/Nat Cole/Dean Martin's mic) into a Neve preamp with almost no compression, because the singer's own mic technique — leaning in and backing off — is already doing the compressor's job. The through-line across both halves is the same philosophy stated twice in different words: **the mic and the room reveal a good performer's sound, they don't manufacture one.** A boomy bass is a bass problem, not a mic-placement problem; a vocalist with real mic discipline needs almost no gain reduction. Schmitt also draws a clean line between problems worth chasing at the source (mic choice, distance, isolation) and problems he now just fixes afterward in Pro Tools (light sibilance, plosive pops) — a tape-era engineer explicitly naming what's changed for him in the DAW era.

## Techniques

### Bass — mic'ing, isolation, and the compressor's real job

- **Two mic positions on upright bass, blended for a full tone: one Neumann M149 down by the f-hole (body/resonance), one M149 up near where the player's fingers are (attack/pluck detail).** Both mics run in cardioid, six to eight inches off the instrument as a default distance — pulled back further specifically when the player switches to bowing (arco) rather than plucking (pizzicato), since a bowed passage puts more sustained energy into the mic at the same distance. → [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **Signal path: mic pair → Neve preamp → Summit tube compressor, tapped for only about 1 dB of gain reduction — purely to print tube color, not to control level.** "I just tap it about a dB. I do that to get the tube sound from the tube." The compressor's job here is coloration, not dynamics management — confirmed later in the same breakdown ("the Summit doesn't help that much in controlling it, because I only just tap it"). Owned-lane mapping: Neve preamp → [[UAD Neve 1073]]; Summit-style opto/tube leveler tapped this lightly → [[UAD Teletronix LA-2A]]. → [[UAD Neve 1073]], [[UAD Teletronix LA-2A]], [[Compression Staging]]
- **"If you have a good bass player and he's got a good sound, you should get a great bass sound."** The mic setup's job is to reveal an already-good tone, not manufacture one — validated on-camera when the player calls it the best bass sound he's ever gotten on his own instrument. Direct parallel to this vault's own [[1 Electric Bass DI Track|Bass DI]] chain philosophy ("playing technique is the tone," rated above every piece of gear in a separate blind test — see [[Bass Tone Chain Priority]]).
- **Isolation strategy scales with the ensemble, not a fixed booth rule.** Small jazz trio (piano + bass + drums, e.g. with Diana Krall): bass stays in the same room as the rest of the group, with a couple of **gobos** (portable baffles) around it for light separation, mic'd on the side facing away from the drums. Harder/louder material in the same room setup: bow (baffle) the bass further off the group for more separation. Big band: bass goes into a dedicated **iso booth** — deliberately a decent-sized room, "not huge but good size," specifically so there's room for the bass tone to expand rather than being choked in a small dead box.
- **Boominess is almost always a player/instrument problem, not a mic-placement or compression problem.** "If he's got a boomy-sounding bass, it's pretty hard to not get a boomy sound... most good bass players get a good tight sound." Response when a bass IS boomy: keep the mics close and tight and don't lean on the compressor to fix it after the fact — the Summit "doesn't help that much" for control at only ~1 dB of GR. A source-first diagnosis, not a processing-chain fix. → [[Sound Selection Guide]]

### Vocal — mic choice, technique-as-compression, and what gets fixed later

- **Go-to vocal mic: Neumann U-47, the historical Sinatra/Nat King Cole/Dean Martin mic — but chosen per-voice, not on autopilot.** "My go-to microphone is a U-47. If that doesn't work with the artist, I'll try a Brauner microphone, or I have so many options, so I'll go get one that finally works." The 47 is framed as the mic that "usually works for everybody," with a stated exception that some voices (a "girl singer" is the example given) work better on something else — mic choice stays a per-session audition even with a strong default. → [[Vocal Mic Technique]], [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **A singer's own mic technique can do most of the compressor's job before the signal ever hits a compressor.** U-47 → Neve preamp → compressor, "but we weren't hardly using any compression at all — she's got great mic technique, she leans in and she backs off, so it makes my life so much easier." A direct, named case of the proximity-effect discipline this vault's [[Vocal Mic Technique]] page already teaches (move in for intimacy/quiet lines, back off before it gets boomy/loud) — here demonstrated as something a skilled *performer* self-manages live, not something the engineer rides after the fact.
- **Pop screens now vs. the pencil trick then.** "I always use a pop screen on it since we have them now, and we don't have to worry about the pops. Years ago we used to take a pencil and put it in front of the mic to help diffuse the pops." Same physical principle (break up the plosive air blast before it hits the capsule) — a period detail on what engineers did before pop filters were standard studio kit.
- **Sibilance gets fixed afterward in Pro Tools rather than chased at the mic, unless it's genuinely bad.** "If they're really, really bad, then we'll try to work something — maybe use a different microphone or try different windscreens. But if we do a song and there's a couple of sibilant sounds in it, we can fix it in Pro Tools — we just dip those things out." Explicitly framed as a tape-era-vs-DAW-era mindset shift: "it's not like years ago when you're directly to tape and you couldn't fix those things... today you can." A named case for *not* over-engineering the tracking stage when a cheap, precise fix exists downstream. → [[De-essing]]
- **Vocal room/booth choice scales with how loud the surrounding ensemble is — the same principle as the bass isolation logic above, applied to vocals.** A screaming-loud big band or full orchestra calls for a real vocal booth with good acoustics (named favorites: Capitol Studios' booth, and Avatar Studios in New York) specifically for **control and fixability** — keeping the vocalist isolated enough that a later pitch problem can still be corrected without fighting bleed. Reinforced by a second engineer on camera, recalling a Vince Gill session: being in the iso booth against a loud live orchestra bought "a lot more control, a lot more isolation."
- **Mic distance for vocals: roughly six to seven inches, held consistent, adjusted by ear per singer.** "I always tell them this far from the mic — six inches, seven inches, that's it." On camera, the distance is fine-tuned live by small adjustments (mic up a touch for one singer, angled down slightly for another) until the engineer confirms "this is perfect" — a reminder that a stated default distance is still a starting point to dial in by ear, not a fixed number. → [[Vocal Mic Technique]]
