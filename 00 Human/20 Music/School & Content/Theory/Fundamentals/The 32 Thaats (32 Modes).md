# The 32 Thaats (32 Modes)

Part of [[Music Theory]]. **Thirty-two seven-note modes generated by one rule**, taken from the
Hindustani *thaat* system and presented in Western terms. Every scale the vault already knows —
plus about twenty it doesn't — falls out of a single closed piece of combinatorics.

**Why this widens the library rather than repeating it.** Every other modes note here derives modes
*downward from a parent scale* — [[Diatonic Modes]] from major, [[Harmonic Minor Modes]] from
harmonic minor, [[Scale Modes Reference]] from melodic minor and harmonic major, and the world-scale
table there from a book's catalogue. All of that is **derivative** thinking, and it can only ever
produce scales that already have a parent. This system is **generative and closed**: it states the
rules a mode must satisfy, then produces the complete list — which turns out to include nine modes
that are nobody's mode of anything. See [[SCL-C04 — Parallel vs derivative mode thinking]]; this is
the strongest argument in the library for the parallel side of that split.

## The one rule

Build a seven-note scale by choosing one note from each pair:

| Degree | Choice |
|---|---|
| 1 | **fixed** |
| 2 | ♭2 **or** 2 |
| 3 | ♭3 **or** 3 |
| 4 | 4 **or** ♯4 |
| 5 | **fixed** |
| 6 | ♭6 **or** 6 |
| 7 | ♭7 **or** 7 |

Five binary choices. **2⁵ = 32.** That is the entire system.

**Why 1 and 5 are nailed down**, and this is the good part: a fixed perfect fifth guarantees the
tonic triad is either major or minor, so **every one of the 32 modes has a stable tonic**. Locrian
is not excluded by taste — it is excluded *by construction*, because it has no perfect fifth and
therefore no reliable home. Nick Clark's position on this is a genuinely useful definition:

> A scale is any set of notes spanning an octave. A **mode** is a scale "that can stand on its own
> with a clear tonic, that carries its own unique emotion or energy about it."

Locrian is still a perfectly good *scale* — it is what you play over a viiø7 — it just isn't a
place you can live. Cf. [[Diatonic Modes]] §modal ambiguity.

**The consequence that makes the set worth studying:** because the tonic is always major or minor
and the seventh is always ♭7 or 7, the 32 fall into **four groups of exactly eight**, by tonic
seventh chord — 8 × Imaj7, 8 × I7, 8 × i7, 8 × i(maj7). That is a chord-first index into modal
colour, which is the working musician's entry point ([[SCL-C08 — Matching a scale to a chord]]).

**And the twelve available pitches are not arbitrary either** — they are exactly the twelve notes of
the 12-note 5-limit lattice, i.e. the notes reachable from the tonic by short moves in fifths and
major thirds. See [[The Harmonic Lattice]]. The two systems are the same twelve notes seen from two
directions.

## ⚠️ Correcting the framing: Bhatkhande named ten, not thirty-two

Both the source document and the spec that commissioned this note call it "Bhatkhande's system of
32 thaats." **That is not quite right, and the correction is load-bearing.**

- **V N Bhatkhande codified 10 thaats** — Kalyan, Bilaval, Khamaj, Kafi, Asavari, Bhairavi, Todi,
  Purvi, Marva, Bhairav. Those ten are the ones actually used to classify the raga repertoire.
- **32 is the complete set the same rules generate.** N A Jairazbhoy formalised and numbered it in
  *The Ragas of North Indian Music* (1971). Clark's own text confirms this without drawing the
  conclusion: Jairazbhoy names only "the 10 main thaats, all of the A's except for A7, and then C9"
   — which is precisely Bhatkhande's ten. The other 22 names in the chart below come from the
  musician **Chaitanya Kunte**, not from the classical tradition's own canon.
- **Carnatic (South Indian) music has 72 melakarta**, and the 32 are a proper subset of them. The
  source's conclusion section blurs "thaats" and "melakarta" as if they were the same count; they
  are not.
- **Uptake**: Jairazbhoy reported roughly 20 of the 32 in live use in 1971; today around 29 are used
  in Hindustani practice, though only about half widely.

So: **the *rule* is the tradition's; the *exhaustive 32* is a theorist's extension of it.** Treat
the twenty-two extra modes as legitimate constructed scales, not as inherited raga material.

### The nine orphans — no parent, no modes

**Every scale containing both a ♭2 and a ♯4** has no sibling modes inside the system, and this is
not a coincidence: ♭2–♯4 is the system's only **augmented third**, and an augmented third can sit
nowhere else. These are the nine that make the exercise worth doing, because none of them can be
reached by rotating a scale you already know.

| A8 | Todi | Todi | 1 ♭2 ♭3 ♯4 5 ♭6 7 | i(maj7) |
| A9 | "Composite" | Purvi | 1 ♭2 3 ♯4 5 ♭6 7 | Imaj7 |

### Names to watch — two collisions with the vault

⚠️ **"Hungarian gypsy" (B5) is not the vault's Hungarian minor.** [[Scale Modes Reference]] and
[[Harmonic Minor Modes]] both give **Hungarian minor = 1 2 ♭3 ♯4 5 ♭6 7**, which is **C8** here
(Simhendramadhyamam). B5 is that scale with a ♭7. Different scales, adjacent names.

⚠️ **"Phrygian major" (C6) is the school's [[Harmonic Minor Modes|Phrygian dominant]].** School
vocabulary wins; keep his label as an alias only.

Three that *do* line up exactly with what is already on the shelf: **A9 "Composite" = Composite II**
in the Guitar Grimoire table on [[Scale Modes Reference]]; **A10 Lydian ♭2 = Marva** is the scale
that note already logs as the Lavender Town colour; **C9 double harmonic** is the same scale as
Byzantine / Arabic / "gypsy major."

## What the structure teaches

**Only three step sizes exist between adjacent degrees**: half step (h), whole step (w), and
**augmented second** (a). Never a minor third — the three-semitone gap between two *adjacent scale
degrees* is always spelled as an augmented second, because the letter names have to keep marching.
See [[FND-C07 — Letter distance decides the interval number]].

Which forces exactly **three interval multisets**, and every mode is in one of them:

| Steps | Modes | Character |
|---|---|---|
| 5w + 2h | A1–A6, B1–B4, D1, D2 | the familiar diatonic-family sound |
| 3w + 3h + 1a | A7, A10, B5–B10, C1–C7, C10 | one exotic gap |
| 1w + 4h + 2a | A8, A9, C8, C9 | two gaps — Todi, Purvi, Hungarian minor, double harmonic |

Sub-classified by whether the mode contains **three semitones in a row** (two consecutive half
steps). That can only happen in two places, ♯4–5–♭6 and 7–1–♭2, which is why D1 and D2 — the two
modes with both half steps adjacent — are effectively **a whole-tone scale with one extra note
wedged in**.

**Four modes are palindromes** — the step pattern reads the same forwards and backwards, so the mode
is symmetrical about the tonic: **Dorian** (w h w · w · w h w), **"Hindu"/Mixolydian ♭6**, **double
harmonic**, **Neapolitan major**. All four have a natural 4, and double harmonic is the only
symmetrical one containing an augmented second. Dorian's symmetry is the reason it feels neither
bright nor dark; that fact is worth having on hand for [[Modal Pop]] and
[[POP-C10 — Dorian IV vs Aeolian iv]].

**Alteration count is a binomial distribution.** Counting how many of the five moveable notes are
altered from Ionian: 1 mode with none (Ionian), 5 with one, 10 with two, 10 with three, 5 with four,
1 with all five (Phrygian ♯4). Flip all five and you get a mode's "flipped" partner —
Ionian ↔ Phrygian ♯4, D1 ↔ D2. Clark records this and honestly adds that he isn't sure it is useful.

**Jairazbhoy's adjacency graph.** Because each mode differs from another by one flipped note, every
mode has exactly **five neighbours** one note away. His numbering encodes it:

- Aₙ neighbours Aₙ₊₁, Aₙ₋₁, Bₙ, Cₙ, Bₙ₋₂
- Bₙ neighbours Aₙ, Aₙ₊₂, Cₙ₋₁, Cₙ₊₃, and D1 if n is odd / D2 if n is even
- Cₙ neighbours Aₙ, Bₙ₊₁, Bₙ₋₃, Cₙ₊₃, Cₙ₋₃
- D1 neighbours every odd B; D2 every even B

**The A-series closes into a circle** — all ten of Bhatkhande's thaats form a loop where each step
is one note. That is the modal analogue of the [[Circle of Fifths]], and it is a practical
composing device: to move a piece's colour without breaking it, step one note around the ring.
This is the same one-note-at-a-time logic [[Scale Modes Reference]] already teaches as the
"natural minor → harmonic minor → Phrygian dominant" chain, generalised to the whole system.

**Mathieu's tetrachord shorthand** is the fastest naming method here: split a seven-note scale into
1-2-3-4 and 5-6-7-8 and name each half. Melodic minor = a minor lower half under a major upper half
("major over minor"); Lydian dominant = "mixolydian/lydian." Faster than counting accidentals.

## "Faux chords" — the most useful idea in the document

Here is the payoff for anyone trying to *harmonise* one of these scales, and it is the one place
this note genuinely corrects the naive approach.

In **C harmonic major** (1 2 3 4 5 ♭6 7), the notes E, A♭ and B look like an E major triad. **They
are not.** The interval from 3 to ♭6 is a **diminished fourth**, not a major third — because the
letters E→A are a fourth apart no matter how they're inflected. Play it and the brain, which has
already fixed A♭ as a major third *below* C and E as a major third *above* C, refuses to also hear
A♭ as G♯. Clark's name for the result is a **"faux" chord**: correct on the keyboard, wrong in the
network.

> "Even in equal temperament where the pitches of A♭ and G♯ are exactly the same, it's the note
> relationships we are mapping in our brains that discern how we interpret harmony."

**This is the practical reason the church modes, melodic minor and harmonic minor are the scales
Western harmony actually uses**, and the other twenty-odd are mostly melodic material: they are the
ones whose triads are real. Where the chords come out faux, the mode wants a **drone and a melody**,
which is exactly how the tradition uses it ([[Raga and Tala (Indian Classical Music)]]).

Two numbers worth keeping: across all 32 modes, the **V and ♭VII roots offer the most real chords**
(8 real, 3 faux each); **♯IV offers the fewest** (2 real, 5 faux). If a mode is fighting you
harmonically, that is where it is fighting.

The system also surfaces **20 distinct interval types**, including four the common-practice
vocabulary rarely names: the **diminished fourth** (3–♭6, ♯4–♭7, 6–♭2, 7–♭3), the **diminished
third** (♯4–6, 7–♭2), the **augmented third** (♭2–♯4, the orphan-maker above), and their inversions.
The same distinction drives [[Augmented Sixth Chords]]: A♭–F♯ is an augmented sixth in C and a minor
seventh in D♭, same two keys on the piano. See [[FND-C05 — Interval inversion]] and
[[Chord Naming by Context]].

## What the system deliberately leaves out

No **♯5**, no **♯1**, no **♭5**, no **♯2**. That is a real cost — ♯5 is the major third of the 3,
which is exactly what a III7 or V7/vi wants ([[Secondary Dominants]]) — and Clark says so plainly:
"don't let this system of 32 modes limit your creativity." The closure that makes the set elegant is
the same closure that makes it incomplete.
