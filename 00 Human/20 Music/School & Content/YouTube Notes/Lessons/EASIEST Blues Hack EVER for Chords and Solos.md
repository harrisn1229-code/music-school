# EASIEST Blues Hack EVER for Chords and Solos

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U4Yp0XDPT-k)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U4Yp0XDPT-k)

## Synopsis

A short, focused lesson teaching a single idea two ways — as a comping/rhythm device and as a soloing device — over a 12-bar blues in G (G7–C7–D7). Daniel Seriff calls it the "two-note wonder" (a name he credits to Telecaster player Danny Gatton; he notes it's also called guide tones, diads, or chord-tone targeting elsewhere), and the whole lesson is really a guitar-mechanics walkthrough of [[Guide Tones]]: the 3rd and ♭7th of a dominant 7 chord — a tritone apart — are enough to state the chord, so instead of grabbing a full bar-chord shape you fret just those two notes on the middle two strings (D and G). What makes it worth a dedicated page is the fretboard mechanics: because every chord in a blues is a dominant 7, and a dominant 7's guide-tone tritone is a fixed, moveable two-finger shape, the diad for the IV chord sits exactly **one fret below** the I chord's shape and the diad for the V chord sits exactly **one fret above** it — so the whole 12-bar form's harmony reduces to one shape sliding up and down three adjacent frets. The back half of the video repurposes the same three shapes as soloing targets: solo mostly out of one pentatonic box and land specifically on whichever guide-tone note *isn't* already in the pentatonic scale, since that's the note that most clearly announces the chord change to the listener.

## Techniques

- **The two-note wonder / guide-tone diad shape.** For any dominant 7 chord, the 3rd and ♭7th sit a tritone apart and together state the chord's quality (major 3rd = dominant, not major or minor) without needing the root or 5th. On guitar, grab just those two notes on the **D and G strings** (the middle two of the six) instead of a full bar-chord shape. Explicitly framed as a fix for "too much information" — a full bar chord has too many notes, eats mix space, and gets in the way of a band with keys or a second guitarist.
- **Worked 12-bar-in-G fretboard shape, all three chords, exact frets:**
  - **G7 (I)** — F (♭7) on the **D string, 3rd fret**; B (3rd) on the **G string, 4th fret**.
  - **C7 (IV)** — the identical two-finger shape slid **down one fret** from the G7 position: E (3rd) on the D string, 2nd fret; B♭ (♭7) on the G string, 3rd fret.
  - **D7 (V)** — the identical shape slid **up one fret** from the G7 position: F♯ (3rd) on the D string, 4th fret; C (♭7) on the G string, 5th fret.
  - So the entire I–IV–V of a blues in G lives on **three adjacent frets (2–5) of just two strings**, IV one fret below I, V one fret above I — the whole progression is one shape sliding a fret at a time.
- **Why it still sounds like a full chord with no root present.** In a band, the bass (or keys, or another guitarist) is almost always covering the root; the ear fuses the diad with that root motion into a complete chord, so the two-note shape "completes" the harmony rather than sounding thin. *(This is the guitar-mechanical proof of [[Guide Tones]]'s "a bass player and a horn can imply a full progression with no chordal instrument present at all.")*
- **Decorate the diad with chromatic slides — a half step below, a whole step below, or a half step above.** All three are shown as usable ornaments for sliding into the target diad, not just a static grab — the same "approach chromatically, slide the whole shape in" idea documented on [[Chord Voicings]], applied here to a two-note shape instead of a full voicing.
- **General-purpose partial-voicing principle, not blues-only.** Stated directly: playing only part of a chord (not just this specific diad) is broadly useful whenever a full bar chord is getting muddy in a mix — echoes [[Chord Voicings]]'s "leave space" and [[Masking and Carving]] guidance, extended to any style, not just blues.
- **Song references for the same I-to-IV dominant motion:** "Feelin' Alright" (Joe Cocker) and "Mercy Mercy Mercy" (Joe Zawinul/Cannonball Adderley) — cited as real tunes built on exactly a one-dominant-to-four-dominant move, i.e. songs where this diad alone would carry the changes.
- **Soloing application — target the guide-tone note that ISN'T already in the pentatonic box.** Solo mostly out of G minor pentatonic box 1 (Seriff's own preferred "diagonal pentatonic" variant), and instead of chasing full arpeggios, land specifically on whichever of the current chord's two guide tones is the "odd one out" relative to the pentatonic scale already being played — that's the note that most clearly announces the chord change:
  - **Over G7:** F (♭7) is already inside G minor pentatonic, so it doesn't register as new — the note to deliberately target is **B**, the major 3rd, which sits *outside* the minor-pentatonic box and is the one that makes the ear hear "G7," not just "G minor."
  - **Over C7:** B♭ (♭7) is already inside G minor pentatonic (it's the ♭3 of the G box), so it doesn't do much on its own — the note to target instead is **E**, the major 3rd of C7, again the one that's genuinely outside the pentatonic scale.
  - **Over D7:** C is already inside G minor pentatonic, so the target note is **F♯**, the major 3rd of D7 and the one note of the three genuinely foreign to the minor-pentatonic box.
  - **Caveat stated directly:** this specific version (landing squarely on the major 3rd against a minor-pentatonic backdrop) works because the rest of the band is holding down the root/full chord underneath; played unaccompanied (no bass/rhythm section), the same note choice loses some of its "nailing the changes" effect because there's no root context for the ear to resolve it against.
  - This is the same underlying move as [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]'s Step 4 (outline chord tones instead of running one scale through everything) and [[Guide Tones]]'s "3rd and 7th matter most, root and 5th don't" — narrowed here to "of the two guide tones, target specifically the one the current scale doesn't already give you for free."
