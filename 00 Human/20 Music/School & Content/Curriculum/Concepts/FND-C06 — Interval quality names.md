# FND-C06 — Interval quality names

**Q.** Why isn't "a 4th" or "a 3rd" a complete interval name?

> [!question]- Answer
> The **number** says how far (how many letter names). The **quality** says which colour — perfect, major, minor, augmented, diminished. C→F is a **perfect 4th**; C→E is a **major 3rd**, C→E♭ a **minor 3rd**.

## Why it matters

It is easy to get by on interval *numbers* alone — C→F is unambiguous whether or not you can name its quality. The cost only shows up in ear training, where "a 3rd" with no major or minor attached is half an answer. Quality is the distinction ear training is built on.

## Top note, bottom note — and which one you count from

**Top note** = whichever pitch is physically higher. **Bottom note** = whichever pitch is physically lower. This is about pitch only, never about which note you hear or play first — a melody descending from a high note to a low one doesn't change which one is "bottom."

**Always count from the bottom note, upward.** Once you know which note is lower, forget the order they were played in entirely. Start at the bottom note and count forward through the letters (wrapping G back to A) until you reach the top note. Never start from whichever note sounded first, and never count backward.

**Why this matters — two very different "B to A" intervals, same two letters:**
- **A a whole step below B** (bottom note A, top note B): count forward from A — A, B — a 2nd. Is B in A major (A B C♯ D E F♯ G♯)? Yes → **major 2nd**.
- **A almost an octave above B** (bottom note B, top note A): count forward from B — B, C, D, E, F, G, A — a 7th. Is A in B major (B C♯ D♯ E F♯ G♯ A♯)? No — B major wants A♯. Is B in A major? Yes → **minor 7th**.

Same pitch classes, opposite roles, completely different interval — because which note is physically lower flipped. (2 + 7 = 9: these two are inversions of each other, see [[FND-C05 — Interval inversion]].)

## The major/minor shortcut

2nds, 3rds, 6ths, and 7ths are never "perfect" — only major or minor (see [[FND-C05 — Interval inversion]] for why perfect intervals are the different family). To find which:

1. **Strip every accidental first.** Only the plain letter names matter for this step.
2. **Check: is the top note in the bottom note's major key?** If yes, it's a **major** interval.
3. **If not, check the other direction: is the bottom note in the top note's major key?** If yes, it's a **minor** interval.
4. **Now re-add accidentals one at a time**, tracking how each shift changes the size — moving either note by a half step shrinks or grows the interval by a half step (major → minor, minor → major, or on into augmented/diminished).

### Worked example: C to E♭

- Strip the accidental: C to E. Letter count C-D-E = a 3rd. Is E in C major (C D E F G A B)? Yes → **major 3rd** baseline.
- Re-add the flat: E → E♭ drops the top note a half step. Shrinking a major interval by a half step makes it minor.
- Confirm with the shortcut directly: is the bottom note (C) in the top note's major key (E♭ major: E♭ F G A♭ B♭ C D)? Yes → **minor 3rd**. Matches.

(For the B→A minor 7th case worked step by step, see the "Top note, bottom note" section above — same shortcut, applied to the wider-apart B and A.)

## Drill

Name fully: C→F · C→F♯ · C→E♭ · D→A · D→A♭ · E→C · E→C♯.
