# Composing in Exotic Minor Scales

Two distinct starting points for writing original riffs/progressions in an unfamiliar minor scale (harmonic minor, Hungarian minor, and similar), worked as full short-composition demos over G harmonic minor and E Hungarian minor. Complements [[Harmonic Minor Improvisation Devices]] (soloing over changes that already exist) — this page is about generating the changes and riff material in the first place.

## Method 1 — Harmonize every scale degree, then build cadences (chord-first)

Write out the scale, then stack a third, fifth, and seventh on every degree using only notes found in the scale. Worked for **G harmonic minor** (G A B♭ C D E♭ F♯ — natural minor with the raised 7th): the resulting seventh chords are **i minMaj7 · ii°7(m7♭5) · III maj7♯5 (augmented-major7) · iv m7 · V7 (dominant) · VI maj7 · vii°7 (fully diminished)**. Pick 2-4 of these and sequence them into a progression/cadence — the harmonic-minor-specific colors worth reaching for: the **i minMaj7** (unmistakably signals harmonic minor) and the **III augmented-major7** (a genuinely dissonant, "connected" sound rather than random chromaticism, because it's still drawn from the scale). Good for chord-driven writing (verse/chorus progressions, cadences) rather than riff-first metal.

**Fast voicing trick for the minMaj7 sound:** take a plain power chord with the octave doubled on top, then just move that top octave note down a half step to the major 7th (e.g. G power chord's top G → F♯). One-finger move, immediate minMaj7 dissonance, no new chord shape to learn.

## Method 2 — Scan a scale diagram for interesting shapes (riff-first)

For scales with no obvious chord-first entry point (worked on **E Hungarian minor**: E F♯ G A♯ B C D♯ — harmonic minor with an additional raised 4th), skip the chord-building step entirely. Pull up a full-neck scale diagram that shows **intervals, not just scale-degree dots** (a diagram that only marks fingering positions with no interval labeling doesn't teach the ear anything), and visually scan it for recurring interval shapes worth building a riff from — e.g. a near-chromatic run, a symmetric run of perfect 4ths, or major 3rds sitting a half-step apart. Build the riff directly from whichever shape looks/sounds most distinctive, rather than starting from a chord progression. Framed as the faster, more intuitive entry point specifically for rock/metal riff-and-interval writing (vs. Method 1's chord/cadence focus) and as a good way to get a feel for a brand-new scale you don't know well across the neck yet.

## Practical production notes from the same demo

- **Program a basic drum beat first**, even roughly, before writing the guitar part — helps lock in timing while recording rhythm guitar and generates rhythmic ideas for the riff itself (in the worked example, moving a chord change to land on beat 4 instead of beat 1 read as more interesting once heard against the drum loop).
- **Record until you have two similar-quality takes**, then pan them hard left/right for a doubled rhythm-guitar sound in the pre-production demo.
- **Harmonize a busy lead line sparingly** — over an already-dense Hungarian-minor riff, adding a full harmony would clutter it; instead double the melody in bare octaves at reduced volume so it thickens the line without adding new rhythmic/harmonic complexity.
