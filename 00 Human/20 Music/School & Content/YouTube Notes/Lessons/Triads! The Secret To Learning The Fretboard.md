# Triads! The Secret To Learning The Fretboard

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7WSaJncJ-YE)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7WSaJncJ-YE)

## Synopsis
Jack Ruch's lesson on using triad shapes as a map for moving blues soloing vocabulary around the entire guitar neck. The setup: play the same basic blues solo — a 12-bar blues shuffle in the key of E — three times, in three different neck positions, then show how those three positions connect into one continuous fretboard. The reason three positions specifically: an E major triad (E, G♯, B) repeats across the neck in three close-position inversions/string-set groupings, roughly centered on open position, the 5th fret, and the 9th fret, and those three triad zones are exactly the three spots the solo gets relocated to. The pedagogical point isn't "here are three solos to memorize" — it's a general method: take a short phrase built from ear-picked-up blues vocabulary (not derived from running a scale), and deliberately relearn it in relative terms (same scale degrees, same rhythm) starting from each triad zone in turn, so a lick that started out locked to one spot on the neck becomes playable anywhere. Ruch frames this against his own early learning experience — picking up isolated Freddie King/Albert Collins/Albert King licks that only worked in the one position/key/string-set he learned them in — and the video closes by demonstrating a full 3-chorus solo that ascends through the three zones (low to high) rather than sitting in one spot for the whole form. Tabs/notation/backing tracks are gated behind the creator's Patreon (external, not something to route further here).

## What's taught
- **The premise: three usable neck positions come directly from the three ways an E major triad (E, G♯, B — root, major 3rd, 5th) can be voiced close together on the fretboard.** On the high E string alone the three triad tones land at open (root), fret 4 (3rd), fret 7 (5th), repeating an octave up starting at fret 12 — that's the skeleton. Applied across a full string set rather than one string, the same three notes reshuffle into different inversions (3rd-5th-root, 5th-root-3rd, root-3rd-5th) depending on where they're grabbed, and those inversion points cluster into three usable zones roughly at open position, the 5th fret, and the 9th fret — matching this vault's [[Position 01]], [[Position 05]] and [[Position 09]].
- **Open-position solo, over the I chord (E, bars 1–4 of the 12-bar form, phrase repeated twice):** top-two-strings (B/E string) lick **B → C♯ → E → G** — stated as the 5th, 6th, root and minor 3rd of E — then **open E → G → C♯ → open E**, sliding up to **E on the B string's 5th fret**; then on the G string, **open G → A hammered on to G♯** (the 3rd of E), sounded together with a **C♯+A double stop**.
- **Same open zone, over the IV chord (A7, bars 5–8):** stays in the same physical spot but reframes the notes around the new chord — **A (root of A7) → G (♭7 of A7) → E → D → back to E**, then **slides up to B (the 5th of E major) then to the root**; more double-stop-style upstrokes on the top two strings (B and high E); a **D pulled off down to open B**, then **A**, then **G hammered to G♯**.
- **Same open zone, over the V chord (B, bars 9–12) and the turnaround:** centers on **open B (the root of the V chord)** — **B up to D with a bend**, **open E up to F♯**, **F pulled off to E**, back down through **A, D, to open B** — then back to the IV chord's 3rd (**C♯**) — then a classic open-E blues lick, **B, D, open E** (stated as the 5th, ♭7 and root of E7), **walking chromatically up into the root of the V chord** on the turnaround.
- **The lick isn't derived from running a scale — it's assembled from blues phrasing vocabulary picked up by ear off records.** Named listening recommendations for this style: early **Freddie King**, **Otis Rush**, and (captions garble this to "Hu suin" — almost certainly **Hubert Sumlin**, unverified spelling).
- **The same solo, replayed relative-degree-for-relative-degree, from the 5th-fret triad zone.** Same phrase shape (same scale-degree sequence, same rhythm) relocated to the 5th-fret zone; Ruch doesn't re-narrate fret numbers here, demonstrating by ear instead — the point being made audibly rather than tabbed. **Because there are no open strings available in this zone, the pull-offs/hammer-ons and the picking-hand feel are physically different**, and moving the same note choices off the thin high E string and onto the B/G strings changes the tone noticeably — described as sounding "sweeter and less aggressive" purely from the change in string/hand position, not a different note choice.
- **The same solo again, relocated to the 9th-fret triad zone**, now sitting more on the thicker D and G strings. **Switches to the bridge pickup specifically for this zone**, because the lower/thicker strings start to sound muddy up here — a brighter pickup selection compensates, made as a per-position tone decision rather than a fixed setting for the whole solo.
- **Each position naturally pulls toward different phrasing.** Ruch notes that playing "freely" (rather than deliberately mirroring the earlier zones) in a given position tends to surface different note choices and inflections than the same idea would suggest elsewhere — e.g. the 5th-fret zone sits mostly on the D/G strings, while the 9th-fret zone opens up the B and high E strings for extra reach upward — so each zone isn't just a transposition, it's a different playable range with its own natural tendencies.
- **The generalizable practice method (the actual "secret" of the title): take a simple phrase and deliberately relearn it in every triad zone, rather than only playing it where it's already comfortable.** Framed against Ruch's own early playing — picking up isolated licks from Freddie King/Albert Collins/Albert King records that only worked in the one position/key/string-set he originally learned them in, which he describes as sounding "always kind of the same" and limiting. Deliberately hunting the same lick down in the other two triad zones is what "opens up the fretboard."
- **Two ways to combine the three zones into a real solo:** (1) **jump between zones freely within a single chorus**, mid-phrase, connecting fragments from different zones as convenient; or (2) **play one full chorus of the 12-bar form per zone, ascending** — open position for chorus 1, the 5th-fret zone for chorus 2, the 9th-fret zone for chorus 3 — adding natural variation each time rather than replaying the seed phrase verbatim. The video's closing full-solo demonstration uses approach (2): three choruses, low to high.
- **Practice approach recommended by the creator:** work it out by ear from repeated video-watching first, with tabs/notation/backing tracks as an optional aid (gated behind Patreon, external to the vault).
