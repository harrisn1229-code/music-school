# Biggest Shred Guitar Mistake - How To Fix It! | Hand Synchronization Lesson

## Synopsis

Names hand desynchronization (right-hand picking strokes not perfectly aligned with left-hand fretted notes) as the single most common shred-technique failure, then builds a dedicated exercise around a 14-sixteenth-note pattern deliberately split across a 4/4-then-3/4 measure pairing, so the repeating pattern's start point (and therefore its picking-angle challenge) lands somewhere new and awkward every repetition rather than always resetting cleanly at the barline.

## What's taught

- **Hand synchronization named directly as guitar's #1 shred-technique failure mode** — even a technically "impressive"-sounding fast take can be exposed as sloppy once you listen critically for whether every picking stroke lines up exactly with its fretted note; the demo track is deliberately played at a moderate 180 BPM 16th notes rather than an extreme tempo, specifically to isolate synchronization quality from raw speed as separate variables.
- **The exercise: a 14-sixteenth-note pattern, arranged as one 4/4 measure followed by one 3/4 measure (rather than forced into an even 16-note 4/4-only cycle).** Because 14 doesn't divide evenly into 4/4, the pattern's starting scale-position shifts to a new, less-rehearsed spot in the measure every repetition — deliberately harder than a pattern that resets predictably every bar. An alternative 7/8-meter notation of the identical pattern is also shown (same notes, no shifting accent) specifically to demonstrate that the 4/4-plus-3/4 arrangement's added difficulty comes from the accent shift itself, not from the note content.
- **Picking-angle detail for this specific lick: the hardest transition is an upstroke on the E string into a downstroke on the B string** (skipping the string in between) — worked with a direct before/after picking-angle comparison (an angle that lets the pick escape cleanly toward the body vs. one that gets stuck and forces a jump over the skipped string), same escape-direction logic as this batch's other angle-focused videos.
- **A hidden music-theory detail folded into the lick, applied rather than separately taught:** one note in the pattern's third repetition is raised a half-step (G→G♯) specifically to produce harmonic minor's characteristic sound — presented as routine application of the "raise the 7th degree of natural minor" rule covered in an earlier dedicated harmonic-minor lesson, not new theory content.
- **General reminder woven throughout:** play patterns like this to a metronome or click track specifically because the odd-measure-length pattern makes internal pulse-tracking unreliable without an external reference.
