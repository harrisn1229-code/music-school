# Why You Want To Think in Functional Harmony

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8Lx926gI1QM)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8Lx926gI1QM)

## Synopsis

A short, dense jazz-theory piece from Jens Larsen making the case for **functional harmony** (tonic/subdominant/dominant, "T/SD/D") as the correct default lens for both analyzing and improvising over jazz progressions — not a chord-by-chord lens, but a function-by-function one. The core claim: what a chord *sounds like it's doing* (its function in the key) matters more than its literal chord-symbol identity, because chords that share a function are interchangeable for both comping and soloing purposes — a minor seventh chord can be a tonic chord (E minor 7 in C) or a subdominant chord (D minor 7 in C) depending purely on context, so "it's a minor 7 chord" tells you almost nothing on its own. The video is short on notation/tab (no on-screen chart) but dense in worked examples, all reasoned through verbally with played illustrations: the diatonic T/SD/D grouping in C major, the "IV minor" (minor subdominant) chord family, a worked case for correctly identifying which chord in an embellished progression is structurally load-bearing versus decorative, and two improvisation payoffs (borrowing lines from function-mates, and reinterpreting a progression's function to generate new harmonic material). It closes with a practical ear-training payoff: functional hearing is coarser-grained than exact-chord hearing, which makes it easier to fake convincingly when learning a song by ear off a phone recording. Larsen frames the whole approach as a corrective to how jazz was taught before "the late eighties," when he says functional/tonal harmony wasn't commonly used to explain standards, and pedagogy suffered for it.

## Techniques

- **The three-function grouping (T/SD/D) in C major, from the diatonic 7th chords.** Diatonic 7ths in C: Cmaj7 – Dm7 – Em7 – Fmaj7 – G7 – Am7 – Bø7 – Cmaj7. Grouped: **Tonic** = Cmaj7 (root) + Am7 (shares 2 of 3 tonic notes); **Subdominant** = Fmaj7 (IV) + Dm7 (ii); **Dominant** = G7 (V) + Bø7 (vii, shares the tritone with V). This is the standard classical T/SD/D grouping ([[Chord Progressions]] § *Harmonic function*), stated here specifically as a jazz-soloing tool rather than a classical analysis exercise.
- **Function-mates sound "almost the same" and are swappable in place — worked live on a 1-6-2-5.** Over `Cmaj7 – Am7 – Dm7 – G7`, swapping Cmaj7 for Em7 "sounds almost the same" — demonstrated as audible proof that Cmaj7 and Am7/Em7 genuinely share a functional identity, not just a theoretical one. Same logic run on the subdominant pair: `Dm7 – F6/9 – Cmaj7` (ii → IV6/9 → I) swapped to `Fmaj7 – F6/9 – Cmaj7` (IV → IV6/9 → I) — identical except the first chord, and it "sounds very similar," confirming Dm7 and Fmaj7 are interchangeable subdominant colors over the same functional slot. *(Direct video demonstration, not general-knowledge — the swap-and-compare is the actual teaching device.)*
- **The "IV minor" (minor subdominant) chord family, named explicitly as common in jazz standards — a fuller roster than a single borrowed iv chord.** In C major: **Fm** (or Fm6, Fm-maj7 — "the different variations of F minor"), **B♭7** ("the backdoor dominant," i.e. F minor with a different bass note — same chord family reharmonized as a dominant), **F diminished**, and, as related **secondary subdominants**, **A♭maj7** and **D♭maj7**. All function as subdominant color even though only Fmaj7 (major IV) is diatonic — this is [[Borrowed Chords|modal interchange]] applied specifically to the subdominant slot, with jazz-specific voicings the vault didn't previously have cataloged. → [[Borrowed Chords]], [[Secondary Subdominants]]
- **A worked test for telling a load-bearing chord from a decorative one inside an embellished 2-5-1, using tonic-substitution priority as the deciding factor.** Case 1 (the "safe" 1-6-2-5): `Cmaj7 – Am7 – Dm7 – G7` — since Cmaj7 and Am7 are BOTH tonic, the whole first half of the bar is tonic function; `Dm7–G7` is technically SD→D, but the dominant is the more important event here, so the whole 4-chord unit reduces cleanly to **C major → G7** (T → D). Case 2 (the trap, worked to show the rule can fail if applied blindly): `Gm7 – A7 – Dm7 – G7` LOOKS like the same shape (strip the "2" chord from a 2-5-1, leaving `A7♭9 → G7`), but doing that mechanically produces something that "doesn't sound like a turnaround anymore" — because **Gm7 here is not a ii chord, it's a tonic-function chord**, and the A7 that follows it is only a **secondary dominant embellishment** (V7/ii), not the structurally important move. Correctly reduced, this progression is **Gm7(tonic) → something-that's-G7(dominant)** — i.e. also T → D, once the secondary dominant is recognized as decoration rather than the real event. **The general rule the two cases teach together: before reducing a progression, check whether the "ii" chord in front of a dominant is actually itself tonic-function — if it is, the dominant chasing it is very likely just a secondary-dominant embellishment on top of a tonic, not a real 2-5.** → [[Secondary Dominants]], [[The ii-V-I]], [[Chord Progressions]]
- **Reduction as a soloing tool, not just an analysis exercise — dial back an over-embellished standard's chord count to its bare T/SD/D skeleton specifically to avoid "running up and down arpeggios."** Real jazz-standard progressions are described as "pretty embellished" — more chords than structurally necessary, added purely for flow/interest — and the practical payoff of reducing to function is knowing which chords in a busy chart are safe to *ignore* while soloing, rather than treating every written chord symbol as an equally important target. *(General improvisation principle stated directly in the video, not a specific lick.)*
- **Borrow melodic material from a chord's function-mates, not just the literal chord written on the chart.** Over a Cmaj7 (tonic), lines can freely pull vocabulary from **Em7** and **Am7** material (the other two tonic-function chords) as well as from C major itself — reframes "what can I play over this chord" as "what can I play over this *function*," widening the available vocabulary for a single written chord symbol without changing what's actually being outlined.
- **Reinterpret-then-embellish: swap a chord for a same-function chord, then decorate the substitute to generate a genuinely different progression that still resolves the same way.** Worked on the IV-minor-to-I move: `Fmaj7 – B♭7 – Cmaj7` (SD → V7/SD-flavored backdoor → T) reinterpreted by replacing Fmaj7 with its subdominant function-mate **Dm7**, then turning B♭7 into a full 2-5 (**Fm7 → B♭7**, i.e. iv-minor's own ii-V), then inserting **Em7** as connective embellishment between the tonic and the new subdominant material — net result: `Cmaj7 → Em7 → Dm7 → Fm7 → B♭7 → Cmaj7`, a fully different-sounding progression from the original 3-chord skeleton, built entirely by substituting within a function and re-embellishing, and demonstrated to work identically as a basis for an improvised line, not just as comping. → [[Reharmonization]]
- **The IV-minor-to-I cadence generalizes across its whole function family for soloing — the same target notes work whether the actual chart says `Fmaj7 – B♭7 – Cmaj7` or `Em7 – B♭7 – Cmaj7`.** Because both progressions share the identical subdominant→secondary-of-dominant→tonic function shape, the same melodic line resolves correctly over either — the key connective motion identified is a descending line from **A down to A♭ down to G** landing into the tonic, which works regardless of which specific subdominant chord (F major or E minor) is actually voiced underneath it. Practical translation: build one line per *function sequence*, not one line per *chord sequence*, and it transfers across every song that shares the shape. → [[The ii-V-I]]
- **Functional hearing as an ear-training shortcut for learning songs off a phone recording, especially useful for teaching.** When a student brings in a song on their phone, the bass is usually clear enough to identify chord quality precisely, but functional hearing lets you get "close enough" even when exact voicing/quality is ambiguous — "it doesn't really matter too much if it's F minor or B♭7" as long as the *function* (subdominant, in this case) is correctly identified, because that's enough to improvise over and to extract usable material from the song for a lesson. Framed as a direct practical payoff of thinking in functions rather than a separate technique. → [[Transcribing and Playing by Ear]]
- **Historical framing, stated as Larsen's own view rather than a sourced claim:** he says that up through roughly the late 1980s, jazz pedagogy mostly did NOT use functional/tonal harmony to explain standards, and that this was a real gap in how students were taught — framing the functional-harmony approach in this video as a relatively modern pedagogical correction rather than a longstanding jazz-education default. *(Opinion/historical claim as stated on camera — not independently verified here.)*
