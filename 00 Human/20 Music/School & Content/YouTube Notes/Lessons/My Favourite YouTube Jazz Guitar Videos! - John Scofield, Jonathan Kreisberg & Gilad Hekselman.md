## What's taught

- **Motivic development at high tempo — reuse and rhythmically displace theme material inside a solo, not just play new lines over the changes.** Jonathan Kreisberg's solo on "The Song Is You" (a long-form ABA standard, theme stated, break, pickup, straight into the groove at roughly 300 bpm) is singled out for connecting ideas "over a larger context" rather than just stringing clean eighth notes: he pulls material directly from the tune's own theme and displaces it rhythmically — taking a theme phrase and shifting where it lands relative to the beat — which Larsen calls essential for solos that stay interesting rather than reading as scale-noodling, even at a tempo where there's barely room to think. Contrasted with Kreisberg mostly staying inside eighth-note subdivisions (no other rhythmic subdivisions/modulations at that tempo) — the interest comes from *what* gets displaced and reused, not from rhythmic complexity for its own sake. → [[Motivic Development]] (repetition/sequence/displacement as a formal toolkit — this is a real-recording instance of using the tune's own theme as the motive) and [[Syncopation and Metric Displacement]] (the "where it lands relative to the beat" mechanism)
- **Clean time and clear intention as their own kind of virtuosity.** What Larsen names first about Kreisberg specifically: his timing and technique are "extremely clean" — he nails the beat and nails the execution of his lines so cleanly that "the idea is clear," which Larsen frames as impressive in its own right, separate from note choice. A reminder that precision of time-feel and follow-through on an idea is itself a practiceable skill, not just a byproduct of having good lines.
- **A genuinely reactive rhythm-section partner changes what a solo can do.** Larsen calls out the pianist (believed to be Dave Kikoski) as unusually locked into reacting to what Kreisberg plays in real time, in contrast to the rest of that rhythm section, which he describes as playing a traditional hard-bop role — laying down a foundation and letting the soloist color on top rather than interacting. The takeaway: a soloist "keeping it interesting" is partly a solo skill and partly a function of how much the comping is actually listening and answering. → [[Ensemble Listening and Comping Restraint]] (the sideman half of exactly this interaction — knowing whether to follow or contrast a soloist's intention)
- **Displace the theme from the very first phrase, not just inside the solo.** In the Gilad Hekselman / Ari Hoenig performance of "The Way You Look Tonight," the theme itself starts getting rhythmically displaced almost immediately — by the second A section it's already shifted roughly two beats, in half time, described as Gilad actively moving it. This is the opposite end of the same device Kreisberg uses (displacing material *within* a solo): here the displacement starts at the melody statement itself, before any improvising begins. → [[Syncopation and Metric Displacement]], [[Motivic Development]]
- **Strong, simple melodic statements moved around the form beat sparse eighth-note density.** Named as one of the most striking things about Hekselman's playing on this clip: he doesn't lean on dense eighth-note lines — his melodic ideas are comparatively simple, "really strong statements," and the interest comes from how freely and extremely those simple ideas get moved around rhythmically on top of the form, not from note-per-beat density. A useful counter-example to "more notes = more interesting."
- **Counterpoint and rhythmic-device variety between two players who know each other's playing well.** Larsen highlights specific examples of Hekselman using counterpoint against Hoenig's drumming, plus a range of different rhythmic devices — and flags that this specific duo can tip into "too much" when it doesn't click, but here it genuinely works: they're visibly having fun without it reading as overstated. A judgment call about chemistry as much as content — noted as subjective ("you may or may not agree with me"), but instructive as a case study in when rhythmic freedom between two strong players reads as interplay rather than clutter.
- **Alternating between half-time swing and half-time Latin feels within the same performance**, moving back and forth between the two rather than committing to one — part of what Larsen frames as constant surprise in where the performance goes next; tied directly to the extreme, almost-pretend-slower-tempo feel described above (playing a very fast tune while behaving, rhythmically, as if it's much slower).
- **A long, deliberate silence read by the room as the end of the solo — used as a dynamics device, not a mistake.** At one point Hekselman/Hoenig leave such a large gap that the audience nearly starts to applaud, thinking the solo is over — Larsen frames this as one of the clip's best moments precisely because open, sparse playing at high tempo can easily read as "boring and undynamic," and this performance avoids that by using space dramatically enough that it registers as a real event rather than an absence. → [[Solo Dynamics and Arc]] (arc-level pacing and held-back payoff moments — this is the same "spend a scarce resource deliberately" logic applied to silence instead of register)
