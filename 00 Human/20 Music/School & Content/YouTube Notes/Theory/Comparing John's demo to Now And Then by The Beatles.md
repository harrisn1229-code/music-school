# Comparing John's demo to "Now And Then" by The Beatles

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xk88M4ABo_4)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xk88M4ABo_4)

## Synopsis

A before/after arrangement comparison — John Lennon's incomplete 1970s home demo vs. the finished 2023 track — showing the concrete arrangement decisions Paul McCartney and Ringo Starr made to turn a rough sketch into a finished song: tempo change, lyric-gap patching, cutting a whole section, and writing a brand new section specifically to justify reusing archival material.

## Techniques

- **Tempo change to shift emotional character**: the demo sits around 80 BPM; the final version raised it to 88 BPM — described as trading some of the demo's mournful, lamenting quality for a more upbeat, driving feel. A concrete example of tempo alone re-coloring a song's emotional read without touching harmony or melody.
- **Patching an incomplete demo vocal with a new lyric line**: where John's original vocal trails off into unclear/unfinished syllables (end of verse 2), Paul added the line "we'll love you" at that exact spot to complete the thought — a direct, minimally-invasive way to finish a collaborator's unfinished vocal idea without rewriting the surrounding material.
- **Cutting a structurally awkward section rather than forcing it to work**: John's demo included a pre-chorus section in E major, sitting between the A minor verse and G major chorus — felt "disjointed," so the final version cuts straight from verse to chorus instead. A real editorial call: a section can be interesting in isolation and still get cut because it doesn't serve the song's flow.
- **Writing a brand-new section specifically to host reused material — the standout technique here.** The final version adds an entirely new bridge (not in John's demo at all) built around a D major→D minor "cool change." The reason it exists: this new section was written specifically so the team could splice in a snippet of vintage Beatles backing vocals from "Because" (Abbey Road) — the chord move creates the exact harmonic context needed to make the sampled vocal moment sit naturally. **A reusable idea for working archival/found material into a new arrangement**: rather than trying to force a found sample into an existing section, write a new section whose harmony is designed around what the sample needs.
- **Deliberate key ambiguity as a transition device**: the new bridge starts on D minor right after a G major chorus — technically still in A minor, but because it arrives straight off a G major chord, the ear briefly can't tell what key it's actually in. Used here as a smoothing device between sections rather than a firm modulation.
