## What's taught

- **Diatonic passing chords, skip-then-walk-down variant.** Going from Cmaj7 to Dm7, skip UP to **Fmaj7** first, then walk back down through **Em7** to land on **Dm7** (Fmaj7 → Em7 → Dm7) — "usually a fairly neutral sound," and because it's just moving stepwise inside the scale, several of these can be chained into a longer run of diatonic chords without ever sounding weird or forced. → [[Passing Chords]] § 3
- **Diatonic passing chords, moving-up-with-inversions variant.** From Dm7, add an **Em7** then continue up to **Dm9** — the smoothness comes from choosing inversions of each chord so the voicing itself climbs stepwise rather than jumping, i.e. picking the *voicing*, not just the chord symbol, to keep the movement smooth. Same diatonic-family device as the walk-down above, run in the opposite direction. → [[Passing Chords]] § 3, [[Chord Inversions]]
- **Diminished passing chord — dominant-diminished half-step-below (the standard device already on [[Passing Chords]] § 1), reconfirmed with a fresh worked example.** Going from Cmaj7 up to Dm7: play the Cmaj7 line, add **C♯dim** on beat 4 (a half step below the D target — functions like a rootless A7, Dm7's own V7), then resolve to Dm7. → [[Passing Chords]] § 1, [[Diminished Chords]]
- **New device — same-root diminished neighbor tone over a static MAJOR chord, resolving back to itself.** Not a device already on [[Passing Chords]]: instead of placing the diminished a half step below the *next* chord, build it on the *current* chord's own root as a passing embellishment that returns home — worked on Cmaj7: play the chord, move to **C diminished** (same root, C), then resolve straight back to **Cmaj7** — "a nice way to add a little bit of movement, a little bit of color to a static chord." → [[Passing Chords]] § 1 (added below), [[Diminished Chords]]
- **New device — same-root diminished neighbor tone over a static DOMINANT chord, resolving back to itself.** Same mechanism as above, worked on G7 instead of a major chord: play G7, move to **G diminished** (same root as the G7), then resolve back to **G7** — described as functioning like its own suspension, a color/movement device rather than a chord change. → [[Passing Chords]] § 1 (added below), [[Diminished Chords]]
- **Diatonic passing chords stacked inside a longer worked turnaround, walking a static ii up to its V.** Instead of sitting on Dm7 before G7: **Dm7 → Em7 → G7** (walking up), then back to Cmaj7 — same "walk the ii up to the V" device already on [[Passing Chords]] § 3, reconfirmed with a fresh worked example inside this video's own 2-5-1 skeleton.
- **Dominant passing chord — A7 (V7 of ii) used decoratively to approach Dm7, with an explicit "why it doesn't sound weird" explanation.** Instead of the C♯dim7 above, substitute the *dominant* of D minor — **A7** — to get to Dm7: Cmaj7 → A7 → Dm7. Larsen's stated reason it sounds natural rather than "a far-out stretch": the underlying progression (C → Dm → G7) already commonly runs with an A7 folded in as a parallel/secondary-dominant progression, so listeners' ears already expect it. → [[Passing Chords]] § 4, [[Secondary Dominants]]
- **Extended worked turnaround chaining several of the above into one phrase.** Cmaj7 → A7 (into Dm7) → a second A7 (extending/re-resolving to Dm7, stretching the ii chord longer) → on beat 4, **G7♭13** → resolving to **C6/9**. Shows the dominant-passing-chord device (A7) combined with an altered-dominant color tone (♭13) heading into the final tonic, landing on a 6/9 voicing rather than a plain major triad. → [[Passing Chords]] § 4, [[Altered Chords]]
- **Guitar voicing equivalence: the "Cmaj7" shape Larsen keeps using is literally an Em7 shape over a C bass.** Explained visually rather than by naming notes: start from a familiar **Cmaj9** shape, strip the root, and the three remaining notes (3rd–5th–7th of Cmaj9, i.e. E-G-B) are the same notes as an Em triad; add the 9th (D) on top and the whole shape is identical to a standard **Em7 grip** — so "Em7 shape, C in the bass (or implied)" reads as **Cmaj9-no-root**. A concrete instance of the general "same shape, different chord" principle already on [[Chord Voicings]] (rootless voicings, guide tones over extensions). → [[Chord Voicings]], [[Guide Tones]]
- **Chromatic passing chords approaching a target from BELOW, worked as a two-hop chain rather than a single slide.** Approaching Dm7 from below: play a melody over Cmaj7, then **Em9**, then in front of that an **E-flat... (spoken as "C sharp minor 9," a half step below Em9)** sliding chromatically up into Em9, which resolves to Dm7. A second chromatic approach chord, **A♭7**, slides down into **G7**, which is then altered before resolving to Cmaj7. Larsen's stated practical trick, restated from [[Passing Chords]] § 2: think about the chord you're heading TO, then just slide the shape chromatically into place — don't worry about naming the passing chord itself. → [[Passing Chords]] § 2
- **Chromatic passing chords approaching a target from ABOVE AND below, worked in the same phrase.** On Cmaj7, approach from below by dropping to **Bmaj7** then sliding back up. On Dm7, approach from ABOVE instead: play **A♭m7(add11)** first, then resolve down into Dm7 → G7 (altered) → and finally approach the closing **Cmaj7** from above too, with **D♭maj7** sliding neatly down into it. Demonstrates both approach directions (chromatic-from-above and chromatic-from-below) back to back inside one turnaround, confirming [[Passing Chords]] § 2's note that this family works either direction. → [[Passing Chords]] § 2
- **Overall framing: think in melodic statements, not chord symbols.** Larsen's closing point, stated as the actual skill behind all four families above: once you start using passing chords, you have to think melodically about where the line is going rather than reading/naming each chord symbol as it goes by — naming them correctly "isn't going to make a ton of sense" as a way to use them in real time.
