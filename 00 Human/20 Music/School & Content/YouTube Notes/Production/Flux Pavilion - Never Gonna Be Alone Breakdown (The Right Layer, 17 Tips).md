# Flux Pavilion: The RIGHT Layer Can TRANSFORM Your Song ("Never Gonna Be Alone" Breakdown) [17 Tips]

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C75vq9Wf7d8)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C75vq9Wf7d8)

## Synopsis

Flux Pavilion breaks down "Never Gonna Be Alone," walking through the small layers and deliberately-imperfect choices that turn a decent bass-music track into a professional one. The throughline: perfection isn't the goal — vibe is. A vocal chain built to sound like a "badly recorded old sample," a granular vocal-as-wavetable soundscape, a hand-played filter sweep, and a single reversed ride cymbal buried under the drums all do more for the track than any one "correct" production move. He's explicit that if a flaw disappears once everything else is playing, it's not worth fixing — test on a big system and trust what you hear over what you think should be technically clean.

## Techniques

- **Layer an imperfect vocal chop under the lead sample, buried enough it reads as one sound.** He sang "I can't stop" himself and layered it into the vocal part of "Never Gonna Be Alone" — it sounds noticeably different from the finished song in isolation, but "production and general weight of stuff can hide a multitude of sins." If a layer disappears once the mix is full, it did its job; don't chase fixing something nobody will notice. → [[Layering and Stacking]]
- **Lo-fi vocal chain for intentional "bad sample" character:** trash/distortion preset (jumps through presets rather than dialing from scratch) → drive/pre-gain pushed → bitcrusher (dirties it up, makes it read as sampled rather than recorded) → ping-pong delay. Goal is a glitchy, degraded vocal specifically because a clean one would kill the vibe of an otherwise polished track. → [[kHs Bitcrush]]
- **Vocal-as-wavetable granular manipulation:** loaded a vocal sample into Serum as a wavetable to get granular-style audio manipulation that's hard to achieve with a normal sampler — the technical starting point (chasing a Nero-on-"Tentacles"-style sound) didn't land as planned, but the granular texture it produced became a core soundscape layer. → [[Serum 2]]
- **Remove OTT from the default spot, or push it to the end of the chain** rather than reaching for it first — a deliberate break from the "OTT on everything" habit. → [[OTT]]
- **Short delay used as a pseudo-release, not an echo:** a very short delay time on a synth voice reads as extra decay/release rather than a repeat — useful when a synth's native release doesn't have the character wanted. Paired with compression and distortion "to round off the sound." → [[Serum 2 FX]]
- **Hand-played filter automation over drawn automation:** runs a filter (his example: a Moog, but a cheap MIDI controller knob works) live while playing along to the song and records the sweep as a take, then edits the result — the goal is the organic, slightly-inconsistent movement a human hand gives a cutoff sweep that automation curves don't. Says once you notice it, "you'll hear it in every song." → [[Automation]]
- **Voice the riff as a chord instead of writing a separate chord part underneath it.** The song's hook is a shoutable riff (a recurring habit across his tracks — "I can't stop," "gold dust" are the same idea) — rather than composing new harmony to sit under it, he re-voices the riff itself as a full chord, so the hook and the harmony are the same idea instead of two competing ones.
- **A one-moment filtered "blur" right after the drop hits** — a brief textural dip built from the same granular soundscape layer, placed right as the drop lands, before it "sucks you back in." A half-second of contrast makes the drop's full-force moment land harder by comparison.
- **One reversed, processed ride-cymbal sample layered under the whole drum bus** to make hits feel "thrown" rather than programmed — a single extra sample doing a disproportionate amount of the work for how natural the drums feel. → [[Layering and Stacking]]
- **Stack a big, noisy clap/cymbal sample under a heavy reverb, then shape its character/tone rather than clean it up** — the point isn't a clean transient, it's a texture you can tune. → [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **Call-and-response arrangement between vocal and bassline:** neither part changes rhythmically very much on its own (both are described as "monotonous" loops), but they're arranged so gaps in one let the other "breathe" — the interplay, not either part alone, is what keeps a repetitive hook from getting old. Bassline note changes under a static vocal loop also recontextualize the same vocal sample without touching it.
- **"Gold dust" mindset — small ear-candy details separate a good track from a professional one.** His explicit advice: give yourself unstructured time to sit and play with a finished-sounding arrangement; the extra micro-layers and one-off moments that come out of that time are what listeners register as "something else," even though no single one of them is a big move. → [[Ear Candy and Hiding Synths]]
