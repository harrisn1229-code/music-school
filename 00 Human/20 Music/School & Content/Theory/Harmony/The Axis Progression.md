# The Axis Progression

**Four chords — I, V, vi, IV — and the four rotations of them that account for an enormous share of popular music.**

```progression-songs
```

[[Chord Progressions]] already lists `I–V–vi–IV` as "the modern pop 4-chord loop." This page is what that entry doesn't say: what it's called, why the rotations feel different, and what to do with it.

## The family

All four rotations use the same four chords. In C major: **C, G, Am, F.**

| Rotation | In C | Feels |
|---|---|---|
| **I–V–vi–IV** | C G Am F | Bright, lifting, optimistic |
| **vi–IV–I–V** | Am F C G | Darker, yearning, "sad" |
| **IV–I–V–vi** | F C G Am | Suspended, unsettled start |
| **V–vi–IV–I** | G Am F C | Delayed arrival — lands home last |

Sometimes called the "Axis" progression, and the four-chord loop the Axis of Awesome built their medley on.

**Examples:** "Let It Be," "Don't Stop Believin'," "With or Without You," "Someone Like You," and several hundred others.

## Why the rotations feel different

The interesting part, and the placement question you missed (POP-13).

**The chords are identical. What changes is which one the ear hears as home.**

Whichever chord the loop *starts* on — and returns to — gets heard as the tonic. Start on C and the loop is in C major: bright, resolved, the vi is a passing shade. Start on Am and the *same four chords* read as A minor: the C now sounds like ♭III, a moment of relief in a minor context rather than home.

**Same pitch content, different perceived tonic, opposite emotional result.** That's a genuinely useful thing to be able to do deliberately — you can flip the mood of a section without changing a single chord.

## Relative major and minor

The mechanism underneath it. **C major and A minor share a key signature** — the same seven notes, a different tonal centre.

So switching a chorus from C major to A minor doesn't change which notes are available; it changes which one everything resolves to. The section darkens without any new material, and without the modulation machinery a real key change needs. That's POP-15, where you had "relative minor" and "happy to sad" but not the mechanism.

**Practically:** if you want a darker chorus, try starting the same loop on vi before writing new chords.

## Why it works at all

Three reasons worth knowing, because they generalize past this one progression:

1. **All three functions are present.** I and vi are tonic, IV is subdominant, V is dominant — the full T→SD→D cycle in four chords. See [[Chord Progressions]] → *Harmonic function*.
2. **It resolves and restarts smoothly.** IV → I at the loop point is a plagal cadence, which closes gently enough to repeat without sounding like a full stop each time.
3. **The chords share notes.** C and Am share C and E; F and Am share A and C. Smooth voice leading means it loops without seams.

## Using it without sounding like everything else

Since it's the most-used progression in popular music, the interest has to come from elsewhere:

- **Change the rotation** between sections — verse on vi, chorus on I, same chords.
- **Move the bass** under it — `C – G/B – Am – F` gives a descending line. See [[Loop-Based Harmony]].
- **Reharmonize the last bar** so each pass ends differently.
- **Swap one chord** for a borrowed neighbour — F becomes Fm on the last pass. See [[Borrowed Chords]].
- **Change the harmonic rhythm** — two bars per chord instead of one changes the whole feel.
- **Add a secondary dominant** — `E7` before the `Am` pulls much harder. See [[Secondary Dominants]].

## From videos

- **A fifth named rotation, distinct from the four basic cyclic rotations above — IV–I–vi–V, "the new go-to" replacing the classic I-V-vi-IV as pop's most common ordering per Bennett.** Not reachable by cyclically rotating the classic loop (rotating I-V-vi-IV only produces the three others already in the family table); this swaps the position of vi and V relative to "IV–I–V–vi." Songs: "Learning to Fly" (Tom Petty), "Fast Car" (Tracy Chapman, both original and its 2020s cover-hit revival), "High Hopes" (Panic! at the Disco, key of F), "Mercy" (Muse, 2015, built on this loop start-to-finish). **iii-for-V substitution on a repeat**: "High Hopes" swaps its closing V for iii on the second pass — justified because V and iii share two of three notes, letting iii stand in for V with "a slightly different flavor" while approaching the next IV chord from the opposite direction (3 up to 4 rather than 5 down to 4). **Why the loop feels perpetual**: the progression reaches V — maximum tension, primed for a textbook V→I perfect cadence — but instead resolves to IV, a **deceptive/interrupted cadence** built into the loop's own structure; every repetition sets up and denies the expected resolution, and even the I chord doesn't feel "done" because it isn't reached via a real perfect cadence. → [[Cadences]] (deceptive/interrupted) — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Pop music has a new favourite chord progression]]
- **When did I–V–vi–IV become so popular? A dated historical timeline, from 446 collected songs plotted by release year.** Earliest confirmed use: "To Know Him Is to Love Him" (Phil Spector, 1958) — NOT "Auld Lang Syne" as sometimes claimed; only modern reharmonizations fit that traditional tune to this progression, the original melody/harmony doesn't use it. "Don't Think Twice, It's All Right" (Bob Dylan, 1963) — a fleeting early instance. "One Fine Day" (Carole King/Gerry Goffin, 1963) — a near-miss with a minor iv instead of the canonical major IV. 1970s (~23 examples found) — still just "another" occasional choice. 1980s (~30 examples) — the point songs start being built ENTIRELY as a loop of this progression rather than splicing it among others ("So Lonely," The Police, 1978; "With or Without You," U2). 1990s — becomes a pop-punk cliché (nicknamed "the pop punk chord progression"), possibly popularized in that scene by The Clash, The Only Ones, then Misfits. 2000s — the true boom, "the go-to progression" the way 12-bar blues was the go-to in the 1950s. **Peak: ~2011** (30 songs that year), just after the Axis of Awesome comedy sketch (~2009) gave the progression its "Axis" nickname by mocking its ubiquity — after which usage falls sharply, reading as "sonically dated" to 2000s pop; by the 2020s usage is back near 1990s levels. Bennett predicts a future nostalgia-driven resurgence, drawing an explicit parallel to the 1970s revival of 1950s 12-bar-blues rock and roll. **Repeat-artist counts**: 9 Ariana Grande songs, 19 Taylor Swift songs, at least 15 Blink-182 songs (consistent with the pop-punk association) all confirmed using this exact progression. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[When did the Axis chord progression become so popular?]]
- **Taylor Swift's five most-reused chord progressions, ranked by count — every one turns out to be an Axis-family rotation, making her catalog a clean single-artist case study in how reordering the same four chords changes their character.** #5 — **IV–V–vi** (9 uses; song: "Fearless") — unresolved subdominant opener for instant momentum, resolves to the "softer" tonic-function vi rather than full tonic. #4 — **vi–IV–I–V** (9 uses; "I'm Only Me When I'm With You") — minor-flavored Aeolian opener, through subdominant tension, to the true tonic, then straight back into dominant tension to keep moving. #3 — **I–vi–IV–V, doo-wop/"50s" changes** (17 uses; "The Way I Loved You") — the classic full T→(T)→SD→D cycle. #2 — **I–V–ii–IV, Bennett's "Believe" progression** (20 uses; "The Story of Us") — fully diatonic, loops cleanly. #1 — **I–V–vi–IV, the canonical Axis Progression itself** (21 uses, a clear margin over #2) — sole harmonic basis of "All Too Well," including its 10-minute re-recording; framed explicitly as evidence her songwriting is lyrics-led rather than harmony-led, the familiar chord bed functioning as a stable emotional foundation so the lyric can carry the song. **General songwriting-craft takeaway**: reusing a progression across many songs isn't inherently lazy — can be a deliberate artist/genre signature (compare 12-bar blues across an entire genre); a chord progression alone "is not a song," just a color palette the rest of the song differentiates. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Taylor Swift's favourite chord progressions]]
- **A minor-key-rooted Axis relative confirmed by direct comparison, distinct from the two chromatic-alteration variants documented above**: "Otherside" (Red Hot Chili Peppers) and "Numb" (Linkin Park) share what's described simply as "the minor Axis progression" — the same four-chord relationship as the core I–V–vi–IV shape, rooted in the minor key rather than major (i.e., the whole family read from its minor starting point rather than swapping in a borrowed chromatic chord as the "She's Electric" and harmonic-minor variants above do) — confirmed by a RipX vocal/instrumental mashup between the two songs. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[These songs have the same chords... let's swap them! (Numb)]]
- **Two more relatives beyond the four core rotations in the table above — one substitutes a chord, one reorders the standard rotation.** (1) **I–V–ii–IV** — takes the standard I–V–vi–IV and swaps vi for ii; functionally a small change (both vi and ii carry subdominant-adjacent function and share notes) but audibly different, since ii→IV now reads as a small ascending climb (2nd degree up to 4th degree) rather than vi's more settled feel. Songs: "Hot n Cold" (Katy Perry), "Moves Like Jagger" (Maroon 5). (2) **I–IV–vi–V** — the standard four Axis chords, but with IV and V swapped relative to the I–V–vi–IV rotation; because V now falls at the END of the loop instead of 2nd position, the progression builds toward its most tense chord rather than resolving through it early, and the loop point becomes a genuine V→I perfect cadence — a stronger, more "classically correct" resolution than the plagal (IV→I) cadence the standard rotation and its siblings normally lean on. Reads as brighter/more major-scale-forward as a result. Song: "We Are Never Ever Getting Back Together" (Taylor Swift). — David Bennett Music Theory, [[6 common chord progressions and why they work]]
- **Two named variants of the minor Axis Progression (i–♭VI–♭III–V, e.g. Am–F–C–G), each swapping ONE chord for a chromatic alternative that pulls harder toward the tonic — both mixed freely with the plain version, often within the same song.** (1) **"She's Electric" progression — I–III–vi–IV** (C–E–Am–F, spoken as "1-3-6-4") — the same axis-family shape, but with the V chord's usual slot filled by a major/dominant III (E) instead. E functions as **V of vi (a secondary dominant)** — Bennett calls it the "secondary dominant Axis progression" — pulling specifically toward vi rather than a plain tonic-adjacent V, adding forward drive from chord 1 to chord 2. The added chromatic note (G♯, from the borrowed E major) creates a rising chromatic line C→G♯→A gluing the first three chords together. Common variations: IV→iv (a melancholy minor-plagal tag, heard in Green Day's "Murder City" and Billie Eilish's "Happier Than Ever"); vi→V→IV (a stepping-down variant, heard in Bowie's "Lady Stardust"). Song: "She's Electric" (Oasis). (2) **Harmonic-minor Axis progression — i–♭VI–♭III–V(major)** (Am–F–C–E in A minor) — swaps the plain ♭VII-adjacent G for a MAJOR E, borrowed from harmonic minor's raised 7th (G♯) — makes the progression far more directional/functional than the plain version, since a major V has real dominant pull back to i (vs. plain minor-key G, which doesn't). The chromatic glue line here is C→E→A (root motion, not a voice-leading line). **Songs mixing BOTH variants (plain ♭VII-based and harmonic-minor V-based) within the same song**: Green Day — "Holiday," "Troubled Times," "21 Guns"; Bad Religion's "Punk Rock Song" (1996, possibly Green Day's source for the mixing device); Iggy Pop's "The Passenger" (alternates between the two throughout). — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use the "She's Electric" chord progression]], [[Songs that use the Harmonic Minor Axis progression]]
- **Ambiguous tonic loops stay listenable on repeat precisely because it's unclear which chord is "home" — the same mechanism this page names in *Why the rotations feel different*, cited independently in two real pop records.** Jens Larsen, working through his own non-functional jazz vamps, draws a direct parallel to **Coldplay's** four-chord songs and to **Daft Punk's "Get Lucky"** (Dorian-tinged, arguably tonic on the first chord but not clearly so): both loop the same short progression through a whole song without it getting boring, and the stated reason is that the listener never settles on "we're just vamping to a I all the time." Generalized explicitly to "a lot of other pop music... for the last 10 years" leaning on short repeated progressions that need to stay a little vague to survive dozens of repetitions — the pop-songwriting mirror of this page's own point that *which chord reads as tonic* (not the chord content itself) is the variable actually driving the feel. → [[Loop-Based Harmony]], [[Modal Pop]], [[Modal Jazz]] § *Modal islands* — Jens Larsen, [[Jens Larsen - Modern Jazz Harmony - Chord Progressions and Analysis]]
