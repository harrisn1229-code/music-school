# Diatonic Tenths Over a Drone with Chromatic Passing Shapes

A **compositional device**, not a drill — how to generate an original fingerstyle interlude/intro by walking a diatonic interval shape against a ringing open-string drone, then spicing it with chromaticism once the diatonic version already sounds good. Sits next to [[Fingerstyle Thumb Independence]] (the picking-hand mechanics that execute this) and [[Borrowed Chords]] (the theory behind the chromatic half) but is really its own thing: a repeatable **write-a-part-from-scratch** recipe, worked start to finish on an original song rather than taught as an exercise. Jared Borowski demonstrates it live, composing a real intro/chorus part for a song he's working on, and narrates the process as he goes — the point of the video is explicitly the *thought process*, not the finished lick.

## Step 1 — Pick a diatonic interval and an open-string drone, walk the interval through the key
Choose a **compound interval shape** (a 10th — a 3rd plus an octave, i.e. a plain diatonic 3rd stretched across strings so it rings openly rather than clashing) and an **open string tuned to a scale degree of the key** as a drone. Worked in **G major**: open G string as the drone/tonic, and a **10th interval** fretted above it (root + major/minor 3rd an octave up) as the moving voice. Walk that 10th shape **diatonically up the scale, one scale degree at a time**, playing the **open drone string in between each shape** rather than continuously — so the sequence is *shape → drone → shape → drone…*, not a sustained double-stop. Concretely in G: 10th off the 1 (major 3rd), off the 2, off the 3 (using the diatonic 3rd quality at each degree — major off 1/4/5, minor off 2/3/6/7 in a major key), continuing up the neck as far as it stays practical, then the same device works walking back down.

**Why this shape specifically:** a stacked 3rd (a 6th or a plain octave-doubled 3rd) clashes against an open drone a lot more than a **10th** does — putting the octave between the two notes gives enough separation that the drone rings clean under a moving diatonic voice, which is what makes the "drone + moving upper voice" texture work at all. Confirmed as a real classical-guitar idiom, not just a folk/pop trick: he cites the opening of **Francisco Tárrega's "Lágrima"** as the same device — 10ths walked over a (different) drone note — arrived at independently before he made the connection to it.

## Step 2 — Recognize it, don't force it: the "homework" habit
His stated songwriting habit: when something you're playing "sounds lovely," **do homework afterward to find out where it's coming from** — trace the interval/voicing back to a reference (he heard an echo of the Beatles' **"Blackbird"** intro, which is the same idea — thirds walked through a key over an open-string drone — but with an octave *less* separation, i.e. plain 3rds/10ths vs. "Blackbird"'s 3rds). The point isn't crediting a source, it's that **recognizing the underlying device lets you deliberately extend it** — transpose it to a new key (worked live: moving the whole 10ths-over-drone idea from G into A), try it with a different drone note (the D string — the 5th of G — works as an alternate drone when the changes are hard to reach cleanly, "because D is the fifth note of the scale of G"), or reuse the device elsewhere in the same song (a related bridge idea).

## Step 3 — Fingerstyle execution: thumb holds the drone, index plays the moving voice
Once the diatonic shape sounds right, the physical pattern is genuinely simple: **thumb (p) plucks the open drone string(s)** (in the finished part, *two* open strings together — open B and open G) and **index (i) plays the fretted interval shape** above. Counted as a repeating **1-2-3-4-1-2** figure. This is the same thumb/finger voice-independence [[Fingerstyle Thumb Independence]] documents (thumb holds a steady part, fingers move independently above it) applied here to a drone-plus-interval texture instead of that page's root-note-bassline texture — same underlying skill, different musical use.

## Step 4 — Add chromaticism as a passing SHAPE, not a passing note
The move that turns the plain diatonic walk into "his" sound: once you're moving between **two diatonic target shapes** (e.g. the 10th off scale-degree 5 down to the 10th off scale-degree 4), **insert a chromatic passing shape a half-step away from the shape you're heading toward, right before you land on it** — i.e. treat the *whole interval shape* as a unit and approach it chromatically the same way you'd approach a single note by a half step. Stated plainly: "we know that we're going down to this shape here — well, you can just add half steps between them."
- **It's fine for it to sound momentarily wrong.** "Crunchy for a second, but very cool, 'cause then it lands on something so inside after that" — the passing shape's dissonance is the point; judge it by whether the *landing* on the diatonic target afterward feels settled, not by whether the passing shape itself sounds "correct."
- **You don't need the theory to use this.** Try it by ear first, anywhere two shapes are already adjacent in a part you're writing. Naming it after the fact is optional — he offers "tritone substitution" or "approaching [the target] by a half step above with a major chord" as possible labels, but explicitly says the label doesn't matter for using the move, only for talking about it afterward.

## Step 5 — The deliberate version: borrow the ♭6 from the parallel minor
For a more targeted way to choose *which* chromatic note to reach for (instead of only trial-and-error by ear), borrow a note from the **parallel minor** — [[Borrowed Chords]]'s modal-interchange mechanism, stated here from a composer's-workflow angle rather than an analyst's: **"if you're in a major key and you want to borrow one of these minor notes... the most common note to use, if you're going to add one, is the flat six"** — i.e. of all the notes the parallel minor offers to borrow, ♭6 is the single most common one-note reach for adding "a little something" of chromatic color to an otherwise-major piece. The payoff that makes ♭6 specifically useful: **the chord built on the borrowed ♭6 is itself major** (walking the parallel-minor triad ladder: i minor, ii diminished, ♭III major, iv minor, v/V, **♭VI major**, ♭VII major) — so borrowing it hands you a bright, stable-sounding major chord that's still harmonically "outside," rather than a muddy dissonance. **This is a compositional discovery story, not a theory-first derivation**: he found both of the ♭6 moves in his actual piece by ear/feel first ("I wasn't thinking I'm going to add the flat six, I just added the chromaticism that felt good to me") and only recognized afterward that both spots were the same borrowed-♭6 device — direct evidence for his Step 2 habit (write intuitively, analyze/name the device after the fact, then you can reuse it on purpose next time).

## Arrangement note: concentrate the chromaticism in one section
In the finished song, this drone-tenths-plus-♭6-borrowing part is the **chorus/interlude**, deliberately the most harmonically dense section — the **verses are kept plain by contrast**: just a fingerpicked **G → Cmaj7** alternation, nothing else. Worth remembering as a general move: put chromatic/harmonic complexity in one formal section and let a simpler section around it set it off, rather than spreading chromaticism evenly through a song.
