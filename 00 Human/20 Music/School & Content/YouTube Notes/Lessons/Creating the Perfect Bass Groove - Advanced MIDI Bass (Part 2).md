# Creating the Perfect Bass Groove - Advanced MIDI Bass (Part 2)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xGec4a1hcRg)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xGec4a1hcRg)

## Synopsis

Part 2 of the [[How to Write Advanced MIDI Basslines (Part 1) - Essential Knowledge|3-part series]] (→ [[Techniques for Complex Funky Lines - Advanced MIDI Bass (Part 3)]]), same Tone/Note Choice/Groove framework applied to a much more rhythm-driven example over a simple Am–F–G progression. Where Part 1 was about connecting chords smoothly, Part 2 is almost entirely about how the bass interlocks with the kick and snare — note choice stays deliberately simple (mostly octave-jumping) so all the interest can live in the groove and the note-off timing. Two ideas carry the video: splitting a bassline into a "low melody" and a "high melody" that move independently, and treating the ends of notes as being as important as their onsets.

## Techniques

- **Design the bass tone for its rhythmic role, not just for how it sounds solo.** A "fatter"/cooler-sounding preset can actively hurt a percussive groove-bass part if its attack/envelope isn't tight enough — demoed by swapping in a preset-library sound that sounds great alone but "isn't tight enough" once dropped into this groove. Decide *what job the bass is doing* (melodic vs. rhythmic/percussive) before picking the patch. → [[Bass Design]]
- **A punchy, strong-attack bass tone functions like its own percussion instrument**, working with (not just under) the kick and snare — deliberately chosen here because the bassline's whole purpose in this example is rhythm-creation over harmonic interest.
- **Split a bassline into a "low melody" and a "high melody" that move independently, then let them fuse into one perceived line.** Low notes track the chord roots plainly; high notes form their own sustained, slower-moving countermelody that "hangs" on a note (e.g., held on the 5th) while the low notes keep moving underneath, then resolves — described as a kind of counterpoint against the low voice even though both layers are one MIDI part.
- **Enclosure applied to the upper "melody" layer**, same device as Part 1 but used to connect the sustained upper notes between chord changes (e.g., F→A heading toward G, G→B heading toward A).
- **Pentatonic-scale runs that skip the target note on the way back down** — walk up toward a target note, then descend using the same notes but deliberately skip past the note the "melody" is about to land on, so the target note arrives with more impact when it's finally hit (a variant of the enclosure/held-note idea).
- **Runs don't have to default to octaves** — a run built on the 9th and 3rd of the current chord instead of a plain octave jump is offered as an equally valid, "cool" alternative; the choice is a groove/color decision, not a rule.
- **Offset bass notes by roughly a 16th note from the drum hits, rather than always landing exactly on the kick/snare.** With a percussive bass tone, hitting *only* in sync with the drums hides the bass's own percussive character — deliberately placing some bass notes just after a kick (instead of on it) creates syncopation between bass and drums and lets the bass read as its own rhythmic voice. → [[Syncopation and Metric Displacement]]
- **Note-off timing is as important as note-on timing, especially on a sustained bass sound with a hard cutoff.** Cut a sustained bass note off exactly where the snare hits, so the kick effectively "turns the note on" and the snare "turns it off" — a deliberate interlock between the bass envelope and the drum pattern, not an arbitrary sustain length. → [[Bass Design]]
- **Establish one strong rhythmic figure and repeat it rather than constantly varying** — if a groove is strong enough, restating it is more effective than abandoning it for novelty; reserve a small variation (e.g., swapping a 3-note figure for a 4-note one) for the very last repeat as a "final statement" rather than varying every pass.
- **A/B comparison proves the syncopation/note-off techniques matter**: the same line with notes tightly glued to the drum grid and no deliberate note-off placement "still sounds cool" but is explicitly demonstrated as weaker/less interesting than the syncopated, interlocked version — a concrete before/after for why the groove techniques above are worth the extra effort.
