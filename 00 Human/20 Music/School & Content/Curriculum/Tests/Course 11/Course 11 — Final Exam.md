> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **Finish the groove then write around it · land the roots by ear before any decoration**
> The trap in the first is following the kick map instead of the pocket. The trap in the second is treating an inability to hear the roots as a writing problem when it's an ear-training gap.
>
> **Q2.** **Its register — the same line an octave too low goes from fine to undefined mud**
> Move it up rather than processing it. Register is a writing decision, and EQ on a line that's simply sitting too low treats the symptom.
>
> **Q3.** **Octave up and back, occasionally through a passing tone — fills steal the melody's rhythm**
> The 5th above is the classic passing tone. Copying the rhythm rather than the notes is what keeps the added busyness cohesive instead of arbitrary.
>
> **Q4.** **Play a too-busy line through the whole loop, keep only the killer moments**
> Never repeat the same fill in consecutive phrases. Deleting down from too much beats deciding what to add to too little.
>
> **Q5.** **Imagine a bassist physically playing it — the same four-limb constraint as drum programming**
> Applied to one player instead of a kit. Rewrite anything no player would phrase that way, unless the unplayable line is the point.
>
> **Q6.** **Count the distinct melodic lines in the soloed low end — two of them is the usual cause**
> *Rubric (5):* says to solo the low end and count how many distinct melodic lines are running (2) · says two different low lines read as mud rather than as richness, where doubling one line is fine (2) · says muddy low ends are usually an arrangement problem rather than an EQ problem (1)
> The reason this is the more likely cause is that the ear stops separating simultaneous parts as they get lower. Two independent lines in the mid range are heard as two voices; the same two an octave below middle C are heard as one rumble that keeps changing pitch for no reason a listener can follow. No filter recovers that, because the information the ear needs to separate them was never available. So the first move is to count: solo the low end and see whether there is a bass and an 808 both playing something of their own, or a synth bass and a sub with different notes. If there are two, the fix is to delete one or make them the same line, and the EQ decision you were about to make disappears.
>
> **Q7.** **Busyness is decided by what else is moving — and two chords means the riffs are the line**
> Showcase lines work over static held chords. Static harmony moves all the interest into the fills between roots, where one perfectly placed fill can be the whole event.
>
> **Q8.** **Roots — it supports rather than competes**
> The harmony is already supplying the movement and the vocal is already the thing being followed.
>
> **Q9.** **What story is the bass telling · what the keys are telling · do they talk to each other**
> Keep the harmony dead simple — two chords is enough — and let the arrangement carry it as a call and response rather than as stacked parts.
>
> **Q10.** **A bass patch playing its own upper voice a 3rd or 5th above the root, in contrary motion**
> That's genuine two-voice writing rather than a melodic line with a thicker patch, because the two voices move against each other.
>
> **Q11.** **A 16th grid with notes moved off the kick's strong beats · a chromatic walk between phrases**
> The walk steps through out-of-scale passing tones toward the next section's starting note, which is why it works whatever scale the line is in.
>
> **Q12.** **New rhythm, register and contour each section — every line returns to the same root as home**
> That shared root is what keeps four genuinely different basslines reading as one song.
>
> **Q13.** **Analyse both, pick one to own the sub, high-pass the other until the clash stops**
> If high-passing the kick guts it, swap in a kick with more inherent high-end character and cut that one instead. Where the kick is otherwise interchangeable, the source with more low-end content keeps the sub.
>
> **Q14.** **You get two thin sources and the same collision — sidechaining is the version made in time**
> *Rubric (5):* says cutting both weakens each without resolving the overlap (2) · says ownership means one source keeps its fundamental intact and the other gives it up (2) · names sidechaining, or automating kick length by section, as the same decision made in time rather than frequency (1)
> Two sources peaking at the same frequency still peak at the same frequency after you've taken a few decibels out of each — the overlap is unchanged and now neither has the weight it needed. The decision the collision is asking for is which one holds the low end, and the answer has to be one of them. What you cut will sound diminished in solo and won't sound diminished in the mix, because the range it gave up was never audible as its own thing there. The alternative framing is to separate them in time instead: sidechain the bass to the kick so the two never occupy that region on the same instant, or automate the kick's length by section so it gets out of the way where the bass is sustaining. Same decision, different axis.
>
> **Q15.** **Pitch it up an octave or more, tune it there, drop it back — sub pitch is inaudible as pitch**
> The ear genuinely can't do the job at 40 Hz on most systems, so tuning in place is guessing. Same principle as tuning the kick: a low end on the wrong note fights the song.
>
> **Q16.** **Pitch it with warp off · set warp mode to Tones · draw the slides in the transposition envelope**
> Warp off gives a clean stretch when setting the pitch, Tones is the mode built for bass, and the clip envelope is easier than MIDI slides.
>
> **Q17.** **Synth patch · different synth · real or sampled bass · DI plus amp sim — then limit, saturate, sidechain**
> A limiter rather than a compressor, because bass wants a ceiling with its dynamics intact rather than gain-riding that pumps.
>
> **Q18.** **The sound ladder for tone, and swapping the kick rather than gutting it in the sub decision**
> *Rubric (5):* names the sound ladder — changing the source before processing a patch that was never going to get there (2) · names swapping the kick for one with more high-end character when high-passing guts it, rather than cutting harder (2) · states the shared principle that the fix is upstream, and processing only refines a source that was already close (1)
> Both are the same instruction arriving in different clothes. On the tone side, a bass patch that isn't the sound you want does not become that sound through a chain, so the escalation is to change the instrument — a different preset, a different synth, a played or sampled bass, a real signal through an amp — one rung at a time until the source is right. On the low-end side, a kick that falls apart when you high-pass it is telling you it had nothing above the sub to begin with, and the answer is a different kick rather than a more careful filter. The principle underneath both is that a chain refines and does not transform. It's also the drum course's opening claim, which is why it appears in three separate places in the school: the most expensive mistake in production is spending an hour improving something that was the wrong choice five minutes in.
