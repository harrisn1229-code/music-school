# Synthet - Harmonies, Easy!

## Synopsis

A vocal-harmony starter lesson: thirds are the default interval for a harmony line (with sixths as the same relationship an octave apart), built by ear against a C major scale ("three white keys up"). The video's real value is the four-way taxonomy of how two melody lines can move relative to each other — parallel, similar, oblique, and contrary motion — demonstrated over the same melody so the difference is audible, plus a practical caution that parallel-thirds harmonies can clash with the underlying chord and need nudging to a 4th/5th at those points.

## What's taught

- **Thirds are the default harmony interval** — in a major key, stack a harmony voice a third above the melody (three scale steps, i.e. three white keys up in C major); sounds "rich" because thirds are consonant and easy to find by ear (same interval used in the "Happy Birthday" major-3rd vs. minor-3rd test). Sixths are the same relationship inverted (down an octave) and work just as well.
- **The four motion types**: **parallel motion** — both voices move the same direction by the same interval (the default "harmony a third above" approach; usually works, but not always — can go dissonant against the underlying chord at some points, in which case swap that spot to a 4th or 5th); **similar motion** — both voices move the same direction but by different intervals; **oblique motion** — one voice holds a note while the other moves a melody around it; **contrary motion** — the two voices move in opposite directions, and can still blend nicely despite diverging.
- **Oblique motion doubles as a call-and-response-adjacent device** — one singer sustaining a note under a moving lyric ("Tacos, burritos, mangoes...") while another voices a response, works instrumentally too, not just vocally.
- **Practical delivery tip:** wide-mouthed vowels are harder to harmonize cleanly — aim to not over-open the mouth ("la la la," not a wide "ah") for tighter blend.
