# Time Signatures & Note Values

The fundamentals of how rhythm is organized in a piece of music — beats, bars, time signatures, and note-value subdivision. Foundations-tier, sibling to [[Notes & Pitches]]/[[Scales]] but for the time axis instead of the pitch axis. The production/feel half of rhythm (swing, humanization, groove) lives on [[Rhythm and Groove]] and [[Pocket and Microtiming]] — this page is the notation/theory half they point back to. The devices that play *against* the meter — syncopation, hemiola, polyrhythm — are on [[Syncopation and Metric Displacement]].

## Beats and bars
- **A rhythm has no inherent meaning on its own — only in relation to the beat.** The same identical rhythm (e.g. a solo hi-hat pattern) reads as three different things depending on where an accompanying kick/snare places the implied beat underneath it — the beat is the reference point every other rhythm is measured against, and a rhythm heard in isolation is genuinely ambiguous until that reference is supplied. → [[Rhythm and Groove]] — Max Konyi, [Essentials of Rhythm - What Gives Rhythms Their Meaning?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n4Z88ujGkR0)
- **The beat doesn't need a hit on every pulse to be felt.** A sparse kick+snare pattern with real gaps still lets a listener track the beat accurately through the silences — demonstrated further by a pattern that drops into **halftime** (the snare hits half as often) partway through and still stays trackable. The beat is inferred from surrounding hit placement, not just literal hits. → Max Konyi, [Essentials of Rhythm - What Gives Rhythms Their Meaning?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n4Z88ujGkR0)
- **A beat is a regular pulse** measuring the passage of time in music — like a clock's tick, but musical.
- **A bar (or measure) is a container for a musical idea** — a grouping of beats that gives a bassline, chord progression, or melody a defined space to live in. **A bar = one complete pass through the active meter's pattern** — one "1-2" is a bar of duple meter, one "1-2-3-4" a bar of quadruple; every new bar begins exactly on the meter's strong "1." → Max Konyi, [Essentials of Rhythm - Organizing Beats and Bars (Meter)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z6dbDEs4clI)
- **The downbeat is the first (and strongest) beat of a bar** — in a click track it reads as the accented/higher-pitched click; everything else in the bar is metrically weaker by comparison.
- **Feel the pulse before programming it.** Simply counting out loud at a steady pace ("1 2 3 4, 1 2 3 4…") and grouping every four counts into a cycle is enough to internalize what a bar *is* before touching a piano roll. → You Suck at Producing, [Creating Drum Beats Pt I](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EILEUJCbduU)

## Tempo
- **Tempo (BPM) = how many beats fit in one minute** — governs how fast/slow a song feels while every rhythmic relationship (which notes fall where relative to the beat) stays identical regardless of tempo.
- **Tempo is usually constant through a song in electronic/rock/pop, but not always** — classical music routinely uses fluid tempo (rubato: speeding up, slowing down, gaps) as an expressive device; deliberate tempo-change sections are possible in any genre via a DAW's tempo track/automation.
- **Rough genre tempo ranges as starting points:** hip hop ≈ 60–110 BPM · house ≈ 120–130 BPM · dubstep ≈ 130–145 BPM · drum & bass ≈ 150, sometimes up to 190 BPM. Useful as a sensible default when trying to approximate a target genre's feel. → [[Genres Index]] — Max Konyi, [Essentials of Rhythm - What Gives Rhythms Their Meaning?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n4Z88ujGkR0)
- **Pulse / Tempo / Meter as a "beat / rate / ratio" triad** — pulse is the felt heartbeat, tempo is how fast that pulse goes, meter is the ratio describing how pulses group and subdivide. Same three concepts as above, just framed as one system rather than three separate topics.
- **Tempo is indicated three ways in notation**: a metronome marking (MM=120, or "bpm"); an Italian descriptive term (Andante ≈ 76 bpm, "a walking tempo") that marks a *range* rather than an exact number; and diminutives/modifiers that refine a term (Andantino = slightly faster than Andante; *Andante con moto* = "going, with motion"). Italian tempo terms often carry style/character as much as speed — *Allegro con brio* ("lively, with fire") is a manner-of-performance instruction, not just a speed one.
- **Time signatures are never written as fractions** — two stacked numbers, no fraction bar between them. Easy to typo when setting notation from a word processor or DAW export.
- **Arsis/Thesis** — the classical-terminology ancestors of "upbeat"/"downbeat" (from Hellenistic poetic meter): arsis = preparatory/weak, thesis = accented/strong. Worth knowing as jargon, but their meanings have actually flipped at different points in music history, so don't treat them as load-bearing vocabulary. — [[The Elements of Rhythm - Sound, Symbol, and Time - Enrichment|The Elements of Rhythm]], p.20–21

## Reading a time signature
A time signature is two stacked numbers, each with its own job:
- **Top number = beats per bar.** Change it and the bar gets longer or shorter (4 = four beats per bar, 5 = five, up to 99 for odd/prog-rock time feels).
- **Bottom number = which note value receives one beat** — 4 = quarter note, 8 = eighth note, 2 = half note, 16 = sixteenth note. Changing only the bottom number (e.g. 4/4 → 4/8) doesn't change the beat *count*, it changes how much musical "real estate" each beat covers — 4/8 at the same tempo plays back twice as fast because two eighth notes now fit in the space one quarter note used to occupy.
- **Note-value subdivision, by ear:** quarter notes = "1 2 3 4"; eighth notes = "1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and" (twice as many events, twice the density); sixteenth notes = "1 e and a 2 e and a…" (four per beat). In Ableton, right-click the piano roll grid to change the fixed grid between quarter/eighth/16th to draw at the resolution you're counting in.
- **Note length and note placement are independent decisions.** A note's *value* (how long it lasts — quarter, eighth, etc.) doesn't dictate *where* it starts; a quarter-note-length note played starting halfway between two beats is still a quarter note, just placed offbeat. Don't conflate "what value is this note" with "is it on or off the beat." → Max Konyi, [Essentials of Rhythm - Basic Subdivisions (Note Length)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZABmKExO774)
- **Match the DAW grid/quantize resolution to the note value the part is actually using**, rather than defaulting to the finest grid available — if a part only ever uses quarter notes, quantizing to a quarter-note grid gives a more accurate result than a 16th-note grid that doesn't match the part's real rhythmic content. → Max Konyi, [Essentials of Rhythm - Basic Subdivisions (Note Length)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZABmKExO774)

## Common Time and Cut Time are Renaissance holdovers
- **𝄴 ("Common Time" = 4/4) and 𝄵 ("Cut Time" = 2/2) are slang names for two specialized symbols carried over from Renaissance mensural notation**, not modern shorthand invented for convenience — they predate the two-stacked-numbers time-signature format itself. Cut Time's other names are "March Time" and its formal name, *Alla Breve*. — [[The Elements of Rhythm - Sound, Symbol, and Time - Enrichment|The Elements of Rhythm]], p.23

## The Time Signature Table — a method for graphing any meter
A three-row table that unifies simple, compound, and asymmetrical meter into one mechanical procedure instead of three separate rules to memorize:

| Row | What it holds |
|---|---|
| **Pulse** | The felt background beat |
| **First Division** | Does the pulse split into 2 (simple) or 3 (compound)? |
| **Subdivisions** | Further splits of First Division, one layer at a time |

- **For simple meter**, every row just divides by 2, and the written time signature *is* the Pulse row — 2/4 fills Pulse with two quarter notes, First Division with four eighths, Subdivisions with eight 16ths.
- **For compound meter, the written time signature is actually the First Division row, not the Pulse row** — the real trick of the whole method. To find the Pulse: list the written signature's beats grouped in **threes** in the First Division row, then **sum each group of three into one dotted value** and put that in the Pulse row. Worked example for 6/8: six eighth notes → group as two sets of three in First Division → each group of three eighths sums to one dotted quarter → Pulse row holds two dotted quarter notes, which is *why* 6/8 is Compound **Duple** (two pulses) and not "compound sextuple" (it only looks like six because that's the First Division count, not the pulse count).
- **For asymmetrical (odd) meter, the same procedure applies** — see below.
- This reframes "why is a compound pulse a dotted note" as a mechanical table-fill (durational values divide into three dotted parts as well as the addition-plus-half reading — see [[Notation]] §5) rather than a fact to just memorize. — [[The Elements of Rhythm - Sound, Symbol, and Time - Enrichment|The Elements of Rhythm]], p.23–26
- **Want to actually fill this table by hand instead of just reading about it?** → [[Rhythm Notation & Meter-Reading Practice]] — a written/physical drill set covering the table method plus durational-value drawing, pulse-vs-division tapping, conducting patterns, and meter identification from an unmarked rhythm.

**Perception is tempo-dependent, not fixed by the printed signature.** Any compound meter can be perceived as simple at a slow-enough tempo, and — for a specific limited list of simple-triple signatures (3/16, 3/8, 3/4) — the reverse also happens: at a fast tempo, the ear stops tracking the written bottom-number value as the pulse and instead hears it as the First Division of an implied compound meter one level up. — [[The Elements of Rhythm - Sound, Symbol, and Time - Enrichment|The Elements of Rhythm]], p.26–27

## Tuplets (artificial division)
Formally "artificial division" — inserting the *other* meter family's division pattern into a beat that doesn't naturally have it:
- **Triplet** — a compound (3-part) division inserted into a simple-meter beat. The common case most producers already know.
- **Duplet** — the mirror image: a simple (2-part) division inserted into a compound-meter beat (dividing a compound pulse's natural 3 down to 2).
- **Super-triplet / larger tuplets** — the same device spanning *multiple* beats rather than one (a triplet drawn across two beats, or larger irregular groupings like 5-in-the-space-of-4). Artificial division becomes especially common from the 19th century onward as a way to loosen otherwise-rigid beat division — worth reaching for in a DAW the same way: triplet/duplet grid toggles exist precisely to place these against a straight background grid. — [[The Elements of Rhythm - Sound, Symbol, and Time - Enrichment|The Elements of Rhythm]], p.46–47
  - **Real-song worked example of a super-triplet: the "Seven Nation Army" bass/guitar riff (The White Stripes).** BassBuzz's construction method: take the two beats the triplet spans, divide *each* beat into three even parts (six slots total, counted "3 and a, 4 and a"), then play only the 1st, 3rd, and 5th of those six slots — three evenly-spaced hits across the two-beat span, with a rest on slots 2, 4, and 6. Full first-bar count: "One, Two and Three and A, Four and A" — the first two beats divide in twos (straight eighths), the second two divide in threes (the super-triplet), the same "two different subdivisions inside one bar" shape this section describes generally. Beginners typically fake the rhythm by ear and land the notes a little early or late rather than actually subdividing it; slow-tempo practice is the fix. Framed as a general songwriting tool too — dropping a triplet into an otherwise straight-eighths line makes it read as "quirkier" and "catchier" than a line that only ever divides the beat in twos. → [[Bassline Writing]] — BassBuzz, [[5 Popular Beginner Bass Lines (Are You Playing Them Wrong?)]]

## Conducting patterns
A physical model of where the strong beat falls in a bar, worth knowing even outside a conducting context — each pattern's gesture literally traces the strong-to-weak beat path: **Two-pattern** (simple/compound duple) — "away–up." **Three-pattern** (simple/compound triple) — "down–away–up." **Four-pattern** (simple/compound quadruple) — "down–across–away–up." **One-pattern** — fast triple meters conducted as a single gesture per bar. **Six-pattern** — compound duple at a tempo too slow to comfortably conduct in two. — [[The Elements of Rhythm - Sound, Symbol, and Time - Enrichment|The Elements of Rhythm]], p.33–37

## Ear-training: identifying a time signature by listening
1. **Find the bar length first.** Play the track and pause right before the main musical idea feels like it's about to repeat — that's the length of one bar (or the smallest repeatable increment, if the idea spans multiple bars of the same content, e.g. a two-chord riff really only needs counting over half its span).
2. **Count beats inside that span** to get the top number.
3. **Determine the bottom number by how you want to nod your head.** If you want to nod on every single counted beat, the beat unit is probably a quarter note (bottom number 4). If you only want to nod on every *other* counted beat (skipping every second one), the beat unit is probably an eighth note (bottom number 8).
→ You Suck at Producing, [Understanding Beats, Bars, & Time Signatures](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=R_ZLIachQnU)

## Strong/weak beat hierarchy — and why it matters for production
Not all rhythmic positions carry equal weight, and matching velocity to that hierarchy is most of what makes a programmed pattern read as "played" rather than "gridded":
- **Meter is what CREATES the strong/weak hierarchy in the first place** — an undifferentiated stream of identical beats has no structure until it's grouped: **duple** (groups of 2, "ONE-two") gives strong-weak; **triple** (groups of 3, "ONE-two-three") gives strong-weak-weak, uncommon in electronic music, somewhat common in rock; **quadruple** (groups of 4, "ONE-two-THREE-four" — effectively two duples stacked) is by far the dominant organization in modern music (~90% of it), with beat 1 strongest, beat 3 next-strongest, beats 2 and 4 weak. → Max Konyi, [Essentials of Rhythm - Organizing Beats and Bars (Meter)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z6dbDEs4clI)
- **The standard kick/snare backbeat falls directly out of quadruple meter's strong/weak hierarchy**: kick on the strong beats (1 and 3, low/"boom" sound), snare on the weak beats (2 and 4, higher/upper-accent sound) — not an arbitrary convention, a direct application of which beats are metrically strong. → [[Rhythm and Groove]] — Max Konyi, [Essentials of Rhythm - Organizing Beats and Bars (Meter)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z6dbDEs4clI)
- **A drum pattern needs internal variation across its full bar length or it collapses back to feeling like a shorter meter** — a 4-beat pattern where beats 1–2 sound identical to beats 3–4 risks reading as a repeated 2-beat (duple) idea instead of a genuine 4-beat one; a single differentiating hit (e.g. one extra 16th-note hit at the very end of the bar) is enough to make the ear register the full cycle. → Max Konyi, [Essentials of Rhythm - Organizing Beats and Bars (Meter)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z6dbDEs4clI)
- **"A whole note = one bar" is a quadruple-meter-specific shortcut, not a universal rule.** A whole note is always 4 *beats* — in quadruple meter that happens to equal one full bar, but in triple meter a bar is only 3 beats long, so a 4-beat whole note can't fit inside one triple-meter bar at all (the note that fills a triple-meter bar is a dotted half note instead). Keep "beats" and "bar" as separate units when reasoning about note values outside 4/4. → Max Konyi, [Essentials of Rhythm - Organizing Beats and Bars (Meter)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z6dbDEs4clI)
- **Strongest: on the beat** (the numbered positions — 1, 2, 3, 4).
- **Weaker: the "and"** (the off-beat eighth-note midpoint between beats).
- **Weakest: the "e" and "a"** (the sixteenth-note subdivisions flanking the "and").
- A hit landing in a weak spot should generally carry lower velocity than a hit landing on the beat — this single rule does a lot of the "lifelike" work before reaching for any humanize plugin. → [[Rhythm and Groove]] (the full humanization treatment) — You Suck at Producing, [Writing Your First Drum Beats in Ableton](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MPmx6PAgrFM)

## Identifying a changing time signature inside an existing track
- **Solo the instrument that most clearly states the musical idea** (often the bass), find where one phrase starts and ends, and loop-bracket exactly that span (Ableton: Cmd/Ctrl+L) — then count beats against the metronome *inside that loop* to get the real bar length, rather than guessing from the full mix.
- **Insert the time-signature change at the exact point it starts** (right-click → new time signature) and re-verify by continuing to count through the following bars — odd-metered tracks often change signature more than once, so repeat the solo-loop-count process per section rather than assuming one signature holds for the whole piece. → You Suck at Producing, [How to use Polyrhythms & Changing Time Signatures in EDM](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=62kaefeplEI)

## Polyrhythms
A polyrhythm is **two (or more) different beat-groupings occupying the same musical space at once** — e.g. one rhythmic layer dividing a bar into 6 while another simultaneously divides the same bar into 8. Because the groupings don't line up evenly, there's no single sub-pulse that satisfies both; the practical result (see [[Rhythm and Groove]] for how this is played/produced) is alternating which grouping gets emphasis across the bar rather than committing to one subdivision throughout. → You Suck at Producing, [How to use Polyrhythms & Changing Time Signatures in EDM](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=62kaefeplEI)

**Naming convention**: "[layered pulse]-over-[prevailing pulse]" — the SECOND number is always the main time-signature pulse, so 4-over-3 and 3-over-4 are genuinely different polyrhythms even though they use the same two numbers. **3-over-2 ("vertical hemiola")** is the most common/easiest to play: any quarter-note triplet layered over a straight 4/4 pulse qualifies — "Daydreaming" (Radiohead), "Light My Fire" (The Doors, organ solo), "Gloria" (Them, guitar solo), "Is This Love" (Bob Marley). **4-over-3**: "Fake Empire" (The National, 3/4 — piano right hand plays 4 evenly-spaced notes against the left hand's 3). **3-over-4**: "Chariots of Fire" (Vangelis — piano's 3 evenly-spaced notes against the synth's prevailing 4). **8-over-6**: "Lateralus" (TOOL — vocals in an 8-beat pulse over drums in 6/4). **Foundational vs. occasional use**: a polyrhythm used as the whole song's rhythmic bedrock is sometimes called a **cross-rhythm** (Fake Empire, Daydreaming); it can instead appear only briefly as a deliberate disorienting effect. **Keeping a brief polyrhythm disorienting rather than groove-absorbed**: Jacob Collier's "Hajanga" drops a 3-over-4 drum polyrhythm into the verse for only 2 bars at a time before it disappears — too short for the ear to normalize it into the groove, so it reads as equally jarring every time it returns. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use Polyrhythms & Polymeters]]

## Polymeters
A polymeter is **two (or more) genuinely different time signatures played at the same tempo/downbeat** — distinct from a polyrhythm, which stays inside ONE shared time signature. **Notation caveat**: even a true polymeter only gets one time signature written on the page (notating two simultaneously is impractical); the second part is forcibly transcribed inside the chosen signature — that's a transcription convenience, not evidence the part isn't "really" in a different meter. Worked examples: "Kashmir" (Led Zeppelin) — drums solid 4/4, string melody 3/4, same tempo and downbeat (a 4-against-3 polymeter, not to be confused with a 4-over-3 polyrhythm despite the same ratio — the mechanism is different). "Let Down" (Radiohead) — guitar in groups of 5/8 against drums/bass in a clear 4/4, drifting audibly out of phase almost immediately for a chaotic, cascading effect. "5/4" (Gorillaz) — guitar riff establishes 5/4, drums enter in 4/4, producing a 5-against-4 polymeter. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use Polyrhythms & Polymeters]]

## The half-time perception trick
A pattern at a genuinely fast BPM (150-160+, common in trap/dubstep/future bass/reggae) can be made to *feel* half that speed without changing the tempo: move the snare/clap from the standard backbeat (2 and 4) onto **beat 3 only**. The ear latches onto snare placement to judge groove speed, so a snare only on 3 reads as a backbeat at half the actual BPM — the classic "half-time" feel. → [[1 Drums Snare-Clap Track|Drums Snare-Clap Track]] — You Suck at Producing, [Writing Your First Drum Beats in Ableton](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MPmx6PAgrFM)

## 12/8 in depth — the shuffle time signature
12/8 is compound quadruple: 4 strong beats/bar, each dividing into 3 (12 eighth notes total) — equivalent to 4/4 with a triplet feel on every beat, and some songs get transcribed either way depending on preference. **The "shuffle" sound comes from grouping**, not from the raw eighth-note count: the 12 notes group into 4×3, giving a rolling/tumbling feel — "bury a friend" [Billie Eilish, "bury a friend"], "The Way You Make Me Feel" (Michael Jackson), and Led Zeppelin's "Dazed and Confused" (slower) all run on a 12/8 shuffle. **How dense the shuffle sounds depends on how many of the 12 potential 8th notes are actually played**: "Lost in Yesterday" (Tame Impala) hits every one of the 12 for a "full-tilt" density; Stevie Wonder's "Master Blaster" plays only some of them for a more laid-back feel. **The "Purdie shuffle"**: Bernard Purdie's signature half-time 12/8 shuffle groove (heard on Steely Dan's "Home at Last," among many others) was highly influential on 70s/80s rock drumming — John Bonham's shuffle on "Fool in the Rain" and Toto's Jeff Porcaro both drew on it directly. **At slow tempos, 12/8 loses its rolling feel and becomes interchangeable with 6/8**: "Oh! Darling" (Beatles) still swings side to side but loses forward momentum at its slower tempo; Lou Reed's "Perfect Day" gets transcribed by different people as either 6/8 or 12/8 with no real audible difference at that tempo. **12/8 as a natural home for 6-against-4 polyrhythm**: since 12 divides evenly by both 3 (giving the standard 4-beat shuffle grouping) and 4 (giving 3 groups of 4), a groove can mark both simultaneously — Tears for Fears' "Everybody Wants to Rule the World" runs a hi-hat pattern emphasizing 6 beats/bar against a bassline emphasizing 4, a genuine 6-over-4 polyrhythm that fits 12/8's meter naturally rather than fighting it (equivalently readable as a 3-over-2 polyrhythm doubled). — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use 12/8 time]]

## 5/4 in depth
5 quarter-note beats/bar, most commonly grouped 3+2 or 2+3 (both readings are used; which one a piece implies depends on where the accents fall). **"Take Five" (Dave Brubeck Quartet)** groups it 3+2 — effectively alternating bars of 3/4 and 2/4 — giving a "wonky waltz" feel (a 3/4 jazz waltz with every other bar missing a beat); playing the same material straight in 3/4 removes the uneven quality entirely. **The Mission: Impossible theme** groups its 5 beats as 2 dotted quarters + 2 regular quarters (long-long-short-short), composer Lalo Schifrin having derived the exact rhythm from the Morse code for "MI" (dash-dot-dot-dot = "slow slow fast fast"); the same slow-slow-fast-fast shape recurs in Hozier's "From Eden." **Radiohead used 5/4 twice, in different ways**: "15 Step" builds its whole groove from a 2-bar motif (bar 1 = five plain quarters, bar 2 = mixed regular/dotted quarters) that anchors the meter throughout; "Morning Bell" (*Kid A*) was later re-recorded in 4/4 for *Amnesiac* — comparing the two versions directly shows longer notes shortened to fit the melody into 4/4 while each note keeps its relative beat position, a rare case of a band publishing the same "meter conversion" as two finished tracks. **5/4 vs. 10/8, genuinely interchangeable, chosen by feel**: Taylor Swift's "tolerate it" is officially transcribed in 5/4, but co-writer Aaron Dessner describes it as 10/8 — the two are mathematically identical (5 quarters = 10 eighths) and the choice comes down to which grouping makes the actual accent pattern feel intuitive rather than "against the grain" (10/8's finer subdivision fit this song's accents better). **5/4 polymeter examples** (see [[#Polymeters|Polymeters]] above for the general concept): Gorillaz' "5/4" (guitar in 5/4, drums forcibly in 4/4 — a true 5-against-4 polymeter); Muse's "Animals" (multiple polymetric layers — a crash cymbal marking every 2 beats against the band's 5/4 for a 5-against-2 feel, plus a later section where drums drop to a half-time 4/4 under the rest of the band's continuing 5/4 — reportedly inspired by Queens of the Stone Age's "Hanging Tree," which does the same 5-against-4 guitar/snare polymeter during its guitar solo). **5/4 in classical/other repertoire**: Holst's "Mars" (*The Planets*) — a driving 5/4 ostinato deliberately distorts an otherwise march-like (normally 4/4/2/4) rhythm into something "foreboding," matching the movement's war theme; Nick Drake's "Riverman"; Sting's "Seven Days" (snare on beat 4 splits the bar into a 3+2 feel). **The 5/4-vs-5/8 notation choice is a tempo/context decision, not a fixed rule**: 5/4, 5/8, and 5/16 are all "simple quintuple" and mathematically interchangeable (one bar of 5/4 = two bars of 5/8) — pick 5/8 when the felt beats are too fast to comfortably count as quarter notes (the composer's own "The Longest March," felt as "one" per bar despite being 5/8), or when a piece needs to alternate cleanly with a 4/4 section without an awkward tempo-halving instruction (Sufjan Stevens' "Come On! Feel the Illinoise!" — written in 5/8 specifically because it later swaps to 4/4). — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use 5-4 time]]

## 6/4 in depth — and why time-signature choice is sometimes genuinely subjective
6 evenly-spaced beats/bar. Often FEELS like an alternating 4/4-bar-then-2/4-bar rather than 6 equal beats — "Galvanize" (Chemical Brothers), "Enough Space" (Foo Fighters) both read this way, giving forward momentum ("propelled prematurely into the next bar"); could be notated as alternating 4/4+2/4 bars, but a single 6/4 signature is simpler to read. Worked examples: "Schism" (Tool), "Black Days" (Soundgarden), "Full Stop" (Radiohead), "Synchronicity" (The Police, fast tempo + 6/4 = a "frantic" feel), Dave Brubeck's "Pick Up Sticks"/"Everybody's Jumpin'" (*Time Out*). **Accent placement can shift within a fixed beat count**: Alice in Chains' "Down in a Hole" [named as "Dam That River" in this source] has a 12-beat guitar-riff phrase that reads as 2 bars of 6/4 while the riff repeats, then re-reads as 3 bars of 4/4 once the vocal enters — same 12 beats, different felt meter, purely from where the accents land. Courtney Barnett's "Crippling Self-Doubt and a General Lack of Confidence" contrasts a 6/4 verse against a 4/4 chorus specifically so the chorus's return to "familiar" 4/4 feels like a relief. **6/4 vs. two bars of 3/4 — technically the same beat count, chosen by ACCENT fit, not math**: any 6/4 song could be forcibly transcribed as two bars of 3/4, but if the song's natural strong-beat accents don't line up with where 3/4 would place them (every 3rd beat), 3/4 is a "counter-intuitive choice" that doesn't serve the performer, even though it's mathematically valid — "Schism"'s accents specifically don't fit a 3/4 reading. Some songs genuinely sit on the boundary and get published both ways: "Take Me to Church" (Hozier, verse) is heard by this analyst as 64bpm 6/4 but published as 63bpm 3/4 (same music, different chosen pulse); "Electric Feel" (MGMT) is described as either 3/4 or 6/4 depending on who's transcribing it. **Why this ambiguity is a MODERN problem, not a timeless one — the deepest point in this source**: classical composers hand-wrote the time signature onto their own manuscripts as a deliberate authorial choice (Chopin's Nocturne Op. 9 No. 1 could have been 3/4 but Chopin specifically chose 6/4 for its particular accent feel) — there is no debate about historical works because the composer's intent is literally on the page. Modern songwriters publish *recordings*, not manuscripts; the sheet-music transcription (when one exists at all) is usually made after the fact by a copyist choosing whichever signature seems to fit best, not by the songwriter — so for a recording-first song, "what time signature is this actually in" can be a genuinely unresolvable question rather than a fact waiting to be correctly identified. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use 6-4 time]]

## 7/4 in depth
7 quarter-note beats/bar, almost always grouped 4+3 or 3+4 (which softens the "oddness" compared to a truly even split — see the 2+2 = 5 counter-example below). **"Solsbury Hill" (Peter Gabriel)** — intro groups 3+4, then the pattern flips to 4+3 after the intro; briefly drops to a 4/4 bar at the end of the A section specifically because ending on the odd 7/4 pulse would leave the phrase feeling incomplete — an even bar supplies the "rhythmically complete" landing the 7/4 pulse can't. **A 7/4 shuffle needs a genuinely odd notation to be mathematically exact**: since a 4/4 shuffle gets written as 12/8 (each beat divides into 3 instead of 2), the equivalent move for a 7/4 shuffle would technically be 21/8 — Pink Floyd's "Money" IS a 74 shuffle (bassline divides each beat into 3), but 21/8 is avoided in practice ("would probably give most performers IBS") in favor of plain 7/4 with triplets marked explicitly, since legibility outranks mathematical purity. "Money" also isn't purely 7/4 throughout — one verse stretch is actually 4/4 + a one-off bar of 2/4 (14 beats total, which COULD fit a 7/4 reading, but the actual rhythmic accents rule that out), and the guitar solo switches to 12/8 entirely — a reminder to verify by accent, not just by counting beats that happen to sum correctly. **The same "which signature is most performer-friendly" ambiguity as 6/4 vs 3/4 recurs here**: "All You Need Is Love" (Beatles) gets transcribed at least three validated ways — 4/4 with occasional 3/4 bars (probably closest to how the Beatles themselves thought about it), straight 7/4, or alternating 12/8+9/8 (a more legible stand-in for the "true" 21/8 shuffle notation) — a case where multiple time-signature descriptions are all simultaneously valid for the identical recording. **Deliberately foregrounding 7/4's unevenness by contrast**: "Times Like These" (Foo Fighters) opens in plain 4/4 before the 7/4 riff enters, then keeps shifting back to 4/4 and returning to 7/4 throughout — repeatedly resetting the listener's expectation specifically to keep the 7/4 feeling uneven rather than normalized. **Breaking the 4+3/3+4 convention on purpose, tied to lyrical meaning**: Radiohead's "2 + 2 = 5" splits its 7/4 bar exactly down the middle (a genuine 7-against-2 rhythm) instead of the usual 4+3 grouping, producing a pulse that reads as simultaneously odd and even — a deliberate match for the song's Orwellian title (from *1984*) about a world where the arithmetic itself is wrong. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use 7-4 time]]

## Simple, compound & complex meter
- **Simple beat unit** divides in 2 (undotted note gets the beat); **compound beat unit** divides in 3 (dotted note gets the beat) — compound time signatures (6/8, 9/8, 12/8) are "lying": the top number is actually the *division* count, divide by 3 to get the real beat count, and the beat unit is always a dotted note.
- **Why 6/8 feels different from 3/4 despite having the same 6 eighth notes per bar: the grouping.** 6/8 groups its six eighth notes into **two groups of three** (felt "ONE-two-three-FOUR-five-six" → 2 felt beats/bar, each subdividing into 3 like a built-in triplet — the compound feel); 3/4 groups the identical six eighth notes into **three groups of two** (felt "ONE-and-TWO-and-THREE-and" → 3 felt beats/bar). Same note count, different accent grouping = the entire source of 6/8's rolling/lilting feel versus 3/4's more waltz-like three-count. When a piece doesn't feel locked into a clear pulse, explicitly test which grouping (4/4, 6/8, or 3/4) the material actually wants — the same raw material can genuinely support more than one valid reading until a grouping is committed to. → Max Konyi, [The Magic of 6/8 Time](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=F3tNVr0iBzw)
- **Complex meter** — simple and compound meter almost never combined *within one measure* in common practice (only as mixed meter, alternating whole measures — written with two stacked time signatures, e.g. 6/8 + 3/4). 20th-century primitivist composers (see [[20th-Century Harmony]]) broke this: complex meter has BOTH simple and compound beats in the same measure.
- **Odd-meter groupings** (5/8, 7/8, 9/8, 10/8, 11/8…) can each be split multiple valid ways: 5 = 2+3 or 3+2; 7 = 2+2+3, 2+3+2, or 3+2+2; the underlying eighth-note division stays the same length throughout even though the beats it groups into are uneven — matches this page's existing polyrhythm section from the production side. — Toby W. Rush, [[Music Theory for Musicians and Normal People - Enrichment|Music Theory for Musicians and Normal People]], p.5, 17
- **Asymmetrical meter graphs onto the [[#The Time Signature Table — a method for graphing any meter|Time Signature Table]] the same way compound meter does — the written signature is the First Division row, and Pulse is found by summing groups.** Groupings can also be built irregularly out of meters that aren't inherently odd: 8/8 as 3+3+2, 10/8 as 3+3+2+2, and so on — the same "sum the groups to find Pulse" logic applies regardless of whether the top number is odd. **Named real-world examples**: Tchaikovsky's Symphony No. 6 ("Pathétique"), 2nd movement, in 5 — one of the few pre-20th-century asymmetrical examples; Pink Floyd's "Money" in 7 (4+3); Peter Gabriel's "Solsbury Hill," mostly in 7 (4+3); Genesis's "Back in New York City," mostly in 7 (2+2+3); Bartók's *Concerto for Orchestra* IV (Intermezzo), alternating simple duple with 5; Stravinsky's *The Firebird* closing and *The Rite of Spring*; jazz big-band composer/arranger Hank Levy's odd-meter body of work. Asymmetrical meter was rare before the 20th century and became a hallmark of it, in both classical and vernacular genres. — [[The Elements of Rhythm - Sound, Symbol, and Time - Enrichment|The Elements of Rhythm]], p.47–49

## Learn each odd meter as its own feeling state, not as arithmetic
Applying the [[Scale Degree Ear Training|scale-degree feeling-state approach]] to rhythm: 7/8 (in a piece that otherwise reads as 4/4) has a distinct, recognizable "skipped eighth note / record-skip" feeling once you've internalized it — a drummer fluent in odd meters isn't counting in the moment, the meter change registers as a felt discontinuity the same way a scale degree registers as a felt tonal color. 3/4's "waltzing" swing-around feel vs. 4/4's driven, solid feel is the same phenomenon at a more familiar level — most listeners just haven't noticed it as a *character* because nearly all pop/rock listening stays in 4/4 (the "fish doesn't know it's in water" problem). Practical approach: **treat each odd meter as its own individual vocabulary item to learn** (get thoroughly familiar with 5/4, THEN separately with 7/8, etc.) rather than expecting a general "odd meter sense" to emerge from music theory alone — the same years-of-repetition model as scale-degree/chord ear training. — David Bennett, [[David Bennett on Music Theory, Composition & the YouTube Life Sonic Sorcery Podcast No. 04]]

## From videos

- **Commercial-viability data: which alternate meters actually chart, and which essentially never do.** A survey of all top-40 best-selling US songs 2000–2024 (933 songs) found 96.57% in plain 4/4; the 3.43% that aren't split, ranked by frequency: 6/8 by far the most common (Alicia Keys' "Fallin'," Chad Kroeger's "Hero"), then 12/8 (Lewis Capaldi's "Before You Go"), swung 3/4 ≈ 9/8 feel (Alicia Keys' "If I Ain't Got You"), and genuine mixed meter (OutKast's "Hey Ya" — a six-bar loop of 4/4×3, 2/4×1, 4/4×2). **Headline finding**: zero true odd-meter songs (5/4, 7/4) EVER cracked a top-40 year-end chart in the 21st century, despite odd-meter pop songs existing (Hozier's "From Eden," Taylor Swift's "tolerate it"/"closure," all 5/4) — none sold well enough. Not a new pattern: Pink Floyd's "Money" (1973) only reached #92 on its year's US chart despite being one of the best-known odd-meter hits ever. Practical takeaway: IF a hit strays from 4/4, expect 6/8 or 12/8 first, occasionally swung 3/4 or mixed meter — essentially never a true odd meter like 5/4 or 7/4, regardless of era. → David Bennett Music Theory, [[97 percent of pop is in 4-4]]
- **The comprehensive 7/4 grouping taxonomy, plus the felt-pulse 7/4-vs-7/8 distinction.** Most 7/4 songs group as **4+3** ("Have a Good Time" Paul Simon, "Winslow" Bon Iver, "Do You Want It All?" Two Door Cinema Club) — feels like 4/4 with every other bar cut short. Less common: **3+4** ("$20," boygenius). A subdivided variant arguably better called **14/8**, since the eighth note (not the quarter) is the real pulse — grouped 12+2 eighths ("Look at Me Now," Caroline Polachek). And a **5+2** grouping (Coldplay's "Death and All His Friends"). **The "skipped beat" effect**: a mostly-4/4 song dropping an occasional 7/4 bar reads as a beat getting cut, pulling the ear into the next phrase one beat early — Bowie's "Soul Love," "All You Need Is Love," Blondie's "Heart of Glass" and "I Love Rock 'n' Roll," Foo Fighters' "Times Like These" (which layers a SECOND oddity, a 3-bar riff loop instead of 4-bar, on top of its 7/4). **7/4 vs. 7/8, felt-pulse definition**: 7/4 is felt as seven distinct beats; 7/8 (quarter note still the pulse) is felt as 3½ beats per bar. Calibration disagreement: Pink Floyd's "Money" — guitarist David Gilmour calls it 7/8, but by this felt-pulse test it's actually swung 7/4. → David Bennett Music Theory, [[74 Songs in 7/4 time]]
- **A riff notated in plain 4/4 that's felt as anything but, via a hidden 3/4 grouping stacked with a genuine 3-against-2 polyrhythm.** Radiohead's "Myxomatosis": the 16-beat riff's first 12 beats fall naturally into four bars of 3/4, with only the final bar genuinely 4/4 (an extra beat tacked on) — so it reads as "five bars of 3/4, the last one stretched" rather than plain 4/4. Independently, stripped of bar lines, the riff's ten dotted-quarter pulse runs against the hi-hat's straight quarter-note pulse — a real 3-against-2 polyrhythm ("nice cup of tea" mnemonic for the felt grouping) — necessarily interrupted at the same point the hidden-3/4 trick breaks. **Purer companion example**: "Daydreaming" (also Radiohead) uses a cleaner, archetypal 3-against-2 (right hand 3 notes against left hand's 2) with no extra hidden-meter layer on top. → David Bennett Music Theory, [[What time signature is Radiohead's Myxomatosis in]]
- **Polymeter vs. polyrhythm, stated precisely — the two terms are often conflated.** Polyrhythm: two different subdivisions of the SAME bar at the SAME tempo, resynced every bar (e.g. 4-against-3 within one bar). Polymeter: two different time signatures at the SAME tempo but NOT resynced every bar, so the parts drift out of phase and only realign after a full cycle. Worked catalog from King Gizzard's *Polygondwanaland* (2017 — every track uses an odd/unusual meter): "Crumbling Castle" shifts 7/8→4/4→5/8 mid-song, with a 6/8→4/4 cut that reads as a tempo drop even though the BPM never changes, because the felt main-pulse subdivision changes (dotted-8th in 6/8 vs. quarter-note in 4/4) — a **metric modulation**. One track groups its 5/8 bars into larger 5-bar phrase units (a second "5" nested inside the first). Another opens on a 5/8 synth ostinato with an 11/8 drum layer placed over it (11-against-5 polymeter) that gradually TAKES OVER as the dominant felt meter — polymeter used as a structural pivot device, not just a fixed simultaneous texture. Analysis tip: isolating stems with a tool like Moises makes picking apart which instrument carries which meter layer far easier than listening to the full mix. → [[Diatonic Modes]] for the same band's one-track-per-mode companion album — David Bennett Music Theory, [[How King Gizzard use Odd Time Signatures, Polyrhythm & Polymeter]]
- **7/4 with irregular internal grouping, plus tonic-major-over-a-minor-key ambiguity.** Willow Smith's "Symptom of Life" (2024) verse groups its 7/4 bar as four groups of three eighth notes with the last tied over the bar line — the grouping is what reads as "odd," not just the 7 on top — and its pre-chorus stretches to a two-bar/14-beat pattern subdivided 3+5+4+2, displaced against the underlying 7/4 pulse. Harmonically the song sits in A minor but keeps resolving to an A **major** chord instead, with brief Phrygian (♭2) coloring. → [[Chord Naming by Context]] for the tonic-ambiguity angle — David Bennett Music Theory, [[the craziest pop song of the 21st century]]
- **3/4's genre character vs. 4/4, demonstrated by a direct conversion — the meter equivalent of the cut-time-vs-4/4 feel distinction above.** 3/4 reads as "old-fashioned, sweet, sentimental," lighter and less driving than 4/4; the Red Hot Chili Pipers' 4/4 arrangement of "Amazing Grace" (normally 3/4) is noticeably more driving/rock-like than the original — cited as the direct reason 3/4 is rare in rock (a significant part of rock's power comes from 4/4 itself), with Jimi Hendrix's "Manic Depression" and Metallica's "One" (4/4→3/4 mid-song) as the rock songs that lean into 3/4's character rather than avoiding it. **Swung 3/4 = "jazz waltz"**: uneven (long-short) eighth-note pairs within a 3/4 bar — Nina Simone's "Mr. Bojangles" turns an originally-straight 3/4 country song into a jazz waltz purely by adding swing; swung rhythms are conventionally notated AS IF straight, with a small marking indicating swing performance, since that's simpler to write/read than full triplet notation. 3/4's other historical dance forms: the minuet (most famous example, "Minuet in G," long misattributed to Bach, now credited to Christian Petzold) and the sarabande. → [[Songs that use 3-4 time]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [Songs that use 3/4 time](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LtvtkwQjDIw)
- **Cut time (2/2): mathematically identical to 4/4, but notationally signals a different feel — plus its real classical function, *alla breve*.** A bar of 2/2 and a bar of 4/4 both hold two half notes' worth of music, so any passage could technically be written either way — the reason to pick 2/2 is to communicate a strong-weak TWO-in-a-bar emphasis (beats 1 and 3) rather than four roughly-equal felt beats, which reads as noticeably more laid-back/plodding than the same rhythm in 4/4 (demonstrated directly on "Country Roads," John Denver, notated both ways). Cut time's genre home is country/folk for exactly that laid-back feel: "Country Roads," The Beatles' "Maxwell's Silver Hammer," Dolly Parton's "Jolene," Johnny Cash's "Folsom Prison Blues," Bob Dylan's "Maggie's Farm." **In classical scoring, 2/2 solves a fast-tempo readability problem**: the *alla breve* movement of Rachmaninoff's Piano Concerto No. 3, transcribed in 4/4 at 90 quarter notes/minute, would need dense 16th notes throughout; re-notated in 2/2 at 90 HALF notes/minute (identical actual speed), every note value doubles — 16ths become 8ths, 8ths become quarters — making the same fast passage far easier to read. **Notation**: 4/4 = "common time" (a plain C symbol); 2/2 = "cut time"/"cut common time" (the same C with a vertical line through it) — neither symbol has a functional advantage over writing the numeric signature, it's purely convention. → [[Songs that use Cut time]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [Songs that use Cut time](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LTW9UcxHlvQ)
- **19/8, one of the rarest meters covered on this page — a named subdivision traceable to a specific source, plus a performance-mindset quote worth keeping alongside any counting scheme.** Chicago's "Introduction" (19/8 interlude) subdivides as 3+3+2+1+2+2+2, traced directly to jazz-fusion bandleader Don Ellis's track "33221222" (its title literally spells out the same grouping) — Chicago drummer Danny Seraphine credits Ellis's influence and had the subdivision written on his own snare drum to track it live. His stated goal, worth keeping as its own principle: **"always try to make odd time feel like four"** — the target is a rock feel despite the unusual count, not a purely academic display of the meter. A composite 19/4 sibling: the middle section of Soundgarden's "Rusty Cage," best subdivided as three bars of 3/4 plus two bars of 5/4 (same composite-meter family as 14/4 and 10/4 above). Other 19/8 songs: Bill Bruford's "Hell's Bells," Animals As Leaders' "The Problem of Other Minds," Herbie Hancock's "Hidden Shadows." An unused but plausible grouping worth noting as a compositional idea: 4+4+4+4+3, which feels close to fast 4/4 that skips a beat on every fifth rotation. → [[Songs that use 19-8 time]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [Songs that use 19/8 time](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BVvSjkVgBW4)
- **The same 15/8 bar regrouped in genuinely different ways for genuinely different feels — a clean worked case that a fixed beat-count is a grouping choice, not one fixed shape.** 7+8 grouping (the alternating asymmetry itself is the point — "Tubular Bells" producer Tom Newman credited it directly for the piece's mainstream success, since a plain 8+8 bar would let listeners' attention lapse but the odd 7+8 alternation keeps "tripping you up"): Mike Oldfield's "Tubular Bells," Led Zeppelin's "The Ocean" (riff only, verses are 4/4), The Pretenders' "Tattooed Love Boys." A completely different grouping of the same 15 beats — 6/4 + 3/8 — on Biffy Clyro's "Mountains" (UK #5, 2008). Most striking: Dream Theater's "A Change of Seasons," where drummer Mike Portnoy deliberately regroups the identical 15/8 total differently verse to verse (verse 1: 3/4+9/8 = 6+9; verse 2: two bars of 6/8 + one bar of 3/8 = 6+6+3) specifically so the meter doesn't read as static despite the arithmetic repeating. **Simple vs. compound time, generalized**: simple time's prevailing beat is the quarter note (each beat = 2 eighth notes); compound time's prevailing beat is the dotted quarter (each beat = 3 eighth notes), giving compound meters their swing — 6/8 = compound duple, 9/8 = compound triple, 12/8 = compound quadruple, 15/8 grouped as five even 3s = compound quintuple (heard in Queens of the Stone Age's "I Think I Lost My Headache" outro and the opening of Soundgarden's "Limo Wreck"). → [[Songs that use 15-8 time]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [Songs that use 15/8 time](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ERnuYncziSc)
- **Why an x/8 meter feels less stable than the same beat-count as x/4 — the general mechanism behind every odd-8th-denominator meter on this page.** The ear's sense of meter latches onto the quarter-note pulse even when the notated denominator is an eighth note, so a bar of 17/8 (17 eighth notes) only divides into 8½ quarter notes and feels like "9 beats with the last one sliced in half" — same shape at 11/8 (feels like 5½), 13/8 (6½), 15/8 (7½). The identical beat-count written with a 4 on the bottom instead (17/4) feels noticeably more grounded, because it's a complete set of quarter notes rather than a fraction — still odd, but far less disorienting. **Practical notation corollary**: a large top number over a 4 (17/4) is better transcribed as smaller bars (e.g. three bars of 4/4 + one of 5/4) than as one oversized bar — bar lines carry accent information a single giant bar loses. Worked examples: Björk's "Moon" (steady 17/8 ostinato); The National (17/8 grouped 4+5+4+4, easier to follow than an undifferentiated count); Dream Theater's "The Alien" (fast 17/8, feels close to "8½" — won the band their first Grammy); Erasure's "Always" (17/4 chorus, comparatively stable per the mechanism above). → [[Songs that use 17-8 time]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [Songs that use 17/8 time](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3-eYp6HUoCs)
- **Taming an absurd unified meter by subdividing top-down until it's actually performable — plus polymeter as a distinct device from mixed meter.** Tigran Hamasyan describes his track "Entertain Me" as being in 256/16 time; Bennett breaks that down as 7 repetitions of a 35/16 phrase plus one bar of 11/16 (7×35+11=256), and the 35/16 bar itself further splits into 4 groups of five + 5 groups of three — so the practical transcription for a performer is 4 bars of 5/16 then 5 bars of 3/16, not one theoretically-"correct" 256/16 bar. **Polymeter** (distinct from mixed meter, where the whole ensemble changes meter together): the piano runs this odd subdivision while the **drummer just plays plain 4/4** underneath at the same tempo, in time but out of phase — an extreme meter doesn't have to be performed as extreme by every player; anchoring one instrument (drums/bass) to a simple meter is a legitimate way to make otherwise-unplayable material work in a real band. → [[Songs that use 15-8 time]] and similar odd-meter entries above for single-meter cases — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Tigran Hamasyan's crazy polymetric time signatures]]
- **2/4 as a "half bar" briefly dropped into an otherwise 4/4 song — a subtle mixed-meter device most listeners never consciously register.** A single two-beat bar inserted where a 4/4 bar would sit, then straight back to 4/4: "Africa" (Toto, verse), "Revolution" (Beatles, two bars 4/4 then one 2/4), "To the End" (Blur, verse), "Assassin" (Muse, verse). Perceptual effect: creates a "stop-start" feel that breaks 4/4's straight-ahead drive, so that when the song later settles into uninterrupted 4/4 (a chorus, say), that section lands with more relief and drive by contrast — mixed meter used to make a later straight section feel bigger. Can also be baked into the main groove itself rather than a one-off: "Hey Ya" (OutKast) loops a 22-beat pattern of two 4/4 bars + one 2/4 bar + two more 4/4 bars continuously. **Notation-ambiguity point**: any 4/4 song can be equivalently transcribed as continuous 2/4 by doubling the bar count — some analysts argue "Hey Jude" reads more cleanly in constant 2/4 than in 4/4-with-exceptions. → David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use 2-4 time]]
- **3/4-verse-to-stable-4/4-chorus as the same "interruption makes the payoff bigger" device, worked in a specific hit song.** "Take Me to Church" (Hozier) opens its verses in 3/4 with an occasional single bar of 4/4 mixed in at varying ratios between verses, then locks into stable, uninterrupted 4/4 specifically at the chorus — the rhythmic settling reinforces the chorus's arrival the same way the 2/4-insertion technique above uses interruption to make an eventual straight section feel bigger. → [[Cadences]] for this same video's plagal/"Amen" cadence material — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Why 'Take Me To Church' is an unusual pop song]]
- **A deliberately countdown-structured meter intro, distinct from an unpatterned odd-meter shift.** Radiohead's "Sail to the Moon" opens on a repeating phrase built from a bar of 7/4, a bar of 2/4, then a bar matching the first — but each repetition shrinks the outer bars by one beat: 7/4→2/4→7/4, then 6/4→2/4→6/4, then 5/4→2/4→5/4, a numeric countdown (7, 6, 5...) that eventually settles into stable 4/4 as the first verse begins. Thom Yorke's own account of rehearsing it: the band had to count through explicitly ("this one's seven, this one's six, this one's five") specifically so the recorded version would sound effortless rather than counted — a reminder that a deliberately-patterned meter change can (and should) be made to disappear from the listener's conscious perception even while it's mechanically precise. The same song layers a second, unrelated instability on top: random beats spliced out of bars in the final verse, turning several 4/4 bars into one-off 3/4 bars with no repeating pattern — worth keeping as the contrast case for "patterned metric device" vs. "irregular one-off edit." → [[Modal Interchange]] and [[Chromatic Mediants and Pop Modulation]] for this song's other complexity layers — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Radiohead's most complex song]]
- **"Composite meter" — a category distinct from true odd meter, for a time signature that divides evenly on paper (fails the odd-meter test) but still feels unusual because it's built from two DIFFERENT smaller meters glued together rather than one uniform pulse.** True odd meters (5/4, 7/4, 11/4) feel unusual because the beat count itself can't split evenly into 2s or 3s; 10/4 and 6/4 both pass that "evenly divisible" test yet still sound odd in practice, because real songs in 10/4 alternate between two different smaller meters bar-to-bar rather than a genuinely uniform 10-beat feel — the tell is uneven internal groupings (4+4+2, not 5+5; if it divided into uniform 5s you'd just call it two bars of 5/4). Two worked grouping patterns: **4+4+2** ("Everything In Its Right Place," Radiohead — reads as 4/4+4/4+2/4, occasionally dropping to a one-off 9/4 bar); **4-fast-beats+4-slow-beats** (quarter notes then dotted quarter notes — "Go To Sleep," Radiohead, and "Just Like You Imagined," Nine Inch Nails — functionally equivalent to alternating 4/4 and 12/8, arguably the more useful way to actually notate it for a performer). → [[Songs that use 10-4 time]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [Songs that use 10/4 time](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=T6IA2d54NFs)
- **14/4, the same composite-meter category one size up, with two distinct grouping patterns and a genuinely different 14/8 sibling.** 4+2+4+4 grouping: The Strokes' "Two Kinds of Happiness" (first section), Elliott Smith's "Rose Parade." 3-bars-4/4-then-1-bar-2/4 grouping: Fort Minor's "Kenji" — sounds like plain 4/4 except every fourth bar gets cut in half. **A different-denominator sibling that isn't the same shape despite the same top number**: King Gizzard and the Lizard Wizard's "The Fourth Colour" is 14/8 (not 14/4), grouped as four sets of three plus one set of two (4×3+2=14) — effectively 12/8 (compound quadruple) with an extra 2/8 tagged on, worth keeping distinct from the 14/4 examples above since the grouping mechanism differs even though "14" is the shared number. → [[Songs that use 14-4 time]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [Songs that use 14/4 time](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YdRy0ZblGdE)
- **11/8 vs. 11/4 is a notation choice, not a different sound — the deciding factor is tempo, and the same test generalizes to any x/8-vs-x/4 pair.** Test: does the tempo make you naturally count "5½" quarter-note beats per bar (too fast to subdivide further → notate as 11/8) or a genuine 11 distinct beats (slow enough to feel 11 → notate as 11/4)? "Skirting on the Surface" (The Smile) reads as 11/8; Dave Brubeck's "Eleven Four" is unambiguously 11/4. **A one-off odd bar embedded in an otherwise regular-meter song can be reinterpreted as several smaller bars for performer clarity** — "Here Comes the Sun" (Beatles): one passage transcribable as 11/8+4/4+2/4+3/8, argued better split as 3/8+3/8+3/8+2/8, since it tells a performer exactly which beats to accent rather than leaving an undifferentiated 11-beat bar. **Grouping catalog for 11-beat bars** (same beat count, different characteristic feel per grouping): 3+3+3+2 reads as a 12/8 shuffle with the last beat dropped ("11," Primus); 3+4+4 (Crowned and Kissed, Esperanza Spalding); 3+3+3+2 at a slower tempo reads as a lopsided waltz instead of a shuffle (Whipping Post, Allman Brothers); 4+4+3 (a third option). → [[Songs that use 11-8 time]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [Songs that use 11/8 time](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wS9t4Ftkz7Q)
- **The x/8-vs-x/4 instability mechanism, sharpened beyond the tempo test above into a precise, tempo-independent explanation.** Any odd meter with an EIGHT on the bottom (13/8, 11/8, 7/8...) feels less stable than the equivalent meter with a FOUR on the bottom, because an odd top number over 8 always leaves the felt quarter-note pulse with one beat "sliced in half" at the end of the bar — 13/8 settles into a felt pulse of 6½ quarter-note beats, not a clean 7 (13 eighth notes = 6 full quarter beats + one half-beat remainder). A meter with a FOUR on the bottom (13/4, 7/4...) has no such remainder — every beat is felt in full, which is why it reads as more grounded despite the identical odd beat-count. Direct demonstration on identical grouping math: "Weapons Factory" (Super Mario RPG, 13/8, 7+6 split) vs. "The Becoming" (Nine Inch Nails, 13/4, the same 7+6 split felt as quarter notes) — same numbers, different bottom number, audibly different stability. → [[Songs that use 13-8 time]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [Songs that use 13/8 time](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C9NJeteDIys)
- **Mixed meter = the time signature itself keeps changing, distinct from being "in" one fixed odd meter throughout** (a song fully in 5/8 all the way through is just "in 5/8"; mixed meter specifically means unpredictable metric change, a different listening effect). Graduated song examples: a single inserted odd bar reads as a mild accent (Blackbird — mostly 4/4 with single 2/4 or 3/4 bars dropped in; Golden Brown — mostly 3/4, one bar of 4/4 per line); a "half bar" specifically preserves danceability where a full odd bar would break it (Hey Ya — 3 bars 4/4 + 1 bar 2/4 + 2 bars 4/4, the author arguing a 3/4 bar in that slot would likely have killed the song's dance-hit status by changing the pulse rather than just shortening it); layering a second shifting meter onto an already-odd base compounds the exotic feel (Four Sticks, Led Zeppelin — mostly 5/8, cutting to 6/8 for a couple of bars); an unstable verse meter makes a following plain-4/4 section read as a release, independent of harmony (Mountains, Biffy Clyro — verse alternates 4/4/7/8, "wave of relief" when the next section drops into straight 4/4); most extreme cited example, a genuine UK top-10 hit — Turn It On Again (Genesis): verse alternates 6/4 and 7/4, chorus cycles unpredictably through 2/4, 3/4, 4/4, 5/4 with no repeating pattern. → [[Songs that have a Mixed Meter]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [Songs that have a Mixed Meter](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PWHKtkx6F00)
- **The foundational 3/4-vs-6/8 distinction, extended to 12/8 and 9/8 — same note count, different grouping, genuinely different feel.** 3/4 = simple TRIPLE time (3 strong beats, each divides into 2); 6/8 = compound DUPLE time (2 strong beats, each divides into 3) — 6/8 is NOT "six beats," it has 2. "America" (Bernstein) alternates the two bar-to-bar for a felt speed-up/slow-down. **Tempo blurs the distinction in both directions**: a genuine 6/8 played very slowly ("Gravity," John Mayer) can start reading as 3/4 (the ear can't hold the 2-beat grouping and defaults to counting eighths); a genuine 3/4 at a fast tempo can start reading as 6/8 (disputed case: "America," Simon & Garfunkel). **One level further**: 12/8 (compound quadruple, 4 beats ÷3) is a swung/shuffled 4/4 at fast tempos, functionally identical to two bars of 6/8 at slow tempos — which notation a transcriber picks is genuinely a matter of preference (Lou Reed's "Perfect Day" is transcribed both ways). **9/8 has two unrelated uses**: a "jazz waltz" (swung 3/4, technically 9/8) is almost always still NOTATED as 3/4 + a swing instruction rather than true 9/8, since it's easier to read and gives the performer freedom over how much to swing; separately, grouping 9/8 UNEVENLY (4+5 rather than 3+3+3) makes it a genuine odd meter instead ("Seven Days," Sting). Also supplies the precise mechanism behind this note's existing "Daydreaming" (Radiohead) 3-over-2 citation below → [[18 Rhythms you should know]]: piano right hand in 3/4 against left hand in 6/8. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[What's the difference between 3-4 and 6-8 time]]
- **9/8's uneven-grouping use, by name and tradition: "aksak" rhythm, specifically 2+2+2+3 — the Turkish/Balkan tradition behind it, and the piece that puts both 9/8 identities side by side.** Dave Brubeck heard this grouping from street musicians in Turkey, who told him "this rhythm is to us what the blues is to you" — the anecdote behind "Blue Rondo à la Turk" (Dave Brubeck Quartet), which explicitly alternates the Turkish 2+2+2+3 grouping against the regular Western 3+3+3 compound grouping within the same piece. Same pattern underlies the Turkish zeybek and Greek zeibekiko dances. **Why it sounds "odd" is cultural, not acoustic**: most Western music uses isochronous (equal-duration) beat groupings by convention; regions where non-isochronous groupings are commonplace don't hear the same rhythm as unusual — comparable to an unfamiliar language sounding odd only until learned. Further uneven-9/8 examples: 5+4 ("I Hung My Head," Sting); 4+4+1, reading almost as straight 4/4 with one extra eighth-note stuck on the bar's end, described as "janky, chaotic" ("The Crunge," Led Zeppelin; "Gamma Knife," King Gizzard & the Lizard Wizard). A song can layer BOTH readings at once rather than picking one: "Jambi" (Tool) — kick drum holds a strict isochronous 3+3+3 pulse while vocal/guitar phrase freely on top, unlocked from that grouping. A single 9/8 bar dropped into an otherwise-6/8 song reads as a half-bar extension (they share the same eighth-note pulse) rather than a full meter change — "Nothing Else Matters" intro (Metallica); "Anyone Who Had a Heart" (Burt Bacharach) mixes both line-by-line. → [[Songs that use 9-8 time]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [Songs that use 9/8 time](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=I7BxNEhVHhY)
- **Simple time → compound time as a distinct category of meter change, separate from "different number of beats per bar."** "Good Morning, Good Morning" (Beatles) verse 2 shifts from a series of irregularly-barred simple-time bars into 12/8 — NOT a change in beat count (both feel like 4 strong beats) but a change in how each beat subdivides (2 vs. 3). Practically equivalent to, and often easier to transcribe as, 4/4 with a triplet on every beat — confirmed in the drum pattern (hi-hat plays consistent 8th-note triplets against kick/snare marking the 4 strong beats), producing a "shuffled"/swung 4/4 feel. → [[Syncopation and Metric Displacement]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[The Crazy Time Signatures of Good Morning, Good Morning by The Beatles]]
- **A genuinely open, crowdsourced (Reddit-fed) investigation of why 4/4 dominates pop music — 94% of a 450-song, 70-year sample, per the presenter's own count.** "Innate" arguments: bipedal walking/running rhythm (humans are unusually good long-distance runners, possibly incentivizing rhythmic entrainment for pacing), breathing's in-out 2-part cycle, heartbeat pulse (flagged as the weakest argument — not exclusively 4/4-shaped), and symmetry (humans generally prefer it; 4/4 divides evenly at every structural level). "Culturally learned" counter-evidence: many non-Western traditional musics (Greece, Turkey, Bulgaria) routinely use "odd" meters (7/8, 9/8, 5/8, 11/8) as comfortable everyday/dance music. Wrinkle: even non-Western POP leans 4/4 too, attributed to global pop being a Western-derived commercial industry (Turkish pop literally began as "light western music" covers). Further wrinkle: 4/4 doesn't even trace to Western folk tradition (European folk dance leans 3/4/2/4) — the presenter's actual resolution traces pop's 4/4 habit specifically to the **blues lineage** (blues → jazz/R&B/rock and roll → punk/disco/EDM/country/hip-hop), not folk music or a fixed innate preference. Open-ended close: 3/4 was comparatively popular in the 19th century, so 4/4's current dominance may not be permanent. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Why is 4/4 so common?]]

- **A practice method for odd meters: lock onto a repeating one-bar anchor rhythm ("clave"/ostinato) instead of counting beats, with a worked pattern for every odd meter 5/4 through 17/8.** General principle: pick a rhythmic shape that fills exactly one bar and feel *that*, not a number count — the pattern can be embellished as long as its overall length is preserved. Worked patterns: **5/4** = long-long-short-short (two dotted quarters + two quarters — the *Mission: Impossible* theme; that exact rhythm is also Morse code for "MI," presumably the intended pun); **7/8** = quarter+quarter+three-eighths; **9/8** = three quarters + three eighths (grouping the "extra" beat as three 8ths at the end reads more natural than a flat "4/4 + 1" framing); **11/8** = 12/8 (four groups of 3) missing the last 8th note; **13/8** = either five quarters + three eighths, or 12/8 plus one extra 8th thrown in; **15/8** = two bars of 4/4 (16 eighth notes) missing one 8th note; **17/8** = two bars of 4/4 plus one extra 8th (three groups of 4 + one group of 5), or five groups of 3 + one group of 2. General shape across the higher odd meters: frame them as a familiar near-even grouping (12/8, or two bars of 4/4) with one small deviation, rather than an unfamiliar number from scratch — the deviation is what gives the "almost settles into a normal groove, then gets thrown off" feel. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[How to play odd meters WITHOUT counting]]
- **A 25-rhythm named-pattern catalog, from two companion videos, useful as shared vocabulary for talking about rhythm ("that's a tresillo," "that's a shuffle") rather than describing every groove from scratch.** Grouping/meter-based patterns: **tresillo** (3-3-2 division of 8 eighth notes, the rhythmic "cell" underlying several Latin/Afro-Cuban rhythms below — heard in *Clocks* by Coldplay via accent placement on a constant eighth-note arpeggio); **double tresillo** (3-3-3-3-2-2 over two bars of 4/4, working against the meter hard enough to briefly suggest 6/8 or 12/8); **son clave** (3-2 or 2-3 form; = the **Bo Diddley beat** in rock contexts) and **rumba clave** (nearly identical, one note shifted an eighth note later); **habanera** (tresillo played against two quarter notes — *La Paloma*, the *Carmen* "Habanera," tango's *Por una Cabeza*); **standard African bell pattern / bembé** (felt in 12/8, likely historical root of the blues/jazz/rock **shuffle** feel — 12/8 emphasizing the 1st and 3rd eighth note of each beat, equivalently "4/4 with triplets on every beat"); **bossa nova** (archetypal snare grouping ~3-3-4-3-3 — heard outside bossa nova in *Break On Through* by The Doors); **Charleston** (just the first two notes of a tresillo/clave); **Turkish aksak / 9/8 "limping" rhythm** (2+2+3 grouping, felt quick-quick-slow — Dave Brubeck's *Blue Rondo à la Turk*, also found in Balkan folk music); **"5/4 clave"** (a long-long-short-short 4-note anchor pattern for playing in 5/4 — the *Mission: Impossible* theme); **waltz** (3/4 strong-weak-weak). Rock/pop-specific: **gallop** (eighth + two sixteenths, repeating — the "heavy metal gallop"); **"Barbara Ann" rhythm** (a beat delayed by an eighth note, syncopation carrying into the next bar); **skank/ska upstroke** (repeated emphasized offbeat — *Ob-La-Di, Ob-La-Da*). Swing family: **swing ratios** described as long:short eighth-note ratios (3:1 = strong swing, 2:1 = shuffle); **Scotch snap / Lombard rhythm** (the reverse — short note then long; Scottish folk and Baroque origins, also common in current R&B/hip-hop). Full breakdown, song examples, and mnemonics → [[7 Rhythms you should know]], [[18 Rhythms you should know]]. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[18 Rhythms you should know]]
- **Single-beat and half-bar insertions as a Beatles songwriting habit — mostly not a full meter change, just one odd bar dropped into an otherwise steady pulse.** Adding a beat to give a vocal phrase room to breathe: *Across the Universe* (a one-off 5/4 bar = a rest beat at a line's end — removing it makes the vocal feel rushed into the next line); *Don't Let Me Down* (a 5/4 bar at each verse's start, during an unaccompanied moment, plausibly unconscious since there's no band to clash with there). Half-bar/compound-time insertions: *Strawberry Fields Forever* (one-off 2/4 half-bars, plus shifts into 6/8 compound time — main pulse becomes the dotted quarter note instead of 4/4's quarter note, reading as a momentary slowdown even at constant tempo — and a final line trimmed to 3/4). A deliberately jarring skipped beat as an *effect*: *Back in the USSR* (two bars of 3/4 near the end of the second chorus create a "record skip" feel, as if the phrase keeps getting cut off before finally completing on the third pass). A "bloated" bar: *Martha My Dear* (read as 5/4 then back to 4/4). Frequent light insertions across a whole song: *Blackbird* (scattered single 2/4 and 3/4 bars) and *Two of Us* (a 2/4 bar mid-verse; chorus drops to 3/4 for several bars, capped by a 2/4 bar before returning to 4/4). → [[Syncopation and Metric Displacement]] (the related *Happiness Is a Warm Gun* polymeter case) — David Bennett Music Theory, [[The Beatles Time Signature Trick]]
- **Odd-meter counting trick: break a big/scary time signature into small sub-groups instead of counting straight through.** For 7/8 (or 5/4, or a cited 17/8 example), don't count "one through seven" — identify smaller groupings within the actual riff/rhythm (e.g. 2+2+3) and count those instead; claimed payoff is that after enough practice, ideas in odd meters start appearing naturally while writing, without deliberately reaching for them (cites his own riff shifting 5/4→4/4→6/4, written without consciously tracking the meter change). → [[Syncopation and Metric Displacement]] — BERNTH, [[Music Theory for METAL (Beginner's Guide)]]

- **A subdivision-switching timing drill: land immediately in the new note value at the switch, not drift into it.** Built over a real cadence (16th notes for one chord, 8th-note triplets for the next, back to 16ths for the third) — the practice target isn't the tempo or the specific lick, it's arriving exactly in time on the new subdivision right at the barline rather than settling into correct spacing gradually over the first half of the new measure. Build-your-own version recommended: any short chord progression, same one-measure-per-subdivision-switch structure (16ths↔triplets, or a full quarter→eighth→16th "written pyramid"), scaled to current level. — BERNTH, [[90% Of Guitarists DON'T Practice This! (A HUGE Mistake...)]]
- **New content from a third BERNTH odd-meter video — the 2-2-3-in-7/8 grouping itself duplicates the two bullets below and isn't re-logged.** (1) **The same riff can read as either a genuine odd-meter composition or a syncopated 4/4 groove depending purely on the drum arrangement**, not the guitar part — demoed by replaying an identical 7/8 riff with a straighter drum beat underneath, which shifts the listener's perception toward "syncopated 4/4" even though the guitar notes/rhythm never changed; framed as a deliberate arranging choice (accent the riff's own note-groups vs. accent somewhere else) rather than a fixed property of the riff. (2) **Write odd-meter lead lines with their own internal arc, not as a 4/4 phrase that's simply cut one note short** — a stated composition goal distinct from the rhythmic-grouping mechanics: a lead/solo section should feel like it's genuinely built for the odd meter's own phrase length, not like a familiar 8-note idea missing its last note. (3) **Add harmonic-minor and diminished-flavored notes (the raised 7th; the ♭5) into an odd-meter line** as a further layer of interest on top of the metric device itself. (4) Names the **konnakol** (Carnatic Indian rhythm-solfège) system as a recommended alternative to numeric counting, without teaching it — corroborates the syllable-based counting bullet already below with a named real-world tradition. → BERNTH, [[How To Write Riffs & Solos In An Odd Meter (Odd Time Signature)]]
- **Quarter-note triplets are commonly mis-felt as dotted-note rhythms — the fix is thinking in an 8th-note-triplet grid and deliberately skipping every other slot.** Since one quarter-note triplet occupies exactly two 8th-note-triplet positions, counting/visualizing the finer 8th-note-triplet grid (rather than trying to feel the quarter-note triplet directly) locks the timing in instead of approximating it by ear. Also frames quintuplets/septuplets in a plain, non-odd meter as "squeeze N notes into the space normally held by 4" (an 8th-note quintuplet group occupies the space of 4 straight 8th notes), with a second word-mnemonic data point for this page's existing syllable-counting bullet: "UNIVERSITY" (5 syllables) for quintuplet groups. — BERNTH, [5 Levels Of Rhythm Complexity (easy to INSANE)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XCDI1YlQSkI)
- **A second, more worked-through source for this page's odd-meter grouping trick, in 7/8 specifically: group the 7 eighth notes as 2-2-3, mirrored to 3-2-2 across a two-bar phrase, and start every small group with a downstroke so the first note of each group is naturally accented.** Also gives the "why it sounds unstable" reasoning directly: 7/8 is felt against the ear's 4/4 or 8/8 expectation, missing exactly one eighth note from resolving into a familiar grouping. — BERNTH, [[2 Amazing Hacks For Better Riffs]]
- **Odd-meter counting trick: break a big/scary time signature into small sub-groups instead of counting straight through.** For 7/8 (or 5/4, or a cited 17/8 example), don't count "one through seven" — identify smaller groupings within the actual riff/rhythm (e.g. 2+2+3) and count those instead; claimed payoff is that after enough practice, ideas in odd meters start appearing naturally while writing, without deliberately reaching for them (cites his own riff shifting 5/4→4/4→6/4, written without consciously tracking the meter change). → [[Syncopation and Metric Displacement]] — BERNTH, [[Music Theory for METAL (Beginner's Guide)]]

- **An alternative to numeric rhythm counting: assign one distinct multi-syllable word per subdivision level, so the word's own syllable count matches the note density instead of reusing "and" across every level.** Quarter notes get a single beat-syllable word; eighth-note pairs get a two-syllable word (one syllable per eighth); triplets get a three-syllable word; sixteenth notes get a four-syllable word; half/whole notes each get their own sustained word held for 2/4 beats (traditional counting has no standard verbal convention for held notes at all); the two "backwards" syncopated groupings (eighth-tied-to-two-sixteenths, and two-sixteenths-tied-to-an-eighth) each get their own distinct word too. Functionally the same mechanism as established rhythm-solfège pedagogy — Kodály's "ta"/"ti-ti"/"tri-o-la" syllables or the Takadimi system taught in college aural-skills courses — reinvented independently; the transferable idea is mapping spoken-syllable density to note density with one consistent word per subdivision level, not the specific vocabulary. → [[Why does everybody count Rhythm wrong!]] — Brandon D'Eon, [Why does everybody count Rhythm wrong!](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fwkra7vJ6x4)
- **Slow tempos are often harder to lock in than fast ones, and the fix is counting the underlying subdivision (8ths/16ths), not just the beat.** Direct pushback on the common assumption that faster = harder: sitting in the pocket at ~50 BPM is flagged as genuinely difficult unless the subdivision underneath the beat is being actively counted rather than just felt. — Brandon D'Eon, [5 INSANE SOLOS OVER CHORDS MY INSTAGRAM FOLLOWERS SENT ME](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3BnOfswa_RA)
