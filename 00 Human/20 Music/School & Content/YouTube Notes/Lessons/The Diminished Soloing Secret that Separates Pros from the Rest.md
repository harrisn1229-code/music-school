# The Diminished Soloing Secret that Separates Pros from the Rest

## Synopsis
Nathan Borton reframes the diminished sound the way he says players like George Benson and Mark Whitfield actually think about it — not as a diminished arpeggio/chord (root-♭3-♭5-𝄫7) built off the dominant chord's own tones, but as a **13♭9 sound**: the "diminished over dominant" reduces to a plain **major triad a minor 3rd above the chord's root**, played over the dominant bass note (worked example: an **A major triad over a C bass**, i.e. over C7). Because that triad family is symmetric (it repeats in minor thirds — A, C, E♭, G♭ over C7), reframing diminished this way unlocks three concrete benefits he walks through in turn: (1) **triad flexibility** — any one of the four rotations can be used standalone to resolve a dominant chord to its I, or two can be combined, or the triads can be mixed back in with plain diminished-arpeggio vocabulary; (2) **one-idea-into-four-lines** — build a single 5-to-1 line off one triad, then transpose the *exact same line* down in minor thirds through all four rotations to get four distinct usable lines from one piece of work, and the same lines can be chained onto classic altered 5-to-1 jazz vocabulary via chromatic connectors; (3) **patterns** — a Mark Whitfield pattern built from just two of the four triads (C and G♭, a tritone apart) with an optional chromatic pickup note, which can also start from either of the other two triads (A, E♭) or be flipped to start on a different one for variety. He closes by noting the whole system also applies over an actual diminished chord (not just as a dominant substitute) — the only change is which note anchors the bass: think off the chord's natural-7 (i.e. a half step below the stated diminished root) rather than off the stated root itself, since the diminished chord and the dominant 7♭9 it substitutes for share the identical triad family. The video ends with a short one-chorus blues etude combining everything taught (not independently extractable from the transcript — captions don't carry the played audio) and a Patreon plug for tabs/notation, which are paywalled and not available here.

## What's taught
- **The core reframe: diminished-over-dominant = a major triad a minor 3rd above the dominant chord's root, not the diminished arpeggio/chord itself.** Over C7, instead of the diminished arpeggio/chord (C-E♭-G♭-A, root-♭3-♭5-𝄫7), think **A major triad over a C bass** — named directly as the "13♭9 sound" (A over C gives the 13th (A), ♭9 (D♭/C♯ enharmonic), and 3rd (E) against the C7 chord). Credited as the way George Benson and Mark Whitfield think about the sound, and framed as opening up more options than the plain arpeggio/chord reading. → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords]]
- **Benefit 1 — triad flexibility.** The conventional approach plays a diminished arpeggio/chord off the dominant 7♭9's own chord tones for bebop-style lines. Reframed as triads, the same symmetric family gives **four usable major triads a minor 3rd apart** (worked from A: A, C, E♭, G♭, over C7) — any single one can be used to resolve the dominant chord into its I chord (F major here, since C7 is the V of F); **two can be combined** (worked example: A and G♭ together); and the triads can be **mixed back in with the plain diminished arpeggio** vocabulary rather than treated as a full replacement for it. → [[Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords]] § the altered-dominant diminished-scale derivation (the same four-triad-family mechanism, documented there from other videos)
- **Benefit 2 — one line, transposed in minor thirds, becomes four lines.** Build a single line over the A-triad-over-C7-to-F-major resolution: add a **chromatic approach note between the E of the A triad and the 5th (C) of the F major target chord**, producing a usable 5-to-1 line. Because the whole triad family moves in minor thirds, **the identical line shape transposes down through all four rotations** — A → G♭ → E♭ → C — turning one worked-out line into four distinct usable lines with no new construction work, just the transposition. **Chaining onto existing vocabulary:** the same triad-derived lines can be linked to **classic altered 5-to-1 jazz language** already known — worked both linking the C-triad line and the A-triad line to a stock altered 5-to-1 lick via connecting chromaticism, framed explicitly as "finding ways to link these triads... to jazz language that I already know" rather than inventing new vocabulary from scratch. → [[Triad Melodic Devices for Jazz Soloing]] (the general triad-as-melodic-raw-material approach this line-transposition device fits inside), [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]
- **Benefit 3 — patterns, credited to Mark Whitfield.** A pattern built from **just two of the four triads — the C triad and the G♭ triad (a tritone apart, i.e. two of the four minor-third rotations) — over a C7 chord**. Whitfield's variation: add a **chromatic note before the pattern's first note** to vary the sound. The same two-triad pattern shape can also be built from the **other two rotations left out (A and E♭)** instead of C/G♭, and the **starting triad within a pair can be flipped** (start on G♭ or E♭ instead of C or A) for further variation. As with the lines in Benefit 2, these patterns combine with the same jazz-language chromatic-connector approach for full worked examples. → [[Triad Pairs]] (a different two-triad-combination mechanism — melodic-minor scale-adjacent triads rather than this video's minor-third diminished-family triads — worth comparing, not the same derivation)
- **The same system over an actual diminished chord (not a dominant substitute) — the only change is which note anchors the bass.** All the triad/line/pattern material above works unchanged over a real diminished chord too, but instead of anchoring off the chord's stated root, **think off the natural-7 note in the bass** — i.e. a half step *below* the diminished chord's stated root (worked example given: material built for "C dominant" applies unchanged over "D♭ diminished," since D♭'s natural-7 is C). This is the same "diminished chord = rootless dominant 7♭9 a major 3rd above" relationship already documented on [[Diminished Chords]], restated here as a practical bass-note rule for reusing the same triad vocabulary over either chord type. → [[Diminished Chords]]
- **Framing note: the video's own name for this whole system is "the 13♭9 sound."** Stated twice (opening and closing) as the single unifying label for all three benefits above — useful as the searchable term for this specific triad-over-dominant device going forward, distinct from the plain "diminished arpeggio" label the video explicitly contrasts it against.
