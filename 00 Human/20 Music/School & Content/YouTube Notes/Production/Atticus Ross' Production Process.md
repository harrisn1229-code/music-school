# Atticus Ross' Production Process

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cDIGsVWtnFY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cDIGsVWtnFY)

## Synopsis

A short (9:25), interview-style piece with Atticus Ross — Grammy/Oscar-winning composer and producer, best known for his work with Trent Reznor (Nine Inch Nails, How to Destroy Angels, and their shared film-scoring credits) — filmed by Mix with the Masters while breaking down one track. There's no plugin walkthrough or on-screen settings here; this is production **philosophy** first, with a handful of concrete gear/technique mentions embedded in it. Ross opens with a strong anti-over-production stance: he dislikes heavy tuning and the "perfectly gridded" 90s radio-rock sound, where drums are quantized dead-in-time, guitar glitches are cleaned up, and vocals are pitch-locked to a grid — to him that process "sucks the life" out of music, because imperfections carry as much weight as the correct notes. His stated relationship to the computer mirrors Hans Zimmer's from the companion video on this channel ([[Hans Zimmer's use of computers and samples in orchestral music]]): the computer is "an instrument," not a correction tool, and the workflow (Atari sequencer → tape → sample back from tape, historically; now DAW-based, same principle) hasn't changed in spirit even though the tape machine is gone. The track under discussion is built from deliberately disparate elements meant to cohere as an "organic whole without force" — a David Bowie-adjacent harmony (a direct tribute, since Bowie was a mutual favorite of both Ross and Reznor), a marimba with light effects sitting low in the verses (a small, unglamorous "puzzle piece" rather than a lead part — a structural idea Ross traces to Talking Heads' *Remain in Light*), and a rare, out-of-production sampling pedal Ross calls a "teaspoon" (proper-noun ID uncertain — see the transcript note) used to glitch/freeze a word of Trent Reznor's lead vocal on the way into a breakdown. A Roland-style Space Echo runs "all over" that same vocal for its tape-echo color. The one clearly reusable production idea buried in the philosophy: Ross frames varying the *processing* of a part — not just writing a new part — as its own dynamics tool, citing the vocal and a sax line both getting pushed harder into the same "teaspoon" glitch treatment specifically at a structural turn (into the breakdown/end section) rather than throughout.

## Techniques

- **[00:00–01:30] Anti-over-production stance, stated as a working philosophy, not a specific setting.** Dislikes "lots of tuning" and describes a 90s K-Rock production-line sound where a band's guitar/amp choice was dictated by what "K-Rock likes," then drums were snapped perfectly in time, guitar glitches cleaned up, and vocal pitch-aligned to a grid — "every essence of any soul would be sucked out of the music." Direct parallel to the existing "when not to tune" case already logged on [[Vocal Tuning]].
- **[00:00–01:30] The computer as an instrument, not a correction tool — historical throughline from an Atari-based sequencer + tape machine (program beats on the Atari, record to tape, sample back from tape) to a modern DAW setup, framed as the same working principle with the tape swapped for direct-to-computer recording.** "The imperfections are as important as the things that are correct."
