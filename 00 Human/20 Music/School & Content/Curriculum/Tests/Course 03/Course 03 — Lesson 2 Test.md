> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **Derivative rotates a parent scale; parallel alters major or minor from the same root**
> Two routes to the same seven notes.
>
> **Q2.** **Derivative is fast but anchors the ear to the parent; parallel is slower but shows the note**
> Parallel also keeps the mode's own tonic as home, which is the thing that matters when you play.
>
> **Q3.** **Parallel — it works from the root you're on, with no parent scale to find first**
> Derivative requires knowing which parent scale contains your target, which is an extra step and gets slow in unfamiliar keys.
>
> **Q4.** **Lydian ♯4 · Mixolydian ♭7 · Dorian ♮6 · Phrygian ♭2 · Locrian ♭5**
> Lydian and Mixolydian are measured against major; Dorian, Phrygian and Locrian against minor. Locrian is the one exception to one-note-per-mode: against natural minor it carries **both a ♭2 and a ♭5**, and the ♭5 is the one that names it.
>
> **Q5.** **Ionian and Aeolian — they *are* the baselines**
> Ionian is the major scale and Aeolian is natural minor, so there's nothing to alter.
>
> **Q6.** D Dorian: D E F G A B C
> D natural minor with the 6th raised — B♮ instead of B♭.
>
> **Q7.** F Lydian: F G A B C D E
> F major with the 4th raised — B♮ instead of B♭.
>
> **Q8.** E Phrygian: E F G A B C D · G Mixolydian: G A B C D E F
> E minor with a ♭2 gives F♮. G major with a ♭7 gives F♮.
>
> **Q9.** **Its tonic triad is diminished, so there's nothing stable to come home to**
> The ♭5 destabilises the one chord a key needs to be able to rest on.
>
> **Q10.** **It's D minor — a mode doesn't exist until its characteristic note sounds**
> *Rubric (5):* identifies it as D natural minor / Aeolian (2) · says the ♮6 is what distinguishes Dorian from minor (1) · says a mode is defined by the sounding of its characteristic note, not by the label or the scale you had in mind (2)
> D Dorian and D natural minor differ by exactly one note: the 6th. If B never sounds, every note played is also in D minor, so nothing in the music distinguishes the two and the listener hears plain minor. The general lesson is that a mode is a property of what's audible rather than of what you intended — the characteristic note isn't a detail of the scale, it's the entire evidence for it.
>
> **Q11.** **They stop needing a parent scale, and start hearing the mode against a baseline they already know**
> *Rubric (5):* says the derivative view requires locating a parent scale first (1) · says the parallel view works directly from the root you're playing on (2) · says it foregrounds the one altered note, which is the note that has to sound (2)
> Thinking "white notes from D" means the reference point is C major, so the ear keeps resolving toward C and every new mode requires finding a different parent first. Thinking "D minor with a raised 6th" keeps D as home and reduces the whole mode to one alteration against something already fluent. That matters twice over: it makes spelling possible on any root without hunting for a parent, and it puts the characteristic note — the one that has to actually sound for the mode to exist — at the front of your attention instead of buried in a collection.
