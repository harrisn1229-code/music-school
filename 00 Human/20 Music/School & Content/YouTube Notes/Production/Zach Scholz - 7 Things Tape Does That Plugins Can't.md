# Zach Scholz - 7 Things Tape Does That Plugins Can't

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UCN09lA7TW0)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UCN09lA7TW0)

## Synopsis

Zach Scholz pushes back on the idea that tape is only a "warmth" plugin substitute, walking through seven creative uses that go well beyond coloration: bus glue, preamp/transformer character (including deliberately misusing a mic input for fuzz), physical tape delay, tape flanging, sampling off tape, deliberate saturation/clipping, and — his stated favorite — the workflow effect of tape's limitations forcing creative commitment. The throughline is that tape's *mechanical* behavior (a physical gap between record and playback heads, two signals drifting out of phase, degradation compounding on each pass) produces effects a plugin can approximate sonically but can't replicate as a physical process, and that the slower, more deliberate workflow tape imposes is itself a creative tool, not just a coloration one.

## Techniques

- **1. Bus to tape for glue and texture, not just EQ/comp busing** — busing multiple tracks together digitally works, but sending that bus through a physical tape machine adds "a really cool texture and glue" beyond what digital bus processing alone gets you — this is the same principle behind Nate's existing [[Master Chain]] tape-finish stage and [[3 Guitar Bus|Guitar Bus]]/[[3 Bass Bus]] tape stages, all built on [[Kramer Tape]]. → [[Saturation and Distortion]]
- **2. Tape machines aren't just "tape" — the preamps and transformers add their own character** — running mics or instruments through a tape machine's input stage sounds "totally different" from an interface simply because of what's in front of the tape itself; this validates why Nate's [[Sony TC-630]] note frames it as a full tape-color processor (wow/flutter + head-bump warmth + tape compression), not just a "make it sound like tape" plugin stand-in.
- **2b. "Wrong way" mic-input guitar/bass fuzz** — deliberately plugging a guitar or bass into a tape machine's mic input (expecting a low-level signal) instead of its line input overdrives it into analog fuzz, since the instrument's pickup output is much hotter than the input expects. Directly parallels the exact same trick on Zach's TASCAM M-30 mixer (see [[Zach Scholz - Why I Added a 1980s Analog Mixer to My Modern Studio]]) — a "feed it more than it expects" move that generalizes beyond just tape. → [[BASS Slot]]
- **3. Real tape delay comes from the physical gap between the record head and playback head** — as tape moves through the machine it hits the record head, then a fraction of a second later reaches the playback head; that physical distance IS the delay time, and changing tape speed changes the delay time. Feed a mixer's effect send to the tape machine and return the playback as your delay; route that return back into the tape machine again and you get a self-degrading feedback loop — "every time it prints to tape, the signal is getting warmer, it's getting more degraded." Impractical day-to-day (tape has to physically run the whole time) but he calls it worth trying at least once. This is exactly the mechanism [[Kramer Tape]]'s own Delay Time/Feedback controls emulate (its "repro-head delay," not a bolted-on digital delay) — Nate's existing plugin note already documents reaching for that before loading [[EchoBoy]] on a source already going through tape.
- **4. Tape flanging — two near-identical signals drifting in and out of time** — classic flanging is two identical signals where one drifts in and out of phase with the other, producing the "jet airplane" sweep; tape can do this by printing a signal to tape and combining the tape return with the original digital signal in the computer, letting the tape's natural speed drift (vs. the perfectly steady digital signal) create the flange. **Physically touching the reel while it plays lets you steer/predict where the flange lands** — a hands-on performance technique a plugin flanger can't offer. → [[Modulation]]
- **5. Sample off tape — print basic sounds to tape, then load the tape's OUTPUT into a sampler** — once loaded into a sampler, playing different notes pitches the sample and changes its apparent length, and the tape hiss itself starts to read as texture/effect rather than noise. **Doubling down: record to tape at a higher speed, then play back at a lower speed** for extra character on the resulting sample. → [[TEXTURE Slot]], [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **6. Deliberate tape saturation/clipping reads completely differently from digital clipping** — he calls this an "absolute essential" use, especially satisfying on drums; the harmonic/distortion character of driving tape into clipping is qualitatively different from a digital clipper hitting the same ceiling. → [[TRANSIENT Slot]], [[Saturation and Distortion]]
- **7. The real value: tape's limitations force creative commitment** — recording to tape means routing/patching audio, setting levels (sometimes at different speeds), then re-recording it back into the computer and listening — all of which takes real time. That forced pause "clears your head" in a way an all-digital workflow (everything stacking instantly on top of itself) doesn't, and he explicitly ties this to feeling "more satisfied" with the mix in his companion video [[Zach Scholz - I Tried Recording a Song Like It's 1976]], where the same limitations-as-creativity idea was the whole premise.
