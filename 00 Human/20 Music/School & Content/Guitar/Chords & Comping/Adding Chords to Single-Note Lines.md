# Adding Chords to Single-Note Lines

Part of [[Jazz Harmony]] and the guitar side of [[Chord Voicings]] — a structured method for punctuating an improvised single-note solo line with a chord voicing, so a solo line reads as harmonically self-supporting rather than bare. Distinct from [[Melodic-Harmonic Blending (Comping While Soloing)]]: that page's method is a **continuous, in-the-moment read** of how much harmony a phrase needs, blending melody and chord into one texture; this page's method is **narrower and more mechanical** — chords land specifically at phrase endings / target notes, worked as a drill on a single static chord before it's ever applied to a moving progression. Treat this as the earlier-stage, more structured on-ramp; Melodic-Harmonic Blending is the more advanced, freer version of a related idea. Complements [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] (which drills outlining a moving progression) by drilling the opposite half — stopping the line and stating the harmony directly.

## Why add chords to a solo line at all
Three distinct payoffs, all from the same move:
- **Clarifies vague/extension-heavy playing.** A line built mostly from upper extensions can sound ambiguous against the underlying chord on its own; stacking the underlying chord tones under it (as a voicing) makes clear "this vague-sounding line really is meant to be heard against *this* chord," without changing the line itself.
- **Fills space between phrases.** Whenever there's a break in an improvised line, a chord placed in the gap fills the silence and can add a sense of groove/rhythm — an alternative to just sustaining or resting.
- **Emphasizes a specific note.** Adding a chord under one melody note makes that note louder simply because more notes are sounding at once — a way to make a note "stand out" without changing dynamics or technique.

## Where to add the chord: at target notes, after phrase endings
- **The easiest, most natural placement is the end of a melodic idea** — wherever a line lands on a sustained note, that's where a chord goes; it clarifies the melodic idea harmonically right as it resolves.
- **This requires thinking ahead, not reacting** — you have to know your line's target note *before* you get there in order to have the chord voicing ready in time. Larsen frames target-note work (a separate practice skill) as the prerequisite: without a known target note, you can't pre-plan where the chord lands.
- **Landing the chord right *after* the target note, not exactly on it, is usually the more reliable option.** Larsen's own habit, and the reason for his second exercise below: hitting the melody note and the chord simultaneously (effectively strumming) makes it hard to keep the melody note clearly on top/audible — landing the chord a beat after protects the melody note's emphasis, at the cost of a very slightly delayed harmonic confirmation.
- Once phrase-ending chords are solid, the same idea extends past "chord only at the end" — chords can open a phrase, or be woven in as a recurring second layer throughout the line, not just as a closing punctuation mark.

## The two core exercises
1. **Exercise 1 — harmonize the scale, chord landing directly on the note.** Take a single-octave-plus stretch of the mode over the one chord you're drilling (Larsen used A Dorian — the G major scale over A minor 7, i.e. A minor treated as the ii of G major — worked in the open/general area between roughly the 2nd and 8th frets, deliberately not boxed into one fixed position) and add a small chord voicing directly under each scale degree, moving up the register. **Use fewer notes low, more notes high** — the low strings don't sit well under a full voicing (nothing rings under them, and it gets muddy fast, plus the lowest notes want to function as the root rather than be harmonized) — an interval or three-note voicing is enough down low; add notes as you climb. **Priority while doing this: keep the melody note clearly audible on top** — this is the hard part, since striking melody-note-plus-chord together functions like a strum; record yourself to check whether the top note is actually cutting through, and be willing to swap to a different voicing of the same target note if the first choice buries the melody.
2. **Exercise 2 — same drill, chord landing right *after* the note instead of on it.** Identical to Exercise 1 except the chord is delayed to just after the target note rather than struck simultaneously with it — directly addresses the melody-gets-buried problem in Exercise 1 by removing the simultaneity, and matches what Larsen says he actually does most often in his own playing.

## Voicing-building notes
- **Keep voicings small on purpose** — small voicings are faster to grab mid-line and more flexible to move around than full chord-shape voicings, which matters because you're building/finding them in real time while still holding a line together.
- **The same set of notes needs multiple fingerings, chosen by what comes right before it** — because voicings are kept small and mobile rather than tied to one fixed shape, the same target chord will need different fingerings depending on where the hand is coming from in the preceding line; build a small library of fingerings per target rather than one fixed shape per chord.
- **Two named voicing colors from the video** (both worked over A minor 7 / A Dorian, so treat the *relationship*, not the exact grip, as the transferable part): a **shell voicing** (root + 3rd + 7th — see [[Chord Voicings]] §*Which notes actually matter*) paired with an E minor pentatonic melodic fragment resolving to an **A minor 13** voicing; and a genuinely useful reusable trick — **an A minor 11 voicing followed by harmonizing the melody's B (the 9th) with a C major triad shape** ("using this C major voicing for the A minor sound") — Larsen calls this his most-used voicing here, describing it as having "a nice dark sound." *(The C-major-triad-over-A-minor relationship is itself just [[Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords|an upper-structure triad]] — C major triad = the 3rd/5th/7th/9th of Am9 — worth naming explicitly since Larsen doesn't use that term on screen.)*
- **A denser, "second voice" approach for busier lines:** harmonize the first note of a phrase with a full triad, then, rather than reserving chords only for phrase-ends, add a chord after *each* melody note through a rhythmic pattern, functioning as a genuine second contrapuntal voice under the line rather than an occasional punctuation — with an accent chord reserved for the final note of the phrase. This is the most Melodic-Harmonic-Blending-adjacent of the three worked examples — see that page for the fully blended, no-longer-alternating version of this same idea.

## Practice progression (as taught)
1. Drill Exercise 1 and 2 over a single held mode/chord (not yet a moving progression) until you can harmonize essentially any note in the practiced register from memory.
2. Move to improvising simple lines and only *then* practicing landing/placing chords at the phrase endings — start **rubato** (out of time) before trying to lock the chord placement into strict time.
3. Only after that, start varying *where* in the phrase the chord lands (beginning, woven throughout) rather than only at the end.
