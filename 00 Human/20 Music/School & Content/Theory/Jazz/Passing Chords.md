# Passing Chords

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Jazz Harmony]]. A **passing chord** connects two chords that are already part of the progression, filling the gap between them with motion instead of silence. The vault already documents several individual passing-chord devices scattered across [[Diminished Chords]], [[Chromatic Bass Lines]], [[Secondary Dominants]] and [[Chord Substitution]] — this page is the taxonomy that ties them together and answers "which kind do I reach for here."

**The one thing all four types share:** a passing chord is usually *optional decoration*, not a required part of the chart — the band doesn't need to know it's coming, and the bass player can keep walking the original changes underneath it and it still works. The exception is a chromatic passing chord written explicitly into a progression's own harmony (e.g. a i–♭iii°–V) — those DO need everyone to play them, since they change the actual chord being outlined, not just the comping.

## 1. Chromatic passing chords (dominant diminished)

A diminished 7th a half step below the target, functioning as that target's dominant. **C♯dim7 → Dm7** works because C♯dim7 sounds like a rootless **A7** (A7's 3rd–5th–♭7–♭9 traces a diminished-7 shape — the same "7♭9 = rootless dim7 + root a major 3rd below" relationship already on [[Diminished Chords]]), and A7 is Dm7's own V7.

- **Resolves up a half step**, always — that's the simplest way to think about it: whichever note you're tracking moves up a semitone into the target.
- **The payoff is the chromatic bass line it creates**: C → C♯ → D. → [[Chromatic Bass Lines]]
- Because it changes the bass motion of the actual progression, this type is usually written INTO the chart, not left as optional comping decoration — everyone in the band needs to play it.
- Also usable as a whole embellishing device stacked through a **3-6-2-5-1** as an intro.
- **Same-root diminished neighbor tone — a distinct variant, embellishing a STATIC chord instead of approaching the NEXT one.** Rather than building the diminished a half step below the upcoming target, build it on the current chord's own root and resolve straight back to that same chord — works over both a major and a dominant chord: **Cmaj7 → Cdim → Cmaj7**, or **G7 → Gdim → G7** (functions like the chord's own suspension, pure color/movement, no chord change actually happening). → [[Diminished Chords]] — Jens Larsen, [[Passing Chords And How To Sound Amazing With Them]]

## 2. Chromatic passing/approach chords (comping embellishment)

Slide any chord shape a fret up or down from the target and it becomes a passing chord into it — a comping-only device, not written into the chart.

- **Think about the target, not the passing chord.** The practical trick: decide which chord you're going *to*, then just slide your hand up or down a fret to approach it — you don't need to name the passing chord to use it.
- Worked turnaround example (Cmaj7 → A7 → Dm7 → G7 → Cmaj7): **G♯7** slides up to **A7**; **A♭7** slides down to **G7** (same pitches as G♯7, spelled differently because one resolves up and the other down); **D♭6** slides down to **Cmaj7**.
- **Counter-movement variant:** on the D♭6 → Cmaj7 move, the melody goes UP a half step (B♭ → B) while the rest of the chord moves DOWN — melody and harmony pulling opposite directions at the same instant. Worth deliberately voicing for.
- **Neighbor-tone variant (resolves back to where it came from):** a passing chord doesn't have to move on to the next chord in the progression — it can decorate a single chord and resolve back into itself, e.g. **G7 → F♯7 → G7** (chord moves down then back up, sitting under a melody that moves the other direction). Works best with a bit more space per chord — cramped rhythmic real estate makes the round-trip feel rushed.
- Great for harmonizing bebop melodies. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]]
- **Taught as one deliberate step in a full solo-guitar chord-melody build order, not a standalone lick:** after a melody is locked in time and its phrase-gaps are already filled with roots-then-chords, slide that same chord voicing a half step (or two half steps, for a longer chromatic run-up) toward the target as the next layer added — demonstrated over a C major voicing in "All of Me"'s opening. The ordering point is the reusable part: passing-chord chromaticism gets added only after the plain roots-then-chords layer is already solid in time, not before. → [[Chord Melody Arranging]], 🎵 [[🎵 NATHAN BORTON - All of Me|All of Me]] — Nathan Borton, [[THIS Simple Process Creates Successful Solo Guitar Playing]]
- **Melody-first version of the same device: find the chromatic note already sitting in your melody, and harmonize it with the SAME voicing you're about to resolve to, just slid one fret — the passing chord's exact harmonic label barely matters as long as the melody note resolves correctly.** Worked entirely over a G7 target: a 9th (A) walking chromatically down through A♭ to the root is harmonized on the A♭ with the destination G7 voicing itself slid down a fret (readable as A♭7 or a D7 altered — a tritone-sub relationship to G7, but explicitly called "not that important" which label fits); the same device run upward gives a passing chord roughly a half step above the target instead. A stated practical payoff beyond the sound: because the passing chord is usually the identical grip just moved a fret, fast eighth-note chordal lines become a slide instead of a hand reshape — a fingering shortcut for chord-solo/comping runs, not just decoration. Extended into three 2-5-1 chord-solo lines in C (Dm7 → G7[alt] → Cmaj7) using the identical logic — full lines on [[The ii-V-I]]. → [[Chord Voicings]] § *Voice leading is the actual point* (rule 3, "approach chromatically... slide the whole shape in") — Jens Larsen, [[Chromatics Chords - Part 1]]

## 3. Diatonic passing chords

The stepwise-bass cousin of type 2, using chords that stay inside the key instead of chromatic ones. **Core definition:** any chord already present in the parent scale's diatonic set (in C major: Cmaj7, Dm7, Em7, Fmaj7, G7, Am7, Bø7) can be inserted between two chords of the real progression to create stepwise connective motion — no theory beyond "is it in the scale" is required.

- **Stepwise diatonic bass, harmonized:** e.g. connecting C down to Am7 via a **G triad over a B bass note** (G/B) — the bass walks C → B → A while the chord stays diatonic.
- **Walking a ii up to its V:** instead of sitting still on Dm7 before G7, walk **Dm7 → Em7 → Fmaj7 → G7** — diatonic passing chords filling the space, melody kept moving independently of the chord roots for an extra layer of motion.
- Sound: often reads as gospel-flavored when stacked into a turnaround.
- Like type 2, these are usually optional embellishments, not written into the chart — and they still work even if the bass player just plays the original unembellished changes underneath.

### Full worked example: every transition of a 1-6-2-5 in C (Cmaj7 – Am7 – Dm7 – G7)

The same [[Turnarounds#The base form|I–vi–ii–V]] skeleton documented on [[Turnarounds]], embellished with diatonic passing chords at every step. **Rhythm placement rule that keeps it sounding like the original progression:** put the passing chord on the *weak* beat, keep the real progression chord on the *heavy* beat — the ear tracks the real harmony while the passing chord reads as color rather than a competing chord change.

- **Cmaj7 → Am7 (I→vi):** stepwise-between (Cmaj7 → Bø7 → Am7, the direct scale-tone in between — same B half-diminished this page already used above, here reached diatonically instead of chromatically); triad-inversion variant of the same slot (**G/B** in place of Bø7, brighter/more consonant); approach from below (C → G7 → Am7, borrowing a dominant move into a diatonic vi target); full walk (C → F → G → Am, skip down to F then step up through G).
- **Am7 → Dm7 (vi→ii):** chord-below approach (Am7 → Cmaj7 → Dm7 — Am7/Cmaj7 share enough tones that the insert "does no harm" and tends to sit under whatever the melody/solo is doing); full walk-up (Am7 → Bø7 → Cmaj7 → Dm7); approach from above (Am7 → Em7 → Dm7); extended walk-down adding one more diatonic step for a fuller descending line into Dm7.
- **Dm7 → G7 (ii→V):** whole-step-below approach (Dm7 → Fmaj7 → G7 — Fmaj7 functions almost like a "G sus" substitute since the two sound close); stepwise walk-up (Dm7 → Em7 → Fmaj7 → G7); **beat-displacement move (the distinctive one):** because Fmaj7 and G7 sound so similar, push G7 itself off the strong beat and put Fmaj7 on the downbeat instead, landing G7 right after — the passing chord temporarily takes over the harmonic-rhythm slot that "belongs" to the real V chord and it still reads as sensible; full stepwise walk-down mirrors the same idea the other direction.
- **G7 → Cmaj7 (V→I):** full walk-up through the ii-V (Dm7 → G7 → Am7 → Bø7 → Cmaj7) — reuses Am7/Bø7 as passing motion approaching the final I rather than resolving G7 directly; **specific rhythm placement:** because G7 and Bø7 sound so close (Bø7 functions almost like a rootless G9), put *both* on the heavy beats with Am7 slotted on the offbeat between them (on-beat G7 → off-beat Am7 → on-beat Bø7 → I); full stepwise walk-down (G → F → E → D → C), one passing chord per scale step.

**Practice method:** sit with a simple progression (a 2-5-1 or 1-6-2-5) and try inserting different diatonic passing chords by ear, testing each by **playing it as chords first** — the fastest way to check a substitution actually makes sense — before moving the same vocabulary into single-note improvised lines. **Where it earns its keep:** especially valuable comping in a **duo setting behind a horn player or singer**, where reharmonizing a static chord with inversions of itself rarely sounds convincing — more distinct chords via passing motion beats voicing-shuffling the same chord. → [[Turnarounds]], [[The ii-V-I]] — Jens Larsen, [[Jens Larsen - How To Use Diatonic Passing Chords With Easy Practical Examples|How To Use Diatonic Passing Chords With Easy Practical Examples]]

## 4. Dominant passing chords

Use a functioning dominant — the strongest resolution in harmony — as connective tissue between two chords, exactly like a [[Secondary Dominants|secondary dominant]] but framed as decorative/optional rather than a fixed part of the progression.

Two variants, both resolving into the same target:

- **Regular 5–1** — a plain secondary dominant a 5th above the target.
- **Tritone substitute** — the same dominant, transposed a tritone, so it resolves down a half step into the target instead of down a 5th. → [[Chord Substitution]] § *Tritone substitution*

Worked turnaround (targeting a `... A7 – G7 – Cmaj7`-shaped skeleton): an **all-tritone-sub** pass (B♭7→A7, A♭7→G7, D♭7→Cmaj7) versus a **mixed** pass using plain secondary dominants where a tritone sub isn't wanted (E7→A7, D7→G7 — the D7 sits over a static melody note — then still closing on D♭7→Cmaj7, a tritone sub, since going straight V7 there sounds weaker). Underlying principle: **Barry Harris's sixth-diminished-scale system** is built on exactly this same tonic-chord-plus-dominant-diminished relationship, generalized into a full scale. → [[Diminished Chords]] § *Usage — passing chords*

## 5. Passing chords that hide inside their target

The least obvious family — chords that resolve into the target chord without ever fully leaving its harmony, so they read as an embellishment of ONE chord rather than a chord change at all.

- **Minor subdominant suspension.** After a ii–V, instead of resolving straight to I, insert the **iv minor** as a passing suspension: `Dm7 – G7 – Fm6/9 – Cmaj7/9` — the Fm6/9 delays the arrival and then "beautifully resolves" into the tonic extension. → [[Borrowed Chords]] (iv minor borrowed from the parallel minor)
- **Non-dominant (fully) diminished option**, in the same suspension slot as the minor subdominant above — swap in a plain diminished 7th instead of Fm6/9 for a different flavor of the same delay.
- **Dominant with the tonic already in the bass.** A dominant-quality chord voiced *over the target's own root* (e.g. a dominant-flavored stack over a C bass, resolving to C) — deliberately dissonant (tonic and dominant sounding at once) but the resolution lands harder for it. This is what Barry Harris means by "borrowing notes from the diminished chord": chord symbols for these voicings often don't make conventional sense (e.g. a "C6 with two borrowed notes") because the chord is built from diminished-scale logic, not stacked thirds. → [[Diminished Chords]] § *Usage — passing chords*
- **Sharp-4 diminished / "subdominant diminished."** A diminished chord rooted on the raised 4th degree, voiced with the tonic in the bass, arriving from the V: `G7 → F♯dim/C → Cmaj7`. Called out as an especially beautiful, underrated sound, common in standard melodies that have a major 7th in the tune. → [[Diminished Chords]]
  - **Variant: the same chord placed at the END of a full 2-5-1, suspending the I chord's own resolution instead of substituting for the V.** In F: `Gm7 – C7 – [B°/F] – Fmaj7` — delay landing on Fmaj7 by one beat/bar with the ♯IV° first, voiced like an E major triad resolving into Fmaj7. Many standards harmonize the identical sound as a real minor ii-V into the tonic instead (`Bø7 → E7♭9 → F` in this key) — written into **"If I Should Lose You"** (2-5 to B♭, then E diminished/B♭-bass before B♭maj7), **"I've Got You Under My Skin,"** and **"I Remember You"** (2nd bar). Also usable mid-solo as an improviser's variation over an existing chord, not just as composed harmony — needs alert bandmates, weak against a fixed backing track. → [[Diminished Chords]], [[The ii-V-I]] — Jens Larsen, [[No. IV diminished suspension of the I chord]]
  - **Triad-walkdown variant of the same ♯4-to-tonic idea, used as a closing device (not a single passing chord but a whole descending run of triads) with the melody note held static on top.** A traditional ♯IV walkdown starts on the raised 4th of the key and descends chromatically back to I; in a solo-guitar chord-melody ending, the melody note (the tonic, held on top throughout) stays put while **triads underneath walk down chromatically** to outline the same ♯IV-to-I motion — melody static, harmony moving, the mirror image of type-2's "counter-movement" bullet above. A **D♭6 passing chord** (matching this section's own type-2 `D♭6 → Cmaj7` chromatic-approach bullet) is dropped in right before the final dominant for a smoother transition, then resolves to a big open-voiced final tonic chord.
