# The Perfect Ear Training Routine

## Synopsis
Max Konyi lays out a complete, structured daily ear-training routine that touches all four core recognition skills in a single session, rather than drilling them in isolation. Where [[Scale Degree Ear Training]] and [[A Guided Tour of Musical Feeling]] teach *what* the feeling states are, this video is the practical *how-to-practice* companion — a repeatable sequence any session can follow, built around a chosen chord progression (own or borrowed) run through five escalating drills. This is the routine [[Transcribing and Playing by Ear]]'s "daily practice routine" section is built directly from.

## Techniques
- **Four core skills every complete routine needs to touch:** melodic scale-degree recognition, bass-note recognition, chord-quality recognition, and chord-number/position recognition. A routine that only drills one (e.g. only melodic intervals) leaves real gaps in transcription-ready hearing.
- **Drone practice — pure familiarity, not speed.** Sing or play long, sustained tones of each scale degree against a held drone tone; the point at this stage is simply building raw familiarity with each degree's feeling state, not testing recognition speed yet.
- **Melody transcription drill on a deliberately simple, familiar song** (a nursery rhyme is explicitly fine, even recommended, as a starting difficulty): sing along with the recording, then sing it from memory alone, find the tonic and work out the remaining degrees by feel, verify on an instrument, and alternate between listening and singing with the degrees held in mind until it "clicks" — essentially the same 5-step process as [[How to Play Any Melody by Ear - Step by Step Guide]] but framed as a repeatable daily drill rather than a one-off analysis.
- **Melodic improvisation with conscious degree-tracking.** Improvise over a drone or backing track in a known key, but stay consciously aware of which scale degree is being played at every single moment rather than free-noodling on feel alone — the point is building the subconscious sound↔label↔finger association during an activity that's otherwise fun and low-pressure, so the labeling work doesn't feel like a chore.
- **The chord/progression drill sequence, run in this exact order on one chosen progression** (a real song's progression or one you invent):
  1. **Bass notes only.** Play just the bass notes of the progression and sing along with them, holding the scale-degree numbers in mind the whole time — isolates bass-degree recognition from everything else.
  2. **Add full chords back in, name qualities out loud.** Keep singing the bass line while now also actively attending to and naming each chord's quality (major/minor/dominant/etc.) as it passes — layers chord-quality recognition on top of the already-solid bass line.
  3. **Quality-swap drill.** Keep every chord's scale-degree position exactly the same, but change ONE chord's quality (e.g. make a minor iii major) and compare directly against the original — isolates exactly what a quality change alone sounds like with every other variable held constant.
  4. **Position-swap drill (the inverse).** Keep a chord's quality the same but move it to a different scale-degree position (e.g. swap a minor chord that was sitting on position 3 for a different minor chord on position 2 or 6) — isolates what a position change alone sounds like, independent of quality.
  5. **Transpose the whole exercise to a new key and repeat it.** The explicit point: notice what stays constant across the transposition (the numbers, the relationships, the feelings) versus what changes (the actual letter-name pitches) — since the numbers/relationships are the real transferable skill being trained, not memorized pitches in one key.
- **Run the full sequence on a progression you already like**, not an arbitrary textbook one — using real material you're motivated by keeps the drilling connected to music you actually want to play, rather than feeling like abstract theory homework.
