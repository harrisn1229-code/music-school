> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **For the rest of that measure only — the bar line cancels it**
> And only on that exact line or space of the staff.
>
> **Q2.** **No — an accidental doesn't carry to the same letter in another octave**
> That's the half of the rule people miss. It applies to the exact line or space it was written on, and nowhere else.
>
> **Q3.** **A tie joins two notes of the same pitch · a slur joins different pitches**
> Attack the first note of a tie and hold through the second. A slur is an instruction to play legato.
>
> **Q4.** **A hammer-on or a pull-off**
> Legato on guitar means the second note isn't picked.
>
> **Q5.** **A dot adds half the note's own value — a dotted half is 3 beats**
>
> **Q6.** **3½ beats — 2 + 1 + ½**
> The second dot adds half of what the *first dot* added, not half the note again. It's not 4.
>
> **Q7.** **Play an octave higher than written · cancelled by *loco* or the end of the bracket · `8vb` below the staff**
> The dotted bracket running above the notes is part of the instruction, and where it stops, so does the transposition.
>
> **Q8.** **To keep ledger lines off the page**
> A passage three ledger lines up is far harder to read than the same passage written in the staff with an octave sign over it.
>
> **Q9.** **Back to the sign · play from there to the *To Coda* mark · then jump to the Coda**
> Three moves, in that order.
>
> **Q10.** **Going straight to the Coda — step 2 of the scan, the roadmap, is what catches it**
> Reading the navigation before playing a note is exactly what stops you playing into a route you hadn't traced.
>
> **Q11.** **It sounds an octave *lower* than written — effectively a permanent 8vb**
> Written pitch sits an octave above sounding pitch. Direction is the entire content of the fact, and the reverse is the common wrong answer.
>
> **Q12.** **Position, not ledger lines — the same written pitch exists in five or six places on the fretboard**
> Notation tells you what, not where. On a piano the mapping is one-to-one, so the decision never comes up at all.
>
> **Q13.** **The position system — a fixed four-fret window, one finger per fret**
> It makes the mapping unambiguous again, which is the whole reason Volume 1 stays in first position that long: it removes the *where* decision until the *what* is automatic.
>
> **Q14.** **Key and time signature · roadmap · range · rhythm hotspots · a holdable tempo**
> The tempo is the one you can hold through the hardest bar, not the easiest. Step 1 is the one everybody does and on its own it doesn't prevent the derailment. Steps 2 and 5 are what actually save the read.
>
> **Q15.** **The top number counts eighths — you feel two dotted-quarter beats**
> Six eighths written, two beats felt, and the felt half is the one you play from.
>
> **Q16.** **9/8 is three dotted-quarter beats · 12/8 is four dotted-quarter beats**
> Divide the top number by 3 and the real beat count appears.
>
> **Q17.** **Beat 1, then 3, then the backbeats, then the offbeats between them**
> That hierarchy is what makes a placement sound "on" or "off" at all — without it, nothing is early or late relative to anything.
>
> **Q18.** **Emphasis where the meter doesn't expect it**
> Three forms of the same move: accent a weak position, place a note on a weak position and tie it over the strong one, or rest where the arrival is expected.
>
> **Q19.** **The meter has to stay in the listener's head — otherwise it just sounds arbitrary**
> Remove the steady pulse and there's nothing left to play against.
>
> **Q20.** **Swing lengthens the first eighth of each pair and shortens the second**
> A shuffle is that triplet with the middle note removed. Long, short. Swing leans toward a triplet feel without necessarily reaching it.
>
> **Q21.** **Full triplet swing is exactly 2:1 — swing sits anywhere between straight (1:1) and that**
> The triplet itself is three *equal* parts; 2:1 is the long–short left when the middle one goes. That continuum is why swing amount is a percentage rather than an on-off switch.
>
> **Q22.** **Where a part sits relative to the grid, on purpose · late relaxed, early urgent · 5–30 ms**
> Consistently, not note by note. Past roughly 40 ms it stops reading as feel and starts reading as a mistake.
>
> **Q23.** **Elements disagreeing — kick and snare on the grid, hats behind**
> Everything equally late just sounds slow. Pocket is a relationship between parts, not an offset applied to a track.
>
> **Q24.** **Quantize snaps to the grid, humanize adds timing and velocity variation — 50 to 75% strength**
> Quantize is for anything meant to be machine-tight, humanize for anything meant to read as played. Neither belongs at 100%: partial strength pulls a performance toward the grid while keeping its feel.
>
> **Q25.** **The timing and velocity offsets of one piece of material**
> The payoff is lifting the feel off a break you like and applying it to your own stiff MIDI.
>
> **Q26.** **Polyrhythm stacks conflicting subdivisions · hemiola regroups the same pulse — "nice cup of tea"**
> Polyrhythm is classically three against two, two divisions running at once. Hemiola is three groups of two against two groups of three: the pulse is unchanged, but it's reorganised so a different meter is implied.
>
> **Q27.** **The backbeat moves to beat 3 instead of 2 and 4 — the groove feels half as fast at the same tempo**
> Written at 140, felt at about 70. One moved hit does that much because the ear judges groove speed almost entirely from snare placement.
>
> **Q28.** **Boom-bap ~85–95, backbeat on 2 and 4, 8th hats · trap ~130–145 half-time, snare on 3, 16th hats**
> Boom-bap's hats are straight. Boom-bap is swung and laid back with an offbeat open hat and sampled drums. Trap layers 32nd and triplet rolls over the 16th bed, with a tight kit and loose hats.
>
> **Q29.** **Divide 6/8's eighths into two groups of three, and three eighths is exactly a dotted quarter**
> *Rubric (5):* says a dot adds half the note's own value, so a dotted quarter equals three eighths (2) · says 6/8's six eighths group into two sets of three (2) · concludes that the felt beat is a dotted quarter because that's the note value covering one group (1)
> The two facts are one fact seen from either end. A quarter note is two eighths; adding a dot adds half of a quarter, which is one more eighth, so a dotted quarter is three eighths exactly. Compound meter groups its subdivisions in threes rather than twos, so a bar of 6/8 splits into two groups of three eighths — and the note value that spans one of those groups is precisely a dotted quarter. That's why compound meters can only be felt in dotted beats: there is no undotted note value that covers three eighths, so the beat has to be written with a dot or not written at all. The same arithmetic gives 9/8 three dotted-quarter beats and 12/8 four.
>
> **Q30.** **One changes which position the note occupies · the other keeps the position and changes when it arrives**
> *Rubric (5):* says syncopation moves a note to a different metric position, which the listener perceives as a rhythmic event (2) · says microtiming keeps the note on its position and shifts it by a few milliseconds, which is perceived as feel rather than as a different rhythm (2) · notes that one is notatable and the other largely isn't (1)
> Syncopation is a compositional decision and it changes what the rhythm *is*. Move a note from beat 3 onto the "and" of 2 and the listener hears a different pattern — it's notatable, countable, and it survives being played by someone else off the page. Microtiming doesn't change the pattern at all. A hat nudged 15 ms behind the grid is still on the same subdivision, and a transcription of the part would be identical before and after. What changed is how it feels: relaxed rather than urgent, sitting back rather than driving. That's why the two live in different places in the workflow — syncopation is written, and pocket is performed or dialled in afterward. It's also why the sizes aren't comparable: 15 ms is a fraction of any subdivision, and past about 40 ms it stops being feel and starts being heard as a wrong placement.
>
> **Q31.** **Tuplets, syncopation, dotted figures — anything you'd have to count rather than read**
> *Rubric (5):* names tuplets, syncopated figures and dotted rhythms as the specific hazards (2) · says the point is to count them silently before playing rather than meeting them at tempo (2) · explains that these are the figures where the eye can't supply the rhythm automatically, so they cause the stall (1)
> The hazard isn't difficulty in the abstract, it's the gap between recognising a figure and being able to play it without thinking. Straight eighths get read; a triplet against a dotted figure gets counted, and counting takes time you don't have once the bar arrives. So the scan is looking for exactly the places where that will happen: tuplets of any kind, ties across the beat, syncopation where the attack lands on a weak position, and dotted figures where the arithmetic isn't instant. Each one gets counted silently while you still have the thirty seconds. Compound meter deserves the same treatment — a 6/8 bar read in six instead of two is a slower read than it needs to be. The point of finding them in advance is that step 5 depends on it: you can't pick a tempo you can hold through the hardest bar until you know which bar that is.
>
> **Q32.** **The bed is 16ths — the 32nds and triplets are rolls layered on top**
> *Rubric (5):* says the underlying hat pattern is 16ths (2) · says the 32nds and triplet figures are rolls placed over that bed rather than the pattern itself (2) · notes that the shorthand describes the ornament and misses the structure (1)
> Calling trap hats triplets describes what stands out and not what's there. The constant, unglamorous layer is a 16th-note bed running through the bar, and it's what gives the groove its density and its speed at a tempo the vocal can sit over. The 32nds and the triplet figures are rolls — short bursts placed deliberately, usually at the end of a bar or into a transition — and they read as the defining feature precisely because they're occasional. Get the structure backwards and you program a bar of continuous triplets, which is busy in the wrong way and has no bed underneath it. The same misreading explains why people find trap hats hard to program convincingly: the interest is in where the rolls go against a steady layer, not in the rolls themselves.
>
> **Q33.** **Because the ear reads groove speed almost entirely from where the snare lands**
> *Rubric (5):* says the backbeat moves from 2 and 4 to beat 3 alone (2) · says the snare is the reference the ear uses to judge groove speed, so halving the backbeat rate halves the perceived tempo (2) · connects it to the strong/weak hierarchy, where the backbeat is a structural accent rather than just another hit (1)
> The hierarchy explains why one hit can carry that much weight. Beat 1 is the strongest position and the backbeats are the next structural accents, which means the ear treats the snare not as one more drum but as the marker telling it where the pattern's frame is. With a snare on 2 and 4 there are two of those markers per bar; with a snare on 3 there is one. The pulse hasn't changed, the hats are still running at whatever rate they were, and the tempo in the DAW is identical — but the frame the ear is measuring has doubled in length, and perceived speed follows the frame. Which is the whole trick behind trap: written fast enough for busy 16th hats and their rolls, felt slow enough to rap over, at the same time and from one moved hit.
>
> **Q34.** **Both say the pulse is the thing that must not stop — everything else is negotiable against it**
> *Rubric (5):* says the reading rule keeps the pulse by dropping notes, thinning chords or playing only downbeats rather than stopping (2) · says syncopation only means anything while the meter is still being felt (2) · states the shared principle: the pulse is the reference everything else is heard against, so losing it costs more than any individual note (1)
> Both rules are about what a listener is actually tracking. On the reading side, the instruction when you fall behind is to keep going — drop notes, thin the chord, play only the downbeats, rejoin at the next one — and never to stop or go back. That's not a lowered standard, it's a recognition that continuity is what the exercise trains and what a listener notices; a missing note is a detail, a broken pulse is the performance failing. On the rhythm side, the same fact appears as a condition on syncopation: displaced accents are only heard as displaced while the meter is still present in the listener's head, and without it they're arbitrary. Both amount to saying the pulse is the reference frame rather than one element among many. Everything expressive — a syncopation, a laid-back hat, a dropped note in a hard bar — is measured against it, which is why it's the one thing you protect.
>
> **Q35.** **They're the same device — the guitar's octave transposition is what lets guitar parts fit in treble clef**
> Without it, guitar music would sit low enough to need constant ledger lines or a permanent octave sign. Writing it an octave up is that fix made permanent and built into the instrument's convention.
