# Keyscape — Wurlitzer 140B & 200A

**Instrument:** [[Keyscape]] · **Slot:** [[BED Slot]] · **Used by:** [[1 Electric Piano Track|Electric Piano Track]]

## What it's for
140B for a rounder, more "bitey"/acoustic-feeling Wurli tone predating the 200 series; 200A for the classic pop/rock Wurli — the Supertramp/Queen lane. Reach for either when the part wants a reedier, more aggressive alternative to a Rhodes in the same BED slot.

**Correction (2026-08-30, per a Wurlitzer/Rhodes tech historian's direct A/B comparison):** the **140B is solid-state, not tube** — real vintage tube Wurlitzers are the earlier 120/145 series that predate the 140B, not the 140B itself. Both the 140B and 200/200A share the same solid-state pickup technology but sound genuinely different from each other due to construction differences (the 140B reads with more "bite" and a feel closer to an acoustic piano's action). The true tube-era sound — the specific character heard on Ray Charles' "What'd I Say" — comes from the 120/145, has noticeably less sustain than the solid-state models, and is notoriously inconsistent instrument-to-instrument (reed quality varies enough that techs spot-check individual reeds) — not modeled by this Keyscape patch, which only covers 140B/200A. → [[Vintage Gear Worklist]] for the unmodeled 120/145 tube character.

## Settings
- ⑥ Patch: 140B (solid-state, rounder/more acoustic-feeling action) or 200A (the bright classic pop/rock voicing)
- ② Custom controls page: Drive is the pop/rock bite on Wurli patches — push it for the 200A's signature bark, keep it lighter on the 140B for a rounder tone
- ⑤ Drive/EQ knob: additional modeled drive stage before any Ableton saturation — stack the two only if the part needs real grit, not by default
- ③ Tremolo: OFF if [[PanMan]] is doing the movement downstream
- ⑧ Internal FX: reverb OFF always

## In context
- Same BED-slot role as [[Keyscape — Rhodes LA Custom E & Mark I]]; pick Wurli over Rhodes for a more aggressive/reedy comping tone (Supertramp-style) rather than the smoother neo-soul lane.

## From videos

- **Real Wurlitzer engineering context this digital patch doesn't need to worry about, but worth knowing for authenticity**: the 200 (non-A) and 200A differ mainly in amp construction — 200A moved the preamp board to reduce noise, but a "vintage" noise floor is more a symptom of an aging/dying amp than inherent tone character; a tech recommends replacing dead/hissy amps (Vintage Vibe and similar) rather than treating hiss as part of the sound. **Not every Wurlitzer has an effects loop that returns through its internal speakers** (unlike a Rhodes) — plugging a pedal into some units' aux out just sends to the recording desk, bypassing the onboard speakers entirely; a custom board was needed on one unit specifically to route pedal effects back through the internal speaker cabinet with correct impedance (no need to pad the pedal's input level down). **A parallel dry/wet miking-adjacent technique, borrowed from how guitarists reamp**: mult the Wurlitzer's line out BEFORE the effects loop, so you keep a clean, full-transient/full-fidelity dry signal alongside the effected one — since once an effect (e.g. a phaser) is printed onto the signal it can't be un-effected later, preserving the dry copy is the same logic as a guitarist keeping a DI for later reamping. **A deliberate low-end-shaping mic'ing choice**: engineers who close-mic a Wurlitzer's small internal speakers (described as literally car-door-speaker-sized) specifically get less deep low end than the line/DI signal — a way to keep the part from conflicting with a bass part in the mix, achieved by mic choice/placement rather than an EQ move after the fact. A kill switch on the internal speakers lets you record purely DI without speaker bleed when that's preferred instead. → Anthony Marinelli Music (feat. Ben Bove/retrorentals.net), [[Wurlitzer Electric Piano Sound Comparison - 120 vs 140 140B 200]]
