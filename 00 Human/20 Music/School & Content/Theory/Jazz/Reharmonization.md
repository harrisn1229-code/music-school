# Reharmonization

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Jazz Harmony]]. Keeping a melody and rewriting the chords underneath it.

This is the practical payoff of the whole strand, and it's the technique that turns a plain loop into something that sounds composed. It's also the fastest way to get a second section out of material you already have.

## The one constraint

> **The melody note must be a usable tone of the new chord.**

Usable means a chord tone (1, 3, 5, 7) or a functioning extension (9, 11, 13, or an altered version of those). What it must not be is a note the new chord actively contradicts — a natural 9 over a ♭9 chord, or a natural 4 sitting on the 3rd.

Everything else is negotiable. Function, bass motion, density — all of it can change, as long as the tune still fits.

**This is why you reharmonize melody-first.** Write out the melody notes, decide what each one could be *relative to* a new chord, and choose from there. Choosing chords first and hoping the melody survives is how you get the clashes.

---

## The techniques, roughly in order of cost

### 1. Change the chord's function under a held note

The cheapest move. A melody sitting on **C** can be harmonized as:

| Chord | C becomes its… | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Cmaj7 | root | Neutral, home |
| Am7 | ♭3 | Softer, relative minor |
| Fmaj7 | 5th | Plagal, warm |
| Dm7 | ♭7 | Suspended, unresolved |
| A♭maj7 | ♯5 / ♭6 | Borrowed, cinematic |
| F♯m7♭5 | ♭5 | Tense, needs to move |

Same melody note, six different meanings. Cycling through these under a sustained vocal is the entire trick behind a lot of neo-soul.

### 1b. Delayed resolution

A named special case of technique #1, worth its own entry because it's a full systematic device rather than a one-off swap: **hold the melody on its expected tonic note, but harmonize that note with anything *except* the expected tonic chord, and only arrive at the real tonic later** — letting the tension hang longer than expected before the actual resolution. Works over a plain ii–V–I; the tonic note (say C, over a I chord of C major) gets reharmonized as a different chord tone each time:

| Substitute for the tonic chord | Tonic note becomes its… | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cdim7 | root | The classic — early pop/classical/jazz go-to; resolve to Cmaj7 or C triad |
| A♭maj7 (or A♭dom7♯11) | 3rd | Borrowed/cinematic color, same as technique #1's row |
| Fmaj7 | 5th | Reads as a plagal (IV–I) cadence |
| Fm6 | 5th (upgraded) | Jazzier version of the Fmaj7 row above |
| D♭maj7 | major 7th | "Major seven a half step up, then a half step down" — very common jazz move; guitaristic voicing: 3rd inversion with the tonic note in the **bass** (D♭maj7/C-shape), top three notes walk down on the resolve |
| D half-diminished (Dø7) | ♭7 (of the relevant Dm) | Same notes as Fm6, different inversion (Dm6 and Dø7 share a structure) — best voicing: **3rd inversion, Dø7/C** (tonic note stays in the bass through the resolution, only the upper chord moves) |
| B♭9 (B♭dom7 add 9) | 9th | One physical shape, dominant color |

**Delaying progressions (chain two or more of the rows above before resolving):**
- A♭maj7 → B♭9 → C (3rd-row then 9th-row, played slowly)
- A♭maj7 → G7sus4 → C (holds the tonic note inside the V chord's suspension for a second layer of delay)
- E♭m6 → A♭7 → D♭maj7 → C (a full ii–V–I in D♭ major — a half step up from the real key — substituted ahead of the drop back down a half step to the true tonic)
- F half-diminished → Fm6 → E7♯5 → E♭13 → Dm7 → D♭maj7 → C (chromatic bass line walking all the way down to the root before the final half-step resolution — the biggest version of the device)

**Applies to single-note lines too, not just full harmony:** over a plain ii–V heading to the tonic, superimpose/outline a substitute chord's arpeggio in the lead line (e.g. outline D♭maj7 over the V chord) before resolving the melodic line to the real target — same logic purely melodically, so it works even when the rhythm section keeps playing the plain changes.

### 2. Interpolate ii chords

Put a ii in front of any dominant. Doubles the chord count without changing the harmonic path. Detailed in [[Chord Substitution]] § 4. The safest reharmonization there is — it can't clash, because it doesn't change where the progression goes.

### 3. Tritone-substitute the dominants

Turns diatonic bass motion into chromatic descending motion. `Dm7 G7 Cmaj7` → `Dm7 D♭7 Cmaj7`. Detailed in [[Chord Substitution]] § 2.

Check the melody: over D♭7, the melody notes that work well are F (3rd), C♭ (7th), G (♯11) and E♭ (9th). A melody sitting on D natural will fight it.

### 4. Add secondary dominants

Precede any diatonic chord with the dominant a fifth above it. Brightens and adds urgency. → [[Secondary Dominants]]

### 5. Change the bass, keep the chord

A slash chord reharmonizes without touching the upper structure. `Cmaj7` → `Cmaj7/E` → `Cmaj7/G` → `Cmaj7/A` (which is Am9). Cheapest possible variation and it costs one note. → [[Upper Structure Chords]] · [[Chord Inversions]]

### 6. Pedal point

Hold one bass note and move everything above it. The tension between static bass and moving harmony does the work, and it's very forgiving of clashes because the ear attributes them to the pedal. Common on a tonic or dominant pedal.

### 7. Modal interchange

Borrow chords from the parallel minor (or any parallel mode). `Cmaj7 Fmaj7` → `Cmaj7 Fm7`. The single most reliable way to make a bright progression turn bittersweet. → [[Borrowed Chords]] · [[Parallel Minor]]

### 8. Reharmonize the *rhythm*, not the chords

Underrated. Keep every chord and change only where they land — push a chord an eighth early, or hold one an extra bar. Harmonic rhythm is a separate dial from harmonic content and changing it alone can transform a section. → [[Syncopation and Metric Displacement]]

## A worked example

Melody: **E – D – C** over four bars, plain harmonization `Cmaj7 | Cmaj7 | Dm7 | Cmaj7`.

Reharmonized:

| Bar | Chord | Melody note is the… | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cmaj7 | 3rd (E) | Establish home first |
| 2 | E♭maj7 | 7th (D) | Modal interchange — ♭III, sudden warmth |
| 3 | Dm7 → G7 | root (D) → 11th | Interpolated ii–V |
| 4 | A♭maj7 | ♯5 (C) | Deceptive, doesn't resolve — loops back |

Same three notes, entirely different piece. Every melody note is a legal tone of its chord, which is the only rule that had to hold.

## When not to

Reharmonization competes with the melody for attention. If the tune is the point — a strong vocal hook, a memorable lead line — dense reharmonization pulls focus and the song gets worse.

**The rule of thumb:** reharmonize the *repeats*, not the first statement. Let the listener learn the plain version, then change what's underneath it. That way the reharmonization reads as development rather than as confusion, and it gives you a second section for free. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]] · [[Motivic Development]]

On the reduced ii–V, three interchangeable **line-cliché** options are given (all starting on the tune's required E♭m7): a descending minor 7th-to-6th line cliché ("My Funny Valentine"/"Stairway to Heaven"-style), its common alternate voicing, and either played under an **A♭ pedal point** — the explicit trigger for reaching for these: "some movement but you don't want to go far away," usable live while comping. The furthest-out option is **free-association outside-key targeting**: identify only the note(s) the voice-leading needs next, find any chord (in- or out-of-key) containing them, and pivot through it — worked as E♭m7 → B7♭5 → Emaj7(♭5/♯11) → A♭, described directly as "no real logic," just fitting outside-key sounds on purpose. **Form-placement rule threaded through every technique:** how far to push a substitute depends on where it sits in the form — save the "out" moves for a later pass through the A section (after the plain version has been heard once), not the first time through; matches this page's own § *When not to* rule with a concrete worked demonstration rather than just the principle. - **A melody-note-driven trigger for WHEN to reharmonize a dominant chord with a ♯4 (half-)diminished substitute, worked on "All the Things You Are."** Diagnosis step first: analyze the melody note against its own chord, not just the key — on "All the Things You Are," the melody lands on the **3rd of the dominant chord** at one spot, called out as unusually rare (most standards avoid putting a dominant's 3rd in the melody, making it an exposed note that composers tend to reharmonize away from). Substitution given: replace the plain dominant (E♭7, in the key of A♭) with the **♯4 diminished chord** — here **D half-diminished (Dø7)** — which can be expanded further into a full secondary ii–V, **Dø7 → G7 → A♭**, instead of leaving it as a single substitute chord. Generalizable rule stated directly: whenever the melody carries the 3rd of a dominant chord, try the sharp-4 diminished/half-diminished (or its ii–V expansion) in place of the plain dominant — the melody note alone licenses the substitution, no need to reason about "is it really still an E♭7." → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Chords by Key]] (the A♭ harmonic minor ladder this ♯4 chord is drawn from), [[Chord Substitution]] § *Interpolating a ii* — Jens Larsen, [[How To Practice And Use Music Theory in Jazz]]
- **A worked, stackable-levels teaching template for combining §§2–4's techniques in one pass rather than in isolation — a plain 1-4-5 blues in C, reharmonized in three additive layers, comped and soloed after each layer.** Level 1 interpolates major ii–V–I's (§2) at four spots in the form: ahead of bar 4's IV chord (Gm7 → C7 → F7), ahead of bar 6's return to I (Dm7 → G7 → C), replacing bars 9–10's plain V-to-IV move (Dm7 → G7 → C7), and closing the turnaround. Level 2 adds minor ii–V–i's *on top of* Level 1 — targeting the relative minor at bars 2–3 (Bm7♭5 → E7 → Am, explicitly likened to Charlie Parker's *Blues for Alice* changes) and D minor at bar 8 (Em7♭5 → A7alt → Dm) — plus upgrades the turnaround to a full VI–ii–V using A7 as a secondary dominant into Dm (§4, [[Turnarounds]] § *VI7*). Level 3 tritone-substitutes (§3) *every* functioning dominant introduced by the first two levels, not just the form's original V7 — e.g. Level 2's Bm7♭5 → E7 → Am becomes Bm7♭5 → B♭7 → Am. **The soloing payoff stated directly:** think in the reharmonized changes and outline their chord tones — even while the band plays only the plain progression underneath — rather than running the blues/pentatonic scale over the whole form; more chords means more target tones. → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Chord Substitution]] § *Tritone substitution*, [[Turnarounds]] § *VI7*, [[Blues Form and Harmony]], [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] — Jack Ruch, [[Reharmonizing The Blues - 3 Levels]]
- **The mechanism behind the "melody note must be a usable tone" constraint: walk one fixed melody note through all 12 possible bass notes underneath it and hear it become root, 3rd, 5th, 7th, or an extension of a different chord each time** — hold a note (e.g. C) constant as the top voice and move the bass chromatically through all 12 notes: C is the root of C major, the major 7th of D♭maj7, the ♭7 of D7/Dm7, the major 6th of E♭maj6, the ♯5/♭13 of an E altered chord, the 5th of F major/minor, the ♭5 of G♭m7♭5, the sus4/11 of a G chord, the major 3rd of A♭major, the minor 3rd of A minor, the 9th of B♭sus2/B♭9, and the ♭9 of a B altered-dominant chord — twelve legal destinations for one note, purely from changing what's under it. Demonstrated as a worked reharmonization: a plain C major → D minor vamp under a simple melody gets reharmonized by reinterpreting the melody's D note as the 9th of a C9 (stay on C, add color, no chord change at all), then as the 13th of an F dominant 13, then the following note as the minor 3rd of A minor instead of resolving home — plus further substitution options shown on the same melody note: B♭6 (note as the 3rd), A sus (note as the 4th), A♭ major (note as the major 3rd).
