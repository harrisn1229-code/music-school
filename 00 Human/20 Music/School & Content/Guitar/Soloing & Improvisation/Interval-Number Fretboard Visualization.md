# Interval-Number Fretboard Visualization

**Think in scale-degree numbers (1-2-3-4-5-6-7, with flats for altered degrees) instead of shapes or note names when improvising.** A jazz/fusion-borrowed system BERNTH frames as the end state of four progressively more useful ways to see the same arpeggio/scale shape on the neck.

## The four levels

1. **Shape memorization.** "This is what a G chord/arpeggio looks like" — pure finger-pattern recall, the way most players learn open chords. Doesn't transfer to improvisation.
2. **Root-only.** Know where the root sits inside the shape, so the whole shape can be transposed by moving that root (e.g. slide a major7 arpeggio shape up a whole step to get the arpeggio a step higher). How most rock players think. Still limited — the shape only exists as one fixed fingering.
3. **Note names.** See the actual pitches (G, B, D, F♯ for a G major7 arpeggio) rather than a shape — unlocks playing the same notes in a different location or a different order (inversions) without needing a new memorized shape. Impractical in real time: naming notes fast enough to improvise with is hard.
4. **Interval numbers (the target level).** Same G major7 arpeggio, thought of as **1 3 5 7** (root, major 3rd, perfect 5th, major 7th) instead of note names. Faster to think in real time, and — the actual payoff — **directly editable by ear/theory on the fly**: swap the 3 for a ♭3 and the 7 for a ♭7 and the identical shape/fingering becomes a **minor 7 arpeggio (1 ♭3 5 ♭7)**. Same mechanism extends any arpeggio into its parent scale — fill the "holes" of a 1-3-5-7 major7 arpeggio with 2, 4, 6 and the same fretboard area now outlines the full major scale (1-2-3-4-5-6-7), repeatable position to position across the whole neck without ever consulting a scale diagram.

## Practice exercise — rotate the starting interval

Take one arpeggio shape (start with major7: 1-3-5-7) and play it starting from each interval in turn rather than always starting on the root: begin on 1, then on 3, then on 5, then on 7, working the shape from each entry point. Repeat across strings and across multiple arpeggio/scale types. The goal is breaking the "always starts and resolves on the root" habit that keeps a player's lines locked to one predictable shape-start.

## Applied over a chord progression

Over a fast-moving cadence, use the interval numbers to consciously choose an accenting note for each chord change as it happens (rather than running one scale box over the whole progression) — demoed producing a simple eighth-note line over a basic G major cadence that reads as melodically connected to the changes in a way a single static scale shape doesn't.
