# Songs that will help you identify descending intervals

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vJnAnrX2uXQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vJnAnrX2uXQ)

## Synopsis

Part 2 of the song-anchor interval mnemonic system — companion to [[Songs that will help you identify ascending intervals]]. Core premise: the same 12 intervals sound meaningfully different descending than ascending, so a separate set of anchor songs is needed for each direction rather than reusing the ascending set backwards.

## Techniques

- **Minor 2nd** — "I Am the Walrus" (Beatles) opening melody, pivoting E→E♭.
- **Major 2nd** — chorus of "Mamma Mia" (ABBA), E→D. ("Yesterday"'s opening is a commonly-cited example too, but flagged as often mistranscribed — see the presenter's dedicated video on that.)
- **Minor 3rd** — "Hey Jude" opening line (C→A in the key of F) — caveated as sounding more major than minor in context because it's the top half of a major chord; a cleaner "native minor" example is the last two bass notes of "Come Together" (Beatles), heard in a minor-key context.
- **Major 3rd** — bass line opening "The National Anthem" (Radiohead) — goes major 3rd then minor 3rd, useful side-by-side contrast. Also the Westminster Chimes/doorbell tune (Big Ben, key of E).
- **Perfect 4th** — bass riff of "Under Pressure" (Queen) / "Ice Ice Baby" (Vanilla Ice, same riff).
- **Tritone** — bass line of "Black Sabbath" (Black Sabbath): rises an octave then descends a tritone from the high G to D♭.
- **Perfect 5th** — bass line opening "My Girl" (The Temptations), G→C; also the opening two notes of "Meet the Flintstones."
- **Minor 6th** — "One of Us" (Joan Osborne), "What if God was—" A→C♯. Secondary example: "In My Life" (Beatles) opening guitar riff, though the presenter flags it as a weaker example since the riff's most prominent motion is actually ascending first (focus on just the first three notes for the descending minor 6th).
- **Major 6th** — "No Surprises" (Radiohead) opening riff, A→C.
- **Minor 7th** — "Watermelon Man" (Herbie Hancock) main trumpet melody, E♭→F.
- **Major 7th** — flagged as genuinely hard to find a strong example for (an awkward, dissonant, wide interval to sing, rarely used descending in well-known melodies) — best available: the final line of "Have Yourself a Merry Little Christmas," C→D♭.
- **Octave** — "Bulls on Parade" (Rage Against the Machine) guitar riff.
