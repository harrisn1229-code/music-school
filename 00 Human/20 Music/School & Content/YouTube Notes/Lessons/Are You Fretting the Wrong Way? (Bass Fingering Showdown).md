## Techniques

- **The two techniques, defined precisely.** **One Finger Per Fret (OFPF/1FPF):** each finger assigned to one fret within a position (e.g. frets 5-6-7-8 → index-middle-ring-pinky); still allows **microshifting** within the position rather than forcing a static stretch. **Simandl:** from Franz Simandl's 1881 upright-bass method book; on electric bass, index-middle-pinky covering a **3-fret span**, with the ring finger acting purely as a **support** for the pinky rather than fretting independently. The structural difference that drives every round: Simandl reaches 3 frets with 3 active fingers, OFPF reaches 4 frets with 4 active fingers.
- **[Round 1] "Doing It to Death"/"Gonna Have a Funky Good Time" (James Brown, Fred Thomas) — Simandl wins.** Spotlight: the bassline's last four notes use only frets 1 and 3 (fret 2 and fret 4 untouched). OFPF plays it ring-index-ring-index (index=fret1, ring=fret3) — a wasted index-to-ring stretch since the pinky/fret-4 reach it's "paying for" is never used; forcing that stretch risks **slanting the fingers**, pushing the **palm toward the neck** and the **thumb over the top** — named bad mechanics, not just wasted effort. Simandl plays the identical two frets pinky-index-pinky-index — a more neutral, natural hand shape for the same span. Verdict: Simandl, for getting the job done without unnecessary stretching tension.
- **[Round 2] "Time Bomb" (Rancid, Matt Freeman) — One Finger Per Fret wins.** Spotlight: a fast passage spanning a full 4 frets (4-5-6-7). Simandl (3-fret reach) has to **shift** the hand back and forth between index-on-4 and pinky-on-7 repeatedly — tiring at this song's tempo. OFPF stretches to cover the whole 4-fret span with the same index-pinky fingering, holding more tension but eliminating the frantic shifting. Verdict: OFPF, for a more stable hand position across the whole line — named as also cutting **transition noise**, not just fatigue.
- **[Round 3] "Plush" (Stone Temple Pilots, Robert DeLeo) — split, a point to each.** Two spotlights judged separately in the *same* bassline. Spotlight A: bottom of the neck, frets 1-2-3, walking up to fret 3 and no further — same wasted-stretch logic as round 1, Simandl (index-middle-pinky) wins over OFPF's unnecessary ring-finger reach (index-middle-ring). Spotlight B: a 4-fret span (frets 2-5), four notes on four frets — Simandl needs a shift (workable here, there's a full 8th note of gap to shift inside) but OFPF is cleaner: one finger per note straight across, index-middle-ring-pinky, no shift at all. Verdict: a genuine tie within one bassline, because the line moves in and out of a single position and each technique wins at a different point in it — concrete proof the decision is per-passage, not per-song. BassBuzz's own demo plays it Simandl for most of the line, switching to OFPF specifically for the 2-3-4-5 walk-up.
- **Final scoreboard: 2-2, a genuine tie — and the tie is the point, not an accident.** The named myth: *you have to choose ONE fretting technique to learn.* Busted directly by the three rounds showing both techniques winning in different, specific situations. Explicit analogy: arguing one technique categorically beats the other is like arguing a hammer beats a screwdriver — "it depends on whether you're dealing with a nail or a screw."
- **Strengths/weaknesses summary, stated directly after the tie.** **Simandl** saves stretching energy (a real advantage for **small hands**, especially on low, widely-spaced frets), and is worth defaulting to for **sustained grooves** (holding a low-tension shape for 5–10 minutes matters more than reach) — but its 3-fret ceiling means more shifting (and thus less efficiency) once a bassline spans more than 3 frets. **OFPF** gives better efficiency on basslines spanning **more than 3 frets**, which becomes crucial at faster tempos, and can reduce shifting/transition noise **even at slow-to-medium tempo** (the Time Bomb case) — but costs more held tension in the hand to maintain the stretch, especially low on the neck.
- **Decision framework — practice mode vs. performance mode.** **Practice mode** (scales, exercises, skill-building) → bias toward **OFPF**, because practice is explicitly about *developing* the 4-fret-span stretch and finger-independence skills a beginner doesn't have yet — stated caveat: stay out of pain, this is meant to put productive pressure on the stretch, not force injury. **Performance mode** (prepping a bassline to actually play live) → bias toward **Simandl**, because the goal flips to least work for the max result, and most basslines don't actually need more than a 3-fret reach.
- **Two guiding principles for a specific bassline, beyond the practice/performance split.** (1) If the passage is **relatively slow**, default to Simandl regardless of fret span — plenty of time to shift, so the speed penalty doesn't apply. (2) If the passage is **fast AND spans more than 3 frets**, OFPF is the best bet — the exact Time Bomb condition. Named explicitly as *not* an exact science beyond these two principles — hand size and personal taste are real variables the video doesn't try to reduce further, and the stated path to good instincts from there is early guidance from a teacher or course.
