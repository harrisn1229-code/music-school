# 7 Songs That 'Rip Off' Other Tunes

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t4ITk0yrISc)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t4ITk0yrISc)

## Synopsis

A viewer-suggestion follow-up to [[18 Songs That 'Rip Off' Other Hits]], continuing the same plagiarism-vs-influence framing with several legally sharper case studies — most importantly, the doctrine that copyright infringement doesn't require intent to copy, and the observation that lawsuit likelihood tracks a song's revenue far more than the strength of the similarity claim.

## Techniques

- **Copyright infringement does NOT require intent to copy — a legal doctrine, not just a moral judgment**: George Harrison's "My Sweet Lord" vs. The Chiffons' "He's So Fine" (written by Ronnie Mack) — Judge Richard Owen ruled the two songs were "in fact the same song, albeit with different lyrics," despite accepting Harrison's claim he had never heard the original. Harrison still had to pay ~$1.6M, because unconscious/unintentional copying is legally actionable the same as deliberate copying. Cited as one of the first pop-song lawsuits of its kind, shaping the modern culture of copyright litigation in songwriting.
- **Lawsuit likelihood tracks revenue, not similarity strength — restated and given concrete numbers**: Uptown Funk (Bruno Mars/Mark Ronson) drew multiple separate lawsuits (Gap Band + Trinidad James added to writing credits in 2015; Zapp sued 2018 over the guitar riff; Collage sued the same year) specifically because the song went "11 times platinum in the US alone" in its first two years — the video's explicit argument: any songwriter who can make even a trivial infringement claim against a massively profitable song is heavily incentivized to pursue it, independent of how strong the claim actually is.
- **A genre-convention defense, stated explicitly**: most of what Uptown Funk was accused of lifting (rhythmic guitar riff style, vocal-bridge phrasing) are "standard staple elements of the funk genre" shared across many funk songs, not unique to any one source — "there comes a point where the things that are making these songs sound similar are the very same things that place them in the same genre together." Useful general test for evaluating a similarity claim: is the shared element genre-definitional, or actually specific to the accused source?
- **Identifying a shared exotic scale as the real mechanism behind a "spooky" melodic resemblance**: Billie Eilish's "bad guy" instrumental break and the "Plants vs. Zombies" theme both use G minor with a raised 4th — the **double harmonic minor** (a.k.a. **Hungarian minor**) scale — named by Eilish's brother/producer Finneas himself as the actual connection ("it's not at all the same melody, but it's literally Plants vs. Zombies"). → [[Harmonic Minor Modes]].
- **A recursive/ironic lawsuit chain worth knowing as vault-internal continuity**: Radiohead's "Creep" itself was ruled based on The Hollies' "The Air That I Breathe" (Albert Hammond/Mike Hazlewood now credited as co-writers, per [[18 Songs That 'Rip Off' Other Hits]]) — then in 2017, Warner Chapel pursued Lana Del Rey's "Get Free" for allegedly lifting Creep's chord progression and chorus melody, effectively suing on behalf of a song that was itself found to have copied its own predecessor.
- **A small rhythmic alteration is not a legal shield against sampling infringement**: Vanilla Ice's "Ice Ice Baby" sampled Queen/David Bowie's "Under Pressure" bassline with a slightly modified rhythm, and initially argued the change meant the songs "weren't the same" — the change didn't exempt the sample from requiring clearance/royalties regardless (Queen and Bowie eventually received credit and royalties).
