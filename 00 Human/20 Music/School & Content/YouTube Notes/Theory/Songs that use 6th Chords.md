# Songs that use 6th Chords

## Synopsis

6th chords (major triad + 6th degree, a milder color than adding a 7th) traced through Beatles/jazz/folk examples, then two real naming traps worth remembering: the minor-6th-chord's added note is ALWAYS the major 6th (not the minor scale's 6th, despite the chord's own "minor" label), and 6th chords are enharmonically indistinguishable from inverted minor 7th chords — plus a THIRD trap where classical notation uses "6" to mean something completely different (first-inversion, via figured bass) than modern pop/jazz "6" (added 6th degree).

## Techniques

- **Definition**: C6 = C major triad + the major 6th degree (A). Milder color/tension than a 7th chord.
- **Straight uses**: "Somewhere Only We Know" (Keane, D6↔E6 in the chorus). "She Loves You" (The Beatles) — ends on a 6th-chord vocal cluster; McCartney's own account: producer George Martin called the 6th-chord ending "corny" and said he'd never end on one, but the band insisted on keeping it.
- **A "spread" voicing that diffuses the characteristic 6th/5th rub**: "The Fool on the Hill" (The Beatles — auto-caption mangled the title as "Fall on the Hill") opens on a D6 with the 6th voiced an OCTAVE below the 5th rather than right next to it (functionally a Bm7 in the right hand over a D in the left) — softens the dissonant rub you get when the 5th and 6th sit adjacent.
- **More Beatles 6th chords**: "Sun King" (E6), "Michelle" (verse, E♭6).
- **Guitar-native, especially G6**: playable as a fully open chord on the four thinnest strings with no fretting at all — arguably the easiest chord on guitar. "Big Yellow Taxi" (Joni Mitchell, open tuning) builds its opening riff entirely from major-to-6th moves (A→A6 on IV, B→B6 on V) exploiting exactly how easy it is to add the 6th with one extra finger in her tuning.
- **Genre association**: an "old-fashioned, jazzy" sound, extensively used in jazz/swing — "Just a Gigolo" (Louis Prima), "My Baby Just Cares for Me" (Nina Simone).
- **6/9 chord** (6th + 9th together): "Horse With No Name" (America) — same example already logged on [[Extended Chords]] from a different video, confirming the cross-reference.
- **Naming trap #1 — the minor-6th chord's added note is ALWAYS the major 6th, never the minor scale's 6th**: a Bm6 = B minor triad (B-D-F♯) + G♯ (the MAJOR 6th of B), not G natural (the natural-minor 6th) — a common and understandable point of confusion, since "minor 6" sounds like it should mean "the 6th from the minor scale." The "minor" in the chord name refers only to the underlying triad, not to the added 6th. "God Only Knows" (Beach Boys) uses a genuine Bm6 this way. If you actually want the flatted 6th added to a minor triad instead, the chord is a distinct thing — "m♭6" (or "add ♭6") — demonstrated in "Bulletproof... I Wish I Was" (Radiohead).
- **Naming trap #2 — 6th chords and inverted minor 7th chords are enharmonically identical (same notes, different name)**: any 6th chord's third inversion IS a minor 7th chord built a major 3rd higher (B♭6 3rd-inversion = Gm7, identical notes), and vice versa — the same phenomenon as sus4/sus2 first-inversion pairs. Which name is "correct" depends entirely on which note context establishes as the root; when context is genuinely ambiguous (e.g. an unaccompanied vocal chord with no bass to anchor it), either name is defensible. "Bohemian Rhapsody" (Queen, opening a cappella "Is this the real life?") is exactly this case — most published transcriptions land on B♭6 over G minor 7, but Bennett notes it "really doesn't matter" since the notes are identical.
- **Naming trap #3 — classical notation's "6" means something ENTIRELY different from modern pop/jazz "6," despite looking identical**: in classical figured-bass notation, a superscript "6" after a chord letter denotes FIRST INVERSION (because inverting a triad creates a 6th interval between the outer notes), not an added 6th degree — so classical "C6" = "C major, first inversion" (= C/E in modern slash-chord notation), a completely different chord from jazz/pop "C6" (C major + A).
