# How to play GREAT guitar solos in under 5 minutes (pentatonics/triads)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nuK--PBXIg4)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nuK--PBXIg4)

## Synopsis
A short "Five-Minute Monday" lesson from Jesse Glick built around one claim: a guitar player who can work confidently inside a single four-fret span can play "everything they'd ever need," across any genre — provided they're comfortable with both their pentatonic scale *and* triads inside that same span. Worked entirely in the key of C major over a I-IV-V loop (C, F, G). The lesson has three beats: (1) show that all three diatonic triads of the key (C, F, and — with a voice-leading caveat — G) can be found inside one fixed C major pentatonic box (the box rooted at fret 8 on the low E string) without moving the hand; (2) drill physically finding/touching each triad as the chord it belongs to comes around on a backing loop; (3) build an actual solo by starting each chord with its triad (arpeggiated) and continuing into the key's own pentatonic scale — always the same C major pentatonic regardless of which chord is currently sounding, never switching to a chord-specific pentatonic scale. The most useful part of the lesson isn't the note choice (which is fairly simple) but the closing point on **phrasing**: a plain triad-then-scale phrase reads as "robotic," two ideas stapled together, and the fix demonstrated is sliding into the triad rather than picking it cleanly, then generally auditioning by ear what connects smoothly out of a triad and what doesn't. Video is short and fast (~8.5 min packed into a tight edit) and the auto-captions garble a couple of the exact fret/string calls during the triad-shape demonstrations — those spots are flagged rather than guessed at.

## What's taught
- **Core framing (0:00):** the video opens against the "you need to shred the whole neck" misconception — argues a player who's fluent with pentatonic scale + triads inside just four frets covers what they actually need, "no matter the genre or the song."
- **The scale used (~1:00):** C major pentatonic, same pitch content as A minor pentatonic, fingered as the box starting on **fret 8 of the low E string** (not the open-position/root-5 box) — the "second box" shape of the scale.
- **The claim being demonstrated:** all three diatonic triads of the key — C (I), F (IV), G (V) — can be found **inside that same fixed pentatonic box**, without relocating the hand, and the same logic holds for "a different C major pentatonic scale somewhere else [on the neck]" — i.e. it's box-relative, not tied to this one specific fret range.
- **C triad:** built from notes already in the box, landing on **fret 8, fret 7, fret 5** (exact strings lost to a garbled caption stretch — "Here's... Okay, pretty easy so far" — re-check against the source video if precise fingering is needed).
- **F triad:** "the exact same thing" shape as C's — same fret-8/7/5 pattern — just started on the **A string, on the F note**, instead of the low E string. One physical shape covers both the I and IV triads; only the starting string changes.
- **G triad — two versions:** a naive in-box fingering exists (proof that it's technically findable in the same four frets) but is **not voice-led properly** between chords; a second, voice-led G shape is the one actually used in the C-F-G loop. The video names voice leading as the deciding factor for which G shape to grab, without tabbing either shape note-for-note.
- **Backing loop:** a quick C-F-G loop is built to practice/solo over.
- **Practice step 1 — touch the triads on the loop:** before soloing, just find and land on the correct triad, with the correct hand position, as each chord comes around — C triad while C sounds, F triad while F sounds, G triad (voice-led version) while G sounds — purely a targeting drill, not yet melodic.
- **Practice step 2 — triad up, scale down, per chord:** on each chord's change, start by **arpeggiating that chord's triad ascending**, then continue by **descending through the pentatonic scale** — critically, always the **same key-level C major pentatonic scale**, never switching to a chord-rooted pentatonic (explicitly called out: over F, go up the F triad but come back down the *C* pentatonic scale, "because that's the scale for the key," not an F pentatonic scale).
- **Reverse variation:** flip the order — pentatonic scale first, triad second — as a second phrase shape once the basic version is comfortable.
- **The actual hard part, named directly:** blending the triad and the scale so they read as one line rather than "a triad and then a pentatonic... it sounds a little robotic... like you're separating it."
- **Phrasing fix demonstrated:** instead of picking the triad's three notes cleanly, **slide into the first note of the triad** — same three pitches, markedly smoother/less mechanical result. General instinct stated alongside it: listen for "what sounds good after the triad and what doesn't," i.e. treat the triad→scale seam as a phrasing choice to audition by ear, not a fixed rule.
- **Closing demonstration:** plays the loop with triads and pentatonic scale blended together per the above, highlighting the triads again inside the flow of a real solo.
