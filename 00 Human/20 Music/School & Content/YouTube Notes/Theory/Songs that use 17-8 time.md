# Songs that use 17/8 time

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3-eYp6HUoCs)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3-eYp6HUoCs)

## Synopsis

Continues the odd-time-signature series with 17/8 (and its 17/4 cousin), built around one of the series' most useful general principles: an odd meter with an 8 on the bottom feels fundamentally less stable than the same beat-count with a 4 on the bottom, because the ear's pulse tracks quarter notes, not eighth notes.

## Techniques

- **Why x/8 feels less stable than the equivalent x/4** — the core mechanism, generalized: the ear's internal sense of meter latches onto the quarter-note pulse even in an eighth-note-denominator meter. A bar of 17/8 has 17 eighth notes, which only divides into 8.5 quarter notes — so the bar feels like "9 beats with the last one sliced in half" rather than a clean count. Same shape at other odd 8th-denominator meters: 11/8 feels like 5½, 13/8 feels like 6½, 15/8 feels like 7½. Swap the denominator to a 4 (17/4 instead of 17/8) and the same 17-count feels noticeably more grounded, because it's now a complete set of quarter notes rather than a fraction — still odd and unusual, but far less disorienting than its 8th-note-denominator counterpart.
- **Practical notation advice for a large top number over a 4**: rather than writing one giant bar of 17/4, it reads far more usefully to the performer split into smaller bars (e.g. three bars of 4/4 + one bar of 5/4) — bar lines carry accent information a single oversized bar loses entirely.
- **Songs**: Björk's "Moon" (steady 17/8 ostinato throughout); The National (song unspecified in transcript — divides its 17 beats as 4+5+4+4, an internal grouping that makes the meter easier to follow than an undifferentiated 17-count); Dream Theater's "The Alien" (fast 17/8, feeling close to "8½" per the mechanism above — the track that won the band their first Grammy, with John Petrucci's acceptance speech crediting the odd-meter, long-song, guitar-solo-heavy approach the band was once told to abandon for being too uncommercial); Erasure's "Always" (chorus in 17/4 — comparatively stable-sounding despite the odd count, illustrating the x/4-vs-x/8 stability difference directly).
