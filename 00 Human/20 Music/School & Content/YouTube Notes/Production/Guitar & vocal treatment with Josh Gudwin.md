# Guitar & vocal treatment with Josh Gudwin

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_txkFmQVxd4)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_txkFmQVxd4)

## Synopsis

Josh Gudwin walks through his channel-strip decisions on a Justin Timberlake track — a noisy but excellent guitar performance rescued with denoising rather than re-recorded, plus a full lead vocal chain (Sony C800G → GML EQ → SSL bus compressor → subtractive EQ → LA-2A → final EQ).

## What's taught

- **Keep a noisy but excellent performance rather than re-recording it clean — the take was captured in a rush and carries hiss/frequency noise, fixed afterward with heavy denoising (an 18 dB cut at ~3.17 kHz) rather than redone.** Explicit principle stated: "it doesn't matter if you record a bad part as long as the part itself is excellent — everything else takes a back seat." The song became huge despite the noise being present in the original take.
- **Guitar chain: amp sim + multiband compression to tame pick "slap" transients, denoise, then a named vintage plate reverb.** A subtle multiband compression pass specifically reduces the attack "slap" of the pick without changing the overall tone; a **London plate** reverb (his own labeling convention for a specific plate character) is sent to subtly at a low level — described as bringing significant polish for very little wetness.
- **Full lead vocal chain, front to back:** Mic'd through a Sony C800G (into a chain including a Neumann-class mic option and an AKG C12-class option per the era), with a **GML EQ** placed at the end of the mic chain used ONLY to cut harsh frequencies, never to boost — "I don't usually boost on the GML, I like to take away things." Then an **SSL E-series bus compressor** on the lead, run at a very light threshold specifically to "open up" the vocal — described as doing something useful to the sound even in moments where it's barely triggering gain reduction. Then subtractive EQ addressing the C800G's characteristic brightness: de-essing/clipping down excessive S's and breath noise from the mic's top end, cutting **~4.5 kHz** without touching low-mids or lows. That feeds an **LA-2A** (a named favorite vocal compressor), driven lightly — his stated sweet spot is roughly 0-7 on the compression control — with **+3 dB boost added afterward** specifically to restore top-end sparkle lost to the earlier de-essing/EQ cut. Finished with a final light EQ pass (low-room-noise cut, low-mid cut) and a high-pass filter.
- **Subtractive-first philosophy stated directly and applied consistently across the whole chain** — nearly every EQ move described is a cut, not a boost; boosts (like the +3 dB after the LA-2A) are used specifically to restore something that was intentionally removed earlier, not as a first move.
