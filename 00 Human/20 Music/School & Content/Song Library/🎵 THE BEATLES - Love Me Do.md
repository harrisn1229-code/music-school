# Love Me Do — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

The first single, and Pollack's case is that the primitive surface hides real compositional idiosyncrasy: a **thirteen-bar verse** whose phrase lengths follow the lyric scansion, a tonic established with no dominant anywhere until the bridge, and vocal harmony built on open fifths instead of thirds. Whether that originality was intended or a by-product of imperfectly copying their influences is a question he leaves standing.

**Form.** The stock two-bridge layout with one verse dividing the bridges. What's unusual is the instrumental solo sitting inside a repeated *bridge* rather than a verse. The verse is an odd **13 bars phrased AAAB as 2 + 2 + 2 + 7**, following the lyric rather than any metrical template. Every section starts squarely on the downbeat, which contributes to the clunky impression.

**Harmony.** Three chords in the entire song, and two of them do all the work: the tonic is established by **I–IV–I with no dominant at all** ([[HAR-C03 — Plagal cadence]]), and V doesn't arrive until the downbeat of the bridge — where its first appearance becomes an event on its own. The bridge tune then alternates the natural and flat seventh continuously, so even the one place the major mode asserts itself stays contested. The verse's regular harmonic rhythm breaks at the point of greatest lyric stretch, which is what makes the irregular phrasing read as expressive rather than lost.

**Melody.** Mixolydian in effect — heavy ♭7s and no leading tone until the bridge, with the flat seventh doing the mode-defining work ([[SCL-C05 — Characteristic notes]]) — plus bent blue thirds sitting directly on top of major chords containing the natural third ([[BLU-C07 — Why the ♭3 isn't wrong]]). The hook is the harmonica's descending four-note figure, and because it also closes every verse it saturates the record ([[MEL-C07 — What makes a hook]]).

**Arrangement.** About as unadorned as a Beatles recording gets. Two vocal details became house style: **open fifths instead of thirds** on the title phrase, which melds the two voices into a single colour rather than stacking them, and holding one note in the upper part while the lower moves stepwise. Texture cycles deliberately across a verse — harmonica alone, then vocal duet, then a solo voice with the hook figure behind it.
