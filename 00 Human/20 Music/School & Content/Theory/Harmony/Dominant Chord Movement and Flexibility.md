# Dominant Chord Movement and Flexibility

Part of [[Music Theory]]. The vault already teaches dominants **one device at a time** —
[[Secondary Dominants]], [[Tritone Substitution]], [[Backdoor Progression]],
[[Augmented Sixth Chords]], [[Altered Chords]], [[Passing Chords]]. This note is the **map that
holds all of them at once**, from Nick Clark's ~184,000-character study of the subject.

**The thesis in one line:** a key has **12 possible dominant chords**, and each has **12 possible
root destinations** including staying put. Everything the other notes teach is one cell in that
grid.

## Three definitions, all in play

He works with all three at once, and says so — worth adopting, because the disagreements between
them are where most confusion about dominants lives:

1. **The V chord of a key**, with or without a 7th.
2. **Any chord spelled root–maj3–P5–♭7**, at any scale degree.
3. **Any chord whose tension resolves like a dominant** — i.e. dominant *function*, regardless of
   spelling. ([[HAR-C01 — The three functions: T, SD, D]])

> "What it comes down to the most is if the chord in question is able to trick the brain into
> searching for some type of resolution or movement, and how that resolution or movement is carried
> out in respect to the key or tonal center the listener's ears are currently adjusted to."

## The Family of Dominants — the idea worth stealing

**This is the payload of the document.** The mechanism is credited to **Barry Harris**; the
three-family organisation and the mapping of all twelve root motions onto them is Clark's own.

The mechanism: **there are only three distinct fully-diminished 7th chords in 12-TET**
([[Diminished Chords]]). Lower any one note of a dim7 by a semitone and you get a dominant 7th. Four
notes × three dim7s = **exactly the twelve dominants**. So every dominant belongs to one and only
one family, and the family is named by the diminished chord's role:

| Family | Parent dim7 | The four dominants | Frequency |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | leading-tone viiº7 | ♭VII7, ♭II7, III7, **V7** | most used |
| **2** | auxiliary Iº7 | VII7, II7, IV7, ♭VI7 | |
| **3** | passing ♭IIº7 | I7, ♭III7, ♯IV7, VI7 | least used |

**And the twelve root motions partition the same way — four per family.** This is the elegant part,
and it is not in any textbook in this library:

| Family | Root motion | The named device |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | −1 semitone | [[Tritone Substitution]] |
| | +2 | [[Backdoor Progression]] |
| | −4 | |
| | +5 / −7 | the perfect cadence ([[Cadences]]) |
| **2** | +1 | |
| | −2 | V→IV retrogression |
| | +4 | |
| | −5 / +7 | plagal, and the blues IV7→I7 ([[BLU-C02 — Why every chord is a dominant 7th]]) |
| **3** | +3 | |
| | −3 | |
| | ±6 | tritone leap |
| | 0 | no root movement at all |

**The practical payoff, and it is a real one:** Iº7, IVº7 and Vº7 each sit in a *different* family.
So any fully diminished chord can be made the leading-tone diminished of the I, the IV **or** the V
of any target key — which means one dim7 can broker a modulation between **any** two keys. See
[[Modulation (Key Change)]] and [[HAR-C10 — Tonicization vs modulation]].

He also splits destinations three ways rather than two: **resolution** (to something stable),
**retrogression** (to a subdominant), and **prolongation** (to another unstable chord).

## Secondary and substitute dominants, made systematic

The vault's [[Secondary Dominants]] teaches the common ones. His organising rule is cleaner: a
**secondary** dominant has a *diatonic root and a diatonic target*; a **substitute** dominant has a
*non-diatonic root and a diatonic target*.

**Five true secondary dominants**, each with the parent mode it comes from:

| Chord | Function | Parent mode | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| I7 | V7/IV | Mixolydian | |
| II7 | V7/V | Lydian | |
| III7 | V7/vi | Ionian ♯5 (harmonic minor mode) | "probably the most commonly used" |
| VI7 | V7/ii | Mixolydian ♯1 | |
| VII7 | V7/iii | Lydian ♯2 | the least used; two non-diatonic notes |

Five more (IV7, ♯IV7, ♭V7, ♭III7, ♭VII7) break the definition and, he argues, usually **signal a
modulation** rather than a tonicization.

**Six main substitute dominants**: ♭II(♯6) = SubV7 · ♭III7 = SubV7/ii · IV7 = SubV7/iii (the one
exception, diatonic on both counts) · ♭V(♯6) = SubV7/IV · ♭VI(♯6) = SubV7/V — *this is the classical
augmented sixth, French/German/Italian* ([[Augmented Sixth Chords]]) · ♭VII7 = SubV7/vi.

## ⚠️ His hardest claim: most tritone subs are augmented sixth chords

He is consistent about this across every document, and it is worth understanding before dismissing
it as pedantry:

- G7 contains the tritone **B–F**.
- D♭7 is [D♭, F, A♭, **C♭**]. C♭ and F are a *diminished* fifth in the wrong direction — D♭7 does
  **not** contain a B.
- D♭(♯6) is [D♭, F, A♭, **B**] — the same keys on the piano, and it *does* share G7's literal
  tritone.

So the chord that actually substitutes for G7 is, on paper, an augmented sixth. Same argument
underwrites his refusal to write **♯9**: what is written 7♯9 is usually a **7(♭10)** — a minor third
stacked over a major third — and a genuine ♯9 only exists in an augmented-sixth chord where the ♯6
and ♯9 sit a perfect fifth apart. See [[Chord Naming by Context]] and [[The Harmonic Lattice]].

**How to hold this.** He concedes the practical half himself: on a chart, write the easy name.

> "For almost everybody, myself included, we don't consciously make this distinction between the two
> intervals, rather it is a subtle illusion of note associations that happens in our mind's ear."

**School's position:** keep the standard names in concept notes and on charts. Reach for his
spelling when an analysis stops making sense — which is exactly when it earns its keep.

## Negative dominants

The vault has no note on negative harmony at all; it appears only in
[[Music Theory Iceberg]] as a name. His treatment is compact and usable.

**The axis** is the midpoint between I and V (between E and E♭ in C). **The shortcut:** reflect the
root across the tonic, then flip major ↔ minor; maj7 becomes min6, min7 becomes maj6.

| Positive | Negative |
|---|---|
| V | iv |
| I | i |
| ii | ♭VII |
| V7 | iv6 (= iiø7) |
| IVmaj7 | v(♭6) / ♭IIImaj7 |

So the **minor plagal cadence iv→I is the negative perfect cadence** — which is a genuinely useful
reframe of a move that turns up constantly in pop ([[Borrowed Chords]], [[Parallel Minor]]). Lineage
he credits: Hugo Riemann's harmonic dualism → Ernst Levy, *A Theory of Harmony* → Steve Coleman →
Jacob Collier popularising the term.

Geometrically this is just a **reflection on the lattice** — see [[The Harmonic Lattice]].

## Tensions, and how to voice them

Notes of a dominant occupy four slots; the other eight semitones can read as **♭9 (sometimes ♯8), 9,
♯9/♭10, 11, ♯11 (sometimes ♭5), ♭13/♯5, 13**, and rarely 7. His working rules:

- **Every tension needs somewhere to go.** A tension with no resolution is just a wrong note.
- **Remove chord tones to make room.** The 5 goes first, then the root if the bass has it.
- **A sus chord kills the tritone**, so it forfeits dominant tension — which is why 7sus4 is a
  *colour*, not a dominant ([[Suspended Chords]]).
- **♭5 pulls toward secondary dominants** (it stays near the key); **♯11 pulls toward substitute
  dominants** (it becomes the 5 of a target a half step below).

**Voicing, from his conclusion** — the most directly usable paragraph in the document, and it is
piano advice, so it lands straight into [[Piano Chord Accompaniment Patterns]]:

- **Left hand: 5, ♭7, 13**, plus the major 3rd **as a major tenth** if the hand reaches. Played as a
  third instead it muddies.
- **Right hand carries the tensions.** For a deliberately dissonant dominant, almost any right-hand
  chord works "as long as each note feels like it's somewhat resolving."
- **Choose tensions by melodic contour and register, not by specific pitches.**
- **Metrical placement is a variable**: the same dominant on a strong beat and on a weak beat are
  not the same event. Rarely said in pop theory writing, and true — cf.
  [[RHY-C09 — Meter ranks every position strong to weak]].

## Where he gets his evidence

The document is example-driven to an unusual degree. The recurring wells: **MGMT** (~18 songs),
**Masterpiece's barbershop "Tennessee Waltz"** (~15 separate citations — his workhorse), **Stevie
Wonder**, **Michael Jackson**, **Mac Miller**, **Thundercat**, **Queen**. Highlights:

| Device | Where he shows it |
|---|---|
| V7/iii | Mel Tormé, "The Christmas Song" (F♯m7 B7 → Emaj7) — the cleanest on the list |
| V7/vi with ♯5 | Michael Jackson, "She's Out of My Life" |
| Minor plagal (negative perfect cadence) | Queen, "Bohemian Rhapsody" — "carry on, carry on" |
| French sixth as SubV7/V | Masterpiece, "Tennessee Waltz" |
| ♭V7 (V7/♭I) | Thundercat, "Pozole" |
| VII7 resolving **up** a half step | Thundercat, "Walking on the Moon" |
| Extended dominant chain | "Tennessee Waltz" — G7 C7/G F7 B♭ |
| No-root-movement dominant | Louis Cole, "After the Load Is Blown" (C9sus4 → Cmaj9) |
| Tritone move, upper voices near-static | Kanye West, "Violent Crimes" (A7 → D♯+7♭9) |
| Blues IV7 → I7 | Beach Boys, "Good Vibrations" |
| Parallel dominants | Stevie Wonder, "Visions" (♭VII7 ♭VI7 IV7 ♭III7 V+) |
| The only classical example | Liszt, "Un Sospiro" |

## What it adds, and where to be careful

**Adds** — a single structure that explains *why* the named devices are the named devices; the
dim7-brokered modulation trick; a compact working account of negative harmony; the voicing advice.

**Careful with** — his terminology, which is heavily his own:

| His term | Status | This vault |
|---|---|---|
| "Family of Dominants #1/#2/#3" | his systematization of a Barry Harris idea | fine to use, cite as his |
| "negative perfect cadence", "related iv−" | his extension of negative harmony | non-standard |
| "super lydian" (a mode with a ♯1) | coined | non-standard |
| ♭10 for ♯9 · ♯8 for ♭9 · ♭8 | his respelling programme | keep standard names in charts and concepts |
| "IV7/IV", "♭VII7/ii" | naming a dominant by its motion, not by V7/x | non-standard, but readable |
| SubV7, related ii, backdoor, prolongation | standard Berklee/jazz | use freely |
