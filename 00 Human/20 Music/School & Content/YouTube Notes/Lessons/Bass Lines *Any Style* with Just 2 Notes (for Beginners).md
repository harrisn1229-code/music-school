## What's taught

### The 47-second root-fifth primer
- **Root notes come from the underlying chord** — playing just the root of whatever chord the guitar/keys are holding is a bassist's main job, full stop.
- **The fifth is the fifth note of the major *or* minor scale built on that root** — walking up either scale from C (major: C D E F G, or minor: C D E♭ F G) lands on the same G either way, since the perfect 5th is a diatonic note shared by both qualities. That shared-tone property is what makes root-fifth workable without knowing whether the underlying chord is major or minor (see the Strokes example below).
- **Fretboard shape:** up one string, up two frets from the root — the same shape taught in BassBuzz's earlier "shapes" video, [[5 Easy “Shapes” for Kickass Bass Lines (Bassist Shortcuts)]] (Shape 1 there, demoed on "Rikki Don't Lose That Number").
- Adding the fifth on top of root notes injects "energy and flavor... without distracting from the song," and the claim tested through the rest of the video is that this works in *almost any style*.

### The science of why the fifth works (frequency ratios and consonance)
Framed as a direct, physics-grounded answer to "why the fifth, specifically":
- **Pitch = vibration speed (frequency).** Two notes sounding together either lock in ("ahh," stable/consonant) or clash ("AAH," unstable/dissonant) depending on the **ratio between their frequencies**, not on which two notes they happen to be.
- **Consonance gradient by ratio simplicity:** 1:1 (unison/same note) and 2:1 (octave) are the simplest possible ratios and sound maximally stable; ratios get progressively less stable as they get more complex. The **perfect fifth is a 3:2 ratio** — one note vibrating exactly 50% faster than the other — landing in "the perfect sweet spot on the spectrum: enough instability to make interesting basslines, but not so much it distracts from the song and gets you fired." This is the same overtone-series logic as [[Intervals]]'s "Why some intervals sound consonant" section (octave = 2× the fundamental, perfect 5th = 3× the fundamental octave-reduced) — same physics, reframed here directly as *harmonic ratio between two simultaneous or sequential notes* rather than *overtone-series position of a single note*. → links [[BASS Slot]]: this 3:2-ratio argument is the acoustic justification for why root-fifth is the low end's default harmonic vocabulary.
- **Applies to melodic (one-note-at-a-time) basslines too, not just simultaneous dyads** — the ratio logic explains why a bassline built from simple-ratio note *pairs* (root-fifth) reads as stable even played sequentially, one note after another.
- **Worked interval comparisons, stable vs. unstable:**
  - Stable (simple ratio): the root-fifth pattern opening "My Girl" (The Temptations); the root-fifth interval opening "Ninety-Nine and a Half (Won't Do)" (Wilson Pickett).
  - Unstable (complex ratio): the **tritone** opening "YYZ" (Rush); the **minor second** (half step) opening the *Jaws* theme — capped with a "you're gonna need a bigger bass" gag.
- **Stated science takeaway:** the fifth's 3:2 relationship to the root sits at the sweet spot of "interesting but still stable enough for us bass players" — and that holds whether the root and fifth are played together (a guitarist's power chord) or one at a time (how bassists mostly use it).

### Proof-by-subtraction demo — "Sittin' on the Dock of the Bay" (Otis Redding)
The classic root-fifth intro line is played, then deliberately broken by substituting other scale degrees in the same rhythmic slot to show *why* the fifth specifically is the safe choice: the **sixth** ("blech"), the **third** ("not really"), and the **fourth** ("eucchh") all stand out too much and pull attention away from the song when swapped in for the fifth. Re-playing the line with the fifth restored demonstrates how much better the bass sits back in the track once it's in place. → cross-referenced in [[Jamerson's Root-Fifth-Octave Exercise]]'s "generic note set" point (this same song listed there as a root-fifth-skeleton example alongside "I Just Called to Say I Love You" and "Have You Ever Seen the Rain").

### Momentum from the fifth *below* the root — "Under Pressure" (Queen/David Bowie)
- The line starts on the **root** (D, 12th fret of the D string), but the fifth is played **below** the root instead of above it — found by dropping down a string on the **same fret** rather than the up-a-string/up-two-frets shape from the primer. Musically identical note name, just an octave apart from the "up" shape's fifth (a fifth up two frets and a fifth down the same fret land on the same note name, A in this key).
- **Momentum test:** playing the line with the root held the whole time (no fifth at all) is "super boring" by comparison — the one fifth at the end is what creates the pull back toward the root on the next repeat, adding momentum "without drawing too much attention" and without the clashing complex-ratio dissonance that would compete with the vocal melody.
- **General shape takeaway:** root-fifth isn't locked to the "up a string, up two frets" shape from the primer — the down-a-string-same-fret fifth is an equally valid, sometimes more idiomatic, way to reach the same note.

### Versatility across major/minor — "On the Other Side" (The Strokes)
- The line starts **on the fifth**, not the root, and runs fifth-root-fifth-root throughout.
- **The chord progression moves G minor → E♭ major**, but the **same shape** plays the fifth-root pattern over both chords (up a string, up two frets from the root, on both the minor and the major chord) — because the perfect fifth belongs to both the major and minor scale of a given root (see the primer above), the identical fretboard shape works regardless of chord quality.
- **Stated beginner payoff:** since root-fifth works over both major and minor chords built on the same root, it's a "safe choice" for a beginner who can find the root by ear but can't yet distinguish a major chord from a minor one — the two-note vocabulary sidesteps that ear-training gap entirely rather than requiring it.

### The 3-step rhythm generator (adapting root-fifth to any style)
The video's central reusable method — the same two notes, transformed entirely by rhythm:
1. **Pick an iconic bassline from the target style.**
2. **Steal the rhythm** (the rhythmic pattern only — "basslines don't mind" being borrowed this way).
3. **Plug in roots and fifths onto that rhythm.**

Worked in five styles, each against a shared **sample chord chart** (a country/soul-style progression built from G, D, E, and C chords) so the same formula's output can be compared style to style:

- **Country** (also stated to cover polka, bluegrass, and jazz two-beat): source line = Johnny Cash "Walk the Line." Rhythm stolen = **quarter notes** (or half notes, depending how it's counted). Formula result: the source line already alternates **root-fifth, root-fifth** per chord change — described as "very typical of this style." Applied to the sample chart: find each chord's root, play root-fifth-root-fifth, move to the next chord (G → D → E → C). Freely mix the "up" shape and "down" shape (high fifth vs. low fifth) chord to chord for variety.
- **Bossa nova, jazz-style:** source line = Horace Silver "Song for My Father." Rhythm stolen = **dotted quarter, eighth, dotted quarter, eighth** (counted "1, 2-and, 3, 4-and, 1"). Formula result: **root, fifth-fifth, root-root, fifth-fifth** per chord — already present in the source line. Applied to the sample chart: walk the same dotted-quarter/eighth rhythm through the whole progression. → cross-reference [[Bossa Nova (João Gilberto)]] (§ Bass) below.
- **Bossa nova, traditional Brazilian style:** source line = the Getz/Gilberto recording of "The Girl from Ipanema." Rhythm stolen = plain **half notes**. Formula result, worked chord by chord over D♭ → E♭ → A♭ → D♭: mostly **root-fifth** per chord, but with the option to **double up on the root** (root-root) instead of playing the fifth on some repeats, with octave placement varied between repeats too — credited with a shout-out to Adam Neely's mini-documentary on "The Girl from Ipanema" (external reference, linked in the source video's description, not fetched into this vault). Applied to the sample chart: two bars per chord, half notes, mixing root-fifth with occasional root-root doubling, "however you wanna do it."
- **Soul:** source line = "Sittin' on the Dock of the Bay" (Otis Redding), same song as the proof-by-subtraction demo above but revisited here for its rhythm. Rhythm stolen = **dotted quarter, eighth, quarter, quarter** (counted "1, 2-and, 3, 4"). Formula result: bassist **Duck Dunn's** actual pattern is **root, root, fifth, fifth** with a slide, but the video explicitly offers several alternate root/fifth orderings over the same rhythm as equally valid choices to try — root-root-root-fifth, root-fifth-fifth-fifth, and root-fifth-root-fifth — framed as "there is no one soul formula," just a library of soul rhythms to build up by repeating the three-step process on different reference tracks. Applied to the sample chart: the same rhythm tried with several of those root/fifth orderings per chord.
- **Rock:** source line = a clip from "Bonzo Goes to Bitburg" (Ramones). Rhythm stolen from the Ramones clip. Formula result: **no pre-existing root-fifth pattern to borrow** from this source, unlike the other four genres — "you're gonna have to make up your own." Applied to the sample chart: mostly root notes on the stolen rhythm, with fifths thrown in "wherever you want" rather than following a named pattern — explicitly framed as no single rock formula exists given the genre's internal variety, but the same three-step process still gets a usable starting point.

**Stated overall value of the method:** even a total beginner who can't yet learn full songs by ear can still run the three-step generator, because the only ear-training requirement is *hearing the rhythm* of a reference bassline — pitch/harmony knowledge isn't needed to steal step 2.
