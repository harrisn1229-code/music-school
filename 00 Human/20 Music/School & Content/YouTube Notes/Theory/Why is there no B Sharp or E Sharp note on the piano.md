# Why is there no B# or E# note on the piano?

## Synopsis

A full derivation of why the piano keyboard has exactly two gaps (no black key between E-F or B-C), tracing the chromatic scale's origin back to Gregorian chant, the historical order the five black keys were actually added in and why, and why B♯/E♯ still show up correctly in sheet music despite having no dedicated physical key. ## Techniques

See [[Chromatic Scale and Keyboard Layout]] for the full extraction. Summary: the keyboard's layout derives from the major scale's tone-tone-semitone-tone-tone-tone-semitone interval pattern, so gaps only exist where the scale itself is already a semitone apart (E-F, B-C). The five black keys were added historically one at a time for concrete harmonic reasons — B♭ first (to eliminate an undesirable F-B tritone in Gregorian chant), E♭ next (same tritone problem one step down once parallel-4th/5th harmony/organum appeared), then A♭/D♭/G♭ as harmony grew more complex, closing the set once every note had an available perfect 4th/5th above and below it. The earliest known instrument with the now-standard chromatic keyboard layout is a 1361 organ at Halberstadt Cathedral, predating harpsichord/piano by centuries. B♯/E♯ still appear correctly in sheet music (e.g. Beethoven's "Moonlight Sonata") not because the pitch differs from C/F, but because of how accidentals scope across a bar — writing B♯ instead of "C natural" avoids accidentally canceling a key signature's C♯ default for the rest of that bar.
