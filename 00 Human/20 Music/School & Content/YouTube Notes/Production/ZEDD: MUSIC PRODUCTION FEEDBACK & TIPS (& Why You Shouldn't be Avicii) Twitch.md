# ZEDD: MUSIC PRODUCTION FEEDBACK & TIPS (& Why You Shouldn't be Avicii) [Twitch]

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fqHvrBY-UiU)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fqHvrBY-UiU)

## Synopsis

A Twitch VOD of Zedd reacting live to viewer-submitted demo tracks and fielding chat questions, structured as reaction-feedback rather than a scripted lesson. He opens with a stated philosophy — honest critique over polite lying — then works through several submissions covering vocal presence, drum realism, bass attack, snare masking, and sidechain tricks, before pivoting to Q&A on publishing splits, favorite plugins, and his own creative philosophy (why he rarely collabs, why he doesn't chase algorithms, why "Stay" and "Clarity" worked because of the moment he made them in). One clip in this video — the "outdated, sounds like Avicii" critique of a submission — is the exact same segment already captured verbatim in the existing note [[How to Be Unique and Find Your Sound (With Less Stress)]] (a different, shorter Sol State compilation video); that specific critique is intentionally omitted below to avoid duplication, but everything else here is new material unique to this longer stream.

## Techniques

- **Give real feedback, not polite lying.** Zedd's stated approach when producer friends ask for his opinion: tell them what he actually thinks rather than defaulting to "it's awesome" — framed as the entire point of asking for feedback in the first place, and the operating principle for every critique that follows in the stream.
- **A vocal that reads as "far away" needs to be pulled forward and clarified, not just turned up.** On a submission where the vocal sounded distant (possibly recorded too far from the mic), his fix was more compression and more clarity/loudness so the vocal reads as being right in the listener's face — his own personal standard for how a lead vocal should feel, not just "louder." → [[Compression Staging]]
- **Drums stuck between "sounds real" and "sounds programmed" read as an unresolved uncanny middle — commit to one lane.** On the same submission, the drums weren't fully embracing either a realistic-kit character or an obviously programmed/electronic character; his note was to pick a direction rather than leaving them in between, since the in-between reads as unfinished rather than intentional.
- **Try a second layer of Auto-Tune rather than pushing one instance harder for a vocal that needs more obvious correction.** Suggested alongside "definitely more compression" and "voice too far in the background" — if a single Auto-Tune pass isn't giving enough correction, stacking a second layer on top is worth trying before cranking one instance to an extreme setting. → [[Vocal Tuning]], [[Auto-Tune Pro]]
- **Using presets is not a compromise — Zedd treats himself as a musician, not a sound designer, and optimizes for finishing good songs.** His own workflow for a submitted lead synth: browse presets until something in the right range shows up, then tweak from there, rather than sound-designing from scratch — explicitly says he isn't good at coding sound design and doesn't care about "purist" objections to preset use, because his job is making music, not proving sound-design chops.
- **Sound-design purism only actually matters when a track has no harmonic content to fall back on.** His distinction: if a piece of music is just noise/sound-design with no melody or chords underneath it, then the sound design itself has to carry the whole track and purity matters more; if there's real harmonic content (melody, chords), preset use or "impure" sound design isn't a real problem because the song doesn't live or die on originality of the raw sound.
- **Default is sidechaining the bass, but a "real bass" sometimes just gets a low cut instead.** His own rule of thumb in chat Q&A: roughly 90% of the time he sidechains the bass against the kick, but for a bass meant to read as more "real"/organic he'll sometimes skip the sidechain entirely and just cut the lows instead. → [[Sidechaining]]
- **Fake extra bass attack by layering in a trimmed-down transient from any percussive sample instead of over-compressing the bass.** Take any transient-heavy sample — a kick, a snap, a snare, doesn't matter which (he defaults to reusing the same kick already in the song) — trim it down to just a tiny fragment of its own attack (a few ms), sync it to the bass's rhythm, and blend it in instead of compressing the bass harder trying to squeeze out attack that isn't there; this is how tight, punchy patches (his Oliver comparison) get their default snap. → [[Bass Design]], [[Kick Design]]
- **When a snare doesn't "slap," try sidechaining specifically on top of the snare too, not just the kick.** His diagnosis on a submission with a buried snare: heavy compression/limiting on the master can be part of the problem, and the fix worth trying is adding sidechain specifically keyed to duck around the snare hit (not only the usual kick-triggered duck) so the snare gets its own moment of space. → [[Sidechaining]]
- **Skrillex's snares as the reference for "the whole mix ducks so the snare has room."** Counter-example he cites: when a Skrillex snare hits, the rest of the mix visibly ducks away around it, which is why the snare cuts through so hard — contrast with a mix where nothing makes room and the snare just gets buried under everything else.
- **A kick sitting directly under a thin/bright snare masks most of the snare's body — separate the two spectrally or pick a different snare, don't just turn the snare up.** Walked through live with an A/B "does this snare slap" chat poll (majority said no): if the kick's low-mid energy occupies the same narrow band the snare's small amount of body lives in, the kick masks it almost entirely no matter the level; for a bright/thin "weird" snare character specifically, you generally don't want a full kick sitting right behind it — either choose a snare type built to survive that kick, or actively carve the two apart in the spectrum. → [[Masking and Carving]], [[Snare Design]]
- **Make an oversized/long snare read as tight and "compressed" by sidechaining it hard to the kick with an LFO tool, instead of trimming the sample.** If a snare (or clap, or noise layer) feels too long or weird sitting directly on the kick, route it through something like [[LFO Tool]] and sidechain it aggressively off the kick so the tail gets sucked back in right after the hit — it reads as heavily compressed even though the sample itself was never edited or shortened. → [[Sidechaining]], [[LFO Tool]]
- **His label has no publishing rights over his music — publishing typically splits by who's actually in the room.** Publishing ownership sits with him and whoever he co-wrote with, usually split 50/50 for two writers or divided by the number of people who contributed when it's harder to attribute specific parts to specific people.
- **Favorite plugins, by job: Serum for "any sound you could ever want," Sausage Fattener for one-sided loudness/limiting, Gladiator for clarity/spectrum leads, Sylenth1 used almost exclusively for a long stretch of his career.** Serum praised as the most versatile VST he owns — genuinely capable of any sound if pushed. Sausage Fattener is narrow-purpose (makes things loud and limits them) but excellent at exactly that one job. Gladiator gets credit for "incredible" high-end clarity on his big leads/chords. Sylenth1 ("Silent") was his main synth for years. → [[Serum 2]], [[Sausage Fattener]]
- **Learn an actual instrument — his single best tip for getting good at producing faster.** Knowing an instrument means you know which note to play instead of guessing/hunting for it programmatically, which saves enormous amounts of time and lets you play parts in rather than reverse-engineering them — and instrument-played parts sound more real than parts pieced together by trial and error.
- **"There is no right and wrong in music" — his Steve Duda mastering-chain anecdote.** During the "Clarity" era his mastering chain was Waves Broadband EQ → an L2 limiter → a slight low cut, and a lot of deliberate clipping. Steve Duda reviewed it and told him, correctly by conventional standards, that most of it was technically wrong — Zedd's response was to accept that he was doing it "wrong" by the rules while still preferring how his own music sounded and continuing to do it his way; the lesson he draws is that correctness and taste aren't the same axis.
- **Turn your own mastering chain off before bouncing stems, or every stem inherits it and gets processed twice.** Direct warning in the stream: if you're mastering on your own master bus while working, remember to disable that chain before bouncing stems out — otherwise the stems carry your mastering baked in, and it gets stacked again by whoever masters (or you master) afterward. → [[Mix Handoff]]
- **Generally dislikes collaborating because taste differences turn into constant compromise, but the exception is a collaborator who shares his taste (Grey).** His stated reason for mostly avoiding collabs: differences of preference (e.g. one producer wants the snare one way, he wants it another) aren't "right vs. wrong," they're just friction and compromise every time — the one collaborator he specifically calls out as an exception is Grey, because they share taste and even sample libraries.
- **Make what genuinely feels real and exciting to you — his inverted definition of "selling out."** His stated creative rule: only release what feels real and exciting to him, because in his framing making music to satisfy what other people want IS the actual definition of selling out — the opposite of how the term usually gets used.
- **"Stay" worked partly because 100 BPM was genuinely new territory for him.** He credits some of that song's impact to how unfamiliar the tempo range felt while writing it — sitting with real goosebumps at something new to himself specifically, framed as one of the advantages of being established enough to try something outside his usual lane without worrying about the outcome.
- **Refuses to chase algorithms, and connects that directly to why he releases music slowly.** He explicitly calls out producers who watch what an algorithm rewards and adjust their output to feed it as "obnoxious," says he doesn't want to live that way, and ties his own famously slow release pace to the same principle — he only puts out music he's genuinely inspired by rather than optimizing for what performs.
