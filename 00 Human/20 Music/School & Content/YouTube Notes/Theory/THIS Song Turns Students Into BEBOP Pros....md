## Synopsis

Nathan Borton's chord-progression-by-chord-progression guide to soloing over Charlie Parker's "Donna Lee" — not a melody lesson (he points to a companion video for that) but a **vocabulary-building lesson**: for each harmonic segment of the tune he names the underlying device (bebop scale over a I chord, a static/non-resolving "tonic dominant," a secondary dominant borrowed from the IV, a backdoor dominant, a superimposed dominant over a minor ii chord, a diminished chord read as a rootless V7♭9, and a bebop-flavored turnaround using harmonic minor) and plays a worked example line over it. The lesson is dense with reusable jazz-guitar-soloing devices that generalize well beyond this one tune — several of them (the backdoor dominant, the diminished-as-rootless-♭9-dominant device, the bebop major/dominant scales themselves) directly confirm or extend material already documented on [[Bebop Vocabulary]], [[Diminished Chords]], and [[Turnarounds]]. The most valuable single idea is the **"tonic dominant"** framing — a dominant-7th chord that doesn't resolve down a fifth the way a normal V7 does, instead just sitting as its own stable static sound, soloed over with either its own dominant scale or the melodic minor scale built off its 5th. ## What's taught

- **Bebop major scale played through a I chord, deliberately altered at the very end to land on a chord tone of the coming V7 instead of continuing the scale straight** — the actual reusable device, not just "here's the bebop major scale." Over Ab6/Abmaj (I of Ab), starting from any chord tone of Ab major 6 (root, 3rd, 5th, or the 6th itself — he explicitly says "do this with each chord tone"), run the major bebop scale, but where the scale would normally continue on to its own next diatonic/passing tone, instead play a chord tone of the target **F7♭9 (F7alt)** — because harmonically that's where the line needs to go next, the coming chord takes priority over finishing the scale shape "correctly." Matches [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §1's bebop major row (Major + ♯5, passing tone between 5 and 6) — this is the practical application of that scale rather than a new formula. [~1:30] → [[Bebop Vocabulary]]
- **Landing point continues into a diminished arpeggio, then a chromatic walk-down — a three-part line-building recipe for a dominant-7♭9 chord.** Once the bebop-major line lands on a chord tone/extension of F7♭9, continue with (1) a **diminished arpeggio built up from that landed tone** (the ♭9 dominant shares its upper structure with a diminished 7th a half step below the resolving root — same "dim7 substitutes for 7♭9" relationship already documented on [[Diminished Chords]]), then (2) **chromatically walk down** to a chord tone/extension of the next chord, **B♭7**. Full worked example given note-by-note in the video. [~2:10] → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Altered Chords]]
- **"Tonic dominant" — the core reusable concept of the whole lesson: a dominant-7th chord that doesn't function as a normal V7 (it doesn't resolve down a fifth), but instead just sits as its own stable, static sound.** Applied here to B♭7 sitting where a plain diatonic ii chord (Bbm7) would normally go — "this chord is not really in the key of A♭... it's a dominant two chord." Two scale choices given for soloing over any tonic dominant: (1) the chord's own dominant (Mixolydian-family/bebop-dominant) scale, or (2) **the melodic minor scale built off the dominant chord's own 5th** — worked example: F melodic minor over B♭7, since F is the 5th of B♭7. Explicitly generalized ("whatever dominant chord we're playing"), so this is a portable rule, not a one-chord fact. [~5:05] → [[Modal Jazz]], [[Altered Chords]], [[Scale Modes Reference]]
- **A I→I7 move read functionally as a secondary dominant (V7/IV), with the specific guide tone to highlight named.** A♭maj7 becomes A♭7 and "wants to move to" D♭ major — A♭7 is acting as **V7/IV** (IV of A♭ is D♭). The move to emphasize: the A♭maj7's own major 7th (**G**) resolves down a half step to **G♭**, the ♭7 that turns A♭ into A♭7 — the classic I–I7–IV descending-guide-tone device, named here with the exact voice-leading note. Once there, play **5–1 language from G♭ (V) moving to D♭ major7 (I)**, i.e. treat G♭7 as if it were a real V7 of D♭ even though it's really the guide-tone byproduct of A♭ turning into a dominant. [~6:50] → [[Secondary Dominants]]
- **Backdoor dominant, confirmed with a worked example matching the existing [[Turnarounds]] "Backdoor" row exactly.** D♭major7 moves to G♭7 — "the back door dominant moving to one," i.e. the ♭VII7→I move already documented on [[Turnarounds]] (there generalized as B♭7→Cmaj7 in C; here it's the same shape a fifth lower, G♭7→D♭maj7). **Two soloing options given for the backdoor chord itself:** treat G♭7 as another **tonic dominant** (same device as the B♭7 above — its own scale or melodic minor off its 5th), *or* substitute **D♭ minor 6** and use minor-key language over it (parallel-minor substitution, the same family of move as [[Diminished Chords]]'s Keith-Jarrett-style "commit to the substitution one chord early" trick, though here it's substituted directly on the backdoor chord itself rather than pre-announced). [~7:35] → [[Turnarounds]] § *Backdoor*
- **A minor ii–V superimposition device: reading a half-diminished ii chord as a minor triad "over" its own root, then finding and playing that minor triad's own V7 as a bebop-scale line landing on the target.** Transcript is garbled on the exact chord letter here (auto-captions render it "G flat minor 7 flat flat 5," which does not fit the tune's harmony as spoken elsewhere — almost certainly a mis-transcription of a half-diminished ii chord, most likely **C minor 7♭5**, flagged rather than guessed further). The device itself is clear and fully generalizable: (1) hear the half-diminished ii chord as **a minor triad built on its own ♭3, "over" the chord's root** (his example: hear "G♭m7♭5" as "a B♭ minor triad over G" — i.e. reading a half-diminished chord's upper minor-triad structure independently of its root); (2) treat that minor triad **as if it were itself a ii chord** and find *its* V7 a fourth above (B♭ minor's own V would be **E♭7**); (3) play an **E♭ dominant bebop scale over the original half-diminished ii chord**, landing on a chord tone/extension of the real V7 (the "5-7 chord"), then resolve to the real i. Framed explicitly as "superimposing a dominant chord over the two chord" and as the bebop-flavored alternative to the plainer option (ignore the ii chord entirely, play straight 5–1 minor-key language targeting minor-i chord tones instead of major). [~10:15] → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Chord Substitution]], [[Bebop Vocabulary]]
- **Related-sounds substitution: treating a dominant-ii chord and a parallel minor-6 chord as interchangeable soloing targets because they're harmonically related.** Over `Fm6 → C7 → Fm6 → B°`, ignore the C7 entirely and play straight F minor ideas throughout (same logic as ignoring a backdoor dominant's own quality and substituting the parallel minor, above) — **and** connect those F-minor lines to B♭ dominant bebop ideas across the whole progression except the final diminished chord, explicitly because "B♭7 and F minor 6 are related sounds" (B♭7's upper structure shares tones with F minor — both live in the same modal-minor orbit). Alternative, more literal option given too: just outline C7 moving to Fm6 using plain 5–1 language. [~11:45] → [[Modal Jazz]], [[Chord Substitution]]
- **Diminished chord read as a rootless V7♭9 of the NEXT chord — the same device already documented on [[Diminished Chords]], now confirmed in Donna Lee's own changes with the exact resolution named.** The B° in the `Fm6–C7–Fm6–B°` progression resolves up a half step into C minor 7 next — so B° is read as **G7♭9** (a diminished 7th a major 3rd below the dominant's root, i.e. the dominant's upper structure minus its root — the identical relationship [[Diminished Chords]] already documents as "7♭9 = rootless dim7 + root a major 3rd below," run here in the direction a soloist actually meets it: spot the diminished chord, identify what it resolves into a half step up, and play 5–1 language outlining that dominant moving to its target minor). General rule stated directly: **"when you see a diminished chord moving up a half step below a chord, it usually acts as the five of that next chord."** [~12:30] → [[Diminished Chords]]
- **Turnaround: two named approaches, the second one a full worked harmonic-minor-to-short-2–5 line-building method.** Back to the I chord at the tune's end, two options given: (1) the plain option — simple descending 2–5 language moving down in whole steps, covering each two-five in the descending chain (same chained-2–5 logic as [[Turnarounds]]'s "sharp-4 walkdown" section, applied here as a straightforward whole-step-down sequence rather than that specific named chain); (2) the bebop option, worked in full: treat the **first 2–5 of the turnaround as a minor 2–5 into the ii chord**, and over that first 2–5 play the **harmonic minor scale built on the target the minor 2–5 is pulling toward** (his worked example: over "C minor 7♭5" as the ii — same chord-letter caveat as above — start on a chord tone (first pass: the **3rd, E♭**; second pass: the **root**) and **walk DOWN the B♭ harmonic minor scale**, since B♭ is where the minor 2–5 resolves), landing back on the *same scale-degree relationship* to the new chord that you started with relative to the old one (start on the 3rd of the ii chord, land on the 3rd of the target chord four beats later) — then **tag short 2–5 language onto the end of that harmonic-minor run** to finish resolving into the second 2–5 of the turnaround. Demonstrated twice, starting from two different chord tones, to show the landing-relationship rule holds either way. [~13:30] → [[Turnarounds]], [[The ii-V-I]]
