# Tritone Substitution

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Jazz Harmony]]. A **tritone substitution** ("tritone sub") swaps a dominant chord for the dominant chord built a tritone (6 semitones) away — same resolving pull, completely different root motion and color.

## The mechanism

If a progression is C – G7 – C, the tritone sub swaps G7 for D♭7 (a tritone from G): **C – D♭7 – C**. It still resolves convincingly because of what the two chords have in common, not what's different.

Every dominant 7th chord's tension comes from its internal tritone — G7's tritone is between F and B (the ♭7 and the 3rd). A dominant chord built a tritone away from the original root **contains that exact same tritone**: D♭7 also spans F and B (spelled/functioning differently, but the same two pitches). So the pull-to-resolution is fully preserved even though the bass now moves chromatically (D♭→C, a semitone) instead of by a falling fifth (G→C).

**Practical effect**: tritone-subbing a chain of dominants (e.g., a 3-6-2-5 turnaround, Dm7–G7–Cm7–F7) turns fifth-based root motion into a smooth, entirely chromatic descending bassline — every root drops a single semitone from chord to chord instead of jumping around the circle of fifths.

## Origin case study — "Garota de Ipanema" / "The Girl from Ipanema"

The original recording (Pery Ribeiro) uses a plain, un-subbed ii–V–I; nearly every subsequent cover/arrangement tritone-subs the V chord, to the point that most published lead sheets today show the substituted version rather than Jobim's original. This use is the likely direct influence behind the tritone sub in the *Wii Shop Channel* theme, itself an explicit bossa nova homage to Jobim's writing.

## "One Note Samba" — a double sub

Also Jobim (earliest recording João Gilberto): **two separate tritone subs in the same tune** — the V chord (F7, key of B♭) is subbed to B7; the vi chord's own dominant (which would normally set up a 3-6-2-5, Dm7–G7–Cm7–F7) is instead subbed to D♭7. Stacking both subs turns the whole turnaround into a fully chromatic-descending bassline.

## Pop/rock examples — found by ear, not by theory

Several songwriters land on the tritone-sub sound without ever encountering the term — worth remembering when analyzing pop material: the theory is a useful *lens* after the fact, not necessarily the writer's own mental model.

- **"It Keeps You Runnin'" (The Doobie Brothers)** — same tritone-sub-of-a-3-6-2-5 device as "One Note Samba."
- **"Toxic" (Britney Spears)** — minor-key iii→ii→♭II chromatic descent, readable as tritone subs of a 6-2-5.
- **"Things We Said Today" (The Beatles)** — the middle-eight's second rotation swaps the plain V (E) for B♭.
