# Borrowed Chords (Modal Interchange)

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Chords]] / [[Chord Progressions]]. A **borrowed chord** is a chord taken from a **parallel** key/mode (same tonic, different scale) and dropped into an otherwise-diatonic progression for color. Also called **modal interchange**. It's the single biggest source of "sophisticated pop" harmony — the Beatles, Radiohead, and film music live here.

## The idea
- Every major key has a **[[Parallel Minor]]** (same tonic — C major ↔ C minor). Modal interchange borrows chords from that parallel minor (or other parallel modes: Dorian, Mixolydian…) without fully changing key.
- The borrowed chord contains a note outside the home scale — that chromatic note is the "color."

## The workhorses (borrowed from parallel minor, in a MAJOR key)
| Borrowed chord | In C major | Sound |
|---|---|---|
| **iv** (minor four) | Fm | the "heartbreak" chord — IV → iv → I |
| **♭VI** | A♭ | epic, cinematic lift |
| **♭VII** | B♭ | rock/Mixolydian move (I–♭VII–IV) |
| **♭III** | E♭ | darker, bluesy |
| **♭II** (Neapolitan) | D♭ | dramatic pre-dominant |
| **iiø7** (half-dim) | Dø7 | minor-key ii, jazzy pre-dominant |

- The most emotionally reliable is **IV → iv → I**: the major subdominant brightening, then its minor version darkening, then home. Instant bittersweetness.
- From a major key you can also borrow the whole **Mixolydian ♭VII** (rock) or **Dorian** color.
- **♭II isn't actually borrowed from the parallel minor** despite the naming pattern — see [[Neapolitan Sixth]] for the classical convention it almost always follows in practice: near-exclusive use in **first inversion** (hence "Neapolitan **6th**," not "the Neapolitan sixth chord"), resolving to a dominant 7th in 3rd inversion or a cadential 6/4.
- Borrowed ii° is always used in first inversion (it's diminished — same rule as diminished triads generally, see [[Part-Writing]]). The two altered-root chords (♭III, ♭VI) resolve better with contrary motion than strict alteration-direction resolution, to avoid parallel octaves.

## Related: parallel-minor modulation
When a section *stays* in the parallel minor for a while (not just one chord), that's **[[Modulation (Key Change)|parallel-minor modulation]]** — e.g. a bright major verse dropping to the parallel minor for the bridge, then returning ([[🎵 THE BEATLES - Here There and Everywhere]]).

## Hear it
- **[[🎵 THE BEATLES - Here There and Everywhere]]** — middle 8 drops to G minor (parallel minor) then slides home.
- **iv chord** ("heartbreak"): countless Beatles endings and pop ballads use IV → iv → I.

- **A plain-language on-ramp to modal interchange: "flip the major/minor quality (polarity) of any diatonic chord."** Same mechanism as the parallel-key borrowing above, taught bottom-up: flip a major-key chord's quality (I→i, ii→II, IV→iv, etc., omitting the diminished vii°) or a minor-key chord's quality (♭III→iii, iv→IV) to get the same borrowed-chord palette without needing the parallel-scale theory first. **Extension rule for voicing a flipped chord**: keep as many notes of the HOME key static as possible — e.g. flipping C major's ii (D minor→D major) only actually changes one note (F→F♯), so every other white-key note is still safe to stack as an extension on top of the new chord. Combine with inversions for bass variety, and note this generalizes past plain parallel major/minor — any parallel mode (Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian) is fair game, not just the obvious major↔minor pair. → [[Chord Progressions]], [[Extended Chords]], [[Chromatic Bass Lines]] — Max Konyi, [Flip Your Chords for Better Progressions!](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eNeQQAr7R1c)
- **"Magic mode" (mixed mode) — borrow from ALL parallel modes on the same tonic simultaneously, not just one at a time.** Rather than picking a single parallel mode to occasionally borrow from, treat every note from every parallel mode on the tonic as always available to combine freely — the plain-language ceiling case of modal interchange.
