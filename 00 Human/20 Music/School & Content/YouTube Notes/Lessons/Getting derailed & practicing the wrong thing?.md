# Getting derailed & practicing the wrong thing?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d9l4SOLK0v8)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d9l4SOLK0v8)

## Synopsis

A direct Q&A follow-up to [[How I Organize My Guitar Practice (So I Never Want to Stop)]] (the video that introduced [[The Practice Priority Pyramid]]). A viewer commented on that video describing a familiar trap: they set a goal (learn to improvise), laid out a sensible path toward it (analyze progressions, work scale options across positions, isolate changes with a looper, do some ear training) — and then their **technique** kept letting them down (can't play fast enough, fluffed sweeps, string-skipping/synchronization issues), which felt like a separate problem that "derailed" them off the real path. Their question: how do you fit technique work and goal-directed practice together without one hijacking the other? Jared's answer, worked through with two live case studies from his own practicing the day before and the day of filming (a Super Mario Bros. theme arrangement and a 12-bar-blues improvisation drill), is that **the framing itself is the bug**: technique isn't a separate category competing with the goal-path — it's an ingredient *on* that path, and it has to be targeted exactly as specifically as anything else on the checklist. He builds this into a concrete, repeatable loop (play the real thing → hit a "red flag" → isolate and drill the *exact* flub → return to real playing ASAP) and two named techniques (the "speed burst" and cross-string transfer) for actually closing a technique gap once it's been correctly identified. This is a genuinely actionable companion piece to the goals/vision-level material in [[The Practice Priority Pyramid]] and [[Vision-Driven Practice and the Overwhelm Reflex]] — where those answer "what should be on my practice list and why," this video answers "what do I do the moment something on that list breaks down mid-session."

## What's taught

### The reframe: technique is not a separate path, it's an ingredient on the same one
The viewer's question implicitly treats "working toward my goal" (scales, positions, ear training) and "fixing my technique" as two competing uses of practice time — pick one, feel bad about neglecting the other. Jared's core correction: **technique is needed to execute anything** — it's not a fifth category alongside scale-options/positions/ear-training, it's the thing that lets all of those actually come out of your hands. So the fix isn't choosing between "goal work" and "technique work," it's making the technique work **as tightly aligned with the goal as everything else on the list** — not a detour into generic technique exercises.

### Two analogies for "the wrong ingredient"
- **The burger/sandwich analogy.** If a burger is missing its bun, grabbing a baguette or croutons instead "solves" the shape of the gap (something bread-like) but not the actual gap — the burger still isn't right. Applied to guitar: when a specific passage breaks down technically, reaching for a generic, unrelated technique exercise is the baguette. Find and drill the **exact** missing ingredient (the exact flub, the exact arpeggio, the exact scale slur), not an adjacent substitute.
- **The golf-fitness analogy.** A neighbor runs a golf-specific fitness business — targeted exercises (e.g. weighted medicine-ball rotations) built to strengthen the specific muscles/motions used in a golf swing. His pitch: telling a golfer to "just get fit, go hiking on weekends" will genuinely improve their cardio, but won't specifically make them a better golfer. Same logic for guitar technique — general technique drilling has value, but *targeted* technique work aimed at the exact motion your goal requires is what actually moves the needle on that goal.

### Case study 1 — Super Mario Bros. theme arrangement (synchronization + cross-string transfer)
Working an arrangement of the Super Mario Bros. theme for fun/a planned video, Jared noticed a specific weak spot: a finger-1–2-to-4–3 transition where the two fingers weren't landing at exactly the same time (a synchronization problem, not a speed problem per se).
- **The "speed burst" drill** — for a spot like this: play it **slow, then fast, then slow again**, repeated as a set, specifically on the exact transition that's breaking down. *(General starting point — the source doesn't give a fixed rep count or exact tempo ratio for slow/fast/slow, just the pattern itself.)*
- **Cross-string transfer** — a favorite general-purpose technique-building move: take any lick, arrangement passage, or fragment (classical/solo-guitar arrangement material named specifically) and **deliberately move it to a different set of strings**, without worrying whether it sounds "weird" or awkward there. The point isn't to produce something musical on the new strings — it's that displacing a shape from its original comfortable position builds strength/flexibility/dexterity that transfers back to the original spot. Worked over two days (practiced "today and yesterday"); Jared reports it already measurably improved in that short window.
- **Not every technique problem needs new invention** — Jared notes he still does general technique exercises and a standing daily warm-up (the "best warm-up exercise," free PDF at soundguitarlessons.com — not detailed in this video) separately from this targeted work; the targeted drill is *in addition to*, not a replacement for, general conditioning.

### Case study 2 — 12-bar blues improv (alternate hybrid picking + octaves, the full red-flag loop demonstrated live)
Working on a technique he calls **"alternate hybrid picking"** — alternate picking combined simultaneously with the middle (m) and ring/annular (a) fingers — applied to playing in octaves, over a 12-bar blues loop (four bars B♭7, two bars E♭7, back to B♭7, then V–IV–I–V turnaround). Demonstrated live, in real time:
1. Try to improvise for real over the loop while bringing in the octave/alternate-hybrid-picking technique — it comes out sloppy.
2. **Don't immediately default to a generic technique exercise.** Instead, workshop the *exact* motion against the backing track: strip away concern for actual notes/improvisation content entirely, focus purely on "can I keep this clean, can I stay relaxed" at the current tempo.
3. Once clean at that tempo, **push the tempo up** on the isolated technique, still without worrying about note content.
4. **Recombine with real musical content incrementally** — once the technique itself holds up, start layering it back onto actual improvisation (e.g. outlining the chord tones of each change using the octave/hybrid-picking technique, then mapping the next chord the same way) so the technique is being tested inside real harmonic movement, not just in isolation.
5. **When it breaks again, that's expected — go back to the isolated drill, not backward through the whole system.** The loop is iterative, not one-and-done: technique breaks → isolate and drill → test back inside real playing → breaks again → repeat.

### The "red-flag" method — the core actionable loop (the throughline of the whole video)
This is the general method both case studies demonstrate and the single most reusable takeaway:
1. **Play the real thing** — the actual goal, the actual piece/improvisation/passage, not a generic exercise.
2. **When something breaks down, treat that specifically as a "red flag"** — a data point, not a failure. "That was a problem" / "that's a limitation" is useful information, not a reason to feel bad.
3. **Go directly to the exact spot, not the general area.** Identify precisely what broke (which two fingers, which string transition, which tempo) rather than a vague sense that "the sweep section" or "the fast part" is hard.
4. **Build or reach for the most direct, targeted solution to that exact flub** — a speed burst, a cross-string transfer, an isolated-tempo workshop against the backing track — whatever actually targets the specific mechanical gap, not a generic catch-all technique routine.
5. **Test it small and fast — don't commit to a long, unverified detour.** Explicit warning: "don't go off on a monthlong practice of this thing that you think was the solution, because it might not be the perfect solution." Try it, test it briefly, and re-evaluate.
6. **Return to real playing as soon as possible.** The isolated drill is a brief detour to fix one thing, not a new destination — the goal is always to get back to actually playing the target material, where the next red flag will surface naturally.
7. **Repeat the whole loop continuously**, alternating between real playing and short targeted technique detours, rather than doing either exclusively.
8. **Use your own red flags as your practice curriculum**, not someone else's generic list. Direct quote (paraphrased): instead of using what a teacher or a book says to practice as the practice item, use your own real, felt limitations from your own actual playing — that's framed as the highest-leverage source of practice material there is, and the mechanism for becoming "your own best teacher."

### Why technique gains feel different from conceptual gains (expectation-setting)
Technique (strength, flexibility, dexterity) is physically slow to build — described via a **watering-a-plant analogy**: you don't see the plant grow while you're watering it; growth is invisible in the moment and only shows up after repeated sessions with recovery time between them (muscle/tendon adaptation needs rest, not just repetition). This is contrasted directly with **conceptual/vocabulary learning** (a new scale, a new song, a new chord shape), which can feel like real, visible progress within a single sitting *because the underlying technique is usually already in place* — you're just adding a new pattern on top of existing physical capability. Practical implication: don't judge a technique drill's value by same-session results, and don't get discouraged when a technique fix takes days/weeks rather than one practice session — that's the expected shape of technique gains specifically, not a sign the approach is wrong. Ear training is named as having the same slow-gain shape.
