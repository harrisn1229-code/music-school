> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **Stack thirds from the root — two give a triad, three a seventh chord, six a 13th**
> One operation repeated. Each extra third adds one note, so a 13th chord is seven notes deep.
>
> **Q2.** **Major 4+3 C E G · minor 3+4 C E♭ G · diminished 3+3 C E♭ G♭ · augmented 4+4 C E G♯**
> Quality is an order of two intervals rather than a fact memorised per chord.
>
> **Q3.** **The 3rd, up a half step**
> Only the middle note moves, which is why one semitone flips the whole chord's mood.
>
> **Q4.** **Secundal, quartal, quintal — and quartal harmony has no honest name in the tertian system**
> *Rubric (5):* names all three families with their stacking interval (2) · says quartal falls outside the tertian naming convention and gets mislabelled, usually as sus4 (2) · concludes that a chord name describes harmony built in thirds specifically, so the naming system is a construct with a domain (1)
> Secundal stacks 2nds and produces clusters that read as timbre rather than harmony; quartal stacks 4ths; quintal stacks 5ths and respells as quartal, so it is not a separate system. Every symbol in the standard vocabulary measures a distance from a stack of thirds — "sus" says a third was replaced, "add" says one was added, "♭5" says one moved. A stack of 4ths gives those symbols nothing to measure against, so the nearest label is a convenience that misdescribes the chord. The conclusion is not that the system fails but that it has a boundary, and knowing where it sits stops you hunting for a symbol that never existed.
>
> **Q5.** **maj7, dominant 7, minor 7, minor-major 7 — from major or minor triad × ♭7 or natural 7**
> One more third on the triad, with two choices underneath it.
>
> **Q6.** **Dominant 7 on V and half-diminished on vii — either one names its parent key**
> A bare maj7 could be I or IV; a bare m7 could be ii, iii or vi. These two are unambiguous.
>
> **Q7.** **A complete lower structure — a full triad for 9 and 11, a full seventh chord for 13. Not octave placement.**
> Pull the 3rd and the added 2 stays a 2; pull the 7th and the added 6 stays a 6.
>
> **Q8.** **9th drop the 5th · 11th drop the 3rd · 13th drop the 5th, 9th and especially the 11th**
> The 11th chord loses its 3rd because the 3rd and the 11 sit a half step apart and clash.
>
> **Q9.** **A chord played over a bass note that is not its own root — built from the ♭6 of a major key**
> *Also accepted:* the natural 7 of a minor key
>
> **Q10.** **The bass note decides the chord; a bass note already in the upper triad gives only an inversion**
> *Rubric (5):* says the upper chord stays fixed while the bass note changes the composite's name and function (2) · says a bass note that is already a tone of the upper chord yields an inversion and nothing new (1) · names at least two resulting chord types (1) · says the difficulty is the bass motion and voice leading connecting two readings (1)
> An E minor triad placed on the ♭6 of E major reads as a ♭6 major 7♯11 with its own root underneath, a ♭7 dominant 13 a fourth below, a iv minor 6/9 a major third below, an altered dominant, and two rarer suspended-dominant colours. The hand does not move. What stops it being a new chord is the bass note belonging to the upper triad already — then you have an inversion, and the arithmetic gives nothing back. The hard part is not naming these but chaining them, because an upper structure arriving without a line into it reads as a wrong bass note rather than as harmony.
>
> **Q11.** **Sus replaces a chord tone · add adds one · altered shifts one a half step**
> Three different operations that all get called colour, which is why they get confused.
>
> **Q12.** **C E G D · C E G B♭ D · C D G — the 3rd decides, and the 7th separates add9 from 9**
> With the 3rd present the D is a 9; with the 3rd replaced by it, it is a 2.
>
> **Q13.** **♭5, ♯5, ♭9, ♯9, ♯11 — and do not alter a note the melody is sitting on**
> The movable tones are the 5th and the 9th, sometimes the 11th.
>
> **Q14.** **Because the symbol says which key and mode the dominant belongs to, not which notes are literally in it**
> Which is why G7♭9 and G7♭13 are interchangeable in writing, and 7♯11 and 7♭5 share one voicing.
>
> **Q15.** **B D F♯ G♯ — the added 6th is always the major 6th, even on a minor triad. C6 shares its notes with Am7.**
> "Minor" names the triad only. A minor triad with a genuine ♭6 is written m♭6.
>
> **Q16.** **On a jazz chart it means C major plus the 6th; in classical figured bass it means first inversion, C/E**
> The superscript 6 in figured bass measures the outer interval of an inverted triad.
>
> **Q17.** **Diminished 7 is 3+3+3 with four names · augmented is 4+4 with three**
> The interval pattern repeats, so a different note taken as root gives the same pitches back.
>
> **Q18.** **Buys transposability · costs an audible root, a true inversion, and any function of its own**
> Slide a dim7 shape three frets and it is the same chord — which is why the ear cannot find its root.
>
> **Q19.** **Is any note the leading note of what follows? No is a passing chord, yes a dominant substitute.**
> *Rubric (5):* states the test in terms of the following chord's leading note (2) · no means passing, yes means a functioning dominant substitute (1) · gives one chord doing both jobs, e.g. ♯iv° to I against ♯iv° to V (1) · concludes that function is contextual rather than a property of the chord (1)
> Symmetry took the diminished chord's root away, so it carries no function of its own and the job has to be read off what follows. ♯iv° returning to I contains none of I's leading note, so it decorates: it walks 4̂ up to 5̂ and drives nothing. The identical chord moving to V does contain 5̂'s leading note, so it functions, standing in for II7, and it resolves. The general lesson is the one the three functions already teach about ordinary triads, applied to the chord where it is least obvious — function is a relationship between two chords, not a property one chord owns.
>
> **Q20.** **Which chord tone is in the bass — on guitar the equivalent decision is which tones to drop**
> Keep the 3rd and 7th, drop the 5th first and the root second.
>
> **Q21.** **Bass as root · relate every upper note to it as a degree · name from those degrees. B♭/C is C7sus4(9).**
> B♭ is C's ♭7, D the 9, F the 11, and there is no 3rd — hence sus4.
>
> **Q22.** **When the bass note is already a chord tone, and when the composite has no 3rd and no 7th**
> The first is only an inversion. The second is genuinely ambiguous, and context has to decide.
>
> **Q23.** **A name exposes a relationship, not a fact — pick the one matching the sound and the voice leading**
> Not the spelling with the fewest accidentals, and not the one a chart would print.
>
> **Q24.** **E G B C♯ is Em6, C♯m7♭5, or a rootless A9 — and each name predicts a different next chord**
> *Rubric (5):* gives a set of four pitches with three valid names (1) · names all three readings correctly (2) · says each name implies a different resolution (1) · concludes the name records an intention about what follows, so the ambiguity is a licence rather than a defect (1)
> Called Em6 the chord is a coloured minor and behaves like one. Called C♯m7♭5 it is half-diminished and wants an F♯ dominant after it, heading to a minor tonic. Called A9 with the root left to the bass it is a dominant, and it wants to fall a fifth to D. The pitches decide none of that. A diminished 7th gives you four such readings and an augmented triad three, and in every case the name is where you record what you intend the next bar to do. Treating the name as a fact about the notes throws that information away and leaves you asking "what is this chord really", which is the question the system cannot answer and does not need to.
