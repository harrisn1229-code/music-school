# This is how a PRO makes music (16 music production tips from Morgan Page)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=08FTjnFDmtw)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=08FTjnFDmtw)

## Synopsis
A short interview-style breakdown where veteran producer/DJ Morgan Page shares hard-won lessons from a long career, spanning gear discipline, songwriting and melody craft, mixing and mastering workflow, session hygiene and backups, frequency-carving mistakes, and sustainable daily work habits. Sol State frames it as "how a pro makes music," and Page's answers lean less on specific plugin settings and more on judgment calls: knowing when a gear purchase is a distraction rather than a real upgrade, engineering unresolved tension into a hook instead of let it resolve too easily, being honest about what an outside mastering engineer will and won't fix, unlearning an old habit of over-carving frequencies with steep high-pass filters, and resisting the myth that longer studio hours produce better work.

## Techniques
- **Treat gear as a hobby trap, not a shortcut — leverage what you already own instead of chasing the next purchase.** It's easy to lose real production time down an analog-gear rabbit hole convinced the next synth will "suddenly write hit songs" — it won't; the more valuable question now is how to combine and leverage gear already owned in new ways, like running the Ableton arpeggiator against an analog keyboard to generate new sounds and combinations out of existing tools. → [[Arpeggiator]] — Morgan Page (Sol State), [This is how a PRO makes music (16 music production tips from Morgan Page)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=08FTjnFDmtw)
- **Layer sparingly and cut ruthlessly — mute each layer, and if you don't miss it, delete it.** It's very easy to go overboard layering and convince yourself a mix needs eight layers when three will do; anything redundant just masks frequencies that another layer already owns, so be brutal about removing what isn't earning its place. → [[Layering and Stacking]] — Morgan Page (Sol State), [This is how a PRO makes music (16 music production tips from Morgan Page)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=08FTjnFDmtw)
- **Build hooks and melodies on unresolved tension, not full resolution.** The single biggest realization behind writing something catchy: a melody or chord progression that resolves too easily, or lyrics built on rhymes that are too smooth/easy, won't stick — a loop should "repeat and not satisfy itself," a lesson credited to deadmau5. Ear-worms all share something that jumps out as unique but withholds full resolution, satisfying the tension only partially before looping back around. → [[Melody Writing]] — Morgan Page (Sol State), [This is how a PRO makes music (16 music production tips from Morgan Page)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=08FTjnFDmtw)
- **Use stable vs. unstable scale tones (Hooktheory) to engineer that tension deliberately.** You don't need to go deep into music theory, but understanding which scale degrees read as "stable" (resolved, at rest) versus "unstable" (wanting to move) gives a concrete tool for building melodies and chord progressions that create — and only partially release — tension, rather than landing back at the root and going flat/boring. → [[Melody Writing]] — Morgan Page (Sol State), [This is how a PRO makes music (16 music production tips from Morgan Page)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=08FTjnFDmtw)
- **Test every idea on a real piano before trusting the production.** Play the chord progression and sing it over a real instrument (his own seven-year-old Steinway) to judge the cadence — if it's not compelling stripped down like that, no amount of sound design, presets, or chord voicings will save it; production supports a strong idea, it doesn't create one. → [[Melody Writing]] — Morgan Page (Sol State), [This is how a PRO makes music (16 music production tips from Morgan Page)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=08FTjnFDmtw)
- **Write for timelessness, not the current trend.** It's worth investing extra effort into songs that can be transformed and reshaped as the sound of the moment evolves, rather than chasing a production style that will date the song quickly. — Morgan Page (Sol State), [This is how a PRO makes music (16 music production tips from Morgan Page)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=08FTjnFDmtw)
- **Keep an organized reference library matched by key and tempo, not just genre.** Drag reference tracks into a key-detection tool (Mixed In Key) so you can compare keys and tempos directly against your own — both make a real difference to how a mixdown translates and sits next to the tracks you're referencing. → [[Referencing and Translation]] — Morgan Page (Sol State), [This is how a PRO makes music (16 music production tips from Morgan Page)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=08FTjnFDmtw)
- **Study a "gold standard" scene for what full, consistent low end actually sounds like.** He points to a cluster of Dutch mastering engineers as the bar for huge, crisp masters with real bass presence that stay consistent release to release, attributing it partly to a genuinely collaborative culture between engineers rather than any single trick. → [[Referencing and Translation]] — Morgan Page (Sol State), [This is how a PRO makes music (16 music production tips from Morgan Page)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=08FTjnFDmtw)
- **Get your own mix to 80-90% before outsourcing the final polish, and know exactly what an outside engineer will and won't fix.** He uses Wired Masters for stem mastering to close that last 10-20%, but is blunt that a fresh set of ears isn't going to go in and fix an off sidechain, a latency issue, or a weak hook — those have to be solved before handoff; what you're paying for is perspective, not a repair job. → [[Mix Handoff]] — Morgan Page (Sol State), [This is how a PRO makes music (16 music production tips from Morgan Page)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=08FTjnFDmtw)
- **Don't try to fix a mix by crushing the master-bus limiter at the end — narrow the dynamic range earlier in the chain instead.** Pulling the master limiter's threshold down and dumping several dB of gain reduction there to "squash everything together nicely" is, in his words, the worst way to do it; parallel compression and limiting on individual busses and tracks earlier in the signal flow gets a better result and avoids painting yourself into a corner on the master bus. → [[Compression Staging]] — Morgan Page (Sol State), [This is how a PRO makes music (16 music production tips from Morgan Page)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=08FTjnFDmtw)
- **Back up every session to both cloud and physical media, and bounce all your stems down every single time.** After 15-20 years in the game he's watched plugin companies fold, get acquired, or rename products (Auto-Tune, iZotope's "Ozone" losing the "iZotope" from its saved name) — enough version/name drift that opening an old session years later can mean a plugin simply won't load, tuning goes off, and a vocal you need for a licensing deal is suddenly broken. → [[Committing and Printing]] — Morgan Page (Sol State), [This is how a PRO makes music (16 music production tips from Morgan Page)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=08FTjnFDmtw)
- **Don't let fear of being hacked stop you from using cloud backup.** He cites a well-known producer who skipped Dropbox specifically to avoid a hacking risk, then lost four terabytes of sessions to a dead motherboard with no cloud copy to fall back on — a hardware failure with zero backup is the far likelier disaster. → [[Committing and Printing]] — Morgan Page (Sol State), [This is how a PRO makes music (16 music production tips from Morgan Page)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=08FTjnFDmtw)
- **Treat "high-pass everything into tidy frequency pockets" as bad advice you'll eventually have to unlearn.** A piece of advice he followed for years and later had to walk back: chopping the mix into strict frequency ranges and steeply high-passing anything that doesn't "need" a given band (including sends, like high-passing a reverb at 300 Hz) sounds tidy in theory but actually thins the mix out the more plugins and sharper EQ moves get stacked on top of it. → [[Masking and Carving]] — Morgan Page (Sol State), [This is how a PRO makes music (16 music production tips from Morgan Page)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=08FTjnFDmtw)
- **Watch the ~100-150 Hz "second octave" specifically — over-aggressive high-passing quietly guts punch, not just mud.** Realized only recently: years of instinctively keeping the bass and high-passing everything else was losing a meaningful amount of information in that low-mid region — some buildup there is real and needs managing, but treating it as pure mud to always high-pass away produces thin, sub-heavy mixes with no punch. → [[Masking and Carving]] — Morgan Page (Sol State), [This is how a PRO makes music (16 music production tips from Morgan Page)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=08FTjnFDmtw)
- **Ditch the belief that a 12-hour day is the professional standard — you're probably only getting three or four genuinely good creative hours.** Working overtime doesn't produce better work; it produces blindness to what the song actually sounds like and mixes you undo and redo in circles. He points to a book cataloguing famous artists' daily routines, where the common thread among legendary writers and musicians is a focused 3-4 hour daily work block, not marathon sessions. — Morgan Page (Sol State), [This is how a PRO makes music (16 music production tips from Morgan Page)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=08FTjnFDmtw)
- **Find your natural resting point and stop before you're forcing it.** You don't always know where that point is in the moment, so push a session as far as it'll go while it's flowing — but the moment it starts to feel like you're forcing something rather than building on momentum, that's the signal to take a break rather than grind through it. — Morgan Page (Sol State), [This is how a PRO makes music (16 music production tips from Morgan Page)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=08FTjnFDmtw)
