## What's taught

- **The C minor blues form used throughout, with its harmonic ambiguity spelled out.** Four bars of Cm
  (tonic minor), two bars of Fm (iv, tonic minor of the IV) sometimes preceded by a signal C7, back to
  Cm, then a minor ii–V turnaround — but "most of the time" the ii is replaced by its **tritone
  substitute**: D♭7 (tritone sub of the V-of-V / secondary dominant) resolving back to C, then G7 to
  restart the form. Minor blues has fewer distinct chords than a major blues and is framed as a later
  (post-bebop, 50s/60s-and-modal-era) development specifically because it hands the soloist long static
  stretches instead of fast-moving changes.
- **Tonic minor (Cm), scale choice 1 — melodic minor.** The scale he says most active Coltrane material
  actually draws from, even though the Mr. P.C. head itself isn't in melodic minor. The **major 7th**
  is named as melodic minor's single defining, must-emphasize note against a plain minor chord (spell
  out the minor triad + major 7 + major 6 — i.e. treat the chord as **minor-major or minor-6** rather
  than plain minor) — because Dorian and melodic minor share every note except that one, deliberately
  landing on and leaning into the major 7 is what makes a melodic-minor line read as melodic minor
  rather than Dorian.
- **Two arpeggio "pictures" (structures) for the melodic-minor tonic-minor sound.** (1) The chord's own
  **minor-major 7 arpeggio** (1-♭3-5-7); (2) an arpeggio built a **major 3rd up**, described as
  "major 7 sharp 5" — over C minor that's an **E♭ major7♯5 arpeggio** — stated as a very commonly used
  companion shape to the tonic minor-major arpeggio. Practice method given directly: set up a static
  ii–V-style vamp and build simple bass-line-style melodies mixing the plain melodic minor scale with
  one of the two arpeggios, aiming to hit the target/characteristic notes rather than just running the
  scale up and down.
- **Triad pairs on tonic minor — a second, independent worked instance of the vault's existing C minor
  blues triad-pair material.** Names the default "everybody reaches for" pair as the scale's two
  adjacent **major** triads (F and G in C melodic minor) but flags it as missing the 3rd of the sound;
  his own preferred pair is **C major + E♭ augmented** (matches [[Triad Pairs]]'s existing tonic-chord
  pair exactly) — offered as evidence you can find your own favorite pair rather than defaulting to the
  first two adjacent triads. A further alternative given: **G major + A half-diminished/A diminished
  triad**. Generalizes past the tonic chord too: on the iv chord (Fm) his preferred pair is **E♭ + F**
  triads.
- **A dominant-7-sharp-5 arpeggio pair as a third melodic-minor vocabulary source.** Two dominant-7♯5
  arpeggios live inside C melodic minor — one rooted on the **5th** (G7♯5) and one on the **7th** (B7♯5)
  — the one off the 7th (B) is called out specifically as valuable because it groups the leading tone
  and the chord's other characteristic (altered-sounding) notes together in one compact shape.
- **Tonic minor, scale choice 2 — Dorian.** The other most common minor-blues tonic sound, tied
  historically to Miles Davis/Coltrane-era modal playing rather than bebop (where melodic minor was
  already established). The must-hit note for Dorian is the **major 6 / natural 13**, paired with the
  **♭7** — spell those out or the sound reads as generic, characterless minor with no distinguishing
  color (there's no "avoid note" forcing the issue the way melodic minor's major 7 does). Explicitly
  ties Dorian's ambiguity back to Mr. P.C.'s own head: the melody never states the 6th, so the tune
  reads as "probably Dorian" rather than definitively so — it could just as easily be plain minor with
  a ♭7. Material recommended for pulling out the Dorian-13 sound: minor pentatonic is flagged as
  insufficient on its own (it's missing the 13), so add a **minor-6 chord/arpeggio** on top, and treat
  the major 7 (when it appears in a melodic-minor-adjacent line) as a passing/leading tone rather than a
  landing point, to keep the color reading as Dorian rather than tonic-minor.
- **Drop-2 minor-13 voicings as melodic material, not just comping shapes.** States directly that his
  own drop-2 chord-voicing material (referenced as its own separate video) can be **translated into
  soloing lines** — arpeggiate the same voicing shapes you'd comp with, rather than treating voicings
  and single-note vocabulary as separate skill sets. One triad-pair option offered for the Dorian
  13-sound specifically: **E♭ + F** triads (distinct from the melodic-minor tonic pair above).
- **Blues/pentatonic phrasing over the tonic minor — explicitly framed as a phrasing choice, not a note
  choice.** Because minor pentatonic (minus its own ♭5 blue note) is already contained inside both
  Dorian and melodic minor, "playing the blues" over a minor tonic isn't a distinct scale decision — the
  ♭5 passing tone is the only genuinely outside note available. What actually makes a line read as
  "blues" instead of "Dorian" (even over the *identical* note pool) is **phrasing**: repeated notes,
  shorter phrases, slides, and blues-specific melodic shapes rather than scalar/arpeggiated lines. Named
  reference: Coltrane's Mr. P.C. solo doesn't lean on blues phrasing much ("busy with other things"); Pat
  Metheny's solo on the same tune does, in a couple of different ways, and is called out as the stronger
  reference for this device. Also floats **major-scale-over-minor** ("a bit of a B.B. King wrong-note
  type sound") as another available color, again framed as a phrasing/character choice more than a note
  choice.
- **The single biggest through-line of the video, stated explicitly:** you can play Dorian, an implied
  F7 sound (see below), and straight blues phrasing largely from the **same note pool** and still get
  three audibly different sounds — the difference is entirely which notes you choose to spell out/land
  on and how you phrase the line, not which scale box you're technically inside. Called out as something
  "a lot of jazz guitar players get lost" on — treating "the right notes" as sufficient on its own.
- **Exotic scale 1 — the augmented scale, used specifically over the *first* four bars of tonic minor
  (needs an already-established minor sound to sound "outside" against, so it doesn't work as an
  opening statement).** Built as **two augmented triads a major 3rd apart** (a hexatonic/6-note
  symmetric scale) — described as inherently triadic in character, so the practical vocabulary is
  connecting the two component triads melodically rather than running the scale as a line. Notable
  byproducts named directly: the scale also yields a **dominant-7♯5 arpeggio** and a **diminished
  triad**, and specifically a usable **B major7♭5** shape. Target notes for landing the sound: the scale
  omits a plain perfect 5th (replaced by the ♯5/♭6), so G and E♭ (still shared with plain C minor) are
  usable connective tones, but the emphasis should lean into the augmented triad itself for the
  characteristic "synthetic scale" special-effect color.
- **Exotic scale 2 — harmonic minor, used sparingly on the tonic itself (not just its usual role
  voicing the dominant/diminished chords of a minor ii–V).** Framed as part of the required vocabulary
  even if you don't reach for it often — "you should have it available" the same way you'd know the
  diatonic arpeggios over any chord you're not necessarily going to play.
- **Exotic scale 3 — whole tone, over the minor-major tonic sound** (paired conceptually with the
  augmented-scale device above — both are ways of layering a symmetric "outside" color onto an
  established static minor chord, then dropping back out). Named directly as a floating, non-resolving
  sound, useful for shifting character mid-solo.
- **Exotic scale 4 — (whole-half) diminished, worked as a stack of connected triads rather than a run.**
  Worked example: connect the scale's **D, F, and B major triads** as the actual melodic material,
  described as generating a dominant-7(♭13)-type color. General practice habit stated for all the minor
  scales he uses (including whole tone): think in terms of **connected triad/arpeggio material**, not
  the raw scale run top to bottom.
- **Reharm device 1 — superimposing an unrelated chord's sound over the unchanged bass.** Over a
  straight C minor bass line, solo using simple, basic **F triad/F7 material** — the point isn't that F7
  is "correct" (it isn't functionally there), it's that emphasizing F creates a second focal point that
  contrasts against the C-minor bass without ever clashing (nothing in a basic F-triad line is a
  genuinely wrong note against Cm). Explicitly flagged as fragile: **keep the superimposed line very
  basic/triadic** — get too "vague" (generic bebop vocabulary) and the ear stops hearing the
  superimposition at all. States directly the same superimposed material could also just be heard as
  plain Dorian — the *distinction* is in which notes get emphasized and the phrasing, not the notes
  themselves, tying straight back to the video's core theme above.
- **Reharm device 2 — delaying the cadence to stretch the dominant pull.** Instead of playing the short
  one-beat/one-bar C7 "signal" chord that normally cues the move from tonic minor to the iv chord (Fm),
  **expand it** — play a full extended stretch (up to all four bars) of **C7 altered** in place of
  stating the tonic-minor sound at all. Because listeners track the form even without consciously
  naming chords, stretching the dominant before the iv chord creates real unresolved tension and makes
  the eventual resolution into Fm land more powerfully than a plain tonic-minor statement would. Framed
  as generalizable further (a long ii–V–ii–V into C7, or other chords leaning into the iv chord) but not
  developed beyond the basic device in this video. Named comparison: the tune **"Solar"** (a minor blues
  with a full two-part cadence into its iv-equivalent chord) as a related but distinct approach.
- **Historical framing for reharmonizing over static minor chords.** Ties the whole "reinterpret the
  harmony instead of playing exactly what's written" approach back through bebop (Parker superimposing
  dominant/altered color over blues changes) to Jim Hall and Kenny Burrell's more chromatically
  adventurous playing (name-checks a Jim Hall/Paul Desmond album series specifically). States the minor
  blues is a good place to practice this because the slower harmonic rhythm gives room to actually hear
  the reinterpretation happening.
