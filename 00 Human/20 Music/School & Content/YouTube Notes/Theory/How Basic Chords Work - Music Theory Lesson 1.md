# How Basic Chords Work - Music Theory Lesson 1

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5Y01jIorpeA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5Y01jIorpeA)

> [!info] Watch-only page — chords built from intervals, from the ground up
> Michael New's first lesson, and the one that made the channel. It teaches chords as a *consequence*
> of interval arithmetic rather than as shapes to memorise: learn the third, and every triad and
> seventh chord follows. He is a music graduate and full-time teacher, and it shows in the pacing.

## What to watch for

**The claim, in his words:** learning the theory under chords

> …will not only allow you to find any chord you want, anywhere you want, it will also give you a
> solid foundation to build your entire understanding of music theory on.

That is exactly Course 01's premise — **derivation rules**, not memorised tables — which is why this
sits at the front of Class 1.

**What it actually teaches:**

- **Major and minor thirds as the only building material.** Stack M3 + m3 and you have a major
  triad; m3 + M3 gives minor. This is [[FND-C06 — Interval quality names]] doing real work rather
  than being a naming quiz.
- **Counting the letter distance first, quality second** — [[FND-C07 — Letter distance decides the interval number]]. He counts inclusively and out loud, which is the habit worth stealing.
- **Why the pattern generalises** — once thirds are the unit, diminished, augmented and seventh
  chords need no new rule, which is what [[FND-C05 — Interval inversion]] is pointing at from the
  other side.

**The famous correction — read it before you watch.** Present-day Michael pinned an update to his
own description:

> Past Michael chose his words poorly when he said **"this chord doesn't work"** … What he SHOULD
> have said was "by moving notes closer together than a minor third or farther apart than a major
> third, you're creating an entirely new chord that you're almost definitely not expecting."

**This is the more useful lesson of the two.** Nothing in harmony "doesn't work" — stacking
something other than thirds gives you quartal, cluster or added-tone harmony, all of which are real
and all of which show up in the music this vault is actually about. Hold that alongside
[[Making Sense of Rock's Tonal Systems]], which makes the same argument at article length: a rule
that says a sound is wrong is usually a rule that only describes one system.
