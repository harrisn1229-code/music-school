# Reharmonization in a Jazz Guitar Solo - Outside ideas on Solar

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v6lFQD1vTj4)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v6lFQD1vTj4)

## Synopsis

Jens Larsen walks through his own multi-chorus solo over "Solar," explaining the reharmonization he plays *in the melody line* against an unchanged backing track (bass still plays the original changes) — this is soloist-level superimposed reharmonization, distinct from rewriting a chart's actual chords. The core mechanic across almost every example: pick one structure (a scale sound or a triad), and move it in **parallel motion** through the progression, most often upward — which works against Solar's own natural downward ii-V gravity, generating a controlled tension the ear reads as "outside" without ever losing the thread back to the real changes.

## What's taught

- **Parallel-motion superimposition as the default reharmonization engine.** Rather than reharmonizing each chord independently with its own unrelated substitute, pick one sound (melodic minor in chorus 1, minor pentatonic in chorus 2) and slide the *same* structure through the whole progression in parallel — easier to play convincing lines over (the shapes/relationships stay constant) and reads as one coherent idea rather than a series of disconnected tricks. Deliberately moving it **upward** works against the tune's own downward-resolving ii-V pull, which is exactly what supplies the tension.
- **Chorus-by-chorus arc, not a fixed reharm applied uniformly.** Chorus 1 leans on melodic-minor sounds; chorus 2 shifts to minor-pentatonic-based parallel motion (a different colour, same mechanic); chorus 3 deliberately pulls back toward the original changes — a solo-level structure decision (build tension, then release it) layered on top of the reharmonization technique itself.
- **Triad-over-a-different-bass-note as a lightweight way to imply upper-structure/altered sounds without spelling a full chord name.** E.g., playing an F major triad over an E♭ bass implies E♭maj7♯5♯11; a G major triad over the same E♭ bass implies E♭maj7♯5 — cheap (one simple triad shape) but effective because the ear fills in the rest against the sounding bass note.
- **Delayed-resolution surprise — save the biggest reharmonization move for the exact moment a cadence is expected to land.** The strongest "outside" effect in the video comes from playing a genuinely different-sounding chord/triad precisely where the ear expects the plain resolution (e.g. a D♭m7♭5-type sound where a clean D♭maj7 is expected) — the surprise is proportional to how strongly the ear was set up to expect the plain resolution.
- **A practical ceiling on how "spelled out" the substitute harmony should be**, tied to harmonic rhythm: over a fast-moving progression a fully-spelled substitute chord sound can work, but at a slower harmonic rhythm (roughly two bars per chord, as in Solar) spelling the substitute out too completely reads as "obvious and boring" — better to imply it loosely and let lines move somewhat freely rather than rigidly outlining the new "chord."
- **Minor pentatonic works as a parallel-motion reharmonization vehicle just as well as melodic minor** — moved a half or whole step off the real chord (e.g. B♭ minor pentatonic over what's really a Gm7-C7), close enough to the real changes that without a comping instrument stating the original harmony explicitly, the substitution reads as color rather than a wrong note.
