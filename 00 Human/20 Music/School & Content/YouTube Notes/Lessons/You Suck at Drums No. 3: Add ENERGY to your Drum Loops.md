## Techniques
- **B (max-energy) change #1: double the hi-hat rate, then back off if it clutters.** Highlight all hi-hats, hit Divide-by-2 to halve note length (doubling the perceived rate) — sounds higher energy but quickly reads as cluttered at full density, so it's often better to instead activate the *open* hat sparingly (moved up from the closed-hat lane, placed on weaker beats like the "and") rather than just doubling everything.
- **Gate mode stops a sample cleanly instead of letting its tail bleed into the next hit** — note length alone does nothing to a sample's actual playback length; switching a Drum Rack pad to **Gate mode** makes the sample stop as soon as the note ends, so note length finally controls how long the hit rings — critical for open hi-hats that would otherwise smear into the next closed hit.
- **Percussion spacing rule: space hits 3 sixteenth notes apart** for a syncopated stick/percussion layer — a good general starting pattern for adding a secondary rhythmic layer that isn't a straight subdivision.
- **Nudge new percussion right after adding it** so it doesn't collide with an existing element occupying the same slot (here: a stick pattern nudged right so it clears an open hi-hat hit).
- **Highlight the FULL BAR, not just the notes, before duplicating** — Ableton's Cmd+D duplicate uses the highlighted region's length (visible as a blue line across the top) to decide where the copy lands; highlighting only the notes themselves (a shorter blue bar) pastes the duplicate in the wrong spot. Always highlight the complete bar length first.
- **The ride is a slow hi-hat** — where a hi-hat typically plays 8th/16th notes, a ride typically plays quarter or half notes; add a new element (a ride) on the beat as a distinct third or fourth energy-raising change rather than only adding to the hi-hat/kick.
- **Press 0 to deactivate a note instead of deleting it** — a fast way to A/B whether an element earns its place: mute with 0, listen, then either reactivate (press 0 again) or delete outright once you're sure it doesn't add anything.
- **Declutter B by limiting how many elements play at once, not by adding more.** Once a tier gets busy, ease off *carried-over* elements from the previous tier (e.g. remove some of A's kicks) rather than piling new elements onto an already-dense pattern — the change that actually reads is often subtraction, not more addition.
- **Crash-at-start + fill-at-end bookend a section transition, and don't count as one of the three energy changes.** A crash announces the beginning of a new section (swap the ride for a crash on beat one of the new clip); a fill (here: three toms — high, mid, low — placed right at the very end of the bar before the next section) creates a burst of activity that wraps the previous section up. Because these are transitional rather than sustained throughout the loop, they're extra polish on top of the three real energy-raising changes, not one of them.
- **Outro (O): keep it low-energy but audibly distinct from the Intro** — favor removing recently-added elements first and keep only a stripped kick pattern (one kick marking the downbeat) rather than reverting all the way back to the Intro's exact content; the goal is "quiet, but not identical to where we started."
