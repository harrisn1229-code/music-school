# Kurt Rosenwinkel - This Is What Makes His Style Unique

## Synopsis
Jens Larsen breaks down what makes Kurt Rosenwinkel's melodic language distinct, using four short excerpts from Rosenwinkel's guitar solo on "Nefertiti" (Wayne Shorter), taken from Mark Turner's album *Ballad Session*. Larsen frames the video around Rosenwinkel's **melodic language** specifically — not licks or vocabulary in isolation, but *how* the lines are constructed and how they relate to (or deliberately ignore) the underlying harmony — and folds in things Rosenwinkel himself said about practicing this in master classes Larsen attended as a student. Nefertiti is chosen deliberately: as a Wayne Shorter composition its chords are strongly melodic/atmospheric but not functionally connected in the standard ii-V-I sense, so a soloist can't lean on "the changes" the way you can over a functional tune — melody has to be its own organizing principle. The four excerpts each surface a different facet of the same underlying approach: (1) very wide range used inside a short phrase, (2) a melodic idea that keeps moving in one direction *through* a chord change rather than resetting at the barline, (3) large-interval, non-sequential lines built from stacked/adjacent arpeggios and diatonic triads rather than a single scale, and (4) tightly restrained motivic development — building a whole passage from a small pool of notes (an arpeggio) without it reading as an obvious "motif." The most valuable single idea in the video, and the one Larsen explicitly ties back to Rosenwinkel's own stated practice method, is the **melody-first, changes-second** mindset: decide the shape/direction the line wants to take, and treat moving through the chord changes as a constraint to solve *underneath* that shape rather than the thing generating the notes in the first place.

## Techniques (what's taught — solo-analysis / improvisation concepts)

- **Melody-first practicing: let the melodic idea's direction survive the chord change, don't let the chord change dictate the melody.** Rosenwinkel talked in master classes about practicing "having a melodic idea and then having the freedom and the ability to move that across changes without being bothered by the scales or the chord changing." The point isn't ignoring harmony — it's building the skill of carrying an ascending/descending idea straight through a bar line instead of resetting direction every time the chord changes, which is what most players default to. → [[Solo Dynamics and Arc]], [[Never-Ending Scale Exercise]]
- **The "never-ending scale" practice approach (Rosenwinkel's own stated method, via Larsen's master-class notes):** take a chord progression and keep a scale running continuously as if the progression were static/"in time" — play roughly eight notes through a turnaround over altered dominants, then change scale whenever you've played about eight notes, rather than exactly at each chord change. Trains thinking ahead and pre-planning scale changes instead of reacting to the chord in the moment. Same drill generalizes to diatonic triads and diatonic seventh chords run the same way (stack the same "keep moving, change unit every N notes" logic on a different harmonic building block). Full writeup and drill steps on the new note → [[Never-Ending Scale Exercise]].
- **Wide-range excerpt (opening phrase, over A sus4 → D7alt(♭9) with B♭ in the bass → chord → F♯sus4):** starts on a low D and climbs to a high F within one bar — a large fraction of the instrument's range covered in a short span, which Larsen calls "very typical for Rosenwinkel's playing." Line: D major scale (no G) over the A-sus sound, up to F then F♯, skipping to B♭, playing around a B♭ major triad shape for the altered sound, back up to F, then chromatic half-step descent (F → E → D♯) to land the F♯sus4. → [[Solo Dynamics and Arc]]
- **Ascending scale-run melodic idea carried across a chord change without resetting direction:** the phrase above starts as a fairly plain ascending D major scale run over the A sus4, and *keeps ascending in the same direction* once the chord moves to D7alt/B♭ instead of turning around or restarting — the idea becomes "free of the changes." Direct demonstration of the melody-first bullet above. → [[Never-Ending Scale Exercise]]
- **End-of-first-chorus phrase over a non-standard reharmonization of Nefertiti (E sus4 → E♭7♯11 → A7alt ×2 bars → A♭maj7):** Larsen notes the harmony played on this take differs from Nefertiti's usual changes — it resolves more functionally here (a real V–I motion back to the top of the form) than the tune's harmony normally does. Rosenwinkel is noted to consistently emphasize the **♯11 on E♭7** every time it recurs across the whole solo, not just this instance — a deliberate, repeated color choice, not incidental. → 20 Music/Library note below
- **Direction-over-resolution phrasing (same phrase, continued):** a long ascending run from the A up to a high F built from a D♭maj7♯5 arpeggio (D♭–C–up the arpeggio), landing not with the "expected" scale-wise contrary-motion resolution you'd get in a pop-oriented solo, but by continuing to climb from D♭ up to F and then leaping *down a fifth* to B♭. Larsen's point: what matters is the melody's stated direction, not counterpoint-style resolution rules — "that's more important than the counterpoint rules that we have for melody."
- **Two-bar A7alt line built from stacked, non-sequential material (not one scale):** scale-run pickup → up to a note, C♯, down an augmented triad → G, skip up to C to play a descending C♯maj7 arpeggio → back up to G → ascending B♭ major triad → descending diminished triad. Larsen reads the underlying source material as B♭ melodic minor, but the point of the excerpt is that it's **arpeggios/triads tied together, not a straight scale run** — large range, non-predictable movement, "different arpeggios tied together," which is what keeps it from sounding like a mechanical sequence while still holding together. → [[Harmonized Scale Triads for Soloing]], [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]
- **Diatonic triads/arpeggios as the generative unit instead of a single parent scale**, demonstrated twice in the video (the excerpt above, and the E major7♯11 excerpt below) — the same "arpeggiate the moment, not the whole progression" logic already documented on [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]] and [[Harmonized Scale Triads for Soloing]], but shown here at the level of a full worked jazz solo rather than a practice drill.
- **E major7♯11 excerpt:** starts with an E major triad in second inversion from the low B, up to a C♯ minor triad, up to the ♯11 (A♯) then a pentatonic-flavored fragment (A♯, C♯, D) that lands back on the major 7 to close the line — another large-range line, low B up to D♯, that resolves down to C♯ then leaps up to F♯ to set up a half-step chromatic descent into the 5th of the next chord (A). → [[Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords]]
- **Restrained motif development over B♭m7 → Amaj7♯11 → E♭7 (Lydian dominant):** an F minor7 arpeggio stated as the motif over B♭m7 (deliberately using *only* those notes — a small, sharply-limited pool), then the same shape moved/developed across the next two chords: a descending arpeggio, a fifth interval into a descending Amaj7♭5 arpeggio on the Amaj7♯11, then further development on the E♭7 (Lydian dominant) that keeps re-emphasizing the ♯11/Lydian color, closing with a descending augmented triad back down to A. Larsen's explicit point: Rosenwinkel "will stick with a small pool of notes and then make a short melody with that," rather than immediately spreading out across the whole available scale — and does it *without* the motif reading as obvious or repetitive, which Larsen flags as the harder, less common skill ("sometimes you'll find that when people are using motifs it gets a little bit on the obvious side"). → [[Motivic Development]]
- **General trait check-list for "sounds like Rosenwinkel"** (synthesized across all four excerpts, stated or clearly implied by Larsen): (1) use more of the instrument's range inside a single phrase than feels "safe," (2) let a melodic idea's direction survive a chord change instead of resetting at the barline, (3) build lines from adjacent arpeggios/triads rather than one scale stretched over everything, (4) restrict a motif to a genuinely small note pool and develop it across changes without telegraphing it as "the motif."
