# Modern Jazz Harmony - Chord Progressions and Analysis

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H6OCi2gcdb8)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H6OCi2gcdb8)

## Synopsis

A vlog-style theory talk, not a tune breakdown — Jens Larsen states up front that this covers ground his usual videos don't: the huge share of the jazz repertoire *since roughly the mid-1960s* that doesn't run on functional ii–V–I harmony at all, and for which "we don't actually have any real way of analyzing it in a way that really makes sense." Rather than presenting a system, he works through **three original vamps he wrote himself** — a major-key one with a single altered dominant, an all-major-7-chords "islands" progression built from voicings first, and a minor-key one with a folk-flavored minor-to-minor cadence — talking through how he composed each, how he privately rationalizes the chord relationships (mostly *after the fact*, admittedly "backwards rationalizing"), and how he solos over them. The throughline, stated explicitly and repeated several times, is that **vague/ambiguous harmony is not a failure of the composer or the analyst — it's the point.** A vamp that's too clearly in a key gets boring fast when it's looped for a whole song; a vamp with no connection between its chords at all just sounds bad. The craft is calibrating *how* connected the chords are — familiar enough to sound intentional, vague enough to stay interesting on repeat — and he draws a direct line from this to loop-based pop (Coldplay's four-chord songs, Daft Punk's "Get Lucky") doing exactly the same thing with the same underlying reason. He also states a personal analysis method used throughout: borrow a small, admittedly artificial fragment of functional harmony (an "as if this were a ii–V" or "as if this were a IV→iv" reading) between two chords that aren't really functionally related, purely as a private tool for picking scales and target notes — never claimed as the real analysis, just a usable one.

## What's taught

### Vamp 1 — C major with one altered tritone-sub dominant: Cmaj7 – Bm11 – D6 – D♭7alt

The only one of the three vamps with a dominant chord in it, deliberately — Larsen states dominants are rare in vague/modern vamps *because they give too much direction* ("as soon as you have a dominant, that's a dominant that wants to resolve somewhere"), so including one at all is a choice worth explaining. The dominant used is **D♭7, the tritone substitute of the "expected" G7/G7alt** — not far out on its own (§2 tritone sub is standard vocabulary), but he **alters it (D♭7alt)**, and states directly that alteration *weakens* the tritone sub's pull to C: "it doesn't really sound like it wants to go to C, but it still does" — the alteration is chosen specifically to make an already-functional device sound less functional, not for its own color. → routed to [[Chord Substitution]]

**Chord-by-chord:** Cmaj7 (tonic, established quickly) → Bm11 (no ♯9/♯4 sounded but "implied," so it already leans Lydian-toward-D) → D6 (the Lydian lean resolves — Larsen frames this stretch as "a completely gradual shift from C up to something that's really a D") → D♭7alt (back toward C). **A local-functional-fragment analysis method, stated as a general personal tool, not a claim about "real" function:** to make sense of the D→D♭7 move, Larsen invents a small fictional ii–V — hearing D major as if it were "a D-major/D-minor-ish sound you'd find in the key of A major" moving to a G7-type dominant, purely so he has *a* functional relationship (however invented) to reference for scale choice. This gives two usable soloing scales over D♭7alt, both worked out from that invented fragment: (a) **A♭ melodic minor** (the standard altered-scale source for G7, carried over via the tritone-sub relationship — matches [[Chord Substitution]] §2's stated pairing of D♭7♯11/altered with its tritone-sub logic); (b) a **diminished-dominant (half-whole) reading**, arrived at because he "heard an A♭ in there as a note" and went looking for a scale that would contain it — an ear-led choice, not a rule. → [[Scale Modes Reference]], [[Altered Chords]], [[Diminished Chords]]

**Where the "vagueness" is deliberately placed:** Bm11 and D6 use *the same scale* (D major) — no real shift happens on that strong internal move. The actual scale shifts happen on the weaker transitions instead: C→Bm11, and D6→D♭7alt. Stated directly as intentional: **"you kind of have the modulations put in the sort of wrong bars a little bit... that's helping to keep it kind of vague."** Putting the tonal shift where the ear isn't expecting a structural change (rather than on the strong downbeat move) is the actual vagueness mechanism here, separate from the chord choices themselves.

**Soloing, chord by chord:** Cmaj7 — played mostly as a plain maj7 sound, deliberately *saving* the ♯4/F♯ rather than committing to full Lydian, so the Lydian color has somewhere to go later. Bm11 — **Aeolian, not Dorian** (i.e., no natural 9 committed either — the choice to "stick a little closer to C" rather than lean further toward D). D6 — same D-major scale as Bm11 (D6 heard as a rootless inversion of Bm anyway). D♭7alt — the two options above.

### Vamp 2 — "Modal islands" of major-7 voicings, built voicing-first: E♭maj7 – Bmaj7 – Dmaj7 – Cmaj7/E

**Composition method, stated as the actual order of operations:** Larsen builds the interesting-sounding *voicings* first — wide intervals mixed with close ones, deliberately vague/interesting shapes — and only decides the bass note/root **afterward**, once the voicings are already chosen. This is the reverse of picking a root/function first and voicing it second, and it's offered as a reusable compositional order, not just a description of this one vamp. **All four chords are major-7 — no dominants anywhere in this vamp** (a further, more extreme version of Vamp 1's dominant-avoidance principle). Compare a lot of R&B writing, which Larsen names directly as running on the same "major-7-and-minor-9, no real direction, just voice-leadable" chord palette.

**Root relationships are all thirds, never 4ths/5ths — the explicit reason the chords read as "islands":** E♭→B is a **minor-3rd-down / major-6th-up** relationship, sourced as the ♭6 borrowed from E♭ minor — the same relationship, Larsen notes, behind the first chord of bossa nova standards like "Night and Day" and "Tristeza." B major→(not B minor, but) **D major** substitutes a third-related major chord for the "expected" B-minor resolution, chosen for the resulting bass movement. D major→**Cmaj7/E** (E in the bass, not C) — the E bass is what gives this chord its pull *back toward* E♭ again, closing another third relationship (C→E♭). **The stated principle, generalized:** avoiding dominant-to-tonic (5th) and subdominant-to-tonic (4th) root motion — "we don't have like a 5-1 or a 1-4... moving in thirds like this is a little bit more vague" — is specifically what keeps each chord sounding like a self-contained island rather than part of a functional chain. → routed to [[Chromatic Mediants and Pop Modulation]]

**Soloing:** straightforward by comparison — Ionian or Lydian on every chord (all are plain major-7 voicings), with an explicit caution that **most of the voicings include the 5th**, so a full Lydian ♯11 line risks clashing with the voiced 5th underneath it. **A concrete melodic-targeting device, stated as the actual practical tool used (not scale theory):** identify which notes literally *change* between one chord's voicing and the next, and target those specifically — worked example, the G (of E♭maj7) moving to G♭/F♯ (of Bmaj7), and the C moving to B. Landing deliberately on a **newly-introduced note** (one that wasn't in the previous chord's scale) is what makes a harmonic change audible to the listener without any functional cue to rely on — this is offered as the general solution to melody-writing over non-functional chords.

### Vamp 3 — C minor, folk-influenced, the most tonal of the three: Cm11 – A♭maj7 – Gm11 – G♭(D♭/G♭)

Stated as more tonal than the other two specifically because it stays in C minor's orbit for most of its length. **Cm11 → A♭maj7** reads as a fairly ordinary minor-key subdominant move (A♭ is essentially a C-minor triad over a different bass note). **A♭maj7 → Gm11** is less ordinary — Gm11 has a natural 9 (A natural), a sound Larsen ties to how used the ear is to C Dorian, making the "wrong" quality land as expected rather than jarring. **Gm11 → G♭** (voiced as a D♭ triad over a G♭ bass, i.e., G♭ Lydian) is flagged as the vamp's least-defined move — resolving G♭ back to C minor is explicitly the weakest link in the whole progression.

**A device Larsen says he didn't notice until analyzing his own vamp after writing it — a single voicing shape moved chromatically/in parallel across different roots**, functioning as the real glue of the progression more than any chord-by-chord logic: the same ambiguous sus/minor-11-type voicing shape recurs, transposed, under Cm11, A♭maj7, and Gm11. Where the bass moves a half-step *below* the next chord's root before that chord lands (voiced with the *identical* shape, just moved up), it creates **contrary motion between the chord and the bass** — the mechanism he credits with making the progression cohere even though he built it without planning that.

**A folk-derived minor-dominant-to-minor-tonic cadence, named directly as an influence outside the jazz tradition:** Gm11 resolving toward Cm (i.e., a **minor v → minor i**, not the standard dominant V7 → i) is credited to Danish and Swedish folk music, which Larsen says routinely uses this cadence type — no leading tone, no dominant pull, a genuinely different cadential shape from anything functional harmony provides. He states this is **rare-to-absent in the standard jazz repertoire** ("I don't think there are any standards that has that, really — maybe one bossa") but common in "more modern" jazz composition, and something he personally uses often. → routed to [[Borrowed Chords]]

**Two competing "as-if" functional-fragment readings offered for the weak G♭→Cm resolution**, both explicitly local, invented tools rather than claimed real analysis — the same method as Vamp 1's D→D♭7 fragment: (a) hear G♭ as **Lydian** (G♭ Lydian = D♭ major's note collection, which is audibly present in the voicing) and the D♭ triad inside it as a **Neapolitan-subdominant-type** relationship resolving to C minor — see [[Neapolitan Sixth]] for the borrowed-♭II mechanism this echoes, applied here in a minor rather than major context; (b) hear the whole Gm11→G♭ move as a **transposed IV→iv shape** — "B♭ major to B♭ minor," a four-major-to-four-minor cliché (see [[Borrowed Chords]] § *IV → iv → I*) mapped onto this pair of chords a step away from where it "actually" belongs, resolved in a genuinely different place than the cliché normally resolves.

**Soloing:** Cm11 as C Dorian (natural 6 present via the A♭maj7 relationship). A♭maj7 heard with a Lydian-leaning color relative to the Cm tonic even though not played strictly Lydian. Gm11 as G Dorian-ish minor (again the natural 9/A natural). G♭ — Lydian, per the reading above. **The same borrowed-fragment melodic-targeting trick as Vamp 1:** thinking "B♭ major moving to B♭ minor" over the actual Gm11→G♭ chords generates concrete melodic material that fits the real chord change, even though the fictional B♭-major/minor pair isn't literally what's sounding — the value of the invented fragment is exactly that it's *usable*, not that it's correct.

### The general method, stated explicitly as the takeaway (applies across all three vamps)

1. **"Modal islands"** — treat each chord in a non-functional vamp as its own local, stable tonal center with its own scale, rather than trying to find one key that explains the whole progression. Named directly: *"we made it into sort of small islands that don't really connect entirely, but then it still sort of works."*
2. **Borrow a small, invented fragment of functional harmony between two adjacent chords purely as a private analysis/scale-choice tool** — never as a claim about what's "really" happening. Stated as his general working method for *any* harmonically ambiguous piece he has to play, not just his own writing — he says he'd approach a Wayne Shorter or Herbie Hancock tune the same way.
3. **Place the scale/tonal shift on the weak bar, not the strong one**, when the goal is to obscure rather than clarify the tonal center (Vamp 1).
4. **Build voicings before deciding the root/bass** when writing a non-functional progression — function gets assigned last, not first (Vamp 2).
5. **Favor 3rd-related root motion over 4th/5th motion** to avoid accidentally implying dominant-to-tonic function between "island" chords (Vamp 2, also present in Vamp 3's third-relationship framing).
6. **Target the notes that change between adjacent voicings** as the practical, non-analytical way to write a clear melody over harmony that gives no functional cues (Vamp 2, reused in Vamp 3's fragment-derived lines).
7. **A single voicing shape moved chromatically/in parallel can be the real glue of a progression**, discoverable in hindsight even when it wasn't planned that way (Vamp 3).
8. **This is a compositional trade, not an accident:** too tonal/clear and a short repeating vamp gets boring on loop; too disconnected and it just sounds bad. Vague-but-familiar is the target zone.

### Pop-music parallel: ambiguous tonic loops stay listenable on repeat

Named as directly related, not just an aside: **Coldplay's** four-chord songs, and **Daft Punk's "Get Lucky"** (Dorian-tinged, arguably tonic on the first chord but not clearly so), both stay interesting through dozens of loops specifically *because* it stays unclear which chord is home — "you don't really have the idea that you're just vamping to a 1 all the time." Larsen frames this as the same principle his own vamps use, independently arrived at by pop songwriters solving the identical "how do I loop four chords for a whole song without it getting boring" problem. Explicitly generalized to "a lot of other pop music... for the last 10 years" relying on short repeated progressions that need to stay a little vague to survive the repetition. → routed to [[The Axis Progression]]

### On influence — jazz writing absorbs everything you listen to, not just jazz

A methodological aside, stated as directly relevant to how he composes: modern jazz composition is shaped by pop music and folk music the writer listens to, not only by jazz predecessors — explicitly rejecting the idea that a jazz composer's vocabulary comes only from "having listened to Joe Henderson and Charlie Parker." He notes this was equally true for the bebop generation, whose vocabulary absorbed blues, classical music, and the *contemporary* pop music of their own era (i.e., the Great American Songbook). His own Danish/Swedish folk-music background is named directly as the source of Vamp 3's minor-v-to-minor-i cadence.
