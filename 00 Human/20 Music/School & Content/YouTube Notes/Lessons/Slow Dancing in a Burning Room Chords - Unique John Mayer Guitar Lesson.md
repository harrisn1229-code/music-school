## Synopsis

## What's taught

**The harmony, named:** the A→E move in the song is a **plagal cadence** — IV→I in the key of E major (A = IV, E = I) — the "amen cadence," softer than an authentic (V→I) cadence because there's no leading tone pulling into the tonic. Jared calls out labeling the harmony *before* digging into voicings as a deliberate ear-training step, not just theory trivia. → [[Cadences]], [[Chord Inversions]]

**The two triad voicings (on strings D-G-B / 4-3-2), exact frets given:**
- **A triad (root position, 1-3-5), 5th position:** D string fret 7 (A, root) – G string fret 6 (C♯, 3rd) – B string fret 5 (E, 5th).
- **E triad (2nd inversion, 3-5-1), same three strings:** D string fret 6 (G♯, 3rd) – G string fret 4 (B, 5th) – B string fret 5 (E, root, held).
- **Voice leading between them is the whole point:** B-string note (E) **stays exactly the same**, D-string note moves **A→G♯, a half step down**, G-string note moves **C♯→B, a whole step down** — textbook "smallest possible move per voice" voice leading, the same rule (one note stays, one moves a half step, one moves a whole step) Jared frames as how pianists/orchestrators/composers think, applied here to guitar triads instead of full chords.

**Bass-note mute technique (called out as "very important"):** when playing the full chords with open bass strings under these triads (open A under the A triad), the right-hand **thumb has to hop over and mute the open A string** the instant you move to the E chord — otherwise the low A rings through and clashes with the E harmony, sounding muddy. Mute either by hopping the thumb clear or by laying the side of the thumb across the string; either way, muting is not optional here.

**John Mayer's embellishment — the actual "what is he doing" answer:** over the A triad, right before resolving to E, Mayer hammers on to the **3rd (C♯)** from the **major 2nd (B) a whole step below it**, on the middle voice of the triad (the G-string note in the 5th-position voicing: fret 4 → fret 6), then releases/moves on into the E chord. Jared frames it as "2 hammering onto 3" — find the chord's 3rd, find the scale's 2 a whole step below it on the same string, hammer 2→3, then continue into the voice-led E chord as before. This 2→3 hammer-on is the specific device that makes the plain A→E move sound like "the John Mayer thing."

**Finding every instance of the same move on the neck — worked on three string sets:**
- **Strings 4-3-2 (D-G-B):** the 5th-position voicing above — A (frets 7-6-5) → E (frets 6-4-5), 2→3 hammer-on on the G string.
- **Strings 5-4-3 (A-D-G):** a second A→E pairing, found by the same "closest voicing" search; Jared keeps **E as a constant pedal bass note under both chords** here (E is already the 5th of A, and the root of E, so it works under either harmony) — described as "a beautiful way to do a plagal cadence" or "neighbor IV chord." Locate the triad's 3rd on this string set, find the 2 a whole step below it, hammer 2→3 the same way.
- **Strings 3-2-1 (G-B-E, top three strings):** a third A→E pairing at the top of the neck, same method — find the closest E triad to the A triad, then find and hammer the 2→3 embellishment on whichever string carries the 3rd in that voicing.
- Same underlying logic each time: **triad → find nearest triad of the target chord (voice leading) → find the 3rd → find the 2 a whole step below it → hammer 2 onto 3 → resolve.**

**Telecaster-specific tone tip (not the song's own gear, a general technique aside):** on an electric guitar — Jared calls out the Telecaster specifically — a **slight pull on the neck itself** (bending the neck, not a string) produces a subtle vibrato that "breathes some life into notes that are just sitting there and sustaining." A physical vibrato technique distinct from finger/string vibrato.

**The practice exercise (the "milestone" self-test):** play the plain voicing then the embellished (hammer-on) voicing, in time, for each string set in order — low to high: strings 5-4-3, then 4-3-2, then 3-2-1 — chord, hammer-on version, chord, hammer-on version, moving to the next string set without stopping. Framed explicitly as a **defined milestone**, not open-ended practice: a clear pass/fail test ("can I do it now, in time, through all the inversions and positions") that also doubles as a fast way to re-familiarize later if the material goes stale ("it's like an etude, like a real piece of music").

**Practice philosophy (the throughline):** the whole lesson is a demonstration of a general method — take one small, loved musical moment, label its harmony, map its voice leading, find every place that same shape lives on the fretboard, add whatever ornament makes it distinctive, then compress the result into a timed exercise with clear pass/fail parameters. Jared explicitly frames this as optional depth ("I'm not saying we have to or should do this on everything we learn") reserved for moments that especially grab you, and notes that even partial work through the method ("gaining one little voicing") is valuable on its own.
