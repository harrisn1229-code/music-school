> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **The master is the recording, held by the label · the composition is the song, held by the songwriters**
> The master is the specific audio and usually belongs to whoever paid for the session. The composition is the melody, lyrics, chords and structure, and belongs to the writers and their publishers.
>
> **Q2.** **Sampling uses both · interpolation uses the composition only**
> Two rights against one, which means two negotiations against one.
>
> **Q3.** **It removes the label from the conversation entirely**
> You're left negotiating with the publisher alone — usually faster, usually cheaper, and always fewer parties.
>
> **Q4.** **No — replaying a melody still uses the composition. It halves the problem, it doesn't remove it**
> "I played it myself" is not a defence. What it gets you out of is the master side, and nothing more.
>
> **Q5.** **Before release, always — afterwards you're negotiating with no leverage**
> The rights holder now knows the song is working and prices accordingly. Before release you're one request among many; after it you're a party who already infringed and needs a fix.
>
> **Q6.** **Takedown · frozen royalties · a majority publishing share**
> *Also accepted:* a delayed release at the worst possible moment · losing the track's entire income
>
> **Q7.** **Licensed libraries · royalty-free packs · interpolation · replay and rewrite · full clearance**
> Licensed libraries clear both rights up front for a fee and a defined split. Royalty-free packs are cleared by definition, though the licence needs to cover commercial release rather than personal use. Full clearance through a service or attorney is slowest and most expensive, and it's the only route to a specific famous recording.
>
> **Q8.** **There's no bar-count or note-count safe harbour — "how much is enough" is a legal question**
> Producers who rely on a supposed threshold are the ones who end up settling.
>
> **Q9.** **Decide before you build: could you interpolate it, and is the song worth a possible majority split?**
> *Rubric (5):* checks first whether the source is identified and whether it came from a licensed library (1) · asks whether interpolation would work, dropping the master side (2) · frames the real question as whether you're prepared to clear it before release or shelve it, decided now rather than after the mix (2)
> Run the checklist in order and most of it resolves quickly. Do you actually know what the source recording is — an unidentified sample can't be cleared at all, whatever you're willing to pay. Did it come from a licensed library, in which case it's already handled. Could you interpolate it instead, replaying the part yourself so the label drops out of the conversation and only the publisher remains. If it has to be full clearance on a specific famous recording, the arithmetic gets real: holders routinely ask for a significant publishing share, sometimes a majority, plus a piece of the master and a fee up front. The real question underneath all of it is the last one, and it's a decision about your time rather than about law: are you prepared to clear this before release, or to shelve it? A great flip you can't release is a beat, not a song, and deciding that now is much cheaper than deciding it after the mix.
