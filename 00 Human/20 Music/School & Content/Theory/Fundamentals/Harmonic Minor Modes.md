# Harmonic Minor Modes

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Scales]]. The 7 modes generated by starting the harmonic minor scale on each of its own scale degrees — same construction logic as [[Diatonic Modes]], applied to harmonic minor instead of the major scale. For melodic-minor modes, see [[Scale Modes Reference]].

## Building harmonic minor

Harmonic minor = natural minor with the 7th raised back to natural (i.e. major scale with ♭3 and ♭6 only — the 7th stays unflattened). Worked from A: A major (no accidentals) → A natural minor (♭3 ♭6 ♭7 → C, F, G natural) → A harmonic minor (♭3 ♭6, 7th raised back to G♯). One key produces a double-sharp when raised: G♯ harmonic minor's 7th is F♯ raised to **F𝄪 (F double-sharp)**, the only harmonic minor scale that needs one.

## The seven modes (built from A harmonic minor)

| # | Mode | Starts on (of A harm. min) | Formula vs. major | Alt. names |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Harmonic minor (Aeolian ♮7) | A | 1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 7 | — |
| 2 | Locrian ♮6 | B | 1 ♭2 ♭3 4 ♭5 6 ♭7 | Locrian ♯6 |
| 3 | Ionian ♯5 | C | 1 2 3 4 ♯5 6 7 | — |
| 4 | Dorian ♯4 | D | 1 2 ♭3 ♯4 5 6 ♭7 | Ukrainian Dorian (close cousin, not identical) |
| 5 | Phrygian dominant | E | 1 ♭2 3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7 | Spanish Phrygian |
| 6 | Lydian ♯2 | F | 1 ♯2 3 ♯4 5 6 7 | — |
| 7 | Super Locrian 𝄫7 (altered dominant ♭♭7) | G♯ | 1 ♭2 ♭3 ♭4 ♭5 ♭6 𝄫7 | Ultralocrian |

Same relative-approach logic as [[Diatonic Modes]]: each mode is the identical 7-note collection, only the starting note (and therefore which note reads as "home") changes.

## Five shapes, not seven

Although there are seven modes, they reduce to **five distinct fretboard shapes** for practice purposes (the same "modes vs. shapes" simplification [[Diatonic Modes]] doesn't need to make, since harmonic minor's shapes overlap differently) — practice the five shapes, and any of the seven modes is a relabeling of one of them starting from a different root within it.

## Chords built from each mode (worked from A harmonic minor)

Stack thirds (skip a note, add a note) up from each mode's root, using only the notes native to that mode:

| Root (of A harm. min) | Mode | Triad | 7th chord |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Harmonic minor | A minor | A minor(maj7) |
| B | Locrian ♮6 | B diminished | B minor7♭5 (half-diminished) |
| C | Ionian ♯5 | C augmented | C major7♯5 |
| D | Dorian ♯4 | D minor | D minor7 |
| E | Phrygian dominant | E major | E7 |
| F | Lydian ♯2 | F major | F major7 |
| G♯ | Super Locrian | G♯ diminished | G♯ diminished7 |

Stacking further (5th/6th note up) off the tonic mode gives minor(maj9), then a ♭13 that's rarely used musically; sus2/sus4 voicings are also available from the same note collection. **There is no true "key of harmonic minor"** — compositionally, you write in the relative natural-minor key (e.g. A minor) and *borrow* chords from its harmonic minor scale. The single most common borrowed-chord move: replacing the diatonic minor v chord (Em/Em7 in A minor) with the harmonic-minor-derived **major V** or **V7** (E major/E7) for a stronger cadential pull — see [[Borrowed Chords]], [[Secondary Dominants]].

## From videos

- **Phrygian dominant's construction mechanism, its modal identity as the 5th mode of harmonic minor, and two mid-song tonic-pivot examples.** The major-3rd-to-♭2nd gap is an augmented 2nd (3 semitones, wider than the semitone/tone steps most Western scales use exclusively) — the main source of the scale's "exotic" character, and it's also what lets a dominant-7th chord be built directly on the tonic (hence the name). It's the identical 7 notes as harmonic minor built from the 5th degree — same pitch collection, different tonic. **Two songs pivot which note is treated as tonic mid-song without changing pitch content at all**: "Break It to Me" (Muse) — verse in B Phrygian dominant, chorus in E harmonic minor, same 7 notes throughout; the traditional tune "Hava Nagila" does the same between E Phrygian dominant and A harmonic minor. Also yields a chord-quality set rare outside this scale — major tonic + major ♭2 + minor ♭7 in the same key (cited via "Naughty Girl"/"Beautiful Liar," Beyoncé). Known outside Western theory as *maqam Hijaz* (Arabic) and *Escala Andaluza* (flamenco) — the real-world naming behind why Western songwriters reach for it as Middle Eastern/flamenco shorthand (United States of Eurasia, Muse; Killing an Arab, The Cure; Innuendo's flamenco section, Queen). The Andalusian Cadence's dual pop-vs-flamenco tonic reading (same 4 chords, different "home" chord by genre convention) is the chord-progression expression of this same mode-pair and is documented in full on [[Chord Progressions]]. → [[Chord Progressions]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [Songs that use the Phrygian Dominant scale](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-CerXEcCJcY)
- **Phrygian dominant vs. the double harmonic scale — a commonly confused pair that share the same exotic "flavor" but are genuinely different scales, plus a real song that mixes Phrygian, Phrygian dominant, and Aeolian in one passage.** Both feature the lowered 2nd that gives Phrygian its exotic color, but **double harmonic** (Arab-world name: Hijaz) has a *raised* 7th on top, where Phrygian dominant's 7th stays lowered — "Misirlou" (Dick Dale's 1962 surf-rock hit, originally a 1927 Arabic/Eastern-Mediterranean folk tune) is often mislabeled Phrygian when it's actually double harmonic. **"White Rabbit"** (Jefferson Airplane) genuinely swings between plain Phrygian and Phrygian dominant within its opening, switching only the 3rd degree (minor↔major) while everything else holds. **"Pyramid Song"** (Radiohead) blends all three — plain Phrygian, Phrygian dominant, and Aeolian — with a passing E-major-add9 chord (Aeolian's unaltered 2nd) as the pivot; the mix reads as "kaleidoscopic," washing between flavors rather than committing to one. → [[Diatonic Modes]] § *Phrygian in depth* for the plain-Phrygian mechanics (lowered 2nd, London Calling's chord-substitution resolution, the Arabic quarter-tone caveat) this bullet builds on. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use the Phrygian mode]]
- **A raised-4th harmonic minor (the "double harmonic minor" / "Hungarian minor" scale) identified as the shared mechanism behind a "spooky" melodic resemblance between two unrelated tunes.** Billie Eilish's "bad guy" instrumental break and the "Plants vs. Zombies" theme song aren't melodically identical, but both are built on G minor with a raised 4th (the extra alteration on top of plain harmonic minor's raised 7th) — confirmed as the intended connection by Eilish's brother/producer Finneas himself. → [[7 Songs That 'Rip Off' Other Tunes]] for the full copyright/influence context this example was cited in. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[7 Songs That 'Rip Off' Other Tunes]]
- **Song/chord-scale examples for all seven modes, plus a chord-scale-system primer.** Chord scale = a different scale chosen per individual chord during improvisation rather than one scale held over the whole progression — a given chord can have more than one valid chord-scale option (worked example: maj7♯5 takes either **Ionian ♯5** here or **Lydian augmented**, melodic minor's 3rd mode, differing by only the 4th degree). Per-mode notes: **Locrian ♮6** (mode 2) and **Ionian ♯5** (mode 3) have essentially no song-length examples — Ionian ♯5's real use is as the maj7♯5 chord scale above; **Dorian ♯4** (mode 4, = Ukrainian/Romanian Dorian, and Mi Sheberakh in Jewish klezmer) rooted in Ukrainian Duma folk performance (kobzar musicians); **Phrygian dominant** (mode 5, = Spanish Phrygian, Maqam Hijaz in Arabic/Middle Eastern music) is the one genuinely widely-used mode here — *Karma Police* (Radiohead, E), *Dream Brother* (Jeff Buckley, G), *Naughty Girl* / *Beautiful Liar* (Beyoncé), Muse's *United States of Eurasia* interlude; **Lydian ♯9** (mode 6) gives a simultaneous minor-and-major-3rd color, heard in The Beatles' *Blue Jay Way* (C, though the ♯9 color tone reads more usably as E♭ than D♯); **Ultra Locrian / Super Locrian 𝄫7** (mode 7) works as a chord scale over a diminished 7th chord, re-readable as having both a flat 6 and natural 6, both a minor and major 3rd. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[The Modes of the HARMONIC MINOR]]
- **Practice framing: re-root every mode of harmonic minor to the SAME note (e.g. all seven modes played from A) instead of leaving each at its natural starting position on the neck.** Instead of treating harmonic minor's 2nd 3-notes-per-string shape (naturally starting on B) as "just harmonic minor moved up two frets," relocate its root down to A and practice/hear it as **A Locrian ♮6** in its own right — a standalone scale with its own formula and character, not a position of another scale. Repeated for all seven modes on the same root note; stated payoff is that each mode registers as one of seven genuinely distinct-sounding scales rather than interchangeable box shapes of one scale. - **A harmonic-context caveat for the raised-7th substitution, plus a tapped-color-note-in-an-arpeggio layering trick.** Before raising the 7th over a passage, check what the underlying harmony actually contains: safe over a plain power chord (no 3rd, nothing to clash), but risky if the chord/interval contains the natural (un-raised) 7th degree or a minor-7th interval, which would clash against the raised 7th. Separately: layer the harmonic-minor color directly into an arpeggio via tapping — instead of tapping the expected root as the extension note on a plain minor arpeggio (root-♭3-5), tap the raised-7th degree instead for extra exotic color on top of the same basic shape. — BERNTH, [[3 Best Shred Tricks - How To Play Fast Neoclassical Licks!]]
- **A fourth worked example of the V-chord-major substitution, applied as a single cadence point rather than a full-passage mode commitment.** D minor → C major → Bb power chord → **A major** (not the natural-minor-diatonic A minor) — raising the 7th scale degree (C→C♯) turns the v chord major, producing a more resolved, slightly exotic-sounding cadence than plain A minor, demonstrated A/B against the natural-minor version for direct comparison. Explicitly doesn't imply committing the whole piece to harmonic minor — this single-cadence-point usage (borrow the color only where the V chord lands) is described as more common in practice than sustaining the full mode. — BERNTH, [[2 String Shredding Trick - Economy Picking VS Alternate Picking]]
- **The tonic minor-major-7 chord (AmMaj7) as the practical "you need harmonic minor here" signal, plus two ready-to-use cadences with voicings.** AmMaj7's major 7th (G♯) is the exact note that clashes if a soloist defaults to natural minor's plain minor 7th (G) against it — a chord-first framing of the characteristic-note-matching principle. Two cadences: **i-iv-V-i** (AmMaj7 → Dm7 → E7 → AmMaj7) and **ii-V-i** (Bm7♭5 → E7 → AmMaj7, the harmonic-minor analog of the jazz turnaround). — BERNTH, [The Amazing Harmonic Minor Sound & Chords - Guitar Lesson - How To Write In This Key](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=52NpZjGPdYY)
