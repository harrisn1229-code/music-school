# Legato technique for speed and phrasing - Jazz and Bebop Guitar Lesson

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nnAFVTNxW6Y)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nnAFVTNxW6Y)

## Synopsis

Jens Larsen's central thesis: technique should be practiced in isolation but **used in combination** — a transcription of any good player shows several different techniques (hammer-ons, pull-offs, slides, alternate picking, small sweeps) mixed inside a single two-bar line, not one technical idea run straight through. The lesson is built backward from his usual format — instead of ending with worked examples, he opens with three ii-V-I lines in F (all over an altered dominant), analyzes exactly which technique does what and *why* in each one, then gives practice exercises for deliberately mixing techniques. The "why" is the most transferable part: legato (hammer-on/pull-off) and slides aren't just about speed — they're a **right-hand time-management tool** (buying the picking hand time to change strings or shift position) and a **phrasing/accent tool** (a legato note reads softer than a picked note, so technique choice is also dynamics choice). He also covers small two-string sweeps/economy picking as connective tissue between picked "accent" notes. Most valuable idea for practice: don't over-engineer combined-technique scale exercises past the point of usefulness — once the individual pieces are comfortable, switch to slow improvisation instead, because in real playing you don't (and can't) consciously think about which technique to use next.

## What's taught

### Why mix techniques at all
- **Real playing mixes techniques constantly — practice should too.** Transcriptions of good players show several different techniques inside a single two-bar line, not one idea run straight through; working on hammer-on/pull-off (or any technique) purely in isolation, on one scale or one exercise, under-prepares you for how it actually gets used.
- **The end goal is not thinking about technique while playing.** Technique is something you drill and internalize separately; once you're actually soloing, attention goes to the music — what's being played, what everyone else is doing — not to which technique to reach for. The isolated drilling and the mixing exercises below exist specifically so that integration happens automatically later.

### Technique as a right-hand time-management tool
- **Hammer-on/pull-off used specifically to buy the picking hand time to change strings.** In line 1 (see below), a pull-off from A down to G isn't there for speed — the next note (F) is on a different string, and using the pull-off instead of picking the G gives the right hand the extra beat it needs to get positioned on the new string before it has to pick again. The same logic applies to the line's opening hammer-on.
- **Slides do the same job from the fretting-hand side, plus give the picking hand a rest.** A slide covers ground with one pick attack instead of several, which both eases the physical demand and — same as the pull-off above — buys time to prepare the next string change.

### Technique as a phrasing/accent tool
- **A legato note reads softer than a picked note — so technique choice is also a dynamics choice.** Picking a note gives it more attack/volume than hammering onto or pulling off to the same note; choosing legato vs. picking for a given note is a direct way to control which notes in a line pop and which recede, without changing the notes themselves.
- **Stated rule of thumb (from Jens's own teacher): jazz phrase accents mostly land on high notes, off the beat.** Not an absolute — other notes get accented too — but a real starting heuristic for where to put emphasis in a bebop line.
- **Applying it: pick the note you want to pop, use legato for the note right before or after it that you want to recede.** Worked example — line 1's high A: picking it (rather than hammering onto it) makes it land as the phrase's accent point and "gives some life to the phrase"; the same idea runs the other way at a resolution — sliding *into* a resolution note (rather than picking it cleanly) deliberately softens/de-accents the landing, because the note just before the resolution sits higher in pitch than the resolution itself and you don't want the ear pulled toward the arrival.
- **This is a distinct, lower-level companion to [[Solo Dynamics and Arc]]'s "hammer-on/pull-off drilling gives you a controlled dismount" point** — that note covers arc-level dynamics (shaping a whole solo); this is the same mechanism (legato = softer, picked = louder) applied note-by-note within a single line.

### Slides as a dual-purpose device: phrasing *and* position shift
- **A slide can execute a note and physically relocate the hand at the same time.** Worked example, line 3: sliding is used to move from the hand position for a G minor chord idea into the position needed for the C7 altered scale that follows — one physical motion covers both "play this note" and "get in position to play the next phrase's scale," rather than treating position shifts as a separate mechanical problem from note choice.

### Small two-string sweeps / economy picking
- **Small (two-string) sweeps used as connective tissue between picked accent notes**, distinct from full sweep-picking arpeggio runs — see [[Sweep Picking Arpeggio Shapes]] for the larger-scale version of the same downstroke-based mechanic. Worked example, line 2: a downstroke pull-off into a two-string sweep, repeated as a descending "cascade" shape, then on the C7 a downstroke-upstroke-upstroke sweep executing a shape derived from a Db minor-major arpeggio.
- **Downstrokes function as the accent; swept notes are deliberately softer/slightly muffled** — same accent logic as the legato-vs-picked point above, just extended to picking-hand dynamics (downstroke = attack, sweep-through notes = recede) rather than fretting-hand technique choice.
- **Stated stylistic note: early jazz players' sweep notes were often near-ghost-notes/muted; Jens keeps his cleaner and more audible** — called out explicitly as a taste choice, not a "more correct" technique.

### Three worked ii-V-I lines in F (all over an alt dominant) — technique breakdown
1. **Line 1 — Bb major 7 arpeggio idea into a G/A passing tone, resolving.** Opens with an upstroke hammer-on into the Bb maj7 arpeggio shape, picks up to a passing A (outside the arpeggio, a G sits between), pulls off A→G (buys right-hand time before the string-change to F, which is picked), then continues with two slides (again for right-hand rest/ease of execution), and closes by sliding *into* the resolution rather than picking it cleanly — the note just before the resolution is higher in pitch, so sliding into the landing avoids over-accenting the arrival.
2. **Line 2 — cascading descending shapes built from downstroke-pull-off-into-two-string-sweep, repeated.** First descending gesture: downstroke, pull-off, two-string sweep. Second: the same shape, downstroke/two-string-sweep/downstroke. On the C7: downstroke pull-off, upstroke, then a shape derived from a Db minor-major arpeggio executed downstroke-upstroke-upstroke (the sweep/economy-picking idea above), running down the scale and resolving with another slide. Overall shape: several small downward movements, each followed by a move back up, an overall cascading descent.
3. **Line 3 — slides doing double duty (phrasing + position shift), most technique-dense of the three.** Picks the first two notes, slides up into a 16th-note-triplet "trill" feel, goes legato (hammer-on, pull-off), runs up the scale with alternate picking, then slides from C up to Db to shift into the next position (the G minor → C7 altered position shift described above) — nearly the same melodic shape as line 1's phrase, played over G minor this time. On the top note (rhythmically, the "and" of beat 3) the pull-off again both de-accents that note and buys time, resolving back down to A.

### Practice progression for mixing techniques
1. **Drill each technique in isolation first**, as usual (this lesson doesn't cover that step — it assumes it).
2. **Combine legato and alternate picking inside a 3-note-per-string major scale.** Take a scale you already know as 3-notes-per-string (worked in F major, in the position used for all three lines above). Per string: pick the first note, hammer on to the second, pick the third — then reverse it (hammer on first, pick the rest). Also try starting with two picked notes before a hammer-on. *(general starting point — no metronome number given in the source)*
3. **Notice the scale's character changes with the pattern, not just its speed.** Beyond building the mixed technique itself, each pattern makes the scale *sound* different — worth treating that tonal-color shift as a usable option in its own right, not just a side effect of the drill.
4. **Repeat the same drill using slides instead of hammer-ons**, for the slide-specific coordination (the left hand has to actually shift, unlike a hammer-on/pull-off in place).
5. **Economy-pick the same 3-note-per-string scale as a way into the small-sweep technique**, then mix that economy-picking with alternate picking the same way steps 2–4 mixed legato and picking.
6. **Don't keep building more complex combined-technique scale drills past this point — switch to slow improvisation instead.** Once individual technique-in-scale patterns are comfortable, the better next step is deliberately using the target technique (e.g. slides) while improvising slowly over a simple turnaround in F, rather than continuing to design more elaborate mixed-technique scale exercises. Explicitly framed as "not the kind of thing you need to work on for years" — a periodic check-in, not a permanent practice-block topic.
7. **Alternative/parallel method: compose lines deliberately using the target technique and notice where it actually fits well**, rather than only drilling it inside scales — sit down, write lines, and identify the musical contexts (which resolutions, which string changes) where the technique earns its place.
8. **Alternate picking is named as the technique everything else is built on** — Jens states it's what he works on first with any new material, then starts looking for places to substitute in other techniques (hammer-ons, slides, sweeps) rather than picking every note.
