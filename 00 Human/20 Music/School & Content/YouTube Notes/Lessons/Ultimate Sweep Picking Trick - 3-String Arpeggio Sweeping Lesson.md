# Ultimate Sweep Picking Trick - 3-String Arpeggio Sweeping Lesson

## Synopsis

A specific fix for the hardest moment in small sweep-picked arpeggio shapes — the direction-change transition — found by filming and reviewing personal practice footage, plus a broader recommendation to start sweep-picking technique on bigger shapes rather than small ones.

## Techniques

- **Start sweep picking with bigger arpeggio shapes, not small 3-string ones.** Smaller shapes force more frequent sweep-direction reversals for the same amount of musical material, and direction changes are the hardest part of sweep picking — bigger shapes give the right hand more time inside each direction before it has to reverse, making the underlying motion easier to internalize even though bigger shapes are harder to memorize.
- **Practice down-sweeps and up-sweeps separately for weeks before combining them** — the two motions are mechanically distinct enough to deserve isolated practice time rather than being drilled together from day one.
- **The trick itself, worked on a G minor 3-string arpeggio:** the hardest transition is the upstroke-to-downstroke direction change (G on the B string's 8th fret, upstroke, into D on the G string's 7th fret, downstroke) — the pick has to physically jump over the G string to get into position. **Fix: replace the transitional note with a hammer-on** instead of picking it, which gives the picking hand a brief pause to reposition cleanly for the downstroke rather than rushing the jump.
- **Self-diagnosis method: film and review practice footage** to isolate the *exact* transition that's the real bottleneck, rather than assuming or guessing which part of a shape is the problem.
- **Sound-quality target:** the hammered note must be dynamically indistinguishable from a picked note — a listener shouldn't be able to tell where legato was substituted for picking.
