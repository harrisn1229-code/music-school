# Zach Scholz - I Tried Recording a Song Like It's 1976

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6kA1mWeTiZc)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6kA1mWeTiZc)

## Synopsis

The longest and most concrete "recreation" video in the batch — Zach Scholz re-records a song he wrote years ago under self-imposed 1976-style rules: 16 tracks or fewer, all tone decisions locked in before pressing record, no endless revisions (MIDI has to print in real time, not stay editable), a full keys/guitar/bass/drums arrangement, and it has to land with real 1970s charm. He tracks the whole thing through his TASCAM M-30 mixer into his tape machine, consolidating parts down via literal tape bounces to stay under the track ceiling, reuses a specific compressed-snare trick from his own back catalog ("Flashback"), switches on the Hologram Chroma Console mid-song for a guitar swell (the same pedal covered in [[Zach Scholz - This Pedal Turns Clean Guitars Into Vintage Records]]), and closes with a tape-vs-digital reveal he himself calls "very, very close, hard to tell the difference" — reinforcing the thesis of his companion video [[Zach Scholz - Why 70's Records Sound so Warm (How to Get That Character)]] that the *workflow constraints*, not tape's sonic fingerprint alone, are doing most of the work. He ends up feeling MORE satisfied with this constrained re-recording than his original unconstrained version of the same song, despite added stress and having to drop a few ideas to stay under the track limit.

## Techniques

- **Self-imposed 1976 ruleset: ≤16 tracks total, all tone decisions finalized before recording, no editable MIDI (must print in real time), full band arrangement, must read as period-authentic** — the whole video is a test of whether hard constraints like these actually produce more creative/satisfying results, not just a nostalgia exercise.
- **Track-budget math drives every decision from the start** — he flags up front that a single mono guitar plus a reverb return already eats 2 of his 16 tracks, so the count climbs fast; his stated workaround is consolidating multiple tracks down to tape when he's about to go over the limit — but that consolidation is itself a one-way commitment: once bounced to tape, those tracks can't be adjusted individually again. This is the practical, load-bearing version of the "print to commit" idea from his other videos.
- **Acoustic guitar mic'd ~1 ft off a specific body region, deliberately avoiding the boomy spot** — tracked through a Warm Audio WA-87 R2 into channel 6 of the TASCAM M-30.
- **Acoustic guitar processing chain: EQ → compressor → tape saturation stacked on top of the compressor** — strumming hard drives the saturation into "wonky" breakup, and the part reads as a chorus-like stereo double once committed. → [[Saturation and Distortion]]
- **Layering move: a second, fingerpicked acoustic take bussed together with the strummed part** — he calls out how differently a fingerpicked take sits vs. a strummed one and uses that contrast deliberately, then buses the two together before consolidating to tape (rather than keeping them as separate tracks) to stay inside the track budget.
- **Walkthrough of an actual tape-bounce workflow:** headphones on tape monitor, select the tape return as the input source to check levels, adjust the line level while playing the part back, arm the track once happy with level, switch monitoring to tape, then press play simultaneously on the tape machine and in the computer to print the take to tape in sync. A concretely reusable step-by-step for anyone (including Nate, via [[Sony TC-630]]) bouncing a DAW part out to a physical deck and back.
- **Bass (his Höfner — the same [[Hofner B-Bass H-Series]] Nate owns) kept simple: light EQ through the M30, then a UAD Oxide tape plugin used explicitly to "tame the dynamics"** — i.e. reached for as a compression-adjacent tool, not just tone color, plus a bit of breakup/fattening as a side effect; saves the rest of the EQ decisions for the final mix stage rather than nailing everything at tracking. Finished with **very subtle spring reverb**. → [[BASS Slot]]
- **Reused the print-to-commit MIDI technique from [[Zach Scholz - Why 70's Records Sound so Warm (How to Get That Character)]]** — routes a MIDI instrument's output to an audio track so whatever effects are applied print in real time instead of staying editable, on both an organ part and a Mellotron-style string layer; drives a lot of frequencies into tape saturation on the strings specifically for "a little bit edgier" than the plain patch, then buses organ + strings together before sending to tape (another 2-track consolidation).
- **Signature snare trick reused from his own song "Flashback": scoop the fundamental frequency of the snare, THEN compress it hard** — the stated goal is "the close-mic sound of the snare but without so much of the punch, almost like the punch of an overhead" — removing the fundamental first changes what the compressor actually grabs, producing a fat-but-not-punchy snare character. A little spring reverb finishes it. All drums (kit's worth) run through the M30 and get consolidated down to just 2 tracks to hit the track budget. → [[TRANSIENT Slot]], [[Snare Design]]
- **Lead guitar: M30 → Guitar Rig (amp sim) → UAD Oxide for breakup** — he directly A/Bs with and without the Oxide stage to demonstrate how much of the perceived "amp" character is coming from the tape-emulation plugin rather than the amp sim alone.
- **Mid-song switch to the Hologram Chroma Console for a guitar swell, then switches back** — the same pedal covered in [[Zach Scholz - This Pedal Turns Clean Guitars Into Vintage Records]] elsewhere in this batch, here used as a deliberate mid-arrangement texture change rather than a whole-song effect — confirms the Chroma Console's swell module is a real, repeatable arrangement tool for him, not just a demo curiosity. Also adds a touch of **tape echo** on the lead part.
- **Reflection: more satisfied with this constrained re-recording than his original, unconstrained recording of the same song years earlier** — despite added stress while tracking and having to drop a few ideas entirely to stay under the 16-track ceiling; he's specifically curious whether small changes he wants to make now (particularly to the acoustic guitar, which is already committed to tape and can't be revisited) will still feel like improvements if he revisits them later, or whether the "locked" version will keep winning out.
- **States a future goal: a full end-to-end analog session using a Portastudio** — he wants to go further than this hybrid (M30 + tape-assisted, still finished digitally) session into a fully analog signal path, and deliberately kept this challenge "universal" (usable even without a tape deck — track consolidation can be done by printing in the computer instead) so more viewers could try it. Reinforces that a Portastudio is squarely on his own wishlist too — this vault's `Vintage Gear Worklist.md` already carries a **Tascam Portastudio 144/244** entry in its Tape/Echo section.
- **Final tape-vs-digital reveal: mix 1 (tape) vs. mix 2 (digital) — he calls the difference "very, very close, hard to tell"** — an honest, non-hyped result that lines up with his companion "Why 70's Records Sound so Warm" video's thesis: workflow constraints did more perceptual work than the tape pass itself.
