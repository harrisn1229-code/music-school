# Learn this ONE method to improve your guitar solos in under 5 minutes

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SF-1zhL_Tiw)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SF-1zhL_Tiw)

## Synopsis

Another "5-minute Monday" short-form lesson from guitar teacher Jesse Glick, this one built entirely around the **CAGE system** — the five movable open-chord shapes (C, A, G, E, D) used to find the same chord in five places up the neck. Glick assumes the viewer already knows the CAGE chord-shape basics (he points to an earlier video for that) and spends this lesson turning the chord map into a **soloing map**: three escalating ways to use those five anchor positions to navigate the fretboard with intention instead of relying on memorized licks. He walks through finding the major pentatonic scale at each of the five CAGE positions, then layering the full major scale on top of the same five positions for extra color, and closes with what he calls out directly as the most valuable tip in the video — borrowing the rhythm (not the pitches) of a familiar song melody, worked example "…Baby One More Time" by Britney Spears, to practice phrasing independently of note choice. It's a compact, practical sequel to his CAGE-shape and pentatonic-mastery videos rather than a from-scratch theory lesson.

## Techniques

- **[~0:30] Prerequisite reminder — what the CAGE system is.** Five chord shapes (C, A, G, E, D) used to find the same chord all across the neck; worked example is a C chord found as a C-shape, then A-shape, G-shape, E-shape, and finally D-shape grip moving up the fretboard. Full explanation deferred to an earlier video in his catalog.
- **[~1:40] Technique 1 — find the major pentatonic scale at each of the five CAGE root positions.** Fast way to locate the shape: the major pentatonic scale over a chord shares its notes with the **minor pentatonic scale three frets below the same root on the low E string** (e.g. C major pentatonic = A minor pentatonic, same five notes) — so find the minor pentatonic box 3 frets down from the root instead of counting scale degrees from scratch. Demonstrated across all five CAGE positions (C-shape, A-shape, G-shape, E-shape, D-shape) for a C chord; the C-shape and A-shape positions are called out as "a little bit tricky." Practice sequence: drill each position individually first, then solo within one position, then connect the five positions into one continuous line across the neck.
- **[~5:00] Technique 2 — overlay the full major scale on the same five CAGE positions.** The major pentatonic covers an estimated **~80%** of chord progressions a player solos over day to day and is the default vocabulary; the full major scale is layered in on top of the same five root positions **for extra color/flavor** when the pentatonic alone feels thin. Each major-scale shape is shown as a one-octave fingering starting from that position's root — extend a second octave by ear rather than memorizing a separate two-octave shape. Combined pentatonic + major-scale vocabulary across all five CAGE positions is framed as covering an estimated **~90%** of songs a player will ever solo over.
- **[~7:00] Technique 3 (the standout tip) — borrow the rhythm of a familiar song melody to practice phrasing, independent of pitch.** Diagnosis: memorizing scale shapes (pentatonic and major, per Techniques 1–2) doesn't by itself sound musical — that comes from rhythm, which gets under-practiced relative to note choice. Fix: pick a well-known melody (worked example: "…Baby One More Time" by Britney Spears), discard its actual pitches, and replay **only its rhythmic contour** using whichever scale notes are already under the fingers from the position being drilled. Decouples rhythm practice from relearning notes and gives an unlimited, free source of practice material — any melody the player can hum. Explicitly flagged by Glick as the tip worth sticking around for.
- **General framing tip:** the three techniques stack — pentatonic-per-position first, major-scale-per-position layered on second, rhythm-borrowing applied on top of either — rather than being three independent, unrelated exercises.
