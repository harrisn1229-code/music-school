# Taking your ideas to the next level with Young Guru

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2PJzFvkZvCo)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2PJzFvkZvCo)

## Synopsis

The direct sequel to [[Starting a new production with Young Guru]] — Young Guru (longtime Jay-Z engineer/producer) picks up exactly where that video left off: "now that we're outside of Ableton and I've bounced what I did in Ableton, we put it in Pro Tools." This is a screen-recorded walkthrough of an actual idea session (a Chairman of the Board sample flip built with his production partner Volley Contra), and it's the most concrete, technique-dense entry in the pair — where part one was mostly mindset/sourcing, this one shows real editing decisions on real waveforms. Three threads run through it: (1) **phase alignment between layered drum sounds** (kick, 808, sample break) — the biggest single concept he says he tries to teach people, demonstrated by zooming into waveforms and nudging/chopping hits so the peaks of all three line up; (2) **an editing philosophy of addition-then-subtraction** — throw everything in like a De La Soul record, then edit yourself down, illustrated by a vocal-chop layer that runs the whole track in the raw idea but gets muted for the second half in the edit; and (3) **layering Junos under the sample** — simple, human-feel synth parts added as texture, balanced so they feel like they were part of the original record's session rather than sitting on top of it, including copying the same MIDI to a second Juno patch purely to generate an alternate flavor to choose from (even one that ends up unused). It closes with the handoff: Young Guru sends the finished Pro Tools idea session to Volley, who arranges the actual song around it — and the video plays a clip of Volley's arrangement pass so you can hear the raw idea turned into a structured intro/hook. Ends with the origin of their duo name, Coup D'état, and this song being one of the best first tracks the group made.

## Techniques

- **Phase is the single biggest layering concept he says he tries to get across to people — misaligned cycles between layered drum sounds can make you LOSE punch even as you're adding more material.** Definition given on camera, in plain terms: every wave file has a peak then a slope down — "a cycle" — and if a kick's cycle isn't hitting at the same moment as the 808's cycle, which isn't hitting at the same moment as the break beat's cycle, they start to fight each other instead of reinforcing. → [[SUB Slot]], [[BASS Slot]], [[TRANSIENT Slot]], [[Phase Alignment]]
- **The fix is by eye AND by ear, not one or the other: zoom into the waveforms to see exactly where each hit's peak lands, then nudge the 808 and kick tracks in time until the kick-peak, 808-peak, and break-beat-peak line up at (or very close to) the same moment.** The break beat is treated as the fixed anchor — "my break beat is the thing that has the feel, the human feel in it, it's the thing that I'm trying to emulate" — so the kick and 808 get moved to match it, not the other way around. Muting down to just a couple of tracks and zooming into different sections of the arrangement (not just the top) is part of the check — alignment can drift section to section even within one song. *(general starting point — no exact ms nudge amounts given; described qualitatively as "sliding them over" by eye/ear)* → [[Phase Alignment]], [[Kick Design]], [[Bass Design]]
- **Willing to chop and nudge EVERY SINGLE KICK individually for phase alignment once the session is in Pro Tools, even though it's usually unnecessary because it was already accounted for during programming — "now that I have it in Pro Tools, why not, if I have the ability to do it."** Explicit reasoning for spending the time on a detail nobody will ever consciously notice: "no one cares how long a song took to make, no one cares how long a beat took to make — all they do is listen to the song." The time investment is justified purely by the result, not by whether the listener could ever detect the specific fix. → [[Phase Alignment]], [[Committing and Printing]]
- **A named editing philosophy — "more than what you put in, it's what you keep out" — practiced as addition-then-subtraction: throw everything into the idea like a kitchen sink first, then edit yourself down afterward.** Cites this as modeled on favorite groups like De La Soul, where "all these little parts... come in but they all contribute to the record." Explicitly two separate passes, not one: add a bunch of things AND take out a bunch of things — the second pass is where the actual editorial judgment happens. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **Worked example of the addition-then-subtraction move, on the actual session:** a set of vocal-chop/voice layers run through the WHOLE track in the raw idea, but there's a visible mute decision partway through the timeline dropping them out for the second half of the arrangement — subtraction applied to a real element on a real timeline, not just stated as theory. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]], [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]]
- **Layer a simple, deliberately non-virtuosic Juno synth part on top of the sample, played live (not programmed) to keep it human — "you can tell that's me playing it, right, because it's not the most complicated... it's me just with rhythm."** The part is described as going "along with the sample" — following its existing melodic/rhythmic content rather than introducing new harmonic ideas. → [[Layering and Stacking]], [[Roland Juno-106]]
- **The core leveling/balance rule for adding an instrument on top of an existing sample: mix it so it feels like the added part "was there the day that they made the original song" — not so loud it hovers over the sample, not so low you can't feel it.** Stated as his general approach to live shows and to sample-based production broadly, not just this one track: "some things are just feel things that should be underneath, but me, I like to get a perfect blend." Explicitly names the failure mode both directions: too loud and you lose the reason you chose that sample in the first place; too quiet and the layer does nothing. → [[Layering and Stacking]], [[Gain Staging]], [[Sampling and Sound Selection]]
- **The Juno layer is itself arranged with entrances/exits tied to structure — it drops out for a stretch, then returns, specifically timed to what turns out to be the hook section**, rather than running constantly through the idea. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **A second, different Juno part enters for a later section, described the same way (simple, following the sample's own notes) — used as the signal of a NEW section rather than a continuation of the first Juno part.** → [[Arrangement and Song Form]], [[Layering and Stacking]]
- **Copy the exact same MIDI part onto a second instance of the same instrument on a different patch, purely to audition an alternate texture/flavor — even when that alternate ends up unused in the final version.** Described directly: "this Juno 3... it's literally me copying that same MIDI to another channel and getting a different flavor out of my Juno — so it's the same MIDI, but a different Juno patch... I was trying to give myself different sound palettes." At the end of this session that particular alternate wasn't used at all, but the point wasn't to guarantee a keeper — it was to generate an option. → [[Layering and Stacking]], [[Sound Palette]]
- **"No song exists" mindset during the idea-generation stage — he doesn't know what the track will end up being about, or what the collaborator will ultimately do with it; he's deliberately just producing bits and pieces to have things to choose from later, including material he already expects to mute or discard.** Framed as a direct cause of the "more you put in, the more flavor/space the arranger has to work with" principle below — over-generating with no attachment to any one part surviving is what makes the addition-then-subtraction workflow productive rather than wasteful. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **The stated payoff of over-generating options: "the more I put in, the more flavor we have to play with, the more Volley has space to write around, the more things he can do."** More raw material (even material that's later cut) directly expands what a co-writer/arranger can build with — framed as a reason to keep layering rather than stopping early. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **Handoff workflow: once the Pro Tools idea session is where he wants it, he emails the session to Volley, who arranges the actual song structure around the beat.** Volley is described specifically as "very good at arranging a song" — not just writing on top, but making the structural decisions (what plays when) that turn a layered idea into a song. → [[Career & Release]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **Reveal of the arranger's pass, played back on camera as a worked before/after: the finished intro strips the drums out entirely — no kick, no 808 — with the 808 only entering after the first four bars, and the sample loop alone is what actually starts the track; the vocal doesn't come in until later, timed to "the scream" playing off the end of the hook.** A real, audible example of arrangement-by-staggered-entrance built directly from this session's layered idea — worth comparing against this vault's existing "let the beat build" entries on [[Arrangement and Song Form]]. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **A compatible-collaborator trait named directly: a production partner who "understands his voice" delivers vocals back already carrying textures/treatment, not a raw dry take** — a workflow note about what makes a long-running two-person team (see [[Career & Release]] for the MPC → Ableton → Pro Tools pipeline from part one) work smoothly, rather than a technique to apply solo. → [[Career & Release]]
- **The duo's project name origin: "Coup D'état"** — considered "Coup de Grâce" first, settled on "Coup D'état" as more intense ("a small group of people taking over a government, or overthrowing a king"), stated as representative of who they are as a group; this particular song is described as one of the best first songs the duo ever made. *(context/backstory — not a technique, logged for completeness per this vault's "capture everything" standard)*
