# Triad Pairs - How To Use Them On a Minor Blues

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5WSYWASkaGs)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5WSYWASkaGs)

## Synopsis

Jens Larsen's deep-dive lesson on **triad pairs** — combining two triads with no common notes as a
compound source of line material, associated with modern/modal jazz phrasing but usable in any style.
The video opens by playing a full two-chorus solo over a **C minor blues**, then spends the bulk of its
20 minutes breaking that solo down chord by chord: which scale each triad pair comes from, why that pair
fits the underlying chord, and which line-construction pattern is being used to turn the pair into an
actual phrase (as opposed to an audibly mechanical run of inversions). The theoretical core is compact —
harmonize a scale into seven triads, and any two of them that share no notes are automatically adjacent
in the scale — but the video is thorough about applying it: it covers the tonic minor chord, the V7
altered dominant, the iv chord, and two different turnaround voicings across two full choruses, plus four
distinct ways to physically play a pair on guitar (a combined arpeggio line, uneven note groupings to
disguise the mechanical construction, a repeated top-note pedal, and string-set pairing). Larsen
explicitly flags this as longer and denser than his usual videos because of how much ground the one solo
covers. Sales pitch for chord-voicing content and the channel's Patreon bookend the lesson; no tabs or
notation are provided or referenced — everything is described in note names, scale degrees, and inversions.

## What's taught

**Core mechanic**
- **Triad pairs come from harmonizing a scale into seven triads (one per degree) and picking two with no
  common notes.** Worked on C melodic minor (the scale associated with a minor-tonic sound): C minor, D
  minor, E♭ augmented, F major, G major, A half-diminished, B diminished.
- **No-common-notes always means scale-adjacent, in a 7-note scale.** Stated as a general fact and true
  of every pair used in the video: the two triads of a working pair are always next to each other in the
  parent scale's harmonized-triad sequence.
- **Fast shortcut for finding a pair over any chord:** decide the one scale note you *don't* want to hear
  over that chord, drop it, and the remaining six notes split cleanly into the two triads of the pair —
  faster than deriving the full seven-triad table each time.

**Chord-by-chord application over the C minor blues**
- **Bar 1, Cm (tonic minor):** C melodic minor, pair = E♭ augmented + F major. E♭ augmented is the upper
  structure (3rd-5th-7th) of a Cm(maj7); F major adds the root, 6th/13th, and 11th — combined, a
  **Cm(maj7) with a 13 and 11** sound. Construction: arpeggiate E♭aug, step down one scale degree to the
  3rd of F major, play F major first inversion, step back down into another E♭aug.
- **Bar 4, C7 altered (V7 of the iv chord):** scale = D♭ melodic minor, a half step above C melodic minor
  (same relationship documented on [[Scale Modes Reference]] for the altered scale). Pair = E augmented +
  G♭ major — literally the tonic pair's shapes shifted up a half step. Construction: a **4-note pattern**
  on the E-augmented side followed by a **3-note pattern** descending through a second-inversion G♭
  major — deliberately uneven groupings so the line doesn't sound like a straight run of inversions.
- **Bar 5–6, F minor (iv):** rather than F melodic minor (whose major 7th, E natural, sounds like an odd
  clash of C's major 3rd on a minor blues), Larsen defaults to **F Dorian**. Pair = F minor + G minor
  (named as "F minor and G major" in narration but played and resolved as G minor in the worked
  example — F Dorian's own harmonized triads support G minor, not G major). Construction: run up a
  first-inversion F minor triad, step down two scale degrees (not one) to the D, play a second-inversion
  G minor triad, then resolve back into C minor.
- **Alt. tonic-chord pair, C minor + D minor:** C minor as the chord itself, stacked with D minor on top
  (adds the 9, 11, 13) — a Dorian-flavored read of the same tonic even though both triads also live
  inside C melodic minor. Played guitaristically via **string sets**: C minor on the middle string set,
  D minor (2nd inversion) on the high string set, alternating — demonstrated ascending (C→D→C) and
  descending (D→C→D), producing a fast, dramatic low-to-high or high-to-low line.
- **Turnaround 1 (end of first chorus), A♭7 → G7 altered:** A♭7 functions as the tritone dominant of
  D7/a Lydian-dominant chord; its scale is melodic minor built from the chord's **5th** — E♭ melodic
  minor (matches [[Scale Modes Reference]]'s Lydian-dominant-as-melodic-minor-IV-mode entry). Pair = E♭
  minor + F minor — **two minor triads**, a pair quality not used elsewhere yet in the video. Same
  4-note-then-descending-triad grouping as bar 4, giving a 7-note pattern that **floats across the bar
  line** in 4/4 rather than repeating predictably. Moving to G7 altered (= A♭ melodic minor), pair = B
  augmented + D♭ major (2nd inversion) — same repeated-top-note idea, resolving down to G and into C
  minor.
- **13-sound tonic-chord pair (later in first chorus), Cm7:** E♭ major + F major (both first inversion) —
  the plain (non-augmented) upper structure of Cm7 plus the 13/11/root from F major, a **Cm13** color,
  explicitly distinguished from the opening's E♭*augmented* pair (that one implies a major-7, this one a
  plain ♭7 — Dorian, not melodic-minor).
- **Second-chorus 4-bar turnaround approach (Cm → C melodic minor → C7 altered):** worked here primarily
  via **string sets rather than fixed scale positions** — because the pairs used are all two triads of
  the *same quality* transposed, the same physical string-set shape can just be **shifted around the
  neck** chord to chord: E♭ major + F major over the Dorian-sounding tonic, then F major + G major moving
  into C melodic minor proper, then G♭ major + A♭ major moving into C7 altered (= D♭ melodic minor).
  Larsen notes he thinks of these as chord shapes rather than "arpeggios" even when playing them
  arpeggiated.
- **F minor (iv), second chorus:** F minor + G minor pair again (same pair as bar 6), this time played as
  **two notes per string** rather than one note per string — a descending first-inversion F minor
  followed by a descending second-inversion G minor.
- **Cm, second chorus — "complete melodic minor minus the root":** E♭ augmented (first inversion) + D
  minor. E♭ augmented spells the 3rd-5th-7th of Cm(maj7); D minor adds the 9 (D), 11 (F), and 13 (A) —
  together, the entire C-melodic-minor sound except the root. Construction: from G, descend the
  first-inversion E♭ augmented, step down to D minor, step down again to another E♭ triad, ending on G♭
  moving into A♭7. Also demonstrated in root position (E♭ → D minor 2nd inversion → E♭ augmented) as a
  symmetrical alternative for moving across the fretboard, and starting from G with the augmented triad
  instead, to generate further pattern variations.
- **Turnaround 2 (end of second chorus), A♭7 → G7 altered — a new pair quality:** the upper structure of
  A♭7 is a **C diminished triad** (not augmented/major/minor — the first diminished-triad pair in the
  video), combined with a **B♭ major** triad. Together: 3rd-5th-♭7 of A♭7 (from the diminished triad)
  plus a 9♯11-13 color from the B♭ major triad — the full lydian-dominant upper structure minus the
  root. Moving to G7 altered: **A♭ minor + B♭ minor**, the same two-minor-triad device as turnaround 1,
  ascending from B through a first-inversion B♭ minor and resolving to G and C minor.
