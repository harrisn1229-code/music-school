# Jazzy Pop Beat Breakdown | Studio One 6 Demo Song

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bLgGRif-bj8)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bLgGRif-bj8)

## Synopsis

Max Konyi breaks down the musical construction of the "pop disco" first section of the official Studio One 6 demo song he wrote (made in **Studio One**; the techniques below are DAW-agnostic — drum programming, chord-writing method, layering/arrangement all map onto Ableton). Extremely dense harmony lesson: he walks through his complete chord-writing process for a section with jazz-inflected, constantly-changing harmony (sus/no-3rd chords, inverted slash chords, dense 9th/11th voicings) written *around* a pre-existing lead vocal melody, plus his drum-programming layering approach (kick/snare/alternating-velocity hats/ride/tambourine as separate rhythmic subdivisions), a real bass part sourced from a remote collaborator, and an atmosphere-building guitar/pad/string layering approach used to differentiate the chorus from the post-chorus using the *same* harmony played by *different* instruments. A companion mixing-focused video for this same section exists but wasn't part of this transcript pull.

## Techniques

**Drum programming (~1:20–4:30)**
- **Layer rhythmic subdivisions across different drum elements rather than one busy pattern** — 8th-note division on the hi-hats, 16th-note division on the tambourine loop, quarter-note emphasis on the ride cymbal; each element occupies its own subdivision so the groove reads as dense without any single part being busy.
- **Alternate between two different hi-hat samples (not one sample twice) for a more realistic feel** — heavy/soft/heavy/soft in an 8th-note pattern using two different closed-hat samples rather than the same sample at two velocities; open hats dropped in as an occasional "breath" at the end of a phrase.
- **Extra kicks + toms at the end of an 8-bar phrase as a "pre-fill" anticipation device** — a couple of extra kick hits followed by toms right before a section change reads as "something's about to happen" rather than a full drum fill.
- **A dedicated "snare pop" layered specifically with drum fills** (an EDM-inspired hit) used to fill gaps in the vocal phrasing at section transitions (e.g. right as the chorus starts).
- **Different sample sets per section on the same drum-kit structure/rack, not different racks** — the intro section reuses the same underlying kit layout as the main pop section but with its own, differently-processed sample set, so the groove architecture stays consistent while the section still sounds distinct.

**Chord writing method (~4:30–19:00)**
- **Write the top-line harmony note first, then harmonize downward.** For dense funk/comping chord parts sitting under a busy, fast-moving harmony, write the *top* note of each chord first (matching the melody), confirm it feels right, then harmonize in two parts underneath — building 3-note chords from the top down rather than picking full voicings first.
- **Chords with no 3rd (sus chords, slash chords) as the default harmonic vocabulary**, not a special effect — used throughout for an open, ambiguous character that still supports a busy, changing melody without clashing. → [[Suspended Chords]]
- **Write the chords around an existing melody, tracking scale degrees, not just "stacking 7ths and 9ths and hoping it works."** When harmony is dense and changes often, picking generic extended chords by feel WILL clash with the melody — know which scale degree the melody is hitting at each moment (e.g. melody on the 3rd vs. chord voicing on the 9th at the same time) before choosing the chord tones.
- **A melody written to one chord progression can still work over a different, related progression — but only if the new progression preserves the ORIGINAL's functional bass motion/flow**, not just its notes. The vocal melody here was originally written to a simpler reference progression; the final progression is "inspired by it" but denser — it works because the harmonic *flow* (where the functional pulls happen) still matches what the melody was composed against, even though the actual chords differ.
- **Use inversions to control bass-line direction independent of chord identity** — the same chord progression repeated a second time swaps a first-inversion chord for its root-position form specifically to reverse the bass line's direction (was walking up, now walks down) — a deliberate anti-repetition device on a progression's second pass.
- **Walk a descending bass line by inserting an extra passing chord**, harmonizing every bass note rather than leaving one unharmonized — e.g. inserting a brief slash chord solely to give the walking bass note (D♭ → C → B♭) a harmonized landing at each step. → [[Chromatic Bass Lines|Chromatic Bass Lines]]
- **A non-diatonic ("modal") chord dropped into an otherwise-diatonic progression reads as a deliberate color shift** — described as feeling more "serious"/less playful than the diatonic version of the same progression; used sparingly (once per pass) for exactly that reason.
- **When a melodic run lands on quarter notes, harmonize every single note of the run** — a fast descending/ascending vocal run gets a chord under every note rather than one sustained chord under the whole run; work backward from the run's landing chord to figure out the harmonization.
- **Foreshadow a later song section's harmonic surprise by planting a smaller version of it earlier** — a chord quality that appears as a bigger surprise later in the song (a minor section's chord suddenly turning major) gets planted as a subtle single-instance variation earlier in the song (changing one recurring chord from minor to its major equivalent for two beats) so the ear has been quietly primed before the later, bigger version lands.
- **Vocal tuning to enable a harmonic surprise, not just pitch correction** — retuned the lead vocal's actual sung note specifically so a written harmonic surprise (minor chord suddenly landing on a major chord under a sustained vocal note) would actually work against what was sung; also had to retune the harmony stack to match.

**Arrangement / instrumentation contrast (~19:00–24:00)**
- **Use the SAME chord progression/voicings played by DIFFERENT instrumentation to distinguish two adjacent sections, rather than writing new harmony** — post-chorus reuses the verse/chorus progression essentially unchanged, but a sustaining synth pad (instead of stabby piano+synth) doubles the chords, strings enter, and the overall texture goes from "stabby and spacious, lots of gaps" to "everything holding, thick and present" — a full mood shift achieved through texture/instrumentation, not new chords. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **Two rhythm guitars through big reverb + chorus, playing the same chords in different voicings (one lower, one higher), hard-panned L/R** as a pure atmosphere/world-building layer, not a lead part — meant to be felt as ambience, not consciously noticed. Later bounced together, pitched up an octave, and slowly faded in over time as a separate rising atmosphere layer (kept its own bounce artifacts deliberately — "I kind of like the artifacts, it adds some interest"). Dropped out entirely when a new section starts, so its disappearance clears space for what comes next. → [[2 Guitar Group|Guitar Group]]
- **Raise chord voicings a full octave (losing low-mid body/weight) as a section-transition device** — going into the pre-chorus, the exact same chords/voicings jump up an octave, making them read as "bright and stabby" instead of full-bodied, while everything else (drums, harmony) stays the same — pure register change as arrangement contrast.
- **Layer counter-melody + accent instruments that are barely consciously noticed but audibly missed if removed** ("world building") — a counter-melody that rhythmically interlocks with the vocal (sustains where the vocal moves, moves where the vocal sustains), a synth accent doubling the vocal's sustained note with its own LFO/reverb only on that one moment, a background "twinkle bells" figure, and a slow-opening/closing filtered arpeggio — none of these are the point of the section individually, but pulling them all out would be audibly felt. → [[Ear Candy and Hiding Synths]]

**Bass (~24:00–29:00)**
- **Write a placeholder bassline on keyboard/MIDI first, practice it on real bass repeatedly to internalize the feel, THEN have it re-recorded by an actual bass player** if you don't personally play well enough for the part — sent a collaborator (a real bassist) the MIDI reference and had them record a live take, which replaced the MIDI part.
- **Start writing a bassline by vocalizing a rhythm/groove/vibe with no fixed pitches in mind**, record that scratch vocal, then go back and assign actual notes to the vocalized rhythm once the feel is right — pitch comes after groove, same "silhouette-first" logic as melody writing.
- **Roots-only bassline with expressive pitch bend** as the main vocabulary, rather than busy note choices — bend "wherever it felt good," finding small ways to modulate/move/create interest within an otherwise simple roots-only part.
- **"Neurobass"-style layered sound design via repeated bounce-mangle-reload cycles** — take a simple sound, mangle with distortion, bounce, reload into a sampler, mangle again with phasers etc., bounce again — used here for a short growl/rip fill that punctuates section transitions ("almost like a bass fill").

## Sound/tone selection

- **Layered chord-instrument stack: transient piano ("bang, really smacks") + sustained synth layer, played together as one harmonic unit** for the main pop-disco chords — piano supplies the percussive attack, synth supplies the tonal body. → [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **A big two-octave saw-wave stack (a vocoder-adjacent synth patch) doubling the chord progression** as a "fat" pre-chorus transition sound. → [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **Legato string patch** (Presence factory library — "not the greatest sounding thing in the world on its own") used purely as a section-level texture layer in a busy mix, where it doesn't need to be a standout sound to do its job. → [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **A real electric bass (flatwound-adjacent rounded tone), remotely recorded by a collaborator**, used specifically for this pop-disco section rather than the whole song, which otherwise runs MIDI/synth bass.
