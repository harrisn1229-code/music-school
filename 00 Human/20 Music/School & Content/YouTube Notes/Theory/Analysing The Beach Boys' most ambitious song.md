# Analysing The Beach Boys' most ambitious song

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wSbT0ovpr7Y)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wSbT0ovpr7Y)

## Synopsis

David Bennett breaks down "Good Vibrations" (Brian Wilson/Beach Boys) — a "pocket symphony" of tempo, key, and instrumentation changes stitched together from separate multi-day, multi-studio recording sessions, predating episodic songs like "Bohemian Rhapsody." Covers the melodic bass part, the Andalusian-cadence verse, an escalating chorus modulation that doubles as its own return transition, the famous electro-theremin, and reverb used to mask tape-splice edit points between sessions.

## Techniques

- **Melodic bass + a second bass instrument dividing register roles**: the bass guitar (picked, high on the neck) weaves a genuine counter-melody around the vocal rather than anchoring the harmony; a double bass added below it takes over straight root-note duty — two basses at once works because they're doing different jobs.
- **Verse harmony**: an Andalusian-cadence progression (i–♭VII–♭VI–V) in E♭ minor, modulating to the relative major (G♭) at the end of each verse.
- **Chorus in Mixolydian, not plain major**: the chorus sits in G♭ Mixolydian (lowered 7th) for a bluesier color than straight G♭ major.
- **Escalating chorus modulation that solves its own return transition**: the same 4-bar chorus motif restates three times, climbing G♭→A♭→B♭ (a whole step each jump); B♭ then functions as the dominant (V) of the verse's E♭ minor, giving a clean perfect-cadence modulation straight back into the next verse. A later chorus reverses the climb (B♭→A♭→G♭) to avoid repeating the exact same shape.
- **The electro-theremin ("Tannerin")**: not a true hands-in-the-air theremin but a slider-controlled variant (easier to pitch accurately), played by its inventor Paul Tanner on the actual recording — added specifically to push an already "eerie" cello line into something more overt.
- **Reverb used to mask tape-splice edit points between separately recorded sessions**: an abrupt tempo/key/instrumentation shift into a slower F major interlude section is a literal cut between takes recorded on different days/locations; a large, long-decay reverb (including a hanging F11 vocal-harmony chord) disguises both that cut and the transition into the final chorus.
