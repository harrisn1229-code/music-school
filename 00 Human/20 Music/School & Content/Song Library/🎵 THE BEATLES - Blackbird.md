# Blackbird — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

Very few raw materials, recombined so economically that the song sounds far richer than its parts. Two things to take away: the **guitar part is a chain of parallel tenths, and the melodic motion of those tenths — not root progression — is what the ear follows**, so many bars simply do not contain an analysable chord; and the phrasing is free-verse uneven almost everywhere.

**Form.** Verse phrases run **3½ + 4 + 3 + 4** bars; the refrain is **4 + 5** despite a plainly parallel AA' shape ([[ARR-C06 — Section-length conventions, and breaking them on purpose]]). A 4-bar instrumental C phrase compresses the previous six bars and appears after the first verse only. The back half is intricate: refrain into a full instrumental verse, back into a second refrain, then an extension that halts like an ending, then a pseudo-intro made of two phrases elided together — at which point the birdsong enters and the last verse begins.

**Harmony.** Roman numerals are deliberately sparse here. You can name a chord in every bar if you insist, but the motion is carried by **stepwise, partly chromatic bass under parallel tenths**, and naming the passing sonorities misrepresents what you hear ([[POP-C04 — Four chords with a moving bass]], [[HAR-C09 — Non-harmonic tones]]). Structurally, the refrain's first phrase is a long **prolongation of IV** that pulls back at the last moment; the second phrase goes ahead and reaches V-of-V ([[HAR-C07 — Secondary dominants]]). Verses are plain major, refrains shift to a bluesy Dorian.

**Melody.** The song's peak is a high G in the middle of the refrain landing on that V-of-V — highest note and strongest harmonic goal in the same instant.

**Arrangement.** Acoustic guitar and a metronome tick, and that is all. The birdsong is a lesson in effect placement: held back for the second half only, and allowed to run a second or two past the last note.
