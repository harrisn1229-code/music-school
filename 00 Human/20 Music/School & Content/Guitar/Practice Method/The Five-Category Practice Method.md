# The Five-Category Practice Method

Brandon D'Eon's (52-Week Guitar Player, jazz degree) answer to "why do intermediate players plateau despite hours of practice": most drills only train one of **five categories — fretboard knowledge, speed/technique, rhythm, theory, and improvisation** — in isolation, and isolated drills have low transfer. His fix is a small set of drills each deliberately engineered to hit all five at once. Compare [[The Five Essentials of Guitar Practice]] (Daniel Seriff) — same "combine categories instead of isolating them" philosophy from a different jazz-trained teacher, with independently-arrived-at overlap on the backing-track-subdivision mechanism (see Routine 3 below vs. Seriff's Consistent Rhythms drill) — and [[The Five Pillars of Jazz Guitar Practice]] (Nathan Borton). Three teachers converging on "integrate, don't isolate" from different angles is a decent signal this is a load-bearing principle, not one creator's idiosyncrasy.

**Scheduling rule (the load-bearing meta-point): pick exactly one of the four routines below, run it daily for 2–3 weeks before evaluating, then move on if desired.** Don't try to run all four at once — breadth here works against the method. If 12 keys is too slow to sustain when starting out, 6 of the 12 is an acceptable scope; with reps, a session compresses to roughly 30 minutes.

## Routine 1 — Random-key scale-degree naming (fretboard + rhythm + theory)

- Generate a random key (a random-key-generator tool works, or shuffle a deck of the 12 keys yourself), start a stopwatch, then play the major scale of that key **starting on the offbeats**, not the downbeat.
- Say each note's **letter name and its scale degree relative to the key's root** aloud as you play it (e.g. "B-flat — the fourth").
- **Anchor the run to a string other than low E** — find the lowest in-key note on the A string (or another non-E string) without using open strings, and start there. Always defaulting to E is called out as a crutch that leaves the fretboard unmapped everywhere else.
- Play the full ascend-then-descend pass, stop the stopwatch, write the time down. Progress is tracked as **that time shrinking across repeated sessions on the same or different keys.**
- Metronome runs throughout to carry the rhythm component.

## Routine 2 — Sweep arpeggio shape through all 12 keys (technique + rhythm + fretboard + theory)

- Take one sweep-picking arpeggio shape. Instead of drilling it in a single key/position (technique-only, low transfer), run the **same shape through all 12 keys with a metronome**.
- Vary the starting string — don't default to low E every rep.
- Because you're moving the shape through every key, you're forced to know which notes make up each key's arpeggio — the theory component rides along with the technique reps rather than needing a separate drill.

## Routine 3 — Backing-track position/subdivision improv (all five categories — the most complete of the four)

1. Put on a backing track in a known key.
2. Pick one fixed fretboard position/area and stay in it.
3. Pick a rhythmic subdivision — quarter notes, eighth notes, triplets, or sixteenths.
4. Improvise inside that position, holding the chosen subdivision consistently for the whole pass rather than drifting.

Rhythmic drift here is diagnosed as a **technique** problem, not a feel problem. *(Structurally identical to Daniel Seriff's Consistent Rhythms drill in [[The Five Essentials of Guitar Practice]] — same backing-track + fixed-subdivision-improv mechanism, arrived at independently by a different jazz-trained teacher.)*

## Routine 4 — Spoken major scale in every key (theory + rhythm + fretboard, weakest of the four for technique)

- Play the major scale in a key, speaking each note's letter name aloud in time with a metronome, ascending then descending, then move to the next key.
- Explicitly the weakest of the four for raw technical development — it's played slowly — but the constant left-hand pressure needed to keep a slow note sustained (it stops sounding the instant the hand relaxes) still gives it some technique value.

## Improvisation as diagnostic

Improv is treated as the category that most directly exposes weaknesses elsewhere: staying in one fretboard area under improv → a fretboard-knowledge gap; failing to target chord tones → a fretboard-knowledge gap; not knowing what to play over a backing track → a theory gap; messy/out-of-time lines → a rhythm gap needing metronome work. Real-time improv forces all four other categories to fire simultaneously, which is why it's framed as practicing them all by proxy.

- **Three worked examples of building one exercise that trains technique and theory simultaneously, rather than treating them as separate practice blocks.** (1) Practice legato patterns while also targeting fretboard blind spots in the harmonic minor scale — two skills drilled in the same repetition. (2) An alternate/economy-picking lick that switches between three minor modes each repetition — Aeolian, Phrygian, Dorian — forcing a fretting-hand adjustment of the one characteristic scale degree that changes each time (Phrygian: lower the 2nd; Dorian: raise the 6th to major) — picking-hand mechanics stay constant while the fretting hand tracks a live theory decision. (3) Sweep-picking arpeggio shapes in triplets, cycled through minor, major, augmented and diminished qualities on the same shape, with deliberate memorization of where the 3rd and 5th land in each quality. — BERNTH, [[6 Awesome Practice Tricks - Boost Your Guitar Progress TODAY!]]
