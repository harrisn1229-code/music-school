# You Suck at Mixing No. 7: Using Reference Tracks

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x3fMK4tHFA0)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x3fMK4tHFA0)

## Synopsis
Makes the case for reference tracks as a way to establish an objective target, rather than trusting untrained ears alone — and shows the mechanics of doing it honestly. Four reference tracks (which don't need to match genre) are loaded into the project to bracket a target range for treble, sub weight, punch, and loudness; then a free loudness meter and Utility gain stage are used to level-match each one to the mix so the loudness itself is taken out of the comparison, leaving only tonal balance to judge.

## Techniques
- **Reference tracks don't need to match genre — they need to frame a target range** — pick a handful of tracks where each one represents an edge you don't want to cross: the brightest you'd ever want to be, the most sub, the punchiest, the darkest/warmest. If your mix ever exceeds any one of those edges, you've strayed too far from the target, which sits somewhere in the middle of the bracket.
- **Loudness-match references with a free loudness meter before comparing anything** — a free plugin (Youlean Loudness Meter) shows a track's Integrated LUFS reading over time. Read your own mix's Integrated LUFS, then for each reference track, add a Utility before the loudness meter and pull its gain down until it reads the same LUFS-I value as your mix — never use the track's own volume fader for this, since that skews the reading. → [[Gain Staging]]
- **Take loudness completely out of the comparison before judging tone** — an un-level-matched A/B is rigged; louder always sounds better regardless of actual mix quality. Once every reference is gain-matched to your mix's loudness, what's left to compare is the real thing that matters: tonal balance.
- **Flag anything you're consistently missing across ALL of your references, not just one** — go through each reference one at a time and compare a specific property (treble, bass weight, midrange). If your mix is a little different from just one reference, that might be a style choice — but if it's consistently short on the same thing compared to every single reference, that's a real, fixable problem, not a stylistic difference. In the worked example this flagged a dull piano (fixed with a brightening EQ move) and slightly light bass.
- **Re-check against the same references at every stage, including mastering** — reference comparison isn't a one-time step early in the mixdown; return to the same set of tracks periodically throughout mixing and again during mastering so the target stays consistent.
- **The references will translate the same way your mix does** — whatever a cheap pair of earbuds or headphones does to the reference tracks, it'll do to your own mix too, so the comparison holds even on imperfect playback systems.
- **Skipping reference tracks because "my music doesn't sound like anything else" is a trap** — most music is still discovered through algorithmic/playlist placement, and a mix with radically different tonal balance, loudness, or brightness than everything around it on a playlist risks getting skipped over regardless of how the song itself sounds.
