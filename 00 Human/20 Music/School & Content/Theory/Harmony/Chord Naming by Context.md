# Chord Naming by Context

The same notes can have different names depending on the tonal center you're in. This is where slash chords and inversion analysis matter.

## Example: E, G, B, C

These four notes are a **Cmaj7 chord** — C, E, G, B reordered with E in the bass.

### Cmaj7 Inversions

| Inversion | Notes | Feel |
|-----------|-------|------|
| Root position | C, E, G, B | Grounded home base |
| 1st inversion | E, G, B, C | Lighter, open — E in bass |
| 2nd inversion | G, B, C, E | Tension, common as passing chord |
| 3rd inversion | B, C, E, G | Most unstable — half-step B→C pulls to resolve |

### Same notes, different tonal center

| Context | Name |
|---------|------|
| Key of C | Cmaj7 (1st inversion) |
| E as tonal center | Cmaj7/E or Em(add♭6) |
| Jazz/modal | Could read as Asus or Am9 depending on surrounding chords |

**Key principle:** Chord quality is determined by the actual intervals, not the roman numeral case. An uppercase or lowercase numeral signals position/function — the stacked intervals determine major/minor/dominant.

- **Implying a full chord (including the 7th) from just TWO notes, via deliberate omission — worked in "Chopsticks."** Each hand plays only one note, yet the progression reads as a clear V7→I: F+G alone stands in for a full G7 (3rd and 7th present, root and 5th omitted) resolving to E+G (standing in for C major, root omitted), then D+B (a different partial G voicing) resolving again to the same C pair — genuine purposeful voice leading with the minimum possible notes, not just simplification for beginners. → David Bennett Music Theory, [[Where did the Chopsticks piano piece come from]]
- **Any note in a non-tertian cluster can be argued as the root, producing a different valid chord name each time — pick whichever reading is the least of a stretch, not just whichever produces the "simplest" symbol.** Worked example: a guitar's six open strings (E A D G B E) can be named E minor7 add4 (built from the E minor triad hiding in the top three strings, plus the low D and A) OR A9sus4/E (built by treating A as root instead) — same six notes, two defensible names. → [[Quartal Chord Voicings]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [What chord is the open strings of a guitar?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4msfezGlF3o)
- **The diatonic vii° chord (a diminished triad) is routinely mislabeled as a dominant 7th over a bass note instead of named for what it actually is — a concrete, recurring instance of "same-ish notes, different name" driven by transcriber bias rather than tonal-center context.** "M79" (Vampire Weekend) and "Do It" (McCartney), both in the key of G, use F♯° (F♯-A-C) as the leading-tone chord — but the sheet music commonly available for both mislabels it as **D7/F♯** instead (adds a D that isn't actually in the chord). Reasoning offered: an incomplete-sounding diminished triad reads more familiarly as "a dominant 7 chord missing its root," which unlocks known V→I cadence logic, so musicians default to that label even when the notes don't support it. Full rarity mechanism (why vii° is so uncommon in the first place) → [[Diminished Chords]]. → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Secondary Dominants]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[The Rarest Chord In The Key]]
- **A real-world Cmaj7/Em♭6 enharmonic pair, TV-theme soundtracks confirming it's the bass note alone that decides perceived chord quality.** The Stranger Things and X-Files title themes both arpeggiate the identical set of notes; Stranger Things reads as Cmaj7 until a dominating bass note recontextualizes it as Em♭6, while X-Files uses the same voicing spelled directly as a ♭6-minor chord — same pitches, different perceived chord, purely a function of what's underneath in the bass. → [[Slash Chords]]. The ♭6/5th semitone rub is also why this exact chord reads as "spooky" — full mechanism and the Kyle Dixon "X-Files-like sound" interview quote → [[Production/Sound Selection Guide|Sound Selection Guide]] § Synths. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Why Stranger Things & The X-Files sound similar]]
- **A complete chord-symbol reference: triads, 7ths, the 9/11/13 stacking rule, and a worked method for naming an arbitrary note cluster.** Basics: bare letter = major, `m`/`-` = minor, `dim`/`°` = diminished, `aug`/`+`/`(♯5)` = augmented, `sus4`(`sus`)/`sus2` = suspended. 7ths: bare `7` = dominant (never spelled out), `m7` = minor 7, `maj7`/`M7`/`△7` = major 7, `mM7` = minor-major 7, `dim7`/`°7` = full diminished 7 (stack of minor 3rds), half-diminished = `ø7` or (to dodge the confusing symbol) `m7♭5`. **Stacking rule**: a bare `9`/`11`/`13` implies every lower extension is present too (`C9` = C7+9th, `C13` implies 7-9-11 all present) — to get *only* the named degree, you must write `add` explicitly (`Cadd11`). `maj9/11/13` and `m9/11/13` follow the same stacking logic with a major-7 or minor-triad base respectively. `omit`/`no` (e.g. `omit5`) drops an otherwise-implied degree. **Altered-extension naming trap**: a flattened/sharpened upper extension is labeled off the next extension *down*, never combined directly with the root (an E13 with a flat 13 is `E11(♭13)`, never `E♭13` — that misreads as E-flat). Power chord = `5` (root+5th, no 3rd, root often doubled an octave up). **`6` vs `m6` confusion**: both use the MAJOR 6th degree — `m6` does NOT mean "minor chord + minor 6th," a genuine ♭6 must be spelled out as `(♭6)`. `alt` = a dominant 7 with the 5th and/or 9th raised/lowered, left to context. Slash chords aren't always inversions — sometimes just the clearest way to notate an awkward extension (G/A is really A11). **Naming an arbitrary cluster**: find any recognizable triad shape anywhere in the note set (even out of order, even missing a degree), treat it as the anchor, account for every remaining note as a stacked extension above it — multiple equally valid names can describe the same note set; picking one is a communication choice, not a fixed fact. Full worked examples → [[Every possible Chord Symbol EXPLAINED]]. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Every possible Chord Symbol EXPLAINED]]

- **Reinterpret a puzzling chord as its relative major/minor to reveal a hidden repeating relationship — a bridge-unlocking application of "same notes, different context."** On a confusing jazz-standard bridge (four seemingly unrelated opening chords: minor9 → dominant-7-off-the-♭6, repeated twice more at different pitches), the first minor-9 chord's implied major-6 partner, reread as its **relative minor** (a major-6 chord and its relative-minor-7 chord share every note — e.g. G♭6 = E♭m7), reveals that all four chords are actually the *same* relationship repeated three times at three pitch levels, not four unrelated grabs. Framed as reverse-engineering a composer's process: take straightforward diatonic harmony, remove the 2s from in front of the 5s, swap a major chord for its relative minor (or vice versa), and the result reads as obscure even though the underlying structure is simple and repetitive.
