# Songs that use 7/4 time

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-UA6n7iKeYQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-UA6n7iKeYQ)

## Synopsis

The last (and definitional) entry in this batch's odd-time-signature run — precisely defines what makes a meter "odd" (doesn't divide into groups of 2 or 3, not just an odd top number), then works through 7/4's usual 4+3/3+4 grouping, a genuinely odd 21/8 shuffle-notation case, and Radiohead's deliberate break from the 4+3 convention.

## Techniques

- **"Odd time signature," precisely defined**: a meter is odd not because its top number is odd, but because the beat count doesn't divide down into groups of 2 or 3 the way conventional Western meters (4/4, 3/4, 6/8, 12/8) do — 3/4 has an odd top number but isn't an odd signature, since 3 divides cleanly into one group of 3.
- **7/4 usually softens its oddness via 4+3 or 3+4 grouping**: "Solsbury Hill" (Peter Gabriel) — intro groups 3+4, flips to 4+3 after; drops a bar to plain 4/4 at the end of the A section specifically because an odd-pulse ending would feel incomplete.
- **A 7/4 shuffle's mathematically exact notation would be 21/8 (by the same logic that makes a 4/4 shuffle = 12/8), but it's avoided for legibility**: Pink Floyd's "Money" is a genuine 7/4 shuffle (each beat divides into 3, not 2) but gets written as plain 7/4 with triplets marked, not 21/8. "Money" also isn't uniformly 7/4 — a verse stretch is actually 4/4 + a one-off 2/4 bar (14 beats, coincidentally 7/4-shaped by count but not by accent), and the guitar solo switches to 12/8 entirely.
- **Multiple valid, simultaneously-correct time-signature descriptions for one recording**: "All You Need Is Love" (Beatles) is transcribed at least three ways depending on source — 4/4 with occasional 3/4 bars, straight 7/4, or alternating 12/8+9/8 (itself a more legible stand-in for a "true" 21/8 shuffle reading) — genuinely all valid readings of the identical recording.
- **Deliberately resetting the listener's meter expectation to keep 7/4 feeling uneven rather than normalized**: "Times Like These" (Foo Fighters) opens in 4/4, introduces the 7/4 riff, then keeps shifting back and forth between the two throughout — repeated contrast rather than settling into 7/4 as the new normal.
- **Breaking the standard 4+3 grouping on purpose, as a lyrical/thematic device**: Radiohead's "2 + 2 = 5" splits its 7/4 bar exactly down the middle (a 7-against-2 division) instead of the usual asymmetric grouping — producing a pulse that reads as simultaneously odd and even, matching the Orwellian (*1984*) title's premise of broken arithmetic.
- **A historical argument for why odd meters stay rare in pop, from Brubeck himself (quoted in the video)**: exposure drives what songwriters write — audiences and writers who aren't exposed to odd meters don't produce them, a self-perpetuating cycle Brubeck explicitly framed jazz's role as trying to break (citing "Take Five" in 5/4 becoming genuinely danceable pop as evidence it can be done).
