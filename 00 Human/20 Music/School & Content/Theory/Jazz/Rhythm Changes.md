# Rhythm Changes

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Jazz Harmony]]. The **other** standard form besides the blues — the chord progression of Gershwin's *I Got Rhythm*, reused as the skeleton for hundreds of tunes.

```progression-songs
```

Worth knowing for the same reason the 12-bar is: it's a shared 32-bar container that shows up constantly, and its two halves teach two different things.

For the blues, see [[Blues Form and Harmony]].

## The shape: AABA, 32 bars

Four 8-bar sections. The A sections are the same; the B section (the **bridge**) is completely different in method.

### The A section — I–vi–ii–V, twice

In B♭ (the standard key), two bars compressed to two chords each:

```
| B♭  G7  | Cm7  F7  | B♭  G7  | Cm7  F7  |
| B♭  B♭7 | E♭  E♭m6 | B♭/F G7 | Cm7  F7  |
```

Bars 1–4 are **I–vi–ii–V twice** — a turnaround, looped. That's it. → [[Turnarounds]]

Bars 5–8 move to the subdominant and back: `I → I7 → IV → iv → I → V/ii → ii → V`. The `E♭ → E♭m6` is a **borrowed iv**, and it's the moment the A section actually goes somewhere. → [[Borrowed Chords]]

The last A gets a different ending (an *ending* rather than a turnaround) so the tune can stop.

### The B section — the bridge, a cycle of dominants

```
| D7  | D7  | G7  | G7  | C7  | C7  | F7  | F7  |
```

Four dominant chords, two bars each, **each resolving down a fifth to the next**: D7 → G7 → C7 → F7 → (B♭). This is a pure circle-of-fifths run, and it's usually described as **III7 – VI7 – II7 – V7**.

Every chord is dominant, and none of them is diatonic to B♭. That's the point — the bridge leaves the key entirely and walks back in by fifths. → [[Circle of Fifths]] · [[Secondary Dominants]]

**This bridge is a self-contained lesson.** If you want to internalise how dominant motion by fifths works, playing this eight-bar loop teaches it faster than any explanation.

## Why it's worth your time

**The A section is a masterclass in loop writing.** Four bars of nothing but I–vi–ii–V that don't get boring, because bars 5–8 answer them with actual harmonic movement. That question-and-answer structure inside an eight-bar unit is directly transferable to a verse. → [[Loop-Based Harmony]]

**The bridge is a masterclass in contrast.** Not a key change, not a tempo change, not new instrumentation — just a switch from *diatonic and busy* to *chromatic and slow*. Harmonic rhythm drops from two chords per bar to one chord per two bars, a 4× slowdown. That alone makes it feel like a different section.

**The lesson for production:** contrast doesn't require new material. Halving the harmonic rhythm and leaving everything else alone is a legitimate way to build a bridge, and it's cheaper than writing new parts. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]

## Common substitutions

The form is a substitution playground, and every move is from [[Chord Substitution]]:

- **Tritone subs on the bridge dominants:** `D7 → A♭7`, `G7 → D♭7`, giving chromatic descending roots
- **Interpolated ii chords:** `| Am7 D7 | Dm7 G7 | Gm7 C7 | Cm7 F7 |` — the bridge with a ii in front of every dominant
- **Diminished passing chords in the A section:** `B♭ → B°7 → Cm7` in place of `B♭ → G7 → Cm7` → [[Diminished Chords]]

- **A pop crossover of the rhythm-changes swap (I-vi-ii-V in place of the plain doo-wop I-vi-IV-V), confirmed by direct song comparison**: "Birds of a Feather" (Billie Eilish) and "Last Christmas" (Wham!) use the identical variant, in nearly the same key and with matching chord extensions (not just a matching root progression) — a widely-noted similarity once "Birds of a Feather" was released, close enough that a RipX vocal/instrumental mashup between the two works cleanly. **Tuning trivia on "Last Christmas"**: the original recording sits a quarter-tone flat of concert-pitch D, a side effect of the tape being slowed (VarSpeed) during production to hit the desired tempo — slowing analog tape drags pitch down with it, something modern digital pitch-shift (used to correct it for the mashup) can do independent of tempo, but 1980s tape technology could not. → [[Chord Progressions]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[These songs have the same chords... let's swap them! - **A three-strategy soloing framework for the A section — simplify, substitute, or superimpose — anchored by a guest-taught dominant-cycle chord substitution not documented elsewhere on this page.** **Simplify:** the A section's fast I–vi–ii–V motion boils down to a **1–5–1 skeleton (B♭, E♭, F7)** — solo over **B♭ major** for most of the section rather than chasing every chord, demonstrated with two **Charlie Parker**-style licks built from enclosures of B♭'s chord tones (not straight arpeggios), plus a reminder to land on the **9th/13th** for color; alternative: treat the section as a straight B♭ blues. **Substitute (taught by guest Jamie Halloran) — the "Sonny Stitt/Don Byas" dominant-cycle sub:** the first four bars are normally two turnarounds, **1-6-2-5 then 3-6-2-5**; convert every chord to a **dominant 7th**, then extend the bar 3–4 cycle-of-fourths **two bars earlier into bar 1** — instead of B♭7 in bar 1, run **F♯7 → B7 → E7 → A7 → D7**, each resolving up a fourth, landing on the D7 that already belongs in bar 3. Works despite F♯7's apparent unrelatedness to B♭ because both progressions are **destination-oriented**, converging on the same D7 (bar 3) and B♭ (bar 5). Bandstand note: usable without warning the band, but works best at a **fast tempo** with a **strong melodic idea** carrying it. → [[Chord Substitution]], [[Turnarounds]], [[Circle of Fifths]]. **Superimpose:** **B♭ diminished** (arpeggio or triads pulled from the diminished scale, connected together) over the entire first four bars — the same tonic-diminished device as this page's other Nathan Borton video, sourced here from a transcribed solo (captioned "Lewis Smith" on a tune captioned "Bacon" — low-confidence auto-caption renderings) and corroborated against **Benny Golson's "Upper Manhattan Medical Group."** → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] § *Prolongation*, [[Blues Form and Harmony]], [[7 Rhythm Changes Licks Every Jazz Musician Should Know]] — Nathan Borton (guest: Jamie Halloran), [[3 Ways to MASTER Rhythm Changes! Simplify, Expand, and Superimpose!]]
- **A seven-concept vocabulary ladder for soloing over the whole form, framed explicitly as progressively more advanced ways to stop chasing every fast-moving chord.** Named trap up front: most players try to nail every single change of the A section and get "discouraged or frustrated" — every concept below is a reduction that avoids that. **1. Blues.** Treat the whole A section (bars 1–8, the two I–vi–ii–V loops) as one static **B♭7/B♭ blues** sound; a second blues lick over the same reduction credited to **Kenny Burrell**. **2. Major (tonic).** Since bars 1–4 are I–vi–ii–V entirely diatonic to B♭ major, play straight **B♭ major** across the whole four bars instead of blues language — named as a common way jazz musicians handle fast-moving diatonic changes generally. **3. Dominant (V), tonic vs. altered.** Play off the key's **V chord** instead of the I — distinguishes a **tonic dominant** (built from the key's own notes, stable, doesn't pull anywhere) from an **altered dominant** (unstable, wants to resolve), using the tonic-dominant color here; also demonstrates blending major-sound and tonic-dominant language into one line. **4. ii–V vocabulary, short and long.** Short ii–V licks land right before the IV chord and on each A-section turnaround back to I; over **the bridge**, use "long" two-bar ii–Vs instead of one-bar ones, licensed because every dominant chord can also be read as its own relative ii (D7 ≈ Dm7) — the *identical* long-ii–V logic then collapses the **A section's own first four bars** into two long ii–Vs, **Cm7–F7 / Cm7–F7**, a further reduction stacked on top of Concepts 2–3. **5. The walk-up progression**, a named chromatic-connector lick used three ways: enhancing rhythm changes' own opening bars, standalone over a **static key-center chord** (no other harmonic motion needed), and transferred onto **the bridge** by treating each dominant (D7, G7, C7, F7) as its own temporary key center and restarting the same lick from that key's root (e.g. starting on G under the D7 bar, since D7 functions as V of G). **6. Diminished sound (first four bars) + ♯IV°.** **B♭ diminished** played straight through bars 1–4, traced directly to **Ben Webster's solo on "Cotton Tail,"** building tension toward the IV chord's arrival; generalizes to any B♭-diminished-scale material or triads pulled from that scale. Separately, for the form's recurring **♯IV° after the IV chord** (the "subdominant diminished" family, see [[Diminished Chords]]), a specific trumpet-derived lick beyond the plain diminished arpeggio (source rendered only as "Smith" in the auto-captions — low confidence, unconfirmed which trumpet player). **7. Cycling dominants + Coltrane pattern.** An alternative reduction of the *same* first-four-bars slot: start on the **♯5 of the key** and chain dominant chords resolving into each other every two beats until landing on the IV chord — a *downward*-pulling color, contrasted directly with Concept 6's upward-building diminished sound over the identical bars. Solo over this chain with the **Coltrane pattern (1-2-3-5 of each passing chord)**. **Sequencing note, stated directly:** Concepts 6–7 (diminished, cycling dominants) are flagged as advanced subs, "only used if you can clearly play each [simpler] sound" first. → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] § *Prolongation* (Concepts 1–4 are the same "collapse a fast progression to fewer functions" idea, worked here specifically over this form), [[Diminished Chords]], [[Chord Substitution]] § *Coltrane changes*, [[Blues Form and Harmony]], [[The ii-V-I]], [[Turnarounds]] — Nathan Borton, [[7 Rhythm Changes Licks Every Jazz Musician Should Know]]
- **A full connecting-note walkthrough of the A section, chord by chord, for a walking bassline played simultaneously with the comping — two contrasting worked examples over the exact `B♭ G7 | Cm7 F7 | B♭ G7 | Cm7 F7 | B♭ B♭7 | E♭ E♭m6 | B♭/F G7 | Cm7 F7` progression on this page.** Default connecting move: **the 5th (or ♭5) of the current chord** into the next root, called out as both harmonically logical and physically playable — used systematically through the whole A section in the first example. **On the `B♭ → B♭7 → E♭ → E♭m6` borrowed-iv stretch** (this page's chromatic I→I7→IV→iv move), the bassline **repeats the same note** rather than adding motion of its own, because the harmony is already moving chromatically — the connecting-note job is already done by the chord changes themselves. A second, more melodic pass over the identical form swaps some 5ths for **chromatic leading notes and shared-chord-tone connectors** (a chord tone of the current chord that's also a chord tone of the target — e.g. the 3rd of F7 leading into B♭7 — creating a large melodic interval that reads as "almost a dominant resolving," B♭→[implied D7]→G, without the D7 actually being played). **The hand-off from the A section into the bridge:** E♭ used as a chromatic upper-neighbor leading note down into the bridge's opening D7 (this page's § *The B section*). Recurring device: **B♭maj7 resolved with F (its 5th) in the bass**, pointed out as the same pitches as a D minor triad over F.
