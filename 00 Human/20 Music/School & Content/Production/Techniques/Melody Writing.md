# Melody Writing

Writing a single strong melody (contour, motion, range, phrase structure), and writing more than one melodic line that plays well together — giving each melody a distinct job (rhythmic vs. harmonic) rather than writing two lines that compete for the same role. Companion to [[Bassline Writing]] (the low-end equivalent of "give it one job") and [[Arrangement and Song Form]] (where multiple melodic layers get introduced/removed across sections). Motif development is [[Motivic Development]]; the harmony a melody is written against is [[Loop-Based Harmony]] and [[Non-Harmonic Tones]].

## Two complementary roles for simultaneous melodies

- **Rhythm-first melody**: pick ONE note (typically the scale's root) and write an interesting rhythm/groove using just that single repeated note — establish the groove before worrying about pitch at all. Only afterward, shift some of those notes up or down the scale for melodic interest, so the rhythm stays the anchor and pitch variation is layered on top of an already-solid groove, not the other way around.
- **Harmony-first melody**: build a second, contrasting line from longer, more sustained notes, spending the most time on the STRONGEST scale degrees (the root and the fifth), and using weaker scale tones (2nd, 4th, etc.) as brief stepping-stones connecting one strong note to the next. This line's job is harmonic weight, not rhythmic interest — long notes, strong intervals, minimal rhythmic complexity.
- Played together, a rhythm-first melody and a harmony-first melody read as two independent, complementary parts rather than two lines competing for the same "job" — one carries groove, the other carries harmonic identity.

## Contour, motion, and range (writing ONE strong melody)

*Section sourced from* You Suck at Producing, [You Suck at Producing: Melody](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ojyW99sr2_E)

- **Constrain the range with an explicit top and bottom note before writing anything** — pick an upper bound and a lower bound (roughly an octave-plus-a-third apart is a reasonable starting range) and never go outside that band while improvising — this single constraint does most of the work of preventing an aimless, directionless melody; without it, a melody written with "no constraints" tends to wander unpredictably in both pitch and phrase length rather than developing a shape.
- **Balance melodic contour: what goes up should come back down, and vice versa** — contour is the melody's overall shape over time (rising, falling, sitting still); a strong melody has a sense of balance in that shape — after traveling upward for a while, bring it back down rather than letting it drift indefinitely in one direction, and the reverse. This balance, combined with the range constraint above, naturally produces an "arc" shape with a single clear peak note rather than a flat or erratic line.
- **Balance melodic motion: after stepwise movement, take a leap (and vice versa)** — contour also has a *type* of motion, not just a direction: stepwise motion (moving between adjacent scale degrees) reads as smooth/connected, while leaps (larger intervals) read as more dramatic. A strong melody balances the two — after a stretch of stepwise movement in one direction, a leap (often back the other way) keeps the line from feeling monotonous; a melody that's all steps or all leaps reads as one-dimensional.
- **Antecedent/consequent phrasing: repeat the first half of a phrase exactly, vary the second half** — write a melodic phrase, then write a second phrase whose FIRST half is identical to the first phrase's first half (the "antecedent," a question), but whose SECOND half is different (the "consequent," the answer) — same opening, different close, across a pair of phrases. This call-and-response repetition-with-variation structure is what makes a melody read as cohesive and "groovy" rather than as an unstructured stream of notes, and is a much stronger default than either exact repetition (boring) or constant variation (aimless, the "no constraints" failure mode above).

## Melody against harmony

*Added 2026-08-04 — the MEL strand's biggest gap.*

The section above treats melody in isolation: contour, motion, range. But **the same melody note means something different depending on the chord underneath it**, and choosing that relationship deliberately is most of what separates a written melody from a hummed one.

**Which chord tone you land on changes the feeling, not the harmony:**

| Landing note | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Root** | Settled, final, safe. Where a phrase goes to end. |
| **3rd** | Colours the chord — brighter or sadder. More melodic than the root, less final. |
| **5th** | Stable but neutral. Strong without being conclusive. |
| **7th** | Leaning, unresolved, wants to move. |
| **9th** | Open and floating. Colour without changing the chord's function. |

**Landing on the root constantly is the single most common reason a melody sounds boring** — it agrees with the harmony on every downbeat, so nothing is ever in tension. Target 3rds and 7ths on strong beats and the same notes suddenly read as a melody rather than an arpeggio.

The 9th deserves special mention because it's the pop move: it isn't in the triad, so it adds colour without redefining the chord — which is why toplines landing on the 9th feel emotive and unresolved. See [[Loop-Based Harmony]].

**Non-chord tones are where the line lives.** The notes *between* the chord tones — passing tones, neighbours, suspensions — are most of what makes a melody sound composed. See [[Non-Harmonic Tones]].

## Singability, range, and climax

*Added 2026-08-05, sourced from* Max Konyi, [What Makes a Good Melody? | Musical Warp Drive - Part 2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EY-TP6wwbxI).

- **Design for singability, and test it by asking whether the melody could be hummed from memory.** Steps (movement to the next scale tone) are easy to sing; leaps (a third or bigger) are hard. A good melody usually blends both, but when singability specifically matters (writing for a vocalist), shrink oversized leaps down to steps and keep only one or two moderate leaps — a demonstrated revision kept just a single 6th at the very end of an otherwise stepwise line and was audibly easier to sing without losing the melody's identity.
- **Range: an octave to an octave-and-a-half is the practical ceiling most strong, singable melodies actually use.** Past roughly two octaves most melodies start losing singability and listeners start losing track of the contour — wide-range melodies can absolutely work, but treat octave-and-a-half as the default unless there's a reason to go wider.
- **A strong melody usually has one climax — a single high point it rises to and falls from, occurring only once.** It doesn't have to sit in the middle of the phrase (start, end, and anywhere between all work) — the point is a melody that repeatedly revisits its own top note reads as weaker than one with a single clear peak.
- **Outlining chord changes: build the melody substantially from the underlying chords' own tones so it implies the harmony even with zero chords sounding.** Tested directly against a control: a melody built from arpeggiated chord tones still clearly implied the chord progression when isolated with no chords playing, while a melody with constant pitch repetition, static/repetitive rhythm, and arbitrarily-placed notes implied none of the underlying harmony at all in isolation — one of the clearest structural tells of a strong vs. weak melody. See the landing-note table above for which chord tone to target; this is the same idea applied across a full arpeggiated line rather than single landing notes. → [[Non-Harmonic Tones]], [[Chord Progressions]]

## Order of operations: chords first vs. melody first

*Added 2026-08-05, sourced from* Max Konyi, [What Makes a Good Melody? | Musical Warp Drive - Part 2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EY-TP6wwbxI).

- **Chords first (the majority approach, especially in modern/pop music): write the full progression, then compose the melody on top of the already-sounding chords.** The resulting melody is naturally well-integrated with the harmony because it was written in direct response to it.
- **Melody first: write the melody with zero chords sounding, from an internal/felt sense of harmony only, then compose new chords underneath it afterward.** Produces a genuinely different, sometimes more original line, precisely because it isn't pre-shaped by existing harmony — but it commits you to writing fresh chords for it. Recycling old chords under a melody-first line, even in the same key, tends to clash; a melody written free of harmony generally needs harmony written specifically to fit it.
- **Practical takeaway: pick the order deliberately rather than by default.** Chords-first is faster and produces tighter harmonic integration; melody-first is slower but a reliable way to break out of chord-progression-driven melodic habits when writing feels formulaic.
- **A third order, distinct from both above: rhythmic-track-first, where the beat/bass/synth groove is built before any vocal melody exists, rather than starting on a chord instrument at all.** Finneas contrasts his earlier process — sit at a piano, write the song, THEN migrate into a DAW and record vocals/piano/drums/bass around it (Billie Eilish's "My Future" was written exactly that way) — against building from a track's rhythmic components first. His stated conclusion, after a sparse Trilian bass patch reset a stalled verse's whole rhythmic feel and pulled a new vocal groove out of Billie: starting rhythmic-track-first surfaced songwriting choices "that would never have been true" if they'd started on acoustic guitar — the starting instrument/approach actively shapes what the song can become, not just how fast it gets written. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]], [[Bassline Writing]] — Mix with the Masters (Finneas), [[Vocal production for Billie Eilish by Finneas]]

## Writing to a loop vs writing to changes

These are genuinely different problems and the same melody rarely works for both.

**Over moving changes**, the harmony supplies the motion. You can hold one note and let the chords recontextualize it underneath — the same pitch becomes the 5th, then the 3rd, then the 9th, and it sounds like it's moving without moving. Long notes work. The melody can afford to be simple because something else is changing.

**Over a static loop**, nothing does that for you. **The melody has to generate its own movement** — through rhythm, contour and phrasing. A held note over one chord for eight bars is just a held note.

Two consequences:

- **Static loops demand more rhythmic interest** from the topline. This is why rhythm-first writing (above) suits loop-based production so well.
- **Static loops tolerate more repetition.** The listener is already in a looped frame, so a repeating melodic figure reads as a hook rather than as laziness.

See [[Loop-Based Harmony]] for the harmony side of the same distinction.

## Register, range and intensity

**Higher reads as more intense.** Partly convention, partly physiology — a singer audibly works harder at the top of their range, and the ear hears the effort.

That's why the standard arrangement shape works: **verses sit lower, choruses lift.** A large part of what makes a chorus feel like a chorus is simply that it's higher than what came before it.

**Widening the range does similar work.** A melody circling three notes feels contained; one with bigger leaps and a wider span feels dramatic, independent of how high it goes.

**Practical:** if a chorus isn't landing, check where it sits relative to the verse before rewriting it. Moving it up a third often does more than new notes.

## Building tension across a section

How to make a verse *arrive* at a chorus rather than just stop.

**Climb.** Raise the register toward the end of the verse, shorten the note values so the line gets busier, and repeat a fragment in ascending sequence so it gathers.

**Withhold the tonic.** If the melody never lands on the root, nothing feels resolved — so the chorus doing it becomes the release.

**The counter-move works too.** Strip everything back right before the chorus so it arrives against space rather than against a wall. In loop-based production this is often more effective than building, because the drop *is* the arrival.

Pairs with [[Arrangement and Song Form]] — melodic tension and arrangement density should agree, not fight.

## What makes a hook

At minimum three things, in rough order of importance:

1. **Repetition.** A hook has to come back, and be short enough to hold after one pass. This outranks everything else.
2. **Rhythmic distinctiveness.** A good hook is identifiable from its rhythm alone, with the pitches stripped out. Test it by tapping it.
3. **Singable range and clear contour.** If it can't be sung back, it isn't a hook.

Then: **contrast against what surrounds it.** If the verse is busy and low, a sparse high hook lands harder. And **simplicity usually beats complexity** — the hook is the part that has to survive being remembered badly.

## Developing a motif

A **motive** is a short fragment used as raw material to generate much more music. [[Motivic Development]] already documents **eight named ways to develop one** — repetition, sequence, inversion, interval contraction and expansion, diminution, augmentation, rhythmic metamorphosis, imitation.

That note is the reference; the practical point here is that **most strong melodies are one short idea developed, not a long line invented.** Write four bars you like, then generate the rest by transforming it.

## Fixing a boring melody

Triage, most-effective first:

1. **Change the rhythm.** Most boring melodies are boring *rhythmically* — even note values, everything on the beat. This is the highest-leverage fix by a wide margin.
2. **Widen it.** Add a leap, push the top note higher, break out of the three-note orbit.
3. **Move where it sits against the chord.** Target 9ths and 7ths instead of roots and 5ths (see above).
4. **Add space.** Rests make what surrounds them land — see the existing note on breathing.
5. **Displace it rhythmically.** Start the phrase off the downbeat.
6. **Reharmonize underneath it.** Same melody, different chords, completely different meaning.

**If you only do one, do the rhythm.**

## From videos

- **Invert an instrument's control mapping to force genuinely new melodic ideas out of familiar finger patterns — Joe Zawinul's ARP 2600 "backward keyboard" on Weather Report's "Black Market."** Send inverted (negative) keyboard control voltage to oscillator pitch: playing a LOW note produces a HIGH pitch and vice versa — physically the same fingering muscle memory (a player still falls into familiar scale/pentatonic shapes) but the actual melody that comes out is genuinely new, because the mapping between finger position and pitch has flipped. Concretely: fingering an E♭ minor pentatonic shape sounds as B♭ major pentatonic — "flipping the circle of fifths on its head." **Compositional workflow this pairs with**: record long stretches of free improvisation on the inverted instrument, then listen back later and transcribe only the specific moments that land — the inversion generates raw material faster than deliberate composition would, and the editing/selection happens afterward, separately. **The idea generalizes past pitch-inversion specifically**: apply the same "flip one mapping, leave the rest normal" logic to other parameters — e.g. a vibrato LFO that gets SLOWER as pitch rises (the opposite of the usual faster-with-pitch behavior) as a smaller-scale version of the same inverted-thinking principle. → [[Filters and Resonance]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[Life Changing Synth Sound by Weather Report]]
- **The "earworm" mechanism: catchy ≠ good, and catchiness rides on the same pitch-tracking wiring humans use for spoken language, not a separate musical faculty.** Two necessary features claimed: singability (small intervals — 2nds through 5ths — read as easy to sing involuntarily; 6ths+ take deliberate effort) and repetition (a melody heard once doesn't register as intentional; repeated, it does — "it's almost like repetition is what defines melody"). Rhythm has a catchiness sweet spot in both directions: notes too dense are hard to sing along to, notes too sparse make the gap between them noticeable and unsatisfying with no pulse to fill it. A melody written for an instrument rather than a vocalist (Coldplay's "Clocks" piano riff) often has no breathing gaps and resists sing-along despite being catchy. **A named 4-phrase pop-chorus repetition shape**: A, A' (same phrase, subtly changed ending), A (exact repeat), B (new closing phrase that resolves to the tonic AND breaks up what would otherwise be too many A repeats in a row) — worked example, "I Wanna Dance with Somebody" (Whitney Houston). The proposed "why": humans track small pitch changes to extract meaning from speech (most explicit in tonal languages, but present in every language via things like rising pitch for a question) — a melody gets involuntarily "learned" via that same pitch-tracking wiring on repeated exposure, the same way a language is learned, which is why macro-repetition (a song on loop, background music, long game-music exposure) is often enough to embed a tune on its own regardless of the melody's internal structure. → [[Non-Harmonic Tones]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [What makes a melody catchy?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nxXh8PU4KkI)
- **A melody can be reduced almost entirely to one static note and still be catchy — because a melody's rhythm carries as much weight as its pitch content.** "Mr. Brightside" (The Killers) verse stays on one note for 16 bars, working more like a chant/percussion part than a melody — the catchiness comes from rhythm, not pitch variety; the pre-chorus's small handful of new pitches then lands as a big release purely by contrast. **The same static note can sound like it's moving via harmonic recontextualization underneath it**: Jobim's "One Note Samba" holds one pitch while the chords change color around it. **Root vs. 5th are the only two scale degrees stable enough to sustain a genuinely static melody note**: root works indefinitely ("Baby You're a Rich Man," Beatles); 5th works but starts audibly wanting to resolve after enough bars ("Julia," Beatles, 8 bars before stepping down) — a non-resolved degree (4th, 7th) can't sustain the device at all. **A recurring recent-pop chorus shape uses ONLY scale degrees 1-2-3**: root = stability, 2nd = tension/motion, 3rd = a second stable-but-less-resolved landing spot — enough functional variety to build a full melodic arc from almost no pitch material, with a distinct "nursery rhyme" quality when paired with short/snappy rhythms; "Free Fallin'" (Tom Petty) uses the identical 1-2-3 set but with long drawn-out notes, and the nursery-rhyme quality disappears entirely — rhythm, not pitch, drives that effect. **Deliberate pitch-count limitation as a usable creativity exercise**: a 5-composer collaboration each wrote a 1-minute piano piece with an imposed 3-note/2-note/1-note sectional structure specifically to force solutions unavailable under full pitch freedom — general takeaway, a melody's quality isn't measured by how many notes it uses. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs with One Note Melodies]]

- **Voice a counter-melody a semitone apart from the underlying part for deliberate dissonance and tension, rather than a consonant interval.** Recreating Dez Wright's *Wants and Needs* (Drake) sample: a pluck counter-melody deliberately clashes a semitone against the harmony underneath as the starting "dark vibe," washed out afterward with reverb/texture processing. Elsewhere in the same video, a low synth-brass layer stays on ONE chord repeating with slightly different **inversions** instead of a moving progression — harmonic bed via voicing variation, not new chords — and a second melodic layer for an emotional sample is built by **inverting** the chords of an existing layer rather than doubling the same voicing. → [[BED Slot]], [[Sound Selection Guide]], [[Layering and Stacking]] — loner, [dez wright's synth secrets will elevate your melodies!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_8vOz54DTiY)
- **One melody, many instruments — not many melodies.** Writing several different melodic hooks in the same sample tends to dilute the composition (too many competing ideas, nothing sticks). Instead, commit to ONE strong melody and repeat it across different instrument voices in sequence — worked example: piano states the main melody, a plucky synth restates the exact same melody, then a vocal restates it again. Reusing one melodic idea across instrument timbres reads as cohesive and reinforces the hook in the listener's ear rather than confusing them with new material on every pass; reapplied a second time in the same video to add a flute on top of an existing piano/pluck/vocal stack. → [[Layering and Stacking]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]] — loner ft. London Rayne, [this producers unique tricks will tranform your samples!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=INQDFQLR-h4)
- **Round a beat out with a counter-melody plus a light delay on an underlying piano part.** After a chopped-sample-plus-chords foundation is in place, a counter-melody is figured out by ear and a light delay is added to a piano part sitting underneath — a simple last-pass filling-in move once the core idea (source sample + harmony) is settled. → [[Delay]] — loner, [these sampling secrets will instantly elevate your beats!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_97dAWKhLv0)
- **Give the hook element something explicitly "catchy" for the listener to grab onto, distinct from the bass and lead layers around it.** Recreating Future's *Looking Exotic*: a stabby bass opens the sample, then a separate catchy lead is layered against it — described directly as giving "the listener something to grab" — a deliberate hook-writing role assigned to one specific layer rather than expecting the whole arrangement to be catchy at once. Attack automated per-chord elsewhere in the same beat (short stabs vs. longer sustains on a synth part) is a related rhythm/texture-writing move — see [[Automation]]. → [[Bass Design]] — loner, [the secret samplemaker behind Southside's best beats!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WLOHe9CJChY)
- **Two bouncy pianos layered together, both in a major scale, as the West Coast "Mustard" chord identity.** The doubled-piano-plus-major-scale combination specifically is what gives the chords their bounce, rather than a moodier minor-key read — called out as ingrained DNA for the style. Elsewhere in the same video, a two-note power-chord voicing (root + fifth, no third) is used deliberately to keep a pad dark and NOT melodic — the opposite move, same principle: chord density controls how "melodic" a part reads. → [[1 Electric Piano Track|Electric Piano Track]], [[Genre Drum Patterns]] — loner, [the secrets behind DJ Mustard's ICONIC beats!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bID1-whdYB0)
- **Use high notes to voice chord inversions as a way to write melody and rhythm at once, instead of playing a static one-chord-per-bar pattern.** Recreating a soul-sample piano layer: rather than holding plain block chords, voice inversions of the underlying progression up in the higher register — real players on the source records "would never just play a simple one chord per bar progression," so inversions (not new chords) are how period-correct soul piano parts stay moving without breaking the harmony. Pair with a **deliberate major-scale choice for a "hopeful" melodic feeling** when that's the mood target — called out as a recurring Kanye preference, not a one-off. → [[Chord]], [[Scale]] — loner, [how Kanye West changed sampling FOREVER!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AwtJV33IWPA)
- **Mono-voiced chords for a leaner top line.** Set most sounds to mono (one note at a time) even while playing full chords, so only a single note of each voicing comes through — a deliberate thinning move for a lead/accent part rather than a full pad. — loner, [these '100 GIGS' production tricks are INSANE!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IF0K7RVi_rk)
- **Replicate a lead's pitch bends with an LFO mapped to pitch, on any VST synth.** Recreate a source track's audible pitch-bend character by routing an LFO to pitch on the synth used — noted explicitly as portable to any VST synth, not tool-specific. → [[Modulation]] — loner, [these '100 GIGS' production tricks are INSANE!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IF0K7RVi_rk)
- **Pitch a bass sound UP to repurpose it as a melodic/accent element** rather than a low-end part — the same source patch reused higher in the register for a different musical role. → [[Bass Design]] — loner, [these '100 GIGS' production tricks are INSANE!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IF0K7RVi_rk)
- **A flipped sample can be missing emotional content that only chords supply.** After flipping a layered switch-up sample, the synth half of it "felt like it was missing something" — the fix was adding a chord progression, on the reasoning that you can't convey the emotion you want without chords even over an otherwise-complete sample flip. — loner, [these '100 GIGS' production tricks are INSANE!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IF0K7RVi_rk)
- **Descending chord progression for a melancholic mood**, added specifically because the reference song/section reads as melancholic — same descending-for-sad-mood pattern already logged elsewhere on this page (see the Weeknd "Snow Child" bullet below), reapplied to a different source. *(general starting point — exact chords not given)* — loner, [these '100 GIGS' production tricks are INSANE!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IF0K7RVi_rk)
- **Move BOTH the chord progression and the counter-melody stepwise downward at the same time for an easy, reliable "sad" cue.** The Weeknd's "Snow Child" (*After Hours*) pairs sad melodic chords (voiced on a soft synth read as organ-like) with a counter-melody, and both the harmony AND the secondary melodic line descend down the scale simultaneously — not just one or the other. A fast, repeatable move for a minor-key or dark-toned section: write the chord progression descending, then write the counter-melody to also descend against it. → [[Chord Progressions]], [[BED Slot]] — loner, [the production secrets behind the weeknd's evolving sound!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GMeZtNImaT0)
- **Build a counter-melody by reversing and stretching a bounced instrument part, not by composing it forward.** Wrote a counter-melody on an augmented-strings instrument, bounced it to audio, reversed the bounce, then time-stretched the reversed audio for the final part — the melodic contour that survives is whatever the reverse-and-stretch does to the original line, not a line composed to sound that way directly. Revisit and change an earlier melodic layer (here, a Bell melody written earlier in the build) once a new counter-melody is in place — early layered melodic choices are provisional until the fuller arrangement is audible. → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]] — loner, [do you need pedals to make industry level melodies!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H-3SmPmGJOg)
- **ARP added last, specifically to fill the high end and lift energy** — once low/mid melodic layers (key sounds, a pad, a supporting key part) were locked in, an arpeggiator was the deliberate final addition to occupy the top of the arrangement and increase perceived energy — a targeted "what's this missing" fix applied after the harmonic/textural work, not a starting point. → [[Arpeggiator]], [[AIR Slot]] — loner, [do you need pedals to make industry level melodies!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H-3SmPmGJOg)
- **Write the melody first as mumbled, wordless "gibberish," then reverse-engineer the lyric to fit it.** Charlie Puth's process per Jacob Collier: hum/mumble a melody with no real words, discover the verse's rhythmic/pitch shape first — nonsense in the moment becomes the finished verse melody once real words are fit to the same contour. — Charlie Puth / Jacob Collier (Sol State), [5.5 Music Tips from Charlie Puth & Jacob Collier](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PjQr58ob5pY)
- **The pentatonic scale is a recurring thread behind catchy radio/EDM melodies** — skipping the 4th and 7th scale degrees of the major scale (in C major: no F, no B) leaves the five-note pentatonic set that shows up repeatedly in hooks people call "catchy"; cited examples are Marshmello's "Happier" and Porter Robinson & Madeon's "Shelter." Paired with playing around the chord tone underneath and building a theme via repetition-with-variation rather than constant new material. — KSHMR (Sol State), [KSHMR MUSIC PRODUCTION Q&A](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdipHMBxPlA)
- **Pick a vocalist for their songwriting, not just their voice, and work with them repeatedly to grow faster as a producer.** Most people know someone who can sing well, but a great voice isn't what makes a great song — good taste in melody/lyric writing is the harder-to-find trait. Producing for one artist across many songs forces you out of working alone and into building every decision around someone else's vocal and the song you're making together. — KSHMR (Sol State), [KSHMR MUSIC PRODUCTION Q&A](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZdipHMBxPlA)
- **Treat a melody phrase as a question, answered by the next phrase — a call-and-response structure learned from Hans Zimmer.** Framing a melody as "asking something" and then "replying" gives it personality almost automatically. **Humanize a lead two ways at once:** let every note drift slightly in pitch (up or down) instead of holding perfectly static, AND deliberately vary note length (mix long and short notes rather than uniform rhythm) — both mirror how human speech actually behaves and keep an electronic lead from reading as robotic. **Add pitch slides mid-note** (not just between notes) for extra character — the same note plain vs. with a slide added partway through changes its feel meaningfully. → [[Chord Progressions]] — Jay Eskar (Sol State), [Get UNSTUCK Using A "Song Theme"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HJSK_EIGNP8)
- **The "piano test": play the core musical idea on a real piano, sung, with no production at all — if it's not compelling there, no sound design will fix it.** Morgan Page's diagnostic for a new idea: production supports a strong musical core, it doesn't create one; a progression or melody that only works once fancy plugins/presets are added probably isn't the real idea yet. Strips away every crutch simultaneously so the bare idea has to stand on its own. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]] — Morgan Page (Sol State), [This ONE Question Will Improve EVERY Song You Make](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QZf8mzMD0Oo)
- **Collaborative melody writing as "frame, then mess it up": one person sings a rough, not-quite-right idea, the other reshapes it into the final version.** Disclosure's account of writing "Latch" with Sam Smith — Sam would sing something that was "kind of there but not quite," establishing the rhythm first, then Disclosure would take that frame and move notes/timing around until it clicked. Explicit reasoning for why this works: starting from someone else's rough idea instead of a blank page means you're not precious about it being "your" idea, which makes it easier to freely reshape. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]] — Disclosure, [Vocal Secrets Behind "Latch"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=h0x0oorHXmk) (same underlying session as [[How We Made Latch]])
- **Generate melody ideas fast and pick winners after the fact, rather than writing to a predetermined section role** — a jam-based group session where everyone hums/sings hook ideas until one clearly stands out (the others audibly stop), described as "like putting money in a machine" — the strongest idea becomes the chorus, the second-strongest becomes the pre-chorus, decided *after* hearing what came out rather than composing "a chorus melody" and "a pre-chorus melody" as separate assignments. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]] — Disclosure, [How To Get MORE From Your Vocals (12 Tips)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZCpgHFNlXxg)
- **A jumping 4th interval reads as more R&B** — an ascending or descending perfect-4th leap in a melody line, called out by name as a go-to R&B-flavored move. **Harmony trick:** let one harmony voice deliberately NOT follow the lead melody's expected note on a passing tone — the divergence lands a 7th in the implied chord at that moment, which is specifically what makes that harmony sound "tasty"; losing the 7th on the next chord makes the following chord read as more major by contrast. → [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]] — Disclosure, [How To Get MORE From Your Vocals (12 Tips)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZCpgHFNlXxg)
- **Write melody rhythm around whatever already grabbed the ear on that beat — "intersecting rhythms," not stacking on top** — before placing a lead note, check whether another part (a stab, a chord hit, a bass note) already has a prominent rhythmic hit on that beat that the ear is already going to latch onto; if so, deliberately place the melody note somewhere else instead of doubling down on the same beat. Stated as one part of a broader live checklist: rhythm placement relative to other parts → register/octave choice → chord-tone alignment with the harmony underneath → instinct/feel for the rest. → [[Synthwave]] — Max Konyi, [Synthwave in Studio One with Stock Instruments | Weekly Challenge #9](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IA61_2JAd84)
- **Harmonize 2–3 monophonic instrument voices instead of playing one stacked chord** — a classical/orchestral way of building harmony: give each note of a chord (or each line of a moving harmony) to its own monophonic synth voice (slightly different tone, panned across the field) rather than defaulting to one polyphonic chord instrument — reads as a small ensemble rather than a keyboard part, and doubles as a way to write real independent-line counterpoint (voices sometimes moving in similar motion, sometimes contrary, sometimes one holding while another moves) instead of a fixed-interval harmony doubler. → [[Omnisphere]], [[Electro Funk-Hop]] — Max Konyi, [[Beat Breakdown - Groovy Electro Funk-Hop (one of my favourites!)|Beat Breakdown - Groovy Electro Funk-Hop]]
- **The "fake harp" ornament trick: play a random gesture first, conform the pitches to the chord afterward.** When you can't idiomatically play a fast decorative run (a harp glissando, a scalar flourish) live, sweep across the keys with no regard for which notes land — capturing only the timing/gesture feel — then go back and manually correct every note to fit the underlying chord/scale (here, the Lydian-dominant scale over a 13♯11 chord). Timing stays as-played; only pitch gets fixed in the edit pass. → [[Scale Modes Reference]] — Max Konyi, [[Jazzy LoFi Hip-Hop - Beat Breakdown]]
- **Track exactly which chord tone/tension a melody lands on, chord by chord, as a deliberate writing check** — worked example over a half-diminished/minor-6/dominant-9 progression: the line sits on the ♭5 resolving to the ♭3 over the ø7 chord, mirrors that same shape over the m6 chord, then shifts to 5th→♭3→major-3rd as the harmony turns dominant (tracking the natural-4-to-♯4/♯11 color change), and finally the natural 7th falling to the 5th near (not exactly on) the tonic — confirming a melody by naming its landing scale-degree against each chord in sequence, not picking pitches by feel alone. → [[Chord Naming by Context]] — Max Konyi, [[Jazzy LoFi Hip-Hop - Beat Breakdown]]
- **A clean call-and-response hook resets the ear after a maximalist section, and is worth repeating immediately because it's the payoff, not filler** — a chiptune track (Jake Kaufman, Shovel Knight soundtrack) built from constantly-shifting harmony, interweaving melodies, and changing keys/meters ("maximalism") drops briefly into a simple, clear call-and-response figure (melody, then a stabbed chord response) and immediately repeats that figure once more before diving back into denser material — the moment of clarity works specifically because of the density around it, and repeating it once (rather than moving on after a single pass) lets it register as the hook. → [[Motivic Development]] — Max Konyi, [[As I Hear It - The Secrets of Great Music Ep. 01|As I Hear It - The Secrets of Great Music \| Ep. 01]]
- **A dual-melody arranging shortcut: arpeggiate a chord progression for melody one, then start melody two a third up** — write a chord progression, arpeggiate it bottom-up for the first melody, then build a second melody starting a third above the first note, repeating the same melodic shape transposed up (optionally an octave higher for more separation) — two simultaneous melodies read as denser/more "stimulating" than one, a fast way to get a second complementary line without composing it from scratch. → [[BASS Slot]] — You Suck at Producing, [5 Ways To Make Any Genre Sound 'Hyper'](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MFCiyKwWjeQ)
- **Rhythm-first, then pitch-shift: write a single-note groove before touching the scale** — pick the scale's root note, write a rhythmically interesting pattern using ONLY that note, and only once the groove itself is satisfying start moving individual notes up/down the scale for melodic shape — keeps the rhythm from getting muddled by simultaneous pitch decisions. — You Suck at Producing, [Writing Multiple Melodies](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IciswDGYFPY)
- **Harmony-first: long sustained notes on strong scale degrees, weaker tones as stepping-stones between them** — for a second, contrasting melody, spend the most time on the root and the fifth (the strongest scale degrees), using briefer passing notes on weaker scale tones (2nd, 4th) purely to connect one strong note to the next — a harmonically-anchored complement to a rhythm-first line played alongside it. — You Suck at Producing, [Writing Multiple Melodies](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IciswDGYFPY)
- **Genre constrains what melody is "good" — there's no universal best melody, only one appropriate to its context.** A melody appropriate over traditional/classical-leaning triadic chords likely won't fit hip-hop, dubstep, house, or metal; internalize a genre's melodic vocabulary the same way as everything else — by consciously listening within it. — Max Konyi, [What Makes a Good Melody? | Musical Warp Drive - Part 2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EY-TP6wwbxI)
- **Analysis exercise: find 3 favorite songs with a clearly discernible melody and check them against contour, range, singability, and climax.** Some genres (heavy metal, a lot of ambient music) often don't have much of a distinct melodic line at all, so choose songs where melody is genuinely the point — a quick way to sanity-check whether the guidelines above actually hold for the music you personally love. — Max Konyi, [What Makes a Good Melody? | Musical Warp Drive - Part 2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EY-TP6wwbxI)
- **Turn a chord progression into a melody with a Random-style Arpeggiator, then capture the result as editable MIDI** — set an Arpeggiator to **Random** style on a chord track to pick a random note from each held chord in sequence, generating unpredictable-but-usable melodic ideas from harmony you've already written. Since the arpeggiator's live output can't be saved/edited directly, capture it: create a new empty MIDI track, set its **MIDI From** to the arpeggiator's output, arm and record a pass, then move that captured MIDI back onto a normal track, turn the arpeggiator off, and edit the once-generative result freely (move, delete, transpose notes) like any other MIDI clip. → [[Randomized Rack Design]] — You Suck at Producing, [Writing Multiple Melodies](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IciswDGYFPY)
