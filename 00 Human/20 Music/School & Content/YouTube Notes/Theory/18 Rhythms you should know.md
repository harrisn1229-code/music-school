# 18 Rhythms you should know

## Synopsis

The original (earlier) video in this pair (see also [[7 Rhythms you should know]]): 18 named, recognizable rhythmic patterns spanning Afro-Cuban clave, swing/shuffle theory, polyrhythms, and a handful of pop-culture rhythm nicknames — framed as a shared-vocabulary tool for talking about rhythm with other musicians.

## What's taught

- **Son clave** — an Afro-Cuban "master rhythm" template other parts are built around; comes in **3-2** (3 notes then 2) and **2-3** (flipped) forms. The same rhythm is called the **Bo Diddley beat** in North American/European rock (popularized by Bo Diddley's 1955 song).
- **Tresillo** — the first bar of son clave taken alone: a 3-3-2 division of 8 eighth notes (Spanish for "triplet," though it isn't actually a triplet rhythm). Common in 2010s pop; also audible in *Clocks* by Coldplay, where a constant-eighth-note arpeggio's *accents* fall into the same 3-3-2 pattern even though every note is technically equal length. **Full transmission history** ([[Tresillo - The Rhythm of 2010s Pop Music]]): sub-Saharan Africa → Cuban contradanza → European habanera (Carmen) → New Orleans ragtime/jazz (Jelly Roll Morton) → Charleston → Jamaican dancehall/Dembow → reggaetón → 2010s mainstream pop (Despacito).
- **Rumba clave** — nearly identical to son clave, differing by shifting one note an eighth note later; also comes in 3-2 and 2-3 forms.
- **Standard African bell pattern ("bembé")** — traditionally played on a bell to anchor the groove, most naturally felt in **12/8** (sometimes notated as 6/8 or 4/4). Likely historical origin of the **shuffle** feel — a 12/8 rhythm emphasizing the 1st and 3rd eighth note of each beat, equivalently "4/4 with triplets on every beat." Root of jazz/blues shuffle feel, later inherited by rock.
- **Swing ratios** — swing = uneven eighth notes, long-short. Described by ratio: **3:1** (a stronger swing) vs. **2:1** (a shuffle, the long note exactly twice the short one). In real playing these ratios are felt/approximate, not counted precisely.
- **Scotch snap / Lombard rhythm** — the reverse of swing: short note first, then long. "Scotch snap" from Scottish traditional music; "Lombard rhythm" from Baroque usage (cited: a Henry Purcell piece); also common in current R&B/hip-hop (credited to Adam Neely's video on the topic for that observation).
- **Bossa nova rhythm** — a Brazilian style; the archetypal snare pattern is often felt as a 3-3-4-3-3 grouping. Crossover example outside bossa nova proper: *Break On Through* by The Doors.
- **Charleston rhythm** — from the 1920s dance/song of the same name (popularized via the musical *Runnin' Wild*); rhythmically, it's just the first two notes of a tresillo/clave rhythm.
- **Waltz** — the standard 3/4 "oom-pah" dance rhythm (strong-weak-weak); outlasted its dance-music origins (Chopin used waltz rhythm in concert pieces never meant for dancing) and still appears in popular music.
- **"5/4 clave"** — a common 4-note (long-long-short-short) anchor pattern for playing convincingly in 5/4 despite the meter having 5 quarter notes; famous example: the *Mission: Impossible* theme.
- **Triplets, generalized** — fitting a different note count into a space than the meter would normally hold (triplets = 3-in-the-space-of-2; also quintuplets, septuplets, etc.).
- **Polyrhythms**: **3-over-2 (hemiola)**, mnemonic "nice cup of tea" — example: *Daydreaming* by Radiohead (right hand plays 3 against the left hand's 2; **precise mechanism** per [[What's the difference between 3-4 and 6-8 time]]: the right hand plays in 3/4 while the left hand simultaneously plays in 6/8 — written on the page in one unified signature since polyrhythms aren't notated with two at once). **4-over-3**, mnemonic "pass the goddamn butter" — example: *Fake Empire* by The National (left hand plays 3 against the right hand's 4). Practical point: you don't consciously track both numbers while playing/hearing a polyrhythm — you internalize the single *composite* rhythm the two pulses combine into, which is what the mnemonic phrases are for.
- **"Football clap" / England chant** — a rhythm UK audiences associate with football-chant call-and-response (payoff word "England" on the last two hits); cited in *Let's Go* and in The Jam's "The Whole Point of No Return"-era track "Eton Rifles" material [as referenced, "Self Preservation Society"].
- **"Shave and a Haircut, two bits"** — a classic rhythmic call-and-response fragment (documented as early as 1899); the "two bits" answer is felt as necessary to resolve the phrase, which is why withholding it is a standard musician's joke — demonstrated via a Dexter Gordon recording that plays the call without the answer.
