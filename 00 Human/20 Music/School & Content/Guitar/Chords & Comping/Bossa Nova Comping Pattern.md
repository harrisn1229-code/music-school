# Bossa Nova Comping Pattern

The classic two-bar bossa nova right-hand accompaniment figure, played fingerstyle: a **thumb keeping a steady half-note bass** (the surdo drum's role) against **fingers stabbing syncopated chord tones on top** (the tamborim's role) — one guitar doing the job of a whole rhythm section. This is the guitar engine documented at the top of [[Bossa Nova (João Gilberto)]]'s genre pillars, worked here as its own standalone right-hand-pattern drill rather than folded into a full genre write-up.

## Hand setup

- **Thumb (p):** bass notes only — root of the current chord (upgrade: alternating root/5th, see below).
- **Index/middle/ring (i/m/a):** the upper chord tones, on the top 3 strings of whatever voicing is in use. **No pinky.**
- Works over any chord voicing that separates cleanly into "a bass string" + "a 3-string chord above it" — a 4-string subset of the full chord shape. A **fretted bass note (not an open string)** is preferred so the bass line has full independent control rather than being locked to one open pitch — e.g. for D major 7, fret the root at the 5th fret/A string instead of relying on open low D.

**Worked example — D major 7, fretted-bass voicing** (from [[How to Play a Bossa Nova Guitar Rhythm]]):
- Index: 5th fret, A string (root, D)
- Ring: 7th fret, D string (5th, A)
- Middle: 6th fret, G string (7th, C♯)
- Pinky: 7th fret, B string (3rd, F♯)
- Spelling low-to-high: **1–5–7–3**

## The two-bar rhythm

**Bass (thumb):** half notes on **beats 1 and 3** of every bar, unchanging — the fixed reference the whole pattern is measured against.

**Chord (fingers):**
- **Bar 1** — chord lands *with* the bass on **beat 1** (the only point in the whole cycle where bass and chord attack together), alone on **beat 2**, on the offbeat after beat 3 (**"&" of 3**), and on the offbeat anticipating the next bar (**"&" of 4**).
- **Bar 2** — further offbeat hits through the bar, landing back on the beat only once more, on **beat 4** — the last beat of the two-bar cycle, before it loops.
- **Net result: only 2 of the pattern's roughly 6 chord attacks land on the beat** (bar 1 beat 1, bar 2 beat 4) — everything else is syncopated onto the "&"s. That heavy offbeat weighting is what reads as the bossa "bounce."

This lines up with [[Bossa Nova (João Gilberto)]]'s existing Level 1/Level 2 description (bass on 1 & 3, chords on 2 and the "& of 3," with the anticipation push into the next bar) — same underlying rhythm, this page's version adds the full two-bar hit list and a build-it-yourself practice method rather than just the finished grid.

## How to learn it (incremental-build method)

Don't try to play the finished 2-bar pattern from a chart — build it note by note, always re-anchored to the bass:

1. **Bass alone** — half notes on 1 and 3, rock steady, both bars.
2. **Add exactly one chord attack at a time**, in the order it actually occurs in the pattern (beat 1 together → beat 2 → the offbeats), replaying from the top after each addition — never skip ahead to the full pattern before each single addition feels secure.
3. **If the offbeats are the sticking point, isolate them**: tap your foot on the quarter-note beat and pluck *only* the offbeat 8th notes, skipping every downbeat — compared directly to a **ska/reggae** accent feel. Re-add the bass once the offbeat feel alone is solid.
4. **Metronome with a distinct offbeat click** (a different tone on the "&"s) locks the ear onto the offbeat grid directly.

## Right-hand touch

- **Planting** — pre-place the plucking fingers on the strings just before the attack; this produces the pattern's characteristic rhythmic "bounce" rather than a flat pluck. Same core habit as [[Right-Hand Pinky Fingerstyle Technique]]'s calibration exercises.
- **Optional damping** — briefly muting the ringing notes before the next pluck for a more clipped, percussive color; a stylistic layer on top of the pattern, not required to play it correctly.

## Next level — alternating root/5th bass

Once the static-root version is solid, upgrade the thumb to **alternate root and 5th** on the two bass hits per bar instead of repeating the same note. Worth the extra coordination mainly when **sustaining one chord for a longer stretch** — moving through a fast chord progression, the plain repeated-root version reads cleaner and is what most players actually use in practice.

## Comping adaptation (playing with others, not drilling alone)

When accompanying a melody, vocal, or soloist rather than practicing solo, the strict pattern loosens: on a chord change it's common to play **just the root** instead of the full root+upper-voicing hit, purely to keep the timing smooth, while never breaking the underlying pulse to nail every note of the drilled pattern. Treat the strict two-bar pattern as the *practice target* — internalize it fully — then let live playing be a natural adaptation of it, the same way jazz comping in general trades strict pattern execution for feel once the vocabulary is internalized (see [[Jazz Comping — Rhythmic Motif and Walking Bassline]]).
