# JAZ-C02 — The ii-V-I, major and minor

**Q.** Spell the ii–V–I in C major and in C minor, and say why these three chords rather than any others.

> [!question]- Answer
> Major: **Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7.** Minor: **Dm7♭5 – G7♭9 – Cm7** (Cm6 and CmMaj7 are equally in use).
>
> The reason is root motion. D → G → C is the circle of fifths in miniature — each root falls a fifth, which is the strongest progression in tonal music, and the cell stacks two of them back to back.

## Why it matters

The sentence jazz is written in. Nearly all standard-repertoire harmony is ii–V–I units chained, transposed and disguised, so seeing them is what stops a lead sheet looking arbitrary.

The three minor-key differences are all forced by the scale rather than chosen: the ii goes half-diminished because the ♭5 is simply the 6th degree of natural minor; the V keeps its major 3rd because the raised leading tone is what makes it a dominant at all, and picks up a ♭9 from harmonic minor; and the tonic is deliberately ambiguous between m7, m6 and mMaj7.

## Drill

Write the major ii–V–I in all twelve keys from memory, then the minor version in four. Then play `Dm7 G7 Cmaj7 Cmaj7` as a four-bar loop and notice that it is already a complete, loopable progression with nothing added.
