# Advanced Blues Rhythm Guitar Lesson!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VswkBsiH9Ko)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VswkBsiH9Ko)

## Synopsis

Jack Ruch breaks down four increasingly advanced blues **rhythm**-guitar performances (all over the same slow 12/8 blues backing track), each one adding a new layer of sophistication on top of a single rootless dominant-9 chord shape. The through-line is that nearly everything taught reduces to one small grip — major 3rd, ♭7, 9th, 5th, no root — either **relocated** (moved through the I-IV-V of the form), **slid chromatically** (a half step up or down, reinterpreting the same fingering as a different chord entirely), or **reharmonized** by inserting a functioning ii-V-I ahead of a target chord. Performance 3 explicitly borrows several "snippets" from a Robin Ford performance video (linked in Ruch's own description) rather than being original material — Ruch credits this directly. The vocabulary is squarely T-Bone Walker lineage (named directly, including the "Stormy Monday" half-step-up move), with Robin Ford's voice-leading chord movements layered on top for the more advanced material. Ruch is explicit about teaching by interval relationship rather than by note name, on the reasoning that's how he actually thinks about the material when playing it, and closes by pointing to real recorded performances (not just charts) as the actual way to internalize this style. This is a genuine **rhythm-guitar** lesson — not soloing — though several of the devices (the half-step-up altered-V slide, the inserted 2-5-1) have direct soloing-side siblings already in the vault from the same channel.

## What's taught

- **Rootless dominant-9 base shape** — major 3rd, ♭7, 9th, 5th, no root. The single unit of vocabulary the entire lesson is built from.
- **The major-6 fill (T-Bone Walker signature):** slide the inner two notes of the rootless-9 shape up a whole step, turning ♭7-9-5 into root-3-6 (a major 6 sound); grab the root with the 1st finger. Used as a passing fill between solo lines or vocal phrases. Relocated through all three chords of the blues (worked as G9 → C9 → D9).
- **Half-step-up slide = implied altered V ("Stormy Monday" move):** slide the whole rootless-9 shape up a half step; the same fingering now reads as an altered-dominant voicing a half step above the original root (worked: G9-shape moved up a half step reads as D-altered — ♭7, major 3rd, ♭13, ♭9 of D). Lines up with a bass line walking I→V→I under a static rhythm chord. Soloing pairing given directly: play the altered scale rooted a half step above the original chord.
- **Down-up chromatic neighbor + root stab:** from the base shape, move down a half step, back up to the original, then aggressively stab the root note on the high E string. Named directly as "a classic T-Bone Walker thing."
- **Inserting a functioning 2-5-1 into the blues rhythm part:** drop a full ii-V-I (e.g. Dm7 → G7[altered] → C7/C9) ahead of any target chord in the form — worked resolving to the IV chord and, separately, resolving to the I chord (D7/D-altered → G7). General rule stated directly: look at the chord you're going to and build the matching 2-5-1 in front of it. Two altered-V voicing options given for the inserted "5" (♭7-3-♭13-♯9, or swap in ♭9 for the same family — both derive from that dominant's altered scale). Explicitly usable as a soloist's/rhythm player's private reharmonization, working even when the rest of the band isn't playing it — with a stated caution that it can clash if the band isn't expecting it, especially outside the I→IV spot.
- **Robin Ford voice-leading movements (explicitly credited, borrowed from a linked video):**
  - A three-chord descending pickup into the IV chord: a 13-type voicing (♭7-3-13-9 on top) → an augmented-7th/♭13-type chord → the plain rootless-9 voicing.
  - Over the IV chord: a sus-type chord (a triad a whole step below the bass note) → a "minor-major7"-quality color chord (alternately readable as a dominant stacked with ♭7/9/♯11/13) → up to the top of a dominant-9 chord a 4th above. Stated purpose: builds tension that needs to resolve; the chords voice-lead into one another.
  - Turnaround-section voicings over the V chord: a 6/9-type chord with no 3rd/♭7 (deliberately vague/open), and its equivalent built off the V's own root (a ♭13-type or 7♯9 reading of the same notes) — slid up a half step and back down for extra chromatic tension before resolving.
  - **Repeated ensemble caution:** these voicings can clash against another chordal instrument — play them with a lighter touch, not too heavy-handed, when comping alongside a keyboard or second guitar.
- **High-position 9th-chord "cluster" voicing:** ♭7-9-3-root(top), the 9th and major 3rd only a whole step apart giving it a deliberately dissonant cluster color. Slide the 3rd up a half step to grab the 4th — produces a short 13→9→♯9 sequence functioning as the same altered-V-into-IV move as the half-step-up trick above, fingered from a higher position. Stated to work over any groove feel (demoed on a slow 12/8, explicitly said to also work over shuffle or swing).
- **Stated practice philosophy:** teaches by interval relationship rather than note name (how Ruch says he actually thinks about the material while playing it); recommends listening to real recorded performances to internalize the feel rather than reading a chart cold.
