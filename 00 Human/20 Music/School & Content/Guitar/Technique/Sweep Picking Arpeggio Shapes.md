# Sweep Picking Arpeggio Shapes

A small set of movable arpeggio shapes, played with sweep picking (one continuous pick stroke through several strings, "just like playing a chord," rather than alternate-picking each note) instead of a complex picking system, as the actual mechanism behind Barney Kessel's fast, fluid lines. Complements [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]] — that page's CAGED-adjacency system is about *where on the neck* to find the next chord's arpeggio; this page is about *how the pick hand executes* an arpeggio shape once you're there, plus a distinct core idea: treat each shape as a **flexible visual pattern**, not fixed to the one chord it was first learned over.

## The four shapes
- **Shape 1 — minor 9 arpeggio, bidirectional.** Ascending: up–down–down–down–down–up (the core minor-9 shape with an added note before/after it). Descending (reverse): down–up–up–up–up–down.
- **Shape 2 — minor 7 arpeggio, bidirectional.** Ascending: down–up–up–up. Descending: down–down–down–up.
- **Shape 3 — descending only.**
- **Shape 4 — ascending only.**
- All four appear together within a single Barney Kessel phrase (his solo on "Mean to Me," from the Poll Winners' debut record).
- One note per string throughout — the sweep motion is a single continuous pick stroke, not alternate picking, exactly the motion used to strum a chord but applied one note at a time.

## Getting the motion under the fingers
1. **Isolate the shape and the pick motion first**, before trying to use it musically — practice each shape slowly, ascending and descending, focused purely on a smooth, connected sound through the sweep *(general starting point — no metronome number given in the source)*.
2. Only once the picking motion is comfortable, move to the applications below.

## Applying the shapes for speed with low effort
- **Move a shape chromatically through a static pattern.** Shape 1 (and separately Shape 2) moved chromatically through a **ii-V-I (251)** — see [[The ii-V-I]] — produces a fast, smooth line with little conscious picking effort; the chromatic slide, not raw picking speed, is what reads as "fast."
- **Lead into a shape from a scale.** Worked in F major over **Gm7 → C7 → Fmaj7**: walk up the **G Dorian** scale (see [[Diatonic Modes]]), then link directly into Shape 1 played over the C7, resolving into Fmaj7 — the scale run and the swept arpeggio connect as one continuous line, the same scale-into-arpeggio connecting logic documented generally on [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]].
- **Combine shapes within one line for rhythmic variety.** After an ascending scale-into-Shape-1 line, adding Shape 4 (also ascending) keeps the direction going while varying the rhythmic feel — the combined result reads as far more complex than "a scale plus an arpeggio."

## Top-note guide-tone chaining between shapes
Chain two shapes together by choosing which note each one starts/ends on so the **top notes move by step** — the sweep-picking analogue of a [[Guide Tones|guide-tone line]], tracking the top note of the shape rather than a chord's 3rd/7th. Worked example: Shape 1 descending over a **Gm** chord, then Shape 2 descending but deliberately started on **A♭** as its top note — producing a chromatic half-step connection between the two shapes' top notes — closed out with one of Kessel's original major sweep phrases.

## The core secret: shapes as flexible patterns, not chord-locked licks
The central idea of the source lesson: although each shape is technically derived from a specific arpeggio quality (minor 9, minor 7), the *visual pattern* on the neck is reusable over chords the arpeggio doesn't strictly belong to. Two worked reharmonizations:
- **Shape 3 over an unrelated dominant.** Kessel originally played it over an **E♭7**; the identical shape also works cleanly over a **C7**.
- **Starting a shape on the ♭9 for an altered-dominant color.** Starting Shape 3 on the **♭9 of a C7 chord (D♭)**, instead of its "home" starting degree, produces a strong altered-dominant sound — same underlying move (borrowing a familiar shape to voice an altered dominant) as the melodic-minor/altered-scale substitutions documented via Jens Larsen on [[Scale Modes Reference]] § *From videos* and [[Altered Chords]].
- **Stated generalization:** "just because you first learn to sweep a G minor 9 shape doesn't mean it's the only way to play that shape" — deliberately experiment with a familiar shape over other harmonic contexts rather than filing it under one chord quality permanently.

## Splicing shapes into longer lines
- **Combine two shapes to cover more fretboard in one continuous phrase** — e.g. Shape 1 spliced with a second sweep pattern lifted from the tail of Kessel's original phrase; the combined phrase covers noticeably more neck than either shape alone.
- **The pick-hand motion must line up across the seam** between the two shapes for the splice to feel smooth — this is the one hard technical requirement of combining shapes.
- **A spliced phrase is reversible** — playable backward (descending) as well as forward.
- **Full worked 251 line (assembled from every technique above):** open with a spliced multi-shape phrase, use Shape 1 to transition smoothly from the V chord into the I chord (the scale-into-shape application above), and close with a signature major "tag" phrase — stitched into one continuous fast line covering a lot of the fretboard, playable very fast with surprisingly little conscious effort once internalized.

- **Extend a major-arpeggio sweep with tapped notes on both the highest AND lowest string, not just the top.** Named as a fresh idea specifically because most players only ever extend a sweep upward (tapping past the top string); tapping an extension on the *lowest* string of the shape too, on the way back down, is far less commonly heard and worth deliberately including. - **Start with bigger shapes (fewer direction changes), practice each sweep direction separately, and a hammer-on trick for the hardest transition in a small shape.** Small 3-string shapes force frequent sweep-direction reversals — the genuinely hardest part of sweep picking — for the same amount of material; bigger shapes give more time inside each direction before it has to reverse, even though they're harder to memorize. Practice down-sweeps and up-sweeps in isolation for weeks before combining. **The trick:** on a small shape's hardest upstroke-to-downstroke transition (worked in G minor: an upstroke on the B string into a downstroke on the G string, requiring the pick to jump over the G string), replace that transitional note with a **hammer-on** instead of picking it — buys the picking hand a brief pause to reposition cleanly. The hammered note must be dynamically indistinguishable from a picked one. Found via filming and reviewing practice footage to isolate the exact bottleneck transition rather than guessing. — BERNTH, [[Ultimate Sweep Picking Trick - 3-String Arpeggio Sweeping Lesson]]
- **A lightweight 2-string economy-picking entry point: one downstroke per string, everything else legato.** For a 2-string lick, reduce picking to a single downstroke per string and replace every other note with a hammer-on/pull-off — same exact notes and phrasing as an alternate-picked version, but the string-transition moment (the hardest part of the phrase at speed) is removed structurally rather than practiced through. Start on an upstroke to land in position for the downstrokes that follow. Framed as a good on-ramp into economy picking before attempting full multi-string sweep shapes. — BERNTH, [[2 String Shredding Trick - Economy Picking VS Alternate Picking]]
- **Switching sweep direction mid-arpeggio, not just sweeping straight up-then-down, named as the real distinguishing skill.** Most sweep-picking exercises only train ascending-then-descending through one arpeggio shape; a direction-changing pattern (worked in 8th-note triplets) that switches sweep direction partway through — same string-transition-difficulty problem as alternate picking, just applied to the sweep motion — is what actually makes a sweep-picked line sound musically distinctive rather than mechanical. Ranked as a genre-specific "often" (not universal-daily) practice topic. — BERNTH, [[Guitar Exercises Ranked By Importance]]
- **The core beginner mental-model reframe: sweep picking borrows the chord-strum motor pattern, not the individual-note-picking pattern.** Most players' hands already have two separate motor connections — "arpeggiate/solo mode" (individual note-by-note picking, e.g. alternate picking) and "chord/strum mode" (one continuous motion across strings) — and default to the first for an arpeggio, which caps out hard as tempo rises. Sweep picking means deliberately tearing apart that default association and re-routing arpeggio-playing onto the strum-mode motion instead. Framed as the single biggest obstacle for most beginners, more than any physical difficulty — practiced on a worked Cmaj7 shape (10th fret D · 9th fret G · 8th fret B · 7th fret high E), watching for zero individual string attacks before any speed work. — BERNTH, [[Best Beginner Sweeping Exercise - Master Sweep Picking TODAY!]]
- **Beginner anchor technique: lock the first and last note of the arpeggio to the metronome, let the middle notes fall into place around them.** Direct fix for the classic beginner "confusing mess of dead notes in the middle" sound — trying to place every note correctly at once is harder than nailing the two anchor points and letting the rest resolve rhythmically around them over repetition. Paired with a two-phase daily routine: slow-and-perfect first, then a deliberately fast, sloppy, no-stakes pass to break the individual-string-attack habit at speed — argued as more effective than a strict linear BPM climb. — BERNTH, [[SWEEP PICKING for Beginners - The 5 BEST Exercises!]]

- **A small wrist-rotation trick for the hardest sweep-picking moment — reversing direction at the top of an arpeggio (upstroke into downstroke) — solved by anchoring the hand at the top note and rotating from the wrist rather than trying to physically jump the pick over the string.** Normal ascending/descending sweeping uses a relaxed hand that follows the strings with no fixed anchor, changing picking angle continuously string to string; but the up-then-down direction change specifically gets the pick stuck in the "dead zone" between two strings if played that way. - **Sweep-picking mechanics checklist: grip, muting, and shape choice.** Use the same pick grip for sweep picking as for alternate picking (readjusting grip between the two costs speed when switching mid-line — a conscious trade-off, sacrificing a little sweep speed for a consistent grip since alternate picking is used more). Keep right-hand placement in one fixed zone. No death grip on the pick. Slant the pick into the direction being swept. Mute unplayed strings with both hands, and use a fret wrap (or a sock) to kill open-string ring-out as the sweep ascends. Prefer 7th-arpeggio shapes over triads when sweeping — the extra note means more hammer-ons/pull-offs woven into the motion. Never drill a sweep shape in total isolation — always practice the notes immediately before/after it too, or it won't transfer into real lines. — Brandon D'Eon, [I'll teach ya how to sweep](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AXsjSYeXdKE)
- **Practice-scheduling tip, not a new shape: drill one sweep shape through all 12 keys with a metronome, varying the starting string, instead of drilling it in one key/position only** — running the shape through every key forces the underlying arpeggio-tone knowledge to ride along with the technique reps.
