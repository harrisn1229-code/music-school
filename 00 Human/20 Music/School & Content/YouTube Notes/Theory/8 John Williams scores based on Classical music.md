# 8 John Williams scores based on Classical music

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-VYzEtIbeU)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-VYzEtIbeU)

## Synopsis

A side-by-side listening comparison of John Williams film cues against the classical pieces that clearly inspired them — Stravinsky, Holst, Tchaikovsky, Dvořák, and Korngold — framed explicitly as legitimate creative borrowing (adapting an idea into a new melody) rather than plagiarism, since Williams never copies verbatim and always develops the borrowed material into something distinct.

## Techniques

- **A dissonant orchestral polychord, borrowed near-directly**: Stravinsky's "Rite of Spring" stacks alternating E♭ minor and D♭ minor chords over a sustained D minor bass to create its famous dissonant, mysterious sonority. Williams uses almost the same polychord for the C-3PO/Tatooine desert scene in *Star Wars* — the only real difference is the bass alternates between D minor and C rather than sustaining D minor throughout. A second, more percussive polychordal string motif elsewhere in *Star Wars* draws on the same Stravinsky piece.
- **A near-direct orchestral chord lift, with one note changed**: Holst's "Mars, Bringer of War" (from *The Planets*) supplies the dramatic chord under the Death Star destruction scene — Williams changes one note of the chord and slightly adjusts the rhythm, keeping the effect essentially intact.
- **A recognizable orchestral color lifted for a similar mood**: Holst's "Neptune" (also from *The Planets*) is audibly close to the *Return of the Jedi* opening cue — not identical, but a clear stylistic influence in texture and harmony.
- **A melodic interval borrowed and adapted, not the whole theme**: Tchaikovsky's Violin Concerto opens with a leap from A up to F; Princess Leia's theme borrows that exact opening leap, then develops it into a distinct melody of its own.
- **Genre-appropriate borrowing for thematic/seasonal association, not just melodic inspiration**: Williams' *Home Alone* score echoes the frantic energy of the Trepak ("Russian Dance") from Tchaikovsky's *Nutcracker* — a deliberate choice, since audiences already associate *Nutcracker* music with Christmas, so leaning on that association makes the *Home Alone* score read as "Christmassy" by cultural shorthand rather than needing to build that association from scratch.
- **A rhythmic/intervallic motif borrowed for its tension-building shape**: the alternating-semitone two-note motif that opens the *Jaws* theme parallels a similar tension-building semitone pivot at the opening of the fourth movement of Dvořák's Symphony No. 9 ("From the New World") — both pieces diverge quickly after the shared opening gesture.
- **A direct opening-phrase homage**: the 1942 film *Kings Row*'s opening theme (Erich Wolfgang Korngold) opens identically to Williams' famous *Star Wars* main title theme before the two pieces diverge into distinct material.
- **The framing that ties every example together**: in every case, Williams borrows a specific idea (a chord, an interval, a rhythmic gesture) as a starting point, then develops it into new, distinct music — never copying verbatim or presenting the borrowed idea as his own unaltered creation. Adaptation of an existing idea into new material, not verbatim reuse, is the working line this video draws between homage and plagiarism.
