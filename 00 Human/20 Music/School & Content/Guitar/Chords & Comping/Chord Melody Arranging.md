# Chord Melody Arranging

Part of [[Guitar Positions]] and the guitar side of [[Chord Voicings]] — **chord melody** (aka solo guitar arrangement) is playing the melody on top while filling in harmony underneath it, one instrument covering both parts, usually with **rootless chord shapes** (the melody note stands in for the top voice, so the shape only needs the rest of the chord's color). The analytical trick that makes this workable in real time, rather than a slow puzzle, is [[Diatonic Modes|modal]] renumbering applied note-by-note as the harmony moves.

## The core idea: renumber off the chord, not the key
Read every melody note as a scale degree **of whichever chord is sounding right now**, not as a scale degree of the parent key. This is [[Diatonic Modes]]'s "relative approach" (same 7 notes, different tonic) applied live, beat to beat: the same physical note can be "2 of C" while a C chord is under it, then "4 of D" a beat later once the harmony moves to Dm — same pitch, different functional meaning, different go-to shape to reach for. Thinking "1-2 of the C scale" when a Dm chord (the ii chord) is actually sounding hides the real relationship; thinking "4-5 of the current chord's root" reveals it, and it's that revealed relationship that tells you which memorized shape fits.

## Memorized "go-to" shapes, moved across roots
Build a small vocabulary of chord shapes tagged by **which scale degree of the current chord's root the melody note lands on**, then reuse the exact same physical shape over any chord of that quality just by sliding it to the new root — the shape doesn't change, only which chord root it's being renumbered against. Demonstrated over a Let It Be-style G→Am→F→C→G→F passage: a single memorized rootless minor-7 shape (melody note on the 4th, no root voiced — the open A string is available as an optional root if wanted) gets reused as the harmony walks through F ("6-5-5"), C ("4-3-4-3"), and G ("6-5-6-5") — full worked-out passage on [[Chord Melody Made Simple (Part 5): Modes of the C Major Scale]]. This is the direct payoff of the renumbering habit: once you can instantly say "that's the 4th of whatever chord I'm on," you already know which go-to shape covers it, so arranging speeds way up and the choice sticks in memory longer.

## The drill: run every mode of the key, saying the numbers out loud
Homework, done in the open position in the key of C: play up and down the C major scale, but say the scale-degree numbers **relative to whichever chord/mode you're currently drilling**, not relative to C. Same 7 notes each time, renumbered off a different chord tone:

| Chord (degree) | Mode | Ascending | Descending |
|---|---|---|---|
| C (I) | Ionian | 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 1 | 1 7 6 5 4 3 2 1 |
| Dm (ii) | Dorian | 1 2 ♭3 4 5 6 ♭7 1 | 1 ♭7 6 5 4 ♭3 2 1 |
| Em (iii) | Phrygian | 1 ♭2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7 1 | 1 ♭7 ♭6 5 4 ♭3 ♭2 1 |
| F (IV) | Lydian | 1 2 3 ♯4 5 6 7 1 | 1 7 6 5 ♯4 3 2 1 |
| G (V) | Mixolydian | 1 2 3 4 5 6 ♭7 1 | 1 ♭7 6 5 4 3 2 1 |
| Am (vi) | Aeolian | 1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7 1 | 1 ♭7 ♭6 5 4 ♭3 2 1 |
| B° (vii°) | Locrian | 1 ♭2 ♭3 4 ♭5 ♭6 ♭7 1 | 1 ♭7 ♭6 ♭5 4 ♭3 ♭2 1 |

Formulas match [[Diatonic Modes]]'s table exactly (same modes, same parent scale) — this drill is that table converted into a spoken, played exercise rather than a lookup. **Mastery bar:** get each mode's spoken-numbers-while-playing pass correct three times in a row before moving to the next one; no metronome required to start (add timing later once the numbers come without a struggle).

## Baby-step variant: note, then root
Before attempting a full up-and-down run in a new mode, alternate **melody note → chord root**, back and forth, to hear the actual interval rather than only reciting its name — e.g. play a note, then immediately play/sound the Dm root, to internalize what "that specific distance" sounds like before trying to name every degree in sequence. Useful specifically as the on-ramp into a mode that still feels unfamiliar (worked example: getting the Dorian sound under your ear before drilling the full Dorian run).

## Lock your view onto the closest root, not always the lowest
Rather than computing every interval against one fixed low root on the neck, mentally re-anchor to **whichever instance of the current chord's root is physically closest** to the note you're on, wherever that falls on the fretboard — recomputes intervals faster and transfers regardless of which octave or string the phrase sits in. Worked example over a mid-neck D (Dorian root, the ii chord): reading a run of notes around it as 4, ♭3, 2, 1 (root), ♭7, 6, 5 by treating that specific D as "home," not the open low D.

## Two valid ways in — fretboard-first or name-first
Either on-ramp is fine and they converge with repetition: **fretboard-first** — you already feel/see the shape and interval (e.g. "that's a 4th") and only then attach the scale-degree name to it; or **name-first** — drill the spoken number sequence until you know it cold, and the fretboard association builds from repeating it. Which one is faster depends entirely on what you already know well (an interval you already recognize easily is a shortcut; one you don't, isn't) — don't force one approach if the other is working.

## Practice mindset: practice perfectly, don't demand perfect playing
Set the goal as **getting each mode's drill correct three times in a row**, not flawless execution on the first pass. Reframe "I didn't play it perfectly" (a bad outcome to dread) into "I practiced with full focus and followed the drill's own rules" (a good outcome, achievable every time) — this removes the perfectionism block that otherwise stalls repetitive drilling. The drilling itself can feel genuinely unpleasant while you're still weak at a mode — that's expected and not a sign to stop.

## Step-by-step lead-sheet method (the deliberate, on-paper counterpart to the live method above)

Where the section above is the *real-time* method (renumber on the fly, reach for a memorized shape), this is the *slow, working-it-out* method for arranging a full lead sheet ahead of time — four repeatable steps per melody note:

1. **Root of the chord** — just the chord symbol's letter name (G half-diminished → G; C7♭9 → C; Fm6 → F).
2. **Letter name of the melody note** — read it off the staff with the "FACE in the spaces" shortcut (the spaces of a treble-clef staff spell F-A-C-E bottom to top; everything else is alphabetical around them), then apply the key's sharps/flats.
3. **Chord-tone number of the melody note relative to the root** — the hard step. Two ways in: a visual interval-number chart anchored on the current root (e.g. [guitarcientist.com](https://guitarcientist.com) generates one per root), or counting the major-scale formula (whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half) up from the root by ear/hand. Counting straight up gets you close ("some kind of 3") but not exact — you still need the letter name from step 2 to know major vs. flat.
4. **Find/build a voicing with that chord tone as the top note.** A hand-built reference works better indexed **by which string the melody note lands on** (string 1 / string 2 / string 3), not just by chord type — that's the actual lookup you need mid-arrangement. Keep a 3-note and a 4-note version per slot, plus a 2-note fallback for when the melody note isn't a chord tone at all.

**No-chart fretboard-only variant:** memorize the natural half-steps (E–F, B–C; every other adjacent natural note is a whole step), walk the alphabet from any known note to find any other note by ear/hand, then apply the major-scale interval formula from the root to get the chord-tone number without a chart — you still need each chord type's own spelling (e.g. half-diminished = root ♭3 ♭5 ♭7) to build the voicing itself. **Deriving inversions the same way:** from a known voicing, find the next-closest chord tone on the same string, moving down, for every note simultaneously — repeat and you walk the shape down through its inversions without a reference at all.

## Physical execution: don't harmonize every note

The single highest-leverage habit for actually *playing* a chord melody smoothly (as opposed to arranging it on paper) is choosing, note by note, when to play a full chord shape and when to just play the single melody note and let the previous harmony (or nothing) carry through. This is a deliberate artistic choice, re-decidable every time you play the piece — not a rule to solve once. Two real payoffs, not just one: it's **physically easier** (fewer full-hand jumps between shapes, since you're often landing the melody note alone with one finger before adding the rest) and it **sounds better** (space between chord grabs reads as phrasing rather than a wall of block chords; even solo/unaccompanied, mostly-single-notes-with-occasional-chords still sounds fully harmonized). A concrete glide move for a sustained/repeated melody note across a chord change: play the first chord shape, glide the fretting finger (often the 4th) down to the next melody note while it holds, sound that note alone, then land the next full chord shape from the same position — smoother than jumping cold into the next shape. Demonstrated across "What Is This Thing Called Love" with rootless dim7-as-C7♭9 and rootless G7-altered voicings (guide-tones-non-optional / root-and-extension-interchangeable logic — see [[Guide Tones]], [[Diminished Chords]], [[Altered Chords]]) and a one-note half-step voice-leading link between a half-diminished ii shape and its rootless V7♭9, described as a "piano left-hand comping" effect. → [[Chord Melody Arranging is Miserable (Until You Do It This Way)]] — Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared, [Chord Melody Arranging is Miserable (Until You Do It This Way)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vwpRSw5HP1Y)

- **Call-and-response construction for solo blues chord-melody: state the shell voicing, then answer with a single-note line, repeated bar to bar — plus a ghost-note pseudo-bass and a melody-first priority rule.** Because a moving single-note melody leaves no spare fingers for a full voicing, state a **shell voicing** (root-3rd-7th) first to establish the harmony, then move to an unaccompanied melodic line to answer it — worked bar-by-bar over a 12-bar blues (C7 shell → C6-arpeggio melody with a ghost-note bass fill; F7 shell → F6-arpeggio melody). **Ghost-note/repeated-note pseudo-bass:** with no room for a real walking bass under the solo, repeat the chord's *lowest* voiced note (often the 3rd, not the root) on the beat to imply forward motion — explicitly not a real bass line, just enough movement to keep the groove alive. **Melody-first priority:** work out the melody line first and let it land cleanly on its target note, then add the underlying chord afterward almost as an embellishment — sometimes omittable entirely without hurting the phrase. - **A stretch-triage rule for physically impossible voicings: when a chord shape can't be fully held, sacrifice melody last, harmony second, bass third.** If a stretch forces a note to drop, let a harmony note cut off before the melody does — "let the melody never get cut off and let the harmony sacrifice, don't do the other way around" — and avoid dropping the bass note too if it can be helped; the tune "will still really sound like the song" as long as the melody survives intact.
