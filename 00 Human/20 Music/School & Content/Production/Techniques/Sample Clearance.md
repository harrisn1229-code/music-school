# Sample Clearance

**Not a craft note — a release-blocker note.** [[Sampling and Sound Selection]] covers choosing sounds and flipping them. This covers whether you're allowed to put the result out.

> [!warning] Not legal advice
> This is a working producer's summary of how clearance operates. For an actual release with an actual sample in it, a music attorney or a clearance service is the right call — the cost of asking is far below the cost of not.

## The thing to know: there are two rights

A recorded song is **two separate pieces of intellectual property**, owned by different people, cleared separately.

| Right | What it is | Usually held by |
|---|---|---|
| **Master** | The specific *recording* — the actual audio | The label, or whoever paid for the session |
| **Composition** (publishing) | The underlying *song* — melody, lyrics, chords, structure | The songwriters and their publishers |

**Sampling uses both.** Lifting audio from a record means you're using their recording *and* the song inside it. Two clearances, two negotiations, two sets of fees.

This is the distinction the placement asked about and the one worth internalizing, because it's what makes the alternative below work.

## The one clean exception: public domain

Composition copyright expires **70 years after the composer's death** in most countries — after that, the underlying song needs no clearance at all, from anyone, for any use. This is why so much classical-derived pop material (Pachelbel's Canon in D under Maroon 5's "Memories"; Leoncavallo's *Pagliacci* under Queen's "It's a Hard Life"; Bach's Orchestral Suite No. 3 under Procol Harum's "A Whiter Shade of Pale," see [[Songs Inspired By Bach]]) never triggers a lawsuit even when the melodic lift is note-for-note close — the pieces have been in the public domain for centuries. **The recording itself is still a separate right**: a specific performance/recording of a public-domain piece can still carry its own master-rights clearance requirement even though the composition underneath it is free to use — public domain status applies to the *composition*, never automatically to any given *recording* of it. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[14 Songs That 'Rip Off' Classical Music]]

## Sampling vs interpolation

**Interpolation** is replaying or re-recording the part yourself so it sounds like the original without using the original audio.

- **Sample** → master + publishing. Two rights.
- **Interpolation** → publishing only. **One right.**

That's why interpolation is the standard move on a recognizable hook: it removes the label from the conversation entirely and leaves you negotiating with the publisher. It's usually faster, usually cheaper, and always fewer parties.

It is *not* a loophole. Re-playing a melody is still using the composition, and "I played it myself" is not a defence. What it does is halve the problem.

## Clear before release, never after

The rule with the least ambiguity and the highest cost when broken.

Clearing after a track has traction means negotiating with **no leverage** — the rights holder knows the song is working and prices accordingly. Before release you're one of many requests; after release you're a party who already infringed and needs a fix.

What an uncleared sample can cost:

- **Takedown** from streaming and distribution
- **Frozen royalties** — income accrues but doesn't pay out
- **The whole track's income**, if the settlement is a majority publishing share
- **Delayed release** while it gets sorted, usually at the worst moment

On a recognizable sample, holders routinely ask for a significant publishing share — sometimes a majority — plus a percentage of the master and an upfront fee. That's the trade to weigh *before* building a song around it. ([Symphonic](https://blog.symphonic.com/2026/04/16/how-to-clear-a-sample/), [Tracklib](https://www.tracklib.com/blog/music-clearance))

## The practical routes

Roughly in order of how much friction each carries.

1. **Licensed libraries** — [Tracklib](https://www.tracklib.com) and similar clear both rights up front for a fee and a defined split. Zero negotiation. The catalogue is the constraint, not the paperwork.
2. **Royalty-free / one-stop sample packs** — cleared by definition. Check the licence covers commercial release and not just personal use.
3. **Interpolation** — replay it. One right instead of two.
4. **Replay *and* rewrite** — change enough that it's your composition. There is no bar-count or note-count safe harbour; "how much is enough" is a legal question, not a rule of thumb, and producers who rely on a supposed threshold are the ones who end up settling.
5. **Full clearance** — a clearance service or attorney approaches both sides. Slowest and most expensive, and the only route for a specific famous recording.

## Before you build a song around a sample

- [ ] Do I know what the source recording actually is? (An unidentified sample can't be cleared.)
- [ ] Is it from a licensed library — already handled?
- [ ] Could I interpolate it instead and drop the master side?
- [ ] If this needs full clearance, is the song worth a possible majority publishing split?
- [ ] Am I prepared to clear it **before** release, or to shelve it?

The last one is the real question. A great flip you can't release is a beat, not a song — deciding that early is cheaper than deciding it after the mix.

## What this doesn't cover

Broader music-business material — splits, PRO registration, royalties, distribution, sync — is still unwritten in the vault. Flagged as parked in [[Coverage Review 2026-08-09]] §3 and genuinely live given the releases in `Originals/`.

## From videos

- **The "eighth note" rule, stated directly by a working composer as his own practical reasoning — a concrete quotable threshold, not just "melody matters most."** Steven Schwartz's *Wicked* hides the first SEVEN notes of "Somewhere Over the Rainbow" inside its recurring "Unlimited" theme, capped deliberately at seven and no more, because (his own words) "when you get to the eighth note in a melody, people can come and say you stole our tune." Disguised further via simultaneous rhythm AND harmony change — same 7 pitches, completely different rhythm, moved off the original's conventional I-vi progression onto a single static Lydian-colored suspended chord (A maj9♯11, the IV chord voiced for a magical/ethereal feel) instead of any tonic-functioning harmony. → [[Modal Interchange]] for the Lydian reharmonization technique — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Over The Rainbows melody is hidden in Wicked]]
- **Two production/legal techniques not otherwise on this page.** Vocal-removal-before-AI-stem-separation: Portishead's "Glory Box" samples Isaac Hayes' "Ike's Rap II," which has Hayes SPEAKING over the sampled section — they reconstructed a clean instrumental loop by exploiting the small silent gaps between his spoken phrases across multiple loop rotations, which happened to line up just enough to stitch a full loop together. Interpolation chosen specifically to cut legal exposure: Oasis's "Go Let It Out" drum groove sounds re-performed rather than lifted from its Johnny Jenkins source, likely a deliberate choice to interpolate rather than sample. **Also**: Gnarls Barkley's "Crazy" pitch/tempo-shifts an 8-bar score-cue sample; Black Box's "Ride On Time" got sued over an uncleared vocal sample and reissued with the part re-sung by session singer Heather Small (later M People's lead singer); House of Pain's "Jump Around" stacks three samples in its intro alone, one of them reached via an intermediate track that had already sampled the true original first — a multi-hop chain inside a single song. → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]] for the loop-reconstruction craft — David Bennett Music Theory, [[15 Songs you didn't know sample other songs]]
- **A single-artist case cluster spanning the full spectrum, from openly-admitted-and-unpursued to two actual lawsuits.** Oasis/Noel Gallagher: "Cigarettes and Alcohol" fuses TWO separate riff elements from one source (T. Rex's "Get It On") into a single new riff. "Whatever" vs. Neil Innes' "How Sweet to Be an Idiot" — identical opening melody, chords AND key, resulted in a lawsuit and a songwriting credit for Innes (who got a cheeky retaliatory quote of "Whatever" into his own later track). "She's Electric" note-for-note matches "While My Guitar Gently Weeps" in places, source acknowledged. "Supersonic"'s "My Sweet Lord"-derived guitar solo is described by Gallagher himself as unconscious absorption, not deliberate lifting — distinct from the deliberate-combination cases above. "Half the World Away" vs. Bacharach's "This Guy's in Love with You" (chord-progression-only likeness) was resolved amicably with no suit. **The two that actually cost Oasis money**: "Step Out" vs. Stevie Wonder's "Uptight" forfeited 10% royalties + shared credit and got pulled from its planned album; "Shakermaker" vs. "I'd Like to Teach the World to Sing" (itself originally a rewritten Coca-Cola jingle) drew a direct Coca-Cola lawsuit. → David Bennett Music Theory, [[10 Oasis songs that 'Rip Off' other songs]]
- **The same public-domain source used differently by two different artists, plus a repeated structural device across an artist's own catalog.** Mozart's Requiem in D minor gets sampled from two different movements by two different artists: Julian Casablancas' "Human Sadness" loops a chord progression from the *first* movement (plus a lo-fi excerpt from Piano Sonata No. 16 at the outro), while Evanescence's "Lacrimosa" draws from the famous *third*-movement Lacrimosa instead — same source work, non-overlapping use. **A structural device an artist reused across two different albums**: Alicia Keys opens both *Songs in A Minor* (with Beethoven's "Moonlight Sonata," performed in its original key despite the album title) and *As I Am* (with Chopin's Nocturne No. 20) the same way — an unabridged classical excerpt that's then flipped into something new by the addition of a hip-hop drum beat partway through. Other confirmed borrows: Zayn's "Blue" (Bach's Prelude No. 1 in C major); Deep Purple quoting both Beethoven's 7th Symphony and Tchaikovsky's *Romeo and Juliet* in their "We Can Work It Out" cover; Ritchie Blackmore's own credited Mozart influence on "Highway Star"'s arpeggio section; Neil Diamond's "Song Sung Blue" (Mozart Piano Concerto No. 21); Take That's "Forever Love" (Verdi's Dies Irae); Alicia Keys' "Karma" (Brahms' Violin Concerto in D major); and Brahms' "Wiegenlied" lullaby quoted by both Beyoncé ("Sweet Dreams") and The Crystals ("Santa Claus Is Coming to Town") specifically for its built-in cultural sleep-association. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[13 Songs Based On Classical Music]]
- **The two most-sampled drum breaks in music history, and why a rhythm-only sample sidesteps clearance friction over melody/harmony (though not master-rights clearance itself).** The Amen break (The Winstons, "Amen, Brother," 1969) and the Funky Drummer break (James Brown, 1970) are both isolated drum-break moments (all other instruments dropping out) sampled across an enormous range of songs and genres — because a pure drum recording carries no key/tonality, it doesn't collide with a new song's harmony the way a melodic sample would, even though the underlying master-recording clearance question is identical either way. → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]] for the full reasoning — David Bennett Music Theory, [[18 Songs That Sample Other Songs]]
- **A resolved-by-permission-plus-credit case, and a calibration spectrum from "unlitigated similarity" to "deliberate celebrated quotation."** Dead Can Dance's "Indus" (1996) closely resembles The Beatles' "Within You Without You," reportedly without the band being especially familiar with the source (possibly converged on via shared Indian instrumentation rather than deliberate copying); once noticed, Dead Can Dance contacted George Harrison directly, who granted permission with his publisher requiring a co-songwriter credit — resolved without any lawsuit. **The spectrum, worth keeping as a calibration set**: The Offspring's "Why Don't You Get a Job" vs. Beatles' "Ob-La-Di, Ob-La-Da" (similar melodic contour, different actual scale degrees — 1-2-3 vs. 1-3-5 — never litigated in ~25 years); Radiohead's "Karma Police" vs. Beatles' "Sexy Sadie" (a shared rare chord progression plus a similar piano arpeggio, judged as influence not infringement); Bad Religion's "You" directly quoting "We Can Work It Out" (deliberate, celebrated homage — the opposite end of the spectrum from a hidden or denied borrow). — David Bennett Music Theory, [[6 Songs That Copy Beatles Songs]]
- **The style-vs-melody line, stated precisely, plus the *Blurred Lines* precedent that eroded it.** A musical style is "just a checklist of generic features" — two songs sharing a style/vibe (a chord progression shape, an anticipated rhythm, a general feel) isn't itself evidence of infringement, since those features are shared across an entire genre, not owned by any one song. Melody is the actual load-bearing distinguishing feature in practice: identical chords/style/arrangement (a 12-bar blues vamp) still distinguishes "Johnny B. Goode" from "Hound Dog" purely by melody. The video's central concern: *Marvin Gaye's estate v. Robin Thicke/Pharrell Williams* ("Blurred Lines" vs. "Got to Give It Up") found infringement based on shared vibe/style/aesthetic rather than melody or chords — a precedent argued to make future "sounds vaguely similar" suits easier to win, shifting copyright's practical use away from protecting a composer's actual musical ideas and toward letting rights-holders extract money from any stylistically-adjacent hit. Case study: "Thinking Out Loud" (Ed Sheeran) vs. "Let's Get It On" (Marvin Gaye) — the shared features (anticipated rhythm, similar-not-identical progression, melodies starting on beat 2) are argued to be generic soul-genre features rather than a genuine melodic lift; a stronger, less-litigated candidate for actual influence is offered instead — Van Morrison's "Crazy Love" (predates "Let's Get It On" by 3 years), whose chorus melody is genuinely close when transposed into the same key. **The rhythm-side of the same suit, argued from a different video**: the shared element the Gaye estate pointed to was specifically a *pushed rhythm* (chords anticipating the downbeat) — ruled too generic a device, on its own, to be owned by any one song, since pushed/anticipated rhythms recur across the whole soul and pop catalog. → [[Syncopation and Metric Displacement]] for the anticipation/push device itself and more examples of it — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Ed Sheeran didn't copy Marvin Gaye... he copied a different soul song]], [[What is Syncopation?]]
- **A dispute resolved by adding a co-writer credit rather than going to court**: Netta's "Toy" (Israel's 2018 Eurovision winner) drew attention for resembling The White Stripes' "Seven Nation Army" — after Universal Records/Jack White raised the issue, Jack White was simply added as a credited co-writer rather than the matter escalating to litigation, a lighter-weight resolution path than most of the lawsuit-driven cases catalogued on this page. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[A Beginners Guide to the Eurovision Song Contest]]
- **More interpolation case studies: it isn't limited to melody, and re-recording still requires clearance.** Interpolation can target the accompaniment instead of the vocal hook: Amy Winehouse's "Tears Dry on Their Own" interpolates the *instrumental* of "Ain't No Mountain High Enough" (Marvin Gaye & Tammi Terrell); "Rapper's Delight" (Sugar Hill Gang) raps over a re-recording of Chic's "Good Times" instrumental, not the original audio. **A commonly-misidentified case worth keeping as a calibration example**: "Gangster's Paradise" (Coolio) is widely assumed to sample "Pastime Paradise" (Stevie Wonder), but the instrumentation was entirely re-recorded — interpolation, not sampling, despite sounding nearly identical. **Crediting isn't automatic just because it's a re-recording, not a sample**: Sugar Hill Gang initially didn't credit Chic's Nile Rodgers and Bernard Edwards at all on "Rapper's Delight" — they were added only after Chic threatened legal action, a reminder that interpolation still owes a songwriting credit/clearance even with zero master-rights exposure. → David Bennett Music Theory, [[13 Songs that Interpolate other songs]]
- **A full lawsuit-outcome catalog centered on one throughline: whether a writer gets credited seems to track whether anyone sues, not how big the borrow is.** TLC's "Waterfalls" opens with a line lifted from Paul McCartney's own "Waterfalls" — acknowledged by McCartney, never sued, TLC's writers never credited him. Michael McDonald's "Keep Forgetting" opens with the identical borrow shape from Chuck Jackson's 1962 song of the same name — but here Leiber/Stoller DID end up credited. **A "knowing vs. unknowing" double-lift inside the same song**: Rod Stewart's "Do Ya Think I'm Sexy?" unknowingly lifted its main melody from George Benson's "Taj Mahal" (Stewart's own account: it "resurfaced" from memory; settled, royalties paid) while separately, knowingly, lifting its string line from Bobby Womack's "(If You Want My Love) Put Something Down on It" (Stewart's defense: an arrangement line isn't the same as a melody line — contested, but never actually litigated). **The full Bittersweet Symphony case through its 2019 resolution**: The Verve cleared their "Last Time" sample with Decca but not with former Stones manager Allen Klein, who held the copyright — Klein took 100% royalties and full Jagger/Richards writing credit, even though the sampled string arrangement was actually composed by David Whitaker (uncredited, unpaid); Jagger and Richards voluntarily returned the credit and royalties to Richard Ashcroft in 2019. **A shared generic chord progression producing convergent, not copied, melodies**: Coldplay's "Viva la Vida" was separately accused by Joe Satriani, Cat Stevens, and Creaky Boards — all four songs share the same very common I-V-vi-IV progression, and melodies are written to fit their chords, so independent convergence is the more likely read than any one song copying another (Satriani's suit was dismissed). → [[Chord Progressions]] for the underlying I-V-vi-IV progression referenced in the Viva la Vida case — David Bennett Music Theory, [[9 Songs That 'Rip Off' Other Songs]]
- **Multi-hop sample chains — a hit built on a sample of a song that's itself a sample of an earlier song — plus a production note on slowing a sample changing its pitch.** Jay-Z & T.I.'s "Swagger Like Us" ← M.I.A.'s "Paper Planes" ← The Clash's "Straight to Hell" (1982). Lady Gaga's "Poker Face" ← Boney M.'s "Ma Baker" (1977) ← the traditional folk song "Sina." Jeremih's "Don't Tell 'Em" ← Snap!'s "Rhythm Is a Dancer" (1992) ← Nu Cleus's "Automan" (1984) — Snap! significantly slowed the "Automan" sample, which lowered its pitch as a direct consequence of the slowdown (unless pitch-locked). Kanye West's "Stronger" ← Daft Punk's "Harder, Better, Faster, Stronger" ← Edwin Birdsong's "Cola Bottle Baby" (1979). Worth checking a sample's own lineage before building on it, both for crate-digging leads and for understanding the full clearance chain. → David Bennett Music Theory, [[9 Songs that sample songs that sample other songs]]
- **Film-score borrowing specifically: orchestral chords/intervals/motifs lifted and adapted, not melodies sampled — and the "adapt, never copy verbatim" line drawn as the homage/plagiarism boundary.** John Williams borrows repeatedly from classical sources: Stravinsky's "Rite of Spring" dissonant polychord → *Star Wars*' Tatooine desert cue (near-identical chord, bass note changed); Holst's "Mars" → the Death Star destruction chord (one note changed); Holst's "Neptune" → *Return of the Jedi*'s opening texture; Tchaikovsky's Violin Concerto opening leap (A→F) → Princess Leia's theme; Tchaikovsky's Nutcracker Trepak → *Home Alone*'s frantic cue (deliberately reused for its existing Christmas association); Dvořák's Symphony No. 9 opening semitone motif → the *Jaws* theme's alternating two-note figure; Korngold's *Kings Row* opening phrase → the *Star Wars* main title's opening phrase. The video's own framing, consistent with this note's public-domain-composition guidance above: Williams borrows a specific idea as a starting point in every case, then develops it into distinct new music — never presenting the unaltered original as his own, which is what separates homage from infringement even when the influence is unmistakable to the ear. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[8 John Williams scores based on Classical music]]
- **A "spot the sample" comparison method — pitch-shift both recordings into the same key before comparing — plus more melodic-lift cases with wildly inconsistent legal outcomes.** Bruce Springsteen's "Outlaw Pete" (2009) lifts its main hook melody from Kiss's "I Was Made for Lovin' You" (1979), confirmed by Kiss, who chose not to sue ("we like Springsteen"). Huey Lewis and the News's "I Want a New Drug" (1983) vs. Ray Parker Jr.'s "Ghostbusters" theme is the contrast case that DID end in a settled lawsuit — same "who gets sued" inconsistency this series keeps surfacing. → David Bennett Music Theory, [[8 Songs That 'Rip Off' Other Songs]]
- **Four more literal audio-sample cases (distinct from melodic "rip-offs" — the actual recording is reused), skewing toward rhythmic/instrumental hooks rather than vocal melodies.** The Chi-Lites' "Are You My Woman" (1970) horn riff → Beyoncé & Jay-Z's "Crazy in Love" (2003). Andy Williams' "Can't Get Used to Losing You" (1963) → Beyoncé's "Hold Up" (2016). Stevie Nicks' "Edge of Seventeen" (1982) guitar ostinato → Destiny's Child's "Bootylicious" (2001). Kool & the Gang's 1974 instrumental → Will Smith's "Summertime" (1991). → David Bennett Music Theory, [[11 songs you didn't know sample other songs]]
- **The sample-vs-interpolation line drawn with worked pairs, plus the two ways each side handles credit in practice.** Sample (reuses the original recording): Gotye's "Somebody That I Used to Know" loops the actual first-two-chords guitar recording from Luiz Bonfá's "Seville." Interpolation (re-performs the melody, no original audio used): "Timber" (2013) replays — doesn't sample — Lolo's harmonica riff; Coldplay's "Talk" interpolates Kraftwerk's "Computer Love" synth melody. **A borderline third case worth keeping as a calibration example**: Childish Gambino's "Redbone" doesn't sample Bootsy Collins' "I'd Rather Be With You" either, but changes so much of the original that it reads more like a heavy cover than a clean interpolation — Collins and the original writers are still credited as songwriters on "Redbone," the practical outcome converging with a cleared sample even though the mechanism (re-performance, not audio reuse) is interpolation's. → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]] for the George Martin tape-collage precedent on the same page. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[13 songs you didn't know sample other songs]]
- **A larger classical-borrowing batch (19 songs), mostly straight melodic lifts, with two genuinely multi-hop borrowing chains worth noting.** Chain 1: Chopin's Nocturne in F minor No. 1 → Blonde Redhead's "For the Damaged Coda" (subtly modified melody, same chord progression) → self-sampled by B.o.B for "Both of Us" — a rap song built on a rock song built on a Chopin piece. Chain 2: Nino Rota's *What Is a Youth* (1968 Romeo & Juliet film score, itself written in a deliberate neo-Elizabethan style) → Lana Del Rey's "Old Money" (first two bars lifted, then diverges, meter changed 3/4→4/4) — three full stylistic generations removed from actual Elizabethan music. **Meter changes are common enough in these lifts to note as a default move**: Erik Satie's *Gymnopédie No. 1* (3/4) → Janet Jackson's "Someone to Call My Lover" (melody unaltered but stretched to 4/4, chord progression order also reversed) is a second clean example, alongside Old Money above. **Elvis alone has at least 4 more classical-derived songs** beyond the two covered in an earlier video: "Tonight Is So Right for Love" (Offenbach's Barcarolle), "Today, Tomorrow and Forever" (Liszt's Liebesträume), "It's Now or Never" (the traditional/light-classical Neapolitan song "'O Sole Mio," itself relayed to Elvis via a 1949 Tony Martin cover). **Beethoven's Ode to Joy reused at three different fidelity levels**: near-verbatim (The Seekers' "Emerald City," 1967), lowered-by-one-step + simplified harmony (Bright Eyes' "Road to Joy"), and merely similar melodic contour, not a real quote (The Killers' "Mr. Brightside" guitar break — flagged as likely NOT a genuine borrow despite the resemblance, a useful "how similar is similar enough" calibration case). — David Bennett Music Theory, [[19 Songs Based On Classical Pieces]]
- **More public-domain classical borrows, organized by evoked mood rather than just "which piece."** Wedding-themed quotes: Wagner's Bridal Chorus quoted outright at the 3-minute mark of OutKast's "Miss Jackson" (an earlier adapted version of the same melody also forms the song's piano hook, diverging from the original after the first bar); Mendelssohn's Wedding March quoted in Katy Perry's "Never Worn White" (intro) and 10cc's "Une Nuit à Paris" (worked into the "Honeymoon" section). Funeral/gloom-themed: Chopin's Funeral March opens DJ Shadow's "Six Days" and the Minecraft soundtrack's "Death" cue, and closes out a dragged-out quote in Duke Ellington's "Black and Tan Fantasy." Meter-adapted quote: Rufus Wainwright's "Oh What a World" directly quotes Ravel's Boléro main theme, reworked from 3/4 into the song's 4/4. **A borrowing-of-a-borrowing chain**: Beethoven's 5th Symphony opening → reworked into disco by Walter Murphy as "A Fifth of Beethoven" (1976) → that disco version itself reworked again by Robin Thicke's "When I Get You Alone" (2012) — public-domain borrowing isn't limited to one hop back to the original. → [[Chord Progressions]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[14 Songs Based On Classical Music]]
- **Eight Led Zeppelin cases spanning nearly every outcome on the sampling/interpolation spectrum, from settled-with-full-credit to won-in-court.** Direct lyric/melody lift with a proven paper trail: "Whole Lotta Love" lifted its vocal phrasing/lyrics from Muddy Waters/Willie Dixon's "You Need Love" (via the Small Faces' cover, not the original) — 1985 lawsuit settled out of court, Dixon given an official songwriting credit; Robert Plant later admitted the lift was deliberate ("you only get caught when you're successful"). **Uncredited cover treated as an original**: "Bring It On Home" (Willie Dixon, performed by Sonny Boy Williamson II) — Zeppelin spliced Dixon's song with original material but paid no royalties; sued 1972, settled, now credited solely to Dixon. Same 1972 suit also covered "The Lemon Song," which literally started as a live Zeppelin cover of Howlin' Wolf's "Killing Floor" before diverging lyrically. **Cover evolved into an "original" over a touring cycle**: "Dazed and Confused" began as a Jake Holmes folk song, covered live by The Yardbirds (Page's prior band) before Page carried it into Zeppelin with new vocals/lyrics — Holmes sued in 2010, settled, now credited "Page, inspired by Jake Holmes." **Miscredited public domain**: "Babe I'm Gonna Leave You" — both Joan Baez's and Zeppelin's versions listed the song as "traditional" (public domain); it was actually written by Anne Bredon, who was eventually credited and reimbursed on both versions. **Arrangement lift shielded by public domain status**: "Black Mountain Side" borrowed Bert Jansch's original guitar arrangement of a genuinely traditional tune — Jansch's label considered suing but didn't, since the underlying song itself was public domain even though the specific arrangement wasn't. **Uncontested homage**: "Rock and Roll"'s opening drum fill is a direct lift from Little Richard's "Keep A-Knockin'" — no lawsuit, likely because drum beats are harder to copyright than melody/lyrics, and/or because the borrowing reads as overt homage. **Sued and WON**: "Stairway to Heaven" vs. Spirit's "Taurus" (2014 suit, settled 2020 in Zeppelin's favor) — both use a similar A-minor arpeggiated chord progression on acoustic guitar, but the court ruled a common chord progression, even played similarly, isn't infringement on its own — the same generic-building-blocks defense cited in the Def Leppard/One Direction and Townshend/One Direction cases below. → [[18 Songs That 'Rip Off' Other Hits]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[8 Led Zeppelin Songs That 'Rip Off' Other Songs]]
- **Pre-clearing an interpolation before release rather than after, plus a live plagiarism-vs-influence framing test.** Portugal. The Man's "Feel It Still" re-records (not samples) the melody of "Please Mr. Postman," and the band secured the rights and credited the original writers *before* release — the clean version of the "clear before release, never after" rule above, applied specifically to an interpolation rather than a sample. Companion framing worth keeping: generic building blocks (I-IV-V, common blues turnarounds) aren't ownable regardless of similarity — several cited real-world cases (Def Leppard vs. One Direction, The Who's Pete Townshend vs. One Direction) were both declined on exactly that basis. → [[18 Songs That 'Rip Off' Other Hits]] for the full catalog of borrowed-riff/melody pairs and their legal outcomes. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[18 Songs That 'Rip Off' Other Hits]]
- **Deliberately left a near-quote melodic phrase incomplete, in the room, to dodge an interpolation clearance cost.** Recording an electric guitar lick for "Messy," Jared Solomon landed on a phrase close to the Backstreet Boys' "I Want It That Way" melody; rather than scrap the take, the band kept the near-quote but never played the resolving final word/note ("...way") — explicitly because completing it would mean "you gotta pay them a lot of money." The interpolation stayed a "cool little Easter egg" specifically by staying incomplete: a real-time, in-the-room example of stopping a replay short of the melody's most identifiable ("hook") moment rather than relying on a note-count rule of thumb. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]] — Mix with the Masters (Lola Young, Jared Solomon), [Behind the Track｜'Messy' by Lola Young](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=02QKFlkbmYY)
