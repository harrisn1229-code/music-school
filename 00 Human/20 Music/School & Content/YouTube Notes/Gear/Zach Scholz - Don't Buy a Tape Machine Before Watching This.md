# Zach Scholz - Don't Buy a Tape Machine Before Watching This

## Synopsis

Zach Scholz's buyer's-guide framework for getting into tape, built around the question he says most people skip entirely: figure out what you actually want tape *for* before you go shopping for a machine. He splits buyers into three workflows — the analog enthusiast tracking a full band or album to tape, the home producer using tape purely as a coloring tool on top of a DAW-centric workflow, and the mastering/print engineer chasing a clean, high-fidelity transfer with just a touch of tape sound — and uses that split to frame every tradeoff that follows: channel count vs. track count, the three common tape speeds (3¾/7½/15 ips), reel size and NAB hub adapters, vintage-vs-newer machine condition, and cassette as a legitimate budget/character-forward alternative to reel-to-reel. It closes with three concrete, named budget-tier recommendations ($200/$500/$1000+) tied back to those workflows. Its main value here is as a reference/framework video rather than a mix-settings one — it validates the "home producer" workflow Nate already runs his own reel-to-reel for (tape as a color tool, not a full-tracking rig) and supplies vocabulary — track vs. channel count, ips tradeoffs, NAB hubs — useful for evaluating any future tape gear purchase.

## Techniques

- **Decide your workflow before shopping for a machine: analog enthusiast, home producer, or mastering/print** — the video's central framework, since each implies different gear entirely. Analog enthusiast = tracking a full band, an album, or bouncing an entire session to tape, wanting higher fidelity and more channels for efficiency. Home producer = not replacing the DAW, just using tape as a creative tool (e.g. sending a drum bus to tape for saturation, compression, and character) — doesn't need a complex setup and can get great results cheaper and simpler. Mastering/print = printing finished mixes to tape for a clean, high-fidelity transfer with just a subtle touch of tape sound, prioritizing fidelity over character. No workflow is "better" — they're just different use cases, and knowing which applies determines every decision downstream. → [[Saturation and Distortion]]
- **Nate's own [[Sony TC-630]] is a textbook "home producer" machine here, not an analog-enthusiast tracking rig** — its 2-channel/3-head/7" ~1970 build and role as a pure tape-color processor (guitar/synths run through it and printed back into Ableton, never used to track a whole song) matches this video's "home producer" category almost exactly rather than the "analog enthusiast" one. Its selectable speeds (7½ and 3¾ ips per its own note) sit at the two "warmth/character" speeds this video describes, not the 15 ips this video associates with professional/mastering-grade two-track fidelity.
- **Channel count = how many tracks record to tape simultaneously, and a plain 2-channel machine can still take unlimited DAW tracks — just two at a time** — the catch is that every pass printed to tape and brought back into the computer needs to be re-aligned, since tape isn't as timing-accurate as digital and introduces slight drift on each round trip; not hard to fix, but it adds workflow time. Higher-channel machines (4/8/16 tracks recorded at once) solve that drift problem for full-band tracking or converting a whole mix to analog, since everything recorded simultaneously stays in sync with itself — the tradeoff is a bigger, more expensive machine with more electronics to maintain and calibrate. For most home producers he recommends sticking with a 2-channel machine as the sweet spot — e.g. send a stereo drum bus rather than six individual drum mics and get a result surprisingly close while keeping setup and maintenance a lot simpler.
- **Track count is NOT the same thing as channel count — it's how the machine physically divides the tape width, not how many audio channels you get** — on a four-track machine, ¼" tape is divided into four narrow tracks: a stereo pair records onto tracks one and three moving forward, then flipping the tape over (like flipping a cassette) records onto tracks two and four — still only two audio channels, but you double your recording time by using both directions of the tape. A two-track machine instead uses almost the entire tape width for just two tracks, records in one direction only, and is done once you hit the end of the reel — more physical tape dedicated per channel generally means better signal-to-noise ratio, lower noise, and higher fidelity. Two-track machines were more often built for professional applications, so they tend to support higher tape speeds and cost more. Two-track wins for mastering/cleanest-possible fidelity; four-track shouldn't be overlooked for most home producers — it can still sound fantastic and the extra recording time is a real bonus.
- **Worked example of channel count vs. track count: the TASCAM 388** — an eight-channel recorder that fits all eight individual tracks onto a single ¼" piece of tape, where most eight-track machines instead use ½" tape — the 388 packs its tracks much closer together, which is exactly why checking both channel count AND track format matters when comparing machines rather than assuming one predicts the other.
- **Tape speed (ips) trades fidelity against how fast you burn through tape — the three common speeds are 3¾, 7½, and 15 ips** — 15 ips gives excellent clarity, low noise, and is a popular choice for professional recording, at the cost of burning tape fast. 7½ ips is "right down the middle": sounds really good, gives twice the recording time of 15 ips, and many people feel it has more warmth and personality — a great quality/tape-consumption balance for a lot of home studios. 3¾ ips is where tape's limitations show more — more hiss, less top-end detail, a darker overall sound — but for anyone chasing character over absolute fidelity it can sound fantastic. Most consumer/prosumer decks offer two selectable speeds: a deck topping out at 7½ ips usually also has 3¾, while a machine built for 15 ips often includes 7½ as its lower speed; two-track machines are also more often capable of the higher speeds than four-track machines, reinforcing their higher-fidelity reputation. → [[Saturation and Distortion]]
- **Speed doesn't strictly predict quality — every machine is calibrated differently** — his own Teac recorded almost everything at 3¾ ips and still sounded surprisingly good with all its top-end detail intact, so a slower speed alone shouldn't be assumed to mean poor sound quality.
- **Reel size (7" vs. 10.5") trades recording time — and for him, aesthetics — and compatibility only runs one direction** — a bigger reel holds more tape and runs longer before a reel change; he specifically bought his own Teac to accept 10.5" reels, partly for the longer recording time and partly because he just loves how the big reels look as a centerpiece of the studio. Smaller consumer decks are typically built for 7" reels only; larger prosumer/pro machines often support 10.5". Most machines that accept 10.5" reels can also run 7" reels (often via a switch that keeps tape tension correct for the smaller size), but the reverse isn't true — a 7"-only machine cannot play 10.5" reels at all.
- **10.5" metal reels need NAB hub adapters to mount securely; 7" reels don't** — the adapters aren't particularly expensive but they're essential — without them a 10.5" reel won't mount properly on the machine's spindle, while standard 7" reels slide directly onto the spindle with no adapter required. Worth budgeting for as an accessory alongside any 10.5"-capable machine.
- **Vintage vs. newer machines: most reel-to-reels on the market today are 30-60 years old, so treat any purchase as buying a piece of vintage electronics** — newer machines from the late-80s/90s tend to need less restoration simply for being newer, and often have more modern features. Older machines from the 60s/70s carry real charm (design, simplicity, and in some cases even the sound itself), but expect maintenance needs and harder-to-find replacement parts — that doesn't mean avoid them, since some of the best tape machines ever made came from that era and are still running exceptionally well today. How well a specific unit has been cared for matters more than what decade it came from.
- **Cassette is a legitimate, affordable entry point into analog character, not just a lo-fi compromise** — a stereo cassette deck paired with decent tape can sound surprisingly good; it's noisier than reel-to-reel, but that noise is part of the charm, giving unique textures that work well with the right source material. → [[Saturation and Distortion]]
- **His own budget cassette rig: a $40 TASCAM stereo cassette deck** — bought for just $40, still needs a little work, but already one of his favorite things to send tracks through for color; he's previously produced a full song using this exact deck as an effect (referenced as an earlier video on his channel). He reaches for the cassette deck over reel-to-reel specifically when he wants extra grit and texture — the two formats each have their own character, not a strict hierarchy of one being "better."
- **On a tight budget, look at stereo cassette decks before a Porta Studio** — Porta Studios have gotten popular over the last few years and, because of that, tend to cost noticeably more than they used to, while plenty of good stereo cassette decks are still available well under $100. Cassette tape itself is also far cheaper than reel tape — he paid under $20 for two cassettes from ATR Magnetics, versus a brand-new 10.5" reel that can easily run over $100.
- **$200 budget tier: a stereo cassette deck is the best value** — at that price you can find a nice working deck, buy brand-new tape, and pick up cables while staying inside budget; a reel-to-reel could technically be found for $200 too, but finding one in good working condition at that price takes real patience.
- **$500 budget tier: reel-to-reel becomes practical — target a two-channel, four-track machine, ideally from the '70s or early '80s** — that era has a lot of solid, well-built machines available in this range, and for a first-time tape buyer they offer a strong balance of sound quality, affordability, and relatively simple maintenance.
- **$1000+ budget tier: buy for your workflow, not just your budget** — mastering/highest-fidelity work points toward a quality two-track machine; sending multiple sources to tape simultaneously points toward stepping up in channel count (four, eight, even sixteen channels); for someone mainly adding analog character to a recording (his own case) he names his **Tascam 34** — four channels, capable of 15 ips, has pitch control, and is "incredibly versatile," flexible enough to handle anything he needs. At this budget it's also worth paying more for a machine that's already been serviced, since better condition up front saves money and headaches down the road.
- **Closing philosophy: the best tape machine is the one that fits your workflow, that you can afford, and that actually inspires you to make music** — not necessarily the biggest or most expensive one on the market.
