# Learn The Fretboard - This Is A Great Help For Practicing

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JPQkwomSrpc)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JPQkwomSrpc)

## Synopsis
Jens Larsen interviews David Beebe and Tom Quail, developers of **Solo**, a mic-listening fretboard-training app (newly out on Android after an iOS-only launch), on the occasion of the Android release. This isn't a technique breakdown of a lick or a song — it's a practice-methodology conversation about *how to think about the fretboard at all*, and the single idea driving the whole app is stated directly and repeatedly: train every note you play by its **intervallic function relative to the current chord/scale root** (root, 3rd, 5th, ♭7, 9th…) rather than by its absolute note name or a memorized scale/arpeggio shape. The app listens to the guitar through the mic, presents a chord or scale over a set of real chord changes (built-in standards like Autumn Leaves, or a user's own), and waits for the correct functional note before advancing — root-only first, then full chord tones (1-3-5-7), then randomized orderings of those chord tones, then full chord-scales across a large scale library (pentatonics, major-scale modes, melodic-minor modes, harmonic minor/major, symmetrical scales, blues, bebop passing-tone scales). The most valuable material in the video is arguably not the app pitch itself but the meta-discussion in the second half: Jens's own real-time discovery, prompted by the app's faster/more responsive Android note-detection exposing his true reaction speed, that he does **not** actually think in pure intervallic function while playing — he silently translates to note names first — and the resulting three-way discussion of how guitarists (visual/shape-based), non-visual instrumentalists like saxophonists (necessarily note-name-based), and dual-fluent players actually navigate the note-name ↔ interval-function translation layer, including the practical case for being fluent in **both** directions (a rehearsal request phrased as "put the 9th on top" vs. "put the F♯ on top" should land identically). Also useful: the design rationale for *why* randomizing the order of 1-3-5-7 defeats rote arpeggio-shape-running and forces genuine functional recognition, and for why practicing over a song's actual chord changes (not an isolated static chord) is where the drill becomes valuable rather than academic.

## What's taught
- **Core method: think in intervallic/scale-degree function relative to the current root, not absolute note names or a memorized shape.** Worked example: in C major, ascending from C to G is "1 to 5" (a 5th up); *descending* from C down to the same G is technically a 4th interval by distance, but it's still "1 to 5" functionally — the function (which scale degree a note is) doesn't change with direction, only the physical interval-distance does. This is the app's foundational distinction and the thing it drills relentlessly.
- **Why function transfers across chord quality where absolute pitch doesn't:** the 5th of a chord looks and sounds the same functional role whether the chord is major 7, minor 7, or dominant 7 — same with the ♭7. Learning "where is the 5th" once, generically, covers every chord quality at once, instead of separately memorizing "the 5th of Cmaj7" and "the 5th of Cm7" as unrelated facts.
- **App structure, level by level (a genuine practice-ladder, not just a feature list):** (1) find the **root only**, over a set of real chord changes (e.g. Autumn Leaves' first ii–V, Dm7 → G7); (2) find **individual chord tones** in sequence — root, then 3rd, then 5th, then 7th, one at a time, per chord; (3) find the **full 1-3-5-7 arpeggio in order** over the changes; (4) **randomize the order** the app asks for chord tones in (e.g. 1-5-♭3-♭7 instead of always 1-3-5-7) — this is the level the developers single out as "where it gets really powerful," because running a fixed 1-3-5-7 sequence lets a player fall back on a memorized arpeggio *shape* without actually parsing each note's function, while a randomized order forces genuine per-note functional identification; (5) **full chord-scales** (every scale degree, not just the four chord tones) over the same changes.
- **No metronome, no backing track, no time pressure in the drill itself** — the app waits indefinitely for the correct note before advancing, explicitly framed as removing the "cerebral overload" of simultaneously tracking harmony, physical position, AND time, so the player can build pure fretboard-to-function mapping first and add tempo/context later. *(General transferable practice principle beyond this specific app: decouple "can I find it at all" from "can I find it in time" as two separate practice stages.)*
- **Practice constrained to a sub-zone of the neck** (first five frets only, or a limited set of strings) is called out as a standard, deliberately-supported guitar practice technique within the app's level structure — narrowing the physical search space before expanding it, the same principle behind this vault's [[Guitar Positions]] position-by-position practice model.
- **Practicing over a song's real chord changes, not an isolated static chord, is called out by both developers as the highest-value use** — one developer's stated habit is explicitly assigning this as homework to his own students: take whatever song/progression you're currently working on, and drill root/chord-tone/scale-degree finding directly against *that* song's changes rather than a generic exercise, closing the gap between "I can find the 3rd of a Dm7 in isolation" and "I can find the 3rd of the ii chord inside the tune I'm actually learning."
- **Extensive scale library beyond chord tones** — the "full chord scale" level covers pentatonic scales, all seven major-scale (diatonic) modes, all seven melodic-minor modes, harmonic minor, harmonic major, symmetrical scales (diminished/whole-tone family), the blues scale, and bebop passing-note scales — described as "basically all the scales you'd use in a standard jazz or modern contemporary improvisational context."
- **Non-guitar use case, named directly:** because the app listens via microphone rather than requiring a MIDI/fretted instrument, a saxophonist (or any non-fretted player) can use it too — but their translation path runs the *opposite* direction from a guitarist's, since a horn has no fixed visual shape per interval the way a fretted instrument does; they're necessarily thinking in note names first and translating to function, the mirror image of how a shape-based guitarist can think in function first and translate to note names. Namechecked as the same underlying skill trained by the **Coltrane method** and **Jerry Bergonzi's** "melodic structures" approach (1-2-3-5 cells etc.) — both non-guitar-specific systems for drilling the identical note-name↔function translation.
- **The central meta-insight, discovered live on camera:** Jens had assumed he could "think in both" note names and interval function interchangeably and didn't have a strong preference — until the Android version's faster, more responsive note-detection exposed his actual real-time processing speed, revealing he was *always* silently translating to note names first and was never truly thinking in raw function; the slower iOS detection had been masking this by giving him enough slack to keep up either way. **The practical lesson: your own sense of "I can do both equally well" may not survive being timed accurately — a tool with faster/tighter feedback than your current practice setup can reveal a translation bottleneck you didn't know you had.**
- **Two simultaneous "data points" on every note, framed as the actual skill (not an either/or):** when both fluent, a musician holding a note in mind knows it *both* as a scale degree relative to the key **and** as a chord-tone/function relative to the current chord sounding underneath it — described as needing to "know it both ways" rather than picking one system and discarding the other. This directly parallels [[Scale Degree Ear Training]]'s distinction between **key-relative hearing** (scale degree relative to the tonic) and **chord-relative hearing** (scale degree relative to the current chord) — the app trains the same two-layer skill, physically/visually on the fretboard via mic-listening, rather than aurally against a drone.
- **Practical case for genuine bidirectional fluency between note names and interval numbers, with a concrete rehearsal example:** if a bandleader asks a pianist to "put the 9th on top of that voicing" versus "put the F♯ on top" (same note, same chord — an E minor 9), a fluent musician shouldn't blink at either phrasing; that dual fluency (not a preference for one system) is the actually useful professional skill, and it's exactly what deliberately working both the note-name path and the function path (rather than only one) builds.
- **A second player's contrasting starting point, offered as evidence the "right" starting system varies by background:** the other developer describes coming up through classical guitar and note-reading first (not scale shapes), inventing his own first scale (an E minor pentatonic he built without being taught the CAGED-adjacent five-position system), and only later developing a strong visual/shape bias once he started deliberately cataloging interval shapes across the neck — roughly 2–3 years of pure note-name-based scale work, followed by ~18 months of active internal resistance before shifting toward function/shape-based thinking, described as a genuinely difficult mental transition after investing heavily in the other system. **Takeaway for practice planning: expect real resistance if you're deliberately retraining an already-strong note-name (or shape) habit toward the other system — it's a slow transition even when the new system proves more efficient once installed.**
- **Randomized permutation practice generalizes beyond "1-3-5-7 vs random order"** — the app explicitly supports selecting and drilling a *specific* fixed permutation in isolation (e.g. always 1-3-5-7, or always 5-7-3-1) and running that one ordering across the entire set of changes, which is offered as a way to build a specific inversion/voice-leading pattern deliberately rather than only randomizing for difficulty.
