# Chord Substitution

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Jazz Harmony]]. Replacing a chord with a different one that does the same job. This is the engine of variety in jazz harmony, and it follows rules rather than taste.

Bowman's §D is the vault's source for the rules; the worked examples here are original.

## The governing rule

> **Two chords can substitute for each other when they share enough tones and the same harmonic function.**

Function first, tones second. A substitute that shares three notes but resolves the wrong way is a worse choice than one sharing two notes that resolves right. Functions are tonic, subdominant and dominant — see [[Chord Progressions]] § *Harmonic function*.

---

## 1. Diatonic substitution — chords a third apart

Any two diatonic chords a **third apart share two of their three triad tones**, so they can often stand in for each other.

In C major:

| Original | Substitute | Shared tones |
|---|---|---|
| C (C E G) | Am (A C E) | C, E |
| C (C E G) | Em (E G B) | E, G |
| F (F A C) | Dm (D F A) | F, A |
| G (G B D) | Em (E G B) | G, B |

**The classic application:** replace I with vi or iii to keep a loop moving without changing where it sits harmonically. `Cmaj7 Cmaj7 Fmaj7 G7` becomes `Cmaj7 Am7 Fmaj7 G7` — the I–vi–IV–V that half of popular music runs on.

**Caution:** vi is the strongest I-substitute, iii the weakest. iii shares E and G with C but *lacks the root and contains B*, which drags it toward dominant function. Substituting iii for I at a final cadence usually sounds unresolved — which is sometimes the point.

## 2. Tritone substitution — the important one

**Replace any dominant 7th with the dominant 7th a tritone away.**

The reason it works is exact rather than approximate. Take G7 and D♭7:

- **G7** = G B D F
- **D♭7** = D♭ F A♭ C♭

The **B and F of G7 are a tritone apart** — that pair is what makes it a dominant chord. D♭7 contains **F and C♭**, and C♭ is enharmonically B. So *the two chords share the same tritone*, with the 3rd and 7th swapping roles: G7's 3rd (B) is D♭7's 7th (C♭), and G7's 7th (F) is D♭7's 3rd.

Same tension, same resolution, different bass note. → [[FND-C01 — Enharmonic equivalents]]

**What it buys you:** chromatic bass motion. `Dm7 G7 Cmaj7` has roots D → G → C. Substitute and you get `Dm7 D♭7 Cmaj7` — roots D → D♭ → C, walking down by half-step. That descending chromatic bass is one of the most recognisable sounds in jazz and neo-soul. → [[Chromatic Bass Lines]]

**Rules of thumb:**

- Only substitute **dominant** chords. The tritone is what's being preserved, and only dominant 7ths have one.
- The substitute is always a **half-step above the target of resolution.** Resolving to C? The sub is D♭7.
- It pairs naturally with the ♯11: D♭7♯11 is the fullest-sounding version, because the ♯11 of D♭ is G — the root of the chord being replaced.
- Substituting the V of a ii–V–I gives you the extremely common `IIm7 – ♭II7 – Imaj7`.

## 3. Backdoor substitution

**Approach the tonic from ♭VII7 instead of V7.** In C major: `Fm7 B♭7 Cmaj7` in place of `Dm7 G7 Cmaj7`.

B♭7 = B♭ D F A♭. It contains **A♭, the ♭6 of C**, which resolves down to G, and **D**, which is already in the tonic. It's borrowed from C minor — a plagal-flavoured, softer arrival than a real V7.

**When to use it:** when V7 sounds too final or too bright. The backdoor is warmer and more ambiguous, which is why it shows up constantly in gospel, soul and Steely Dan-adjacent writing. → [[Borrowed Chords]] · [[Parallel Minor]]

## 4. Interpolating a ii in front of any dominant

Any dominant 7th can be preceded by **its own related ii chord** — the m7 a fourth below it. This doesn't replace a chord so much as expand one.

`Cmaj7 A7 Dm7 G7` becomes `Cmaj7 Em7 A7 Dm7 G7` — the Em7 is A7's ii.

This is how a sparse progression becomes a dense one without changing its harmonic path at all, and it's the single most useful reharmonization move. → [[Reharmonization]]

## 5. Coltrane changes — worth recognising, rarely worth writing

Substituting a chain of ii–Vs moving in **major thirds** (three key centres dividing the octave evenly), as on *Giant Steps*. Named here so it's recognisable on a chart; it's a virtuoso device and almost never the right answer for the writing this vault serves.

## Choosing between them

| You want | Reach for |
|---|---|
| Keep the loop harmonically still but add motion | Diatonic sub (I → vi) |
| Chromatic descending bass | Tritone sub |
| A softer, less final arrival | Backdoor |
| More chords without changing the path | Interpolate a ii |
| A brighter, more urgent push to a chord | Secondary dominant → [[Secondary Dominants]] |

**The failure mode to avoid:** substituting everything. Substitution reads as sophisticated against a background of plain harmony; wall-to-wall, it reads as directionless. Bowman's own framing is that substitutions must be *"completely compatible"* with what they replace — the test is whether the melody still fits, and a substitution that clashes with the tune is wrong no matter how good the voice leading is.

- **The "Sonny Stitt/Don Byas" dominant-cycle substitution over the first four bars of [[Rhythm Changes]] — extend an existing cycle of fourths two bars earlier than the harmony calls for one, after converting every chord to a dominant 7th.** The first four bars are normally two turnarounds, **1-6-2-5 (bars 1–2) then 3-6-2-5 (bars 3–4)**. Step 1: change the quality of every chord in both turnarounds to a **dominant 7th** — dominant 7ths tolerate reharmonization better than any other quality ("the most flexible harmonic extension you can add to a chord"). Step 2: the bar-3–4 stretch is already a cycle of fourths resolving into D7; **start that same cycle two bars earlier, in bar 1**, replacing the expected B♭7 with **F♯7 → B7 → E7 → A7 → D7**, each chord resolving up a fourth into the next, landing on the D7 that already belongs in bar 3. F♯7 shares no obvious tones or key relationship with B♭ — the substitution works anyway because **both the original and substitute progressions are destination-oriented**, converging on the identical D7 (bar 3) and, further out, the identical B♭ (bar 5); a listener tracks where the line is heading, not each individual chord along the way. Distinct from this page's §4 ii-interpolation (which adds a ii in front of an existing dominant without displacing the harmonic rhythm) — this is a full extended dominant chain that starts earlier than the actual harmony and only rejoins it two bars in. **Practical deployment note:** usable on the bandstand without briefing the rest of the group first, but reliably readable only at a **fast tempo** (the ear tracks melodic shape/destination rather than parsing each reharmonized chord) and with a **confident, strong melodic line** carrying it — a tentative line invites the substitution to read as a mistake.
