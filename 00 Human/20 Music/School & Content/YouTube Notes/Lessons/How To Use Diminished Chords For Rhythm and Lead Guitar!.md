# How To Use Diminished Chords For Rhythm and Lead Guitar!

## Synopsis
Jack Ruch expands a shorts-length idea into a full lesson: how to substitute a diminished-seventh sound over any functioning dominant chord, worked in the context of a real tune — Stevie Wonder's **"Isn't She Lovely"** (key of E). The verse progression (C♯m – F♯7 – A/B – E) sneaks in an A♭7/G♯7 secondary dominant on the turnaround back to C♯m, and the chorus (Amaj7 – A♭7 – C♯m) repeats the same move — giving him a recurring real-song spot to demonstrate the substitution live. He first explains *why* diminished works over dominant chords (a dominant 7 chord already contains a diminished triad; complete it to a full dim7 by adding the ♭9), then teaches two movable rhythm-guitar dim7 grips (a 4-lowest-string voicing and a top-4-string voicing, both sliding in minor thirds), then a single one-octave, four-string dim7 arpeggio shape for lead playing that's fully symmetric — the same fingering repeats every three frets/minor third, so one shape covers the whole neck. The back half of the lesson is entirely about *resolution*: the dim7/altered-dominant sound is deliberately unstable and exists only to voice-lead into the target minor chord, so every worked example is really "here's the tension shape, and here's how I find my way to the nearest C♯m." He closes by playing a full solo over the backing track, cycling the same arpeggio shape through all four of its neck positions (F♯, A, C, D♯) across four passes of the progression, and generalizes the technique beyond this one song to *any* functioning dominant — a secondary dominant like this one, or a plain blues V7 (e.g. B7 in a blues in E).

## What's taught
- **Why diminished substitutes work over dominant chords, harmonic derivation.** A dominant 7 chord is built from two overlapping triads: G♯7 = a G♯ major triad (G♯–C–D♯) plus a C diminished triad (C–D♯–F♯) sharing the C–D♯ interval. Complete the diminished cycle by adding a note a half step above the root (A over G♯ = the ♭9/♭2) and the result is a fully symmetric diminished-seventh chord, stackable and movable anywhere on the neck. This is the same "dominant 7♭9 = rootless dim7 with an added root a major 3rd below" relationship already documented on [[Diminished Chords]] — this video derives it the other direction, building UP from the dominant chord's own triads rather than starting from the dim7 shape.
- **Two go-to rhythm-guitar dim7 voicings, both sliding in minor thirds (every 3 frets = the next inversion of the same notes).**
  - **Grip 1 — lower/mid strings:** notes **C, F♯, A, D♯**. Sliding it up 3 frets inverts to **D♯, A, C, F♯** (same four notes, reordered), up another 3 frets gives the next inversion, and a third 3-fret slide returns to the starting voicing — four positions, one fingering, covering 9 frets of neck.
  - **Grip 2 — top four strings:** the same voicing/note set, refingered because of the B string's irregular tuning; also slides cleanly in minor thirds.
  - **Why it's useful for rhythm:** because these grips recur constantly and move in a fixed 3-fret interval, they open up substitution options anywhere the chorus's A♭7/G♯7 lands, plus give a ready-made device for fills — slide the shape around under a held chord for rhythmic movement without changing the harmony's function. Named as characteristic of **R&B and gospel rhythm playing**, and a device **Joe Pass** uses constantly in jazz comping.
- **Worked substitution over the song's actual turnaround:** chorus is Amaj7 – A♭7(G♯7) – C♯m; any of the movable dim7 grips can drop in on the A♭7/G♯7 beat as a direct substitute for the plain dominant voicing, and the same shape can be sled around during that beat for extra rhythmic motion.
- **Lead: one-octave, 4-string movable dim7 arpeggio shape.** Because the chord is symmetric, one fingering — starting on F♯: **F♯ – A – C – D♯ – F♯ – A** — repeats identically every minor third (3 frets), giving **four usable neck positions from one shape**: rooted on **F♯, A, C, and D♯**.
- **Framing the sound correctly — it's an altered dominant, not a "chord to hang on."** Playing this shape over the G♯7 spot is functionally playing a **G♯7♭9** (a dissonant altered dominant) — its whole job is to build tension and voice-lead into C♯m, so soloing with it is about *briefly* landing in the shape then resolving out, not sustaining it.
- **The core lead skill is resolution, not the shape itself — "figure out how to resolve it" to the nearest chord tone of the target minor chord.** Worked repeatedly from each of the four positions: from the F♯ position, find the nearest C♯m tone and land on it to close the phrase; the same resolve-to-nearest-target logic is repeated from the A, C, and D♯ positions. Practical framing: keep the arpeggio shape dead simple at first (just the plain up shape in one position) and put all the practice effort into the resolution, not variations on the shape.
- **Performance approach used in the demo solo — rotate positions on repeat.** Across the first four times the progression's A♭7/G♯7 spot comes around, he plays a lick starting from a different one of the four arpeggio positions each pass (F♯ → A → C → D♯ in order) — a simple, reusable way to keep repeated dominant hits in a form from sounding identical: cycle the four movable positions of the same shape rather than reusing one lick.
- **Generalizes beyond this song — works over any functioning dominant chord, not just this secondary dominant.** Explicitly extended to (1) any secondary dominant in any tune, and (2) the plain V7 chord of a key — worked example: a blues in E, using this diminished substitution over the B7 (V7) before it resolves back to the I.
- **Song context (specific to "Isn't She Lovely," not a full chord chart):** key of E. Verse: C♯m – F♯7 – A/B (A triad over a B bass note) – E, with an A♭7/G♯7 secondary dominant sneaking in on the way back to C♯m — functions as V7/C♯m (V of ii, same mechanism documented on [[Secondary Dominants]]). Chorus: Amaj7 – A♭7(G♯7) – C♯m, the same secondary-dominant turnaround restated.
