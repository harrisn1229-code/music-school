# Inside World's Rarest Fender Rhodes

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BHiRZ0SWXLU)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BHiRZ0SWXLU)

## Synopsis

Ben Bove (retrorentals.net) shows off an extremely rare Fender Rhodes dealer-demonstration unit — a working one-key cutaway model originally used by Harold Rhodes and colleagues to train CBS/Fender dealers on how the instrument's action and pickup system actually work, since a normal Rhodes hides all of it under the harp/tolex. Genuinely useful mechanism explanation for anyone recording, tuning, or reasoning about Rhodes tone (real or modeled).

## Techniques

- **The key/hammer/damper linkage**: a Rhodes key is a seesaw — pressing it down lifts the hammer to strike the tine while simultaneously pulling a "bridle strap" that releases the damper felt so the tine can ring. One orchestrated mechanical linkage, no separate escapement — the hammer can hyperextend past the strike point if the tine's tip isn't physically there to stop it.
- **The pickup's V-curved profile is the real basis of "Bark"**: as the tine's position moves toward the center of that curve, the tone reads more nasally/overtone-rich. Adjusting that position for more bark also changes damper tension on real hardware, since one mechanical assembly governs both — everything is interrelated in a way a plugin's single Bark knob simplifies.
- **Tines are deliberately UNEQUAL-leg tuning forks**: a true symmetric tuning fork rings almost indefinitely, organ-like, with no natural decay; the asymmetry is what introduces a piano-like decay curve instead of a drone.
- **The tone-bar assembly is spring-mounted on isolation grommets, decoupled from the wood body** — so vibration doesn't get absorbed/damped by the wood, and so each note stays acoustically independent rather than the cabinet homogenizing every note together.
- **Why Rhodes needs individual per-note pickups, unlike a Wurlitzer's shared comb pickup**: a Wurlitzer reed is flat and moves in one plane; a Rhodes tine is round, and as it rings longer its oscillation becomes genuinely circular — which is why each tine needs its own separate, position-adjustable pickup rather than a shared comb (a circularly-oscillating tine in a shared comb would physically clang into its neighbors).
- **Tuning and temperature**: tines sit on a pressure-fit spring, tuned by sliding forward/back — mechanically very stable and not humidity-sensitive, but genuinely temperature-sensitive since the tine is metal (a cold Rhodes can read measurably flat and self-correct as it warms).
- **Hammer-tip material history**: early Rhodes used felt hammer tips like an acoustic piano, but a Rhodes hammer strikes a single tine at one point (vs. 2-3 strings spreading wear on a real piano), so felt would groove and degrade, requiring periodic hand-reshaping. The later switch to neoprene/rubber tips traded a slightly different tone for far better mass-production reliability.
- **A defense of CBS-era Rhodes production**: there were no full-size, mass-produced Rhodes pianos before the CBS era at all — CBS-era units catch flak for cost-cutting, but their genuine mass production is exactly why real Rhodes pianos remain findable and affordable today, unlike rarer boutique-era electric pianos.
