# Star Wars Harmony: 3 Hidden Techniques in John Williams' Music

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BfGGV4ztDGs)

## Synopsis

Max Konyi transcribes three harmonic moments from John Williams' original Star Wars (Episode IV: A New Hope) score entirely by ear, then breaks down what makes each one work: (1) genuinely dissonant polychords (two full unrelated triads stacked, not slash chords) held together by shared common tones, from the "camera pans into space" cue; (2) a long withheld-tonic walking-chord passage building anticipation into the Throne Room theme's main melody, including a five-of-two chord voiced with an unexpected chromatic top note; (3) parallel-planing major triads over a static bass pedal in the Rebel Fanfare, where the bass moves by a DIFFERENT interval than the chords above it when the whole progression transposes — creating a persistent, deliberate mismatch. ## What's taught

- **Polychords = two full triads (or 7th chords) stacked, NOT a slash chord.** A slash chord (C/B♭) is one triad over a foreign bass note; a polychord is two COMPLETE triads sounding together, deliberately not meant to mesh. Three examples from the "into space" cue: (1) C♯ major (spread triad) in the bass with E augmented on top — conflicting E natural (from the C♯ major) against E♯/F (from the E augmented) inside the same chord; (2) A major on the bottom, A minor on top (shared root, conflicting 3rds); (3) E augmented on the bottom, C major on top. **What keeps a polychord from reading as pure random noise: the triads share 1–2 common tones** (here, C and E recur across all three examples) — that shared anchor gives the ear something coherent to hold onto even while the rest clashes. Practical workflow described: pick the shared tone(s) first, then freely try triads above/below by ear until something feels "wrong" in the right way — no derivation from theory required.
- **The Throne Room theme's walking-chord build.** In sharp contrast to the polychord section — this passage is fully diatonic, functional, and tonal. A series of "walking" chords under a soaring melody stays understandable throughout, until a few chromatic notes creep in near the end, building into a crescendo that sits on a pedal 5 in the bass, resolving into the main theme. Key observation: the **tonic chord (D♭, in this cue) only appears once, right at the very beginning, in root position — and then never returns until the next section's main theme begins.** The whole walking-chord passage hits vi, iii, and various other diatonic chords, hinting at and pulling away from the tonic repeatedly, without ever landing back on root-position I. The chromatic notes that eventually creep in are compared to a drum fill destabilizing a steady groove — signaling "something's about to happen" without yet resolving.
  - First non-diatonic chord: a **V/ii (secondary dominant of the two chord)** — a B♭7 — voiced with an E♭ on top (a ♭9 against the B♭7's third, D), giving it real bite before it resolves nicely into the ii chord.
  - Next: a **G♭ minor-major-7 in first inversion** (containing a B𝄫, enharmonically A) resolving via descending half-step bass motion into an A♭ major triad in second inversion (the I chord, inverted) — a IVmM7(1st inv) → I(2nd inv) move; a centuries-old harmonic gesture, but the specific minor-major-7 voicing with the half-step bass descent is unusually rich.
  - Then: a **B𝄫 major7 in third inversion** (a chord ridiculous to spell/say out loud — effectively "A major with its 7th in the bass," or thought more simply as "the 5 of the key in the bass, but play a major triad a half step higher than expected") moving into a suspended dominant (5-7-sus / 5-9-sus) and finally the plain dominant 5-7, which then leads into the main theme.
- **Parallel planing over a mismatched bass pedal — the Rebel Fanfare.** "Planing" = taking a chord shape and moving it exactly in parallel without conforming to the key underneath. Here, major triads plane over a static droning bass pedal (C), tonally implying the key of D♭ even though C itself functionally reads as the 7th of D♭ (a relationship Konyi says isn't really the point — the effect is what matters, not the technical tonal label). Heard as ♭3-1-♭3-1-♭5-4-♭5-4-♭3-1 relative to D♭, feels natural and resolved despite the bass clash, because it comes out of mixed-mode/modal-interchange thinking even though it's not strictly "correct" against the bass. When the whole chord pattern transposes UP A MINOR 3RD (from D♭ to the key of E, functionally F♭), it still sounds internally tonal on its own — BUT the bass does NOT also move up a minor 3rd to preserve the same relationship; it moves up only a HALF STEP (C → C♯). That mismatch — chords say "minor 3rd key change," bass says "half step" — is what generates the bizarre, unsettling color; a matched transposition (bass also up a minor 3rd) would sound conventional and lose the effect entirely. On the final chords, the bass moves again, this time down to a B♭, not following any consistent transposition interval from the previous move. Execution note repeated throughout: the exact transposition amount and the exact bass note at each step are chosen **by ear/feel case by case**, not derived from a fixed rule mechanically reapplied — the conceptual idea (planing triads over a mismatched pedal) is deliberate and theoretical, but the specific intervals used are chosen by ear.
