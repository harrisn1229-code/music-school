## What's taught

**Basics**
- The pentatonic scale is literally a five-note scale; in this lesson it's presented as C D E G A (C major pentatonic), and guitarists commonly play "the minor version" of the identical five notes by starting on A instead of C (A minor pentatonic) — same notes, different felt tonic.

**Positions and pattern practice**
- Learn the scale in as many movable box positions as possible across the neck — boxes can start on any of the five scale tones (C, D, E, G, or A).
- Skip-note practice: run the scale pattern skipping every other note — both a familiarization drill and a source of ready-made melodic patterns.
- Combine approaches: mix straight runs with the skip-note pattern; ascend one box shape then descend the next rather than staying in one position; skip strings (not just scale tones) for a third variation.
- Full detail and cross-links → [[Pentatonic Superimposition and Side-Slipping]] §Foundations.

**Chord shapes from the scale**
- Stack the pentatonic scale's own tones in "thirds" (three scale-degrees up, not three chromatic half steps) from a chosen starting note to build a chord that's entirely pentatonic-scale-derived; slide the same shape through all five scale degrees for a family of usable voicings.
- A second, independently-shaped voicing works the same way, giving a second family of chord colors from the same five notes.
- Arpeggiate the shapes rather than only using them as block chords — turns the voicings into melodic-line material too.
- Full derivation and cross-link to the quartal (stacked-4ths) sibling technique → [[Pentatonic Scale Harmonized in Thirds]].

**Playing one pentatonic scale over unrelated chords**
- Method: compare the pentatonic scale's five notes against a target chord's bass note/root and judge by ear whether it works, rather than deriving the substitution from a memorized mode chart first.
- Worked from A minor pentatonic over six different bass notes/chords: A minor (home), D, B♭ major 7♯11 (yields pretty extensions), G♭ altered (yields altered-dominant color notes), A7 (works, with a tolerable 4th/avoid-note caveat), and C7.
- Full write-up, the generalizable diagnostic, and worked chord-tone reasoning → [[Pentatonic Superimposition and Side-Slipping]] §1.

**Mixing two pentatonic scales**
- Play a minor pentatonic scale built from the 5th of whatever minor chord you're on, alongside the chord-tone pentatonic (e.g. A minor pentatonic + D minor pentatonic over a D minor 7 vamp) — either as two separate shapes in different positions, or blended within one shared position for subtlety.
- Alternate between two pentatonic scales a whole step apart (A minor pentatonic ↔ B minor pentatonic) over a static minor vamp — named explicitly as a fit for modal-vamp tunes like *So What* (Miles Davis).
- Full write-up and cross-link to the *So What* quartal-voicing case study on [[Quartal Chord Voicings]] → [[Pentatonic Superimposition and Side-Slipping]] §2.

**Side-slipping**
- Slide the whole pentatonic scale up a half step, then resolve back down a half step to the original scale — worked as A minor pentatonic → B♭ pentatonic (functioning as a substitute for the V chord) → back to A minor pentatonic.
- The half-step-up-then-back round trip is what makes it a "slip" rather than a key change; the deliberate tension of the substituted scale resolves on the step back down.
- Full write-up → [[Pentatonic Superimposition and Side-Slipping]] §3.
