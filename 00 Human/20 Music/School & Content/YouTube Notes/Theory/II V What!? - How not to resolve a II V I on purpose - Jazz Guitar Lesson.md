# II V What!? - How not to resolve a II V I on purpose - Jazz Guitar Lesson

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yQSJalsXaRs)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yQSJalsXaRs)

## Synopsis

Jens Larsen catalogs five distinct ways to **suspend the resolution of a major ii–V–I** (worked throughout in C: Dm7 → G7 → Cmaj7) instead of just landing on the tonic — the whole video is variations on "the cadence wants to resolve, so build tension by choosing NOT to (or by resolving somewhere unexpected) and use that expectation against the listener." Each device gets a scale/arpeggio choice for soloing over it, a note on when it also works for comping/arranging (which melody notes it tolerates), and — critically — named jazz standards where the device already appears written into real tunes, not just improvised. The five: (1) a diminished chord suspending the tonic (Barry Harris's "tonic diminished"), (2) the borrowed iv-minor chord as the "other" interesting sub-dominant, (3) Cmaj7♯5 as a suspended tonic, (4) an altered dominant sustained straight through the bar line with no resolution at all, and (5) Larsen's own device — hearing the Dm7–G7 as a iv-minor cadence *in A major* and resolving there instead of C, playing clean A major lines over what the bass still calls a C chord. ## What's taught

- **1. Tonic diminished — an F♯dim(/C) suspending the tonic, Barry Harris's "tonic diminished."** Straight cadence Dm7 → G7, then instead of landing on Cmaj7, land on **F♯ diminished with C in the bass** for a bar before resolving — what Barry Harris calls "tonic diminished" (not "sub-dominant diminished," despite F♯ being a sub-dominant-family root) because functionally it's suspending the tonic's own arrival. **Scale for soloing: E harmonic minor** — the closest relative to C major that contains this exact diminished chord; practical target-note trick: think a **B7** sound over it and resolve to a strong note in **E minor** (E, G, B — all land as useful, consonant tones on the following Cmaj7). **Melody-note trigger for using it when harmonizing/arranging a tune (not just soloing):** works when the melody has the **major 7th** of the target chord (B, in C) — cited example: the bridge of **"I Love Paris"** moves through this chord to a B before landing on Fmaj7 — or the **9th** — cited example: **"Stella by Starlight."** **For comping behind a soloist/singer:** safe and commonly used (Larsen: "one that I use quite often"), but only when NOT also playing the melody yourself at the same time — comping this under someone else's melody requires trusting you know where their line is going. → [[Diminished Chords]], [[The ii-V-I]] § *When the I doesn't arrive*
- **1b. A related color: the tritone-relation major triad over the tonic ("Misty" voicing).** A different flavor of the same suspended-tonic-diminished family, named as sounding especially good on **"Misty"**: a **B major triad over a C bass** (a tritone-related major triad, i.e. the F♯dim's own upper-structure color reframed as a plain triad) resolving into Cmaj7. Framed explicitly with the rest of §1 as *optional* to resolve — "you don't have to [resolve it back to C]... you can also display them as a sound that you just substitute for the C." → [[Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords]], [[Chord Substitution]]
- **2. Borrowed iv-minor (F minor) as the "other" interesting sub-dominant.** The second of "the two interesting versions of an F major chord in C major" (alongside §1's F♯dim). Play a plain **F minor triad over a C bass**, resolving to Cmaj7 — soloing concept: **F minor6 / B♭7** (an F melodic-minor-type sound over it), kept deliberately simple since the chord choice itself is already distinct enough not to need a busy line. **Bass-note variant:** playing it over a C bass (Larsen's default, assuming the bassist won't predict the move) reads as an Fm/C polychord-type suspension; playing it over an **A♭ bass instead gives the plainer "borrowed iv" (Fm6) sound** — i.e. C major briefly borrowing from **C minor**, the vault's own documented "workhorse" iv chord. **Melody-note tolerance for comping/arranging:** the root, 3rd, and 5th of the target chord all work fine over it, and the 9th "probably" works too — **only the 5th is flagged as a real problem** for the sharp-4/diminished versions elsewhere on this page, but for the plain iv-minor sound root/3rd/5th/9th are all listed as usable. Also works well as a **passing chord in comping** — "a nice way to add some passing chords... and just change the sound in a ballad for a little bit" (with the caveat: don't surprise whoever you're comping for). **Song examples with this suspension written in:** "Fly Me to the Moon" (end of the first half) and "Bye Bye Blackbird." → [[Borrowed Chords]] § *The workhorses*, [[Parallel Minor]]
- **3. Cmaj7♯5 as a suspended tonic.** A plain major-seven-sharp-five chord standing in for the arrival of Cmaj7. **Scale choices, ranked from "closest to home" outward:** **A melodic minor / Lydian augmented** (named as the most straight-ahead option, "closest to C major"); further out, **augmented scales** and **E harmonic major**. Practical soloing vocabulary: **melodic-minor triad pairs** — described as simply "such a nice thing to work with," no elaborate scale-running needed. **Melody-note rules for comping/arranging — the most specific of any device on this page:** the **5th is the one chord tone that flatly does NOT work** in the melody over this chord (use §1's diminished or §2's iv-minor instead when the 5th is the melody note); the **root** in the melody is possible but not the first choice; **major 7th and 3rd both work well**; the **9th also works well** — and it's offered as a direct substitute for §1's diminished chord over the same "Stella by Starlight" spot. → [[Augmented Chords]], [[Altered Chords]], [[Scale Modes Reference]]
- **4. Sustained altered dominant — suspend by simply not resolving at all.** No substitute chord — just keep playing the altered-dominant sound (G7alt) straight across the bar line where the resolution to Cmaj7 would normally land, for a half bar, one bar, two bars, or longer depending on the tune. **Soloing-only, not comping** — "hard to fit with the melody most of the time," so this is stated as a device Larsen reaches for almost exclusively while soloing. **Placement rule tied to song FORM, not a fixed length:** works best at points where a strong resolution is genuinely expected — end of a section, e.g. the cadence back to the top of an AABA form's second half — and is weaker in a spot like a rhythm-changes turnaround, where the dominant not resolving between bars 2–3 "isn't going to be that effective." Reference point for playing across the bar line this aggressively: **Coltrane**, cited as the most famous example of pushing this kind of harmonic freedom "pretty out there." Note: this suspension type (sustained dominant, no substitute chord) is also common in classical music but comparatively rare as *this specific unresolved-and-static* device in jazz standards — where standards do harmonize a dominant suspension, the harmony has usually actually *changed* rather than merely delayed (see §5). → [[Altered Chords]], [[The ii-V-I]] § *When the I doesn't arrive*
- **5. Larsen's own device — reinterpret Dm7–G7 as a iv-minor cadence *in A major*, and resolve there instead of C.** The most personal/idiosyncratic device on the page, explicitly credited as something Larsen "came up with myself." Mechanism: hear the existing Dm7–G7 not as ii–V of C, but as a **iv-minor cadence in the key of A major** (the same D-minor-to-dominant relationship, reheard a major 6th up), and resolve it to **A major** instead of the expected C. In practice the rhythm section still plays/hears a C chord underneath (Larsen: "most bass players will really go along with" the underlying Dmaj7-with-C-in-the-bass reading of the comping voicing, not a literal key change) — so the soloist is playing **clean A major/A major-7 lines** over what the band is voicing as a C-rooted chord, deliberately NOT thinking C at all while playing. Sounds "atonal and modern" on paper but is heard/thought as "really simple A major" by the player — the modernity is entirely in the listener's ear, not in the improviser's mental model. **Resolution technique when choosing to resolve it back:** analyze each note in the A major line against its nearest chord tone in the target and step it in — worked example: the **F♯** (in the guitar voicing) resolves up to **G**, the **C♯** resolves to **D** — a general chord-tone-proximity method Larsen says he applies to any unfamiliar/modern chord change, not just this one. **Also usable as a straight reharmonization tool** (not just a soloing device) — cited in an arrangement of "Nine Days"; flagged as producing a genuinely large harmonic distance ("if you expect C major and you get A major, that's a big difference") which is exactly why it reads as such a strong surprise. Larsen mentions a related, more extreme reharmonization variant he uses "a lot" for this exact Dm7-G7-to-tonic slot but defers explaining it to a future video — not covered here. → [[Reharmonization]], [[Chord Substitution]] § *3. Backdoor substitution*, [[The ii-V-I]]
- **General framing that applies to all five devices:** resolution is optional, not mandatory — "you can choose to resolve these suspensions and you can choose not to" — but Larsen recommends practicing them *as* suspensions (i.e. actually resolving them back to the tonic) first, because that's genuinely how the ear parses them: not as an alternate arrival, but as tension that wants to move. Also stated as the throughline of Larsen's whole "reharmonization" video series: thinking in **chords and chord progressions**, not scales, is "much more useful and much more real" — his own stated practice habit is to play the underlying chord to internalize its sound before treating anything as "a scale."
