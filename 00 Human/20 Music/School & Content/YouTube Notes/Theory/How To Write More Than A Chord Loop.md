# How To Write More Than A Chord Loop

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V6KQ2MMAO88)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V6KQ2MMAO88)

## Synopsis

A songwriting-craft video, not a "songs that use X progression" catalog like most of this batch — a practical toolkit for developing a song beyond a single repeating chord loop: four concrete strategies for writing a contrasting second section, plus a separate section on using dynamics/arrangement to keep a loop-only song interesting when you deliberately DON'T want to leave the loop.

## Techniques

- **Strategy 1 — move to the IV chord to start section 2**: if your loop starts on I, starting the new section on IV creates an instant "bookend" (signals a new section began) while staying diatonically related (not jarring) — IV is a strong default choice because it's the subdominant: enough tension to feel like forward motion, without the urgency of the dominant. Examples: "Heart of Glass" (Blondie) — verse starts on I, bridge/solo section starts on IV. "Changes" (David Bowie) — verse progressions start on I, the second-half bridge starts on IV.
- **Strategy 1b — if your loop already starts on IV, move to vi instead**: "Dreams" (Fleetwood Mac) — main loop is IV→V; the guitar-solo section moves to vi instead, for the same bookending effect one degree further out. General rule: any strong, closely-related chord from the key works as a section-starting bookend — IV and vi are just the most common choices, not the only valid ones.
- **Strategy 2 — swap one chord in the existing loop**: take your established loop and change a single chord (to another diatonic option) for a new-but-related second progression. "Won't Back Down" (Tom Petty) — main loop Em–D–G; on its 3rd rotation, G swaps to C. "Blue Monday" (New Order) — main loop F–C–Dm; on its 4th rotation, F swaps to G.
- **Strategy 3 — reorder the existing chords (lowest-knowledge-required option)**: since the chords in your loop already work together by definition, simply resequencing them guarantees a new-but-compatible progression without needing to know what OTHER chords exist in the key. "Won't Back Down" reused for its chorus: C–G–D, the same three chords as its verse loop, reordered. "Dakota" (Stereophonics) — verse is E–C♯m–A–E; chorus reorders a subset of the same chords (A and E). Bennett cites reordering as the technique he used for his own song "Full Moon."
- **Strategy 4 — the "emergency stop"**: abandon the loop entirely for a stretch and vamp/hold on a single chord — creates a deliberately *static* contrasting section (a change in kind, not just in chord content). "Shape of You" (Ed Sheeran) — the main loop drives most of the song, but a later section drops out the harmonic instruments (guitar, bass) almost entirely, functionally leaving just a static C♯m — halting the loop's forward motion to create a distinct textural moment.
- **Alternative to developing beyond a loop: stay in the loop, and use dynamics instead**: many classic songs are built on a single loop for their entire duration and stay interesting anyway — because the *arrangement* (relative volume/intensity, instrumentation density, timbral choices) supplies the sense of change and section structure that a chord change would otherwise provide. Without deliberate dynamic contrast, a pure chord loop risks becoming "background noise." **Loud-quiet-loud (grunge-era) dynamics**: "Creep" (Radiohead) — reserved, clean-guitar verse vs. loud, distorted chorus. "Zombie" (The Cranberries) — same device. "Smells Like Teen Spirit" (Nirvana) — the textbook example of the technique, popularized in '90s grunge (credited as pioneered in this alt-rock context partly by Pixies). **Gradual-build dynamics (the alternative to sharp loud/quiet contrast)**: "With or Without You" (U2) — the entire song is one continuous I–V–vi–IV loop with NO distinct quiet/loud sections; instead, intensity/arrangement density builds slowly and continuously across the whole song, so the single unchanging chord loop never feels static because the *production* is always in motion underneath it. **Arrangement-detail dynamics**: "Sweet Home Alabama" (Lynyrd Skynyrd) — a three-chord loop for virtually the entire song, kept interesting via granular arrangement choices — e.g., the drummer switches from full snare hits to side-stick clicks specifically to soften the transition into the verse; the two verses before the first chorus are deliberately broken up by an instrumental guitar-riff break rather than played back-to-back, briefly raising intensity before settling again for the second verse.
