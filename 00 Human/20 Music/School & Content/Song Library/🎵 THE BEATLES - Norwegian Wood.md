# Norwegian Wood — The Beatles

> Harrison's first use of sitar on a Beatles record — one of the first in Western pop. Lennon's ambiguous story of a possible affair ends with possible arson. 12/8 time gives it a Scandinavian folk lilt.

## Production & Arrangement
The **first Beatles record with sitar** (Harrison), and a landmark for bringing Indian instrumentation into Western pop. A 3/4 folk lilt, intimate and dry — acoustic guitar + sitar doubling the melody, close double-tracked vocal, minimal rhythm section.

- The **Mixolydian ♭VII (C)** drone gives it the modal, folky, slightly-Eastern flavor; the shift to D **minor/Dorian** for "she asked me to stay" darkens it.
- Recorded close and warm — the exoticism is in the *instrument choice and mode*, not effects.

## Steal this
- **One unexpected doubled instrument** (sitar here) tracking the vocal melody = instant signature — pick a timbre outside the genre.
- **Mixolydian ♭VII drone** for a modal, folk-rock flavor without leaving the key → [[Borrowed Chords]] · [[Scale Modes Reference]].
- **3/4 lilt** to make a simple folk song feel timeless.
- Dry, close acoustic recording — character from the source, not the chain.

## From videos
- **Alternate-take evolution documented on the 2026 deluxe edition: the band overshot the sitar idea before dialing it back in.** Take 1 is acoustic guitar + vocal only, no sitar. An intermediate take pushes toward *full* Indian instrumentation and, in Giles Martin's own word, "goes too far." Take 3 pulls back toward the familiar released version — a rare, audible case of a band trying a new idea at full strength first and finding the right dose of it on a later pass, rather than building up to it gradually.
- **Original 4-track layout:** main track — John's acoustic guitar + vocal, recorded live with **Paul's bass bleeding into the same track** (he was playing bass in the same room while John sang; the bleed is kept as texture, not scrubbed). Track 2 — George's sitar. An overdub track layers claps + a foot-stomp kick + tambourine (Ringo) with leg-slapping percussion (John), all bleeding together as one "Indian flavour" percussion composite.
- **2026 stereo remix:** John's acoustic guitar moved off-center while his vocal stays dead-center — on the original mono-era stereo mix, guitar and vocal shared *one* side together, and Giles is explicit that a single performer's own guitar-and-vocal take "have to be together" even in a demixed remix, so the split here is partial, not a hard separation. Bass panned only slightly toward the guitar's side for the same reason. George's 12-string panned hard opposite for real width. The claps/leg-slap composite gets a slight stereo split for extra space, framed by Giles as **"do unnatural things to make things more natural — that's my trademark."** → [[Width and Stereo]] — Tape Notes (Giles Martin), [[TN 190 The Beatles (Giles Martin) - Full Video Episode]]

## Pollack's analysis

*Extracted from [[AWP — Norwegian Wood (This Bird Has Flown)]] — Alan W. Pollack, "Notes On…" #78.*

**Form.** Extreme economy: **everything except the bridges is the same eight-bar hook motif**, repeated over a static I-chord drone. In other hands that much unvaried repetition with that little harmonic movement would die on the page; here it buys cohesion. The 16-bar instrumental intro is really two statements of the verse's hook phrase — solo acoustic first, then sitar taking the melody with bass entering underneath — and every verse follows that model. The bridge is also **16 bars**. The outro gives the hook **one** repeat where the song's established pattern would call for two: a rule broken deliberately at the last possible moment.

**Harmony.** The hook stretches across eight bars inside a single harmonic envelope on **I** — a drone rather than a progression ([[POP-C03 — Pedal point]]) — and whether implied changes exist under it is, in his view, arguable and not worth arguing. `Bridge: i (4 bars) – IV (4) – i (4) – ii – V (4)`. The bridge drops to the **parallel minor** and finally releases some harmonic motion, and its key device is the **major IV inside a minor key** — modal and antique-sounding, the exact inverse of the band's usual minor iv in a major key ([[POP-C10 — Dorian IV vs Aeolian iv]]). It rhymes with the melodic ♭7 in the verse hook ([[SCL-C05 — Characteristic notes]]).

**Notable.** The hook is a textbook case of the **downward spiral** paradigm as distinct from the arch ([[MEL-C01 — Melodic contour]]): a full octave descent from the fifth degree that starts as a plain descending scale turning around its upper neighbour, then develops that idea twice via a subfigure dropping twice and climbing once, the leaps widening each pass. The underrated arrangement detail is the **hard pick on the 12-string** — the percussive noise it generates is what makes the sitar's buzz sit naturally in the track. This is one of their rare ternary-meter songs, lilting rather than waltz-like.

**Against this page.** Agrees — his E major is this page's sounding pitch (D shapes, capo 2), and both read the verse's modal ♭7.
