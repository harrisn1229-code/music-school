## What's taught

- **The piece:** a short "Waltz" (Vals) in 3/8, attributed to Ferdinando Kuli/likely Carulli — piece 6 of 7 in Jared's "Seven Easy Classical Guitar Songs for Beginners" series; free sheet music + tabs available via his "solo guitar Arrangement pack" (soundguitarlessons.com).
- **Time signature 3/8** — three eighth notes per measure ("1 2 3" counted on eighth notes), described as "a feeling of three" similar to 3/4 but with the eighth note, not the quarter note, as the beat unit.
- **D.C. al Fine navigation, read off a real chart:** the piece's form is A A B B C C, then a "D.C. al Fine" (misheard/mis-transcribed as "capo aline"/"deapo") sends the player back to the very top, playing through to the marked "Fine" (pronounced "F" on the page) — **crucially, repeats are NOT retaken on the D.C. pass**, so the reprise is A B once through, not A A B B again. Jared also notes the D.S. al Fine variant (jump to a segno sign instead of the very top) as something to recognize if seen elsewhere. General navigation-sign background already lives on [[Notation]] §7 and [[Musical Form]]; this video's value is the worked example on a real chart, kept here rather than duplicated onto those reference pages.
- **Gear aside — Bluetooth foot-pedal page turner.** Uses a pedal called **"Vidami Blue"** to turn tablet sheet-music pages hands-free while playing/practicing multi-page pieces; references a separate dedicated video plus an audience discount link. Not a plugin/production tool — noted here only, not routed to the Plugin Wishlist.
- **Exercise 1 — right-hand pattern extraction.** Isolate the piece's Waltz-feel right-hand pattern as its own standalone drill before adding left-hand notes/fretting: rotate through the patterns actually used in the piece (D-G-B → D-bass-with-B-E-on-top → strings 5-2-1 → D-B-E again), aiming to make the feel automatic and low-tension first, then layer in notes/frets once the hand pattern itself needs no attention. (Piece has 9 such exercises total; only #1 is walked through in the transcript in enough detail to extract.)
- **Backward-chaining practice order** (learned watching a David Russell masterclass): work a passage from its *last* measure backward — nail the final measure alone, then add the one before it, then the one before that — rather than always starting practice at bar 1. Rationale: forward-only practice over-rehearses the beginning and leaves the hardest, least-familiar material (the ending) both under-rehearsed and reached while most fatigued; backward chaining flips that, so confidence *builds* through the piece instead of draining away. Explicitly general-purpose, not piece-specific. Full extraction on **[[Backward Chaining Practice]]**.
- **Deliberate-practice token method**, paired with the above: line up guitar picks or coins on the desk; only slide one over when a take of the current chunk genuinely satisfies you (rejecting takes for a scraped nail, hesitant entrance, tone/timing wobble); require 3 clean takes in a row (5 if there's time) before expanding the chunk. The counting forces active critical listening on every take, which is presented as the real mechanism behind the method working, not just the rep count. Full extraction on **[[Backward Chaining Practice]]**.
- **Bass-note and string muting.** Thumb hops onto a bass string to mute it once it's no longer a chord tone of the current harmony (e.g. muting a low D that isn't in the next chord); a fretting-hand finger mutes a ringing open high string (e.g. high E) the same way. Called "bass note muting 101" — not easy, takes real dedicated attention, references "earlier in the series" (a prior piece's video, not currently in this vault) as where the underlying skill was first taught. Full extraction on **[[Classical Guitar Expression & Muting]]**.
- **Staccato vs. legato as the piece's featured "element of expression."** Definitions match the standard glossary ([[Dynamics & Articulations]]): staccato short, legato long/connected. Applied here to the middle beat of the waltz's low-high-high pattern in the B section; executed via the **right hand** (replanting on the strings early) rather than left-hand damping, giving a "bounce"/dance feel. Presented as interpretive choice, not a rule — acknowledges period-performance-practice specialists might have more prescriptive answers he doesn't claim to have. Full extraction on **[[Classical Guitar Expression & Muting]]**.
- **Bonus tip — never play an exact repeat identically.** On any section that repeats note-for-note, deliberately vary something the second time: timbre (brighter/darker via right-hand position), dynamics (louder/softer), or a touch of tempo flex — Jared's own go-to is timbre + dynamics, aiming for what he calls an "orchestral echo" effect. Framed as intentionality (decide whether you want the repeat identical) rather than a hard rule. Full extraction on **[[Classical Guitar Expression & Muting]]**.
- **Harmonic analysis.** Form: A A B B C C, then D.C. al Fine → A B (without repeats). A and B sections: I–V throughout (C major – G7). C section modulates to the relative minor (A minor) and uses the same i–V7 relationship (Am–E7) — the takeaway Jared draws out explicitly is that even a key change here is still just tonic-to-dominant motion. References his own ~26-part chord theory series (not currently in this vault) for anyone who wants the underlying theory spelled out.
