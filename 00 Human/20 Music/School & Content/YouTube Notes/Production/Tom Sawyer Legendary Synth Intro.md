# "Tom Sawyer" Legendary Synth Intro

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FNYAp0OxD7Q)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FNYAp0OxD7Q)

## Synopsis

A patch recreation of Rush's "Tom Sawyer" bass-synth intro on an Oberheim OB-X (the actual model used in the session), one of the earliest and most influential examples of a rock band putting a synth intro front and center. Doubles as a demonstration of the OB-X/Oberheim filter's most distinctive trait: unlike most synths, it doesn't lose bass fundamental as resonance is pushed up.

## Techniques

- **Two pulse-wave oscillators in unison, equal volume**, with light pulse-width modulation from a sine-wave LFO at ~5-6 Hz — sine (not square) modulation shape specifically gives a smooth "churn" rather than a choppy on/off warble; this PWM churn is called out as a signature, recognizable OB-X texture.
- **Filter swept by decay time alone**: no sustain level on the filter envelope, tuned decay length is what shapes the whole sweep — the audible one-bar (4-beat) sweep on the record is essentially the filter envelope's decay stretched to fill a bar, then holding once it bottoms out. VCA envelope, by contrast, is simply full sustain throughout — the VCA isn't shaping the sweep at all, the filter envelope is doing all the work.
- **Heavy resonance is central to the sound, enabled by a filter-design trait specific to Oberheim's this-era filter**: most synths lose low-end fundamental as resonance is pushed up; the OB-X's filter keeps the sub/fundamental intact even at high resonance ("you don't lose bass"). This is called out as THE reason Oberheim synths of this vintage are a great match for resonant bass sounds — on a synth without this trait, the workaround is blending in a clean sine wave (into the filter input or post-filter into the mixer/VCA) to restore lost fundamental when resonance is pushed hard.
- **Unison voice-stacking for width, at real hardware scale**: the patch runs 16 oscillators on a single note — the OB-X's 8-voice polyphony × 2 oscillators per voice, all stacked in unison on one note rather than spread across a chord — plus detune on top of the unison stack (with the caveat that unison detune "tears" more the further it's pushed, same tradeoff noted elsewhere in this vault's unison/detune guidance).
