> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **I–V–vi–IV · in C that's C, G, Am and F · "Let It Be"**
> *Also accepted:* "Don't Stop Believin'" · "With or Without You" · "Someone Like You" · any rotation given as the headline form
>
> **Q2.** **I–V–vi–IV bright · vi–IV–I–V dark · IV–I–V–vi unsettled · V–vi–IV–I delayed**
> I–V–vi–IV lifts and reads as optimistic. vi–IV–I–V is the same chords reading as yearning. IV–I–V–vi starts away from home so it feels suspended. V–vi–IV–I lands home last, which delays the arrival.
>
> **Q3.** **Nothing in the pitch content — only which chord the ear hears as home**
> Same four chords, same notes, different perceived tonic and an opposite emotional result.
>
> **Q4.** **Whichever chord the loop starts on and returns to**
> Start on C and it's C major, with the vi as a passing shade. Start on Am and the same loop is A minor, and the C becomes a moment of relief rather than home.
>
> **Q5.** **Because a section's mood can be flipped without changing a single chord**
> *Rubric (5):* says the mood changes while the chords stay identical (2) · says only the starting point — where the bar line falls — moves (1) · gives a concrete use, such as verse on vi and chorus on I with the same loop (2)
> The reason this matters more than most theory facts is that it costs nothing to try. You already have the loop; rotating it is a drag of the clip start, not a rewrite. So the move that would otherwise be "write a darker chorus" becomes "play the chorus from the vi and listen". Verse on vi and chorus on I is the standard shape, and it gives a lift into the chorus that the listener reads as a key change even though nothing was borrowed and no note is new.
>
> **Q6.** **All three functions present · IV–I plagal at the loop point · the chords share notes**
> Tonic is covered twice by I and vi, IV is the subdominant and V the dominant, so the full cycle fits in four chords. The IV–I at the seam closes gently enough to repeat without reading as a full stop. And the shared notes — C and Am hold C and E in common, F and Am hold A and C — mean it loops with smooth voice leading and no seam.
>
> **Q7.** **Change the rotation between sections · move the bass under it · reharmonize the last bar**
> *Also accepted:* swap one chord for a borrowed neighbour, such as F becoming Fm on the last pass · change the harmonic rhythm to two bars per chord · add a secondary dominant in front of a chord
>
> **Q8.** **Relative major and minor — same key signature, same seven notes, different tonal centre**
> The relative minor of C major is A minor. Nothing about which notes are available changes; what changes is which one everything resolves to.
>
> **Q9.** **No pivot chord, no new material, and none of the machinery a real modulation needs**
> The section darkens for free, because the note pool never moved.
>
> **Q10.** **Start the same loop on the vi and end it there**
> If that gets you the feel you wanted, you're done — and you've written no new chords.
>
> **Q11.** **Read from C it's home; read from Am it's ♭III, a lift rather than a resting point**
> *Rubric (5):* says starting on C makes it the tonic and the loop reads as C major (1) · says starting on Am makes A minor the tonic and recasts the C as ♭III (2) · says the pitch content is identical, so the whole difference is which chord is heard as home (2)
> The loop is the same four chords in the same order either way. What moves is the listener's reference point. Heard from C, the C chord is arrival — everything else is departure from it and the Am is a passing shade on the way back. Heard from Am, the C chord is no longer a destination but a brightening partway through a minor loop, the ♭III that gives a minor key its one moment of relief before returning to the tonic. Neither reading is more correct; the ear commits to whichever chord the loop keeps coming back to, which is why the bar line is doing all the work.
