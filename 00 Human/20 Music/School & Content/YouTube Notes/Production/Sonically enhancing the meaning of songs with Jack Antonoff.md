# Sonically enhancing the meaning of songs with Jack Antonoff

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BO_ZUv8fjC4)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BO_ZUv8fjC4)

## Synopsis

Jack Antonoff talks philosophy more than settings here — writing and producing as one continuous, non-linear act rather than sequential steps, and choosing every sonic decision (dry vs. doubled/reverbed vocal, synthetic vs. organic sound sources) based on what the lyric is literally saying. The two concrete, reusable production takeaways are his standing vocal-chain mic choice and his workflow philosophy for capturing vocal takes: never make "doing vocals" a separate, high-pressure event — record from the moment an idea appears, because the least self-conscious early takes are usually the realest ones.

## Techniques

- **Dry vs. treated vocal as a literal storytelling decision, not a taste/genre default.** Explicit rule: a lyric addressed intimately to one listener ("I'm talking only to you... I love you") should generally stay dry, because doubling it or adding reverb/delay makes it "go away" no matter how loud it's mixed — treatment reads as distance/grandeur ("the voice of God"), dryness reads as one person speaking directly to you. Decide the vocal's treatment level from what the lyric is actually saying before reaching for doubles/stacks/reverb.
- **Matching a sound's synthesis method to how synthetic/organic the song needs to feel, deliberately avoiding overused sources.** On St. Vincent's "Sugarboy" (*Masseduction*): built the core synthetic texture from **ARPs run off a Moog**, chosen specifically to sound phonetic and "extremely synthetic" without reaching for already-familiar synth sounds. Same principle applied to programmed/synthetic drums elsewhere — "make the drums off some weird modular stuff... don't use sounds that have been used before." General framing: identify whether a song wants to feel synthetic/menacing or organic/folky, then commit the sound-source choice fully in that direction rather than blending.
- **Standing vocal-mic choice: the Wunder Audio CM7 (a U47-style large-diaphragm tube condenser) as the default vocal chain input, preferred over vintage U47 hardware even when one's available in the room.** "Every time I go to a studio there's always a vintage 47 and they always sound great, but I end up using it more on guitars and usually just go through the CM7... it's just like a wonderful jumping-off point."
- **No dedicated vocal booth — mic left permanently set up in the writing/production room so a vocal idea can be captured the instant it happens, rather than waiting for a formal "doing vocals" moment.** Explicit reasoning: turning vocal recording into a separate, announced event makes performers self-conscious ("some part of you gets scared, especially if you're a songwriter... I don't know if I want to say it"), which works against getting a real take. Practice: "the mic is right here, and as we're working or writing, it's just like, oh, let's put it in — even though the lyric is not perfect."
- **Build vocal confidence before the "real" take by stacking easy layers first — get a performer singing before they have to nail anything.** Example: get a quick backup-vocal idea down first ("oh, this would be a cool backup, great, let's get it"), then build it into eight wide, distant-feeling stacks, purely to get the singer using their voice loosely before the pressure of a lead-vocal take.
- **Deliberately keep and comp early, imperfect takes from the writing process itself rather than treating them as scratch — the case study being Lorde's "Liability."** Piano-and-vocal-only song; the standing practice of "just put it in, put it in" during writing produced multiple takes each with a few standout moments and inconsistent tone/character from take to take. Engineer Laura Sisk's approach, quoted directly, is the actual technique: identify which specific moments across different takes are the real "heart and soul," accept they'll sound different from each other, and comp/blend them into one performance rather than discarding the inconsistent ones — "we have the heart and soul here, and I don't care if it's a little... it doesn't have to be perfect, but there's some moments here which totally sound like different vocal sounds — this has to become one thing because this is perfect." Antonoff's stated experience: the very first vocal takes captured during writing are very often (his estimate, "9.99999 out of 10 times") the keeper takes, precisely because they weren't self-conscious "performances."
