# Flip Your Chords for Better Progressions!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eNeQQAr7R1c)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eNeQQAr7R1c)

## Synopsis
Max's plain-language, non-jargon on-ramp to modal interchange: instead of teaching "borrow from the parallel minor," he teaches "take any diatonic chord and flip its major/minor quality (its 'polarity')," which produces the same borrowed-chord palette without requiring the listener to already know modal-interchange theory. He works it in both major and minor keys, adds inversions and extension-choice rules on top, then builds a full worked walking-bass progression combining flipped and un-flipped chords. Explicitly the video referenced inside [[Beautiful Mixolydian Chord Progression - Modal Harmony Tutorial]] ("I did a video on this actually... about flipping polarities of chords").

## What's taught
- **The core trick: take the diatonic chords of a key (major key quality pattern: major-minor-minor-major-major-minor-diminished) and flip any of them to their opposite quality** — a flipped I becomes i (minor), a flipped ii becomes II (major), etc. Diminished vii is left out of the flipping (no simple single-flip equivalent). Same underlying mechanism as [[Borrowed Chords]] (modal interchange from the parallel key), taught bottom-up from "just flip it" rather than top-down from "here's the parallel scale" — genuinely useful as a plain-language restatement worth cross-linking. → [[Borrowed Chords]], [[Parallel Minor]]
- **Works in minor keys too, same logic**: flip ♭III to iii (minor), flip iv to IV (major) — the minor-key equivalent of the major-key flips above, omitting the diminished ii° the same way.
- **Rule of thumb for which extensions to add to a flipped chord: keep as many notes of the HOME key static as possible.** Worked example: in C major, flipping ii (D minor → D major) only actually changes one note (F→F♯); every other white-key note is still "safe" to add as an extension on top of the new D major chord (9th, 13th, whatever), because those notes are unchanged from the original key. This is a concrete, checkable rule for voicing borrowed chords that isn't yet written down anywhere in the vault's harmony notes. → [[Borrowed Chords]], [[Extended Chords]]
- **Combine flipping with inversions for smoother/more varied bass movement** — a flipped chord can still be inverted like any other (e.g., a flipped ii's 3rd or 5th in the bass instead of its root), opening up new bass notes beyond what the un-flipped diatonic chord offered.
- **Practical process for writing WITH the flipping idea rather than converting an existing progression**: start from a scale degree, decide diatonic or flipped, choose a bass movement direction (stepwise diatonic descent vs. chromatic descent through notes that only exist in a flipped chord), and build the full chord (with extensions from the home key) around that bass note. Worked full example descends chromatically through A→G♯→G→F♯→F (using flipped E major over G♯, flipped F minor, a C-over-G inversion, and a flipped D major over F♯) before climbing back diatonically to resolve — effectively a chromatic-bass-line technique built entirely out of flipped/inverted diatonic chords. → [[Chromatic Bass Lines]], [[Chord Inversions]]
- **Framing for further study**: flipping is presented as the *practical shortcut*, with the real theoretical name being modal interchange (borrowing from Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian — not just the parallel major/minor) — an explicit pointer to study modal interchange properly once the ear likes what flipping produces. → [[Borrowed Chords]], [[Scale Modes Reference]]
