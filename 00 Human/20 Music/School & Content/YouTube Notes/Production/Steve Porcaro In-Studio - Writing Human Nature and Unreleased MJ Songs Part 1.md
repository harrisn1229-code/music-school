# Steve Porcaro In-Studio | Writing "Human Nature" & Unreleased MJ Songs - Part 1

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MK_16kD45X4)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MK_16kD45X4)

## Synopsis

An in-studio conversation between Anthony Marinelli and Toto's Steve Porcaro (who wrote "Human Nature" and worked every Michael Jackson album from Off the Wall through HIStory in various capacities) about writing and recording with Jackson. Mostly biography and studio-culture anecdote — the "yatas" nickname story, the origin of the "Human Nature" demo, MJ's turntable-referencing habit — but several passages carry real, specific technique: the actual instrument breakdown behind "Human Nature," a keyboard-transition trick, and Porcaro's own filter/mix philosophy on string patches.

## Techniques

- **A chord-transition trick on an Oberheim: play a chord in unison mode, then take the patch OUT of unison mid-note so the stacked unison voices "peel off" and slide up into their individual, now-separated notes.** A transition device rather than a static patch — the unison-to-split move itself is the effect.
- **"Human Nature" instrument breakdown, as actually tracked**: Rhodes-style part on a Yamaha GS1 (not a real Rhodes — every other song on *Thriller* used a real Rhodes played by Greg Phillinganes, "Human Nature" is the exception), deliberately "ghosting" the vocal melody in the comping; a CS-80 part; string parts split between a Prophet-5 and additional CS-80 work; the bass on a Jupiter-8 in **plain oscillator mode, not unison** (a deliberate choice — Porcaro notes he "had no idea" why the Jupiter was picked over a Moog, since unison was available and unused); the intro arpeggiator sequence also programmed and played on the Jupiter-8, locked to a computer sequencer for tempo-accuracy; live drums doubled/"ghosted" (not exactly duplicated — played along with a human touch) over the LM-1 drum machine part from the original demo.
- **Tailor the envelope to leave room for the drums, specifically the snare** — shaping a pad or comping part's envelope so it "breathes," rather than every part plowing straight through the bar, is described as a deliberate mixing/arrangement discipline applied at the synthesis stage, not just at the mix.
- **A turntable-reference workflow, and the discipline that makes it work**: Michael Jackson would put a reference record on the turntable (naming another artist/song's specific sound) as a *starting point* for a sound, but the explicit rule Porcaro applies is that the reference is not the destination — "you still have to tailor it to the song," since the borrowed sound was built for a different track's arrangement and mix.
- **String-patch realism method: back the filter off deliberately while judging the sound WITH the track playing, not soloed.** Porcaro's persistent struggle was keeping synth strings from sounding like synths — closing the filter down to sound convincing produced something that, heard by itself with the tape stopped, sounded "way too mellow," like alto flutes — but that same setting read correctly once it sat inside the full arrangement. The practical habit this produced: learn to tweak sound *while the track is running*, and resist the temptation to solo and "fix" a patch that sounds wrong alone but is actually correct in context.
- **Print effects (space echo) to tape rather than adding them later in the mix — an "English" studio habit Porcaro adopted over the more common LA approach of recording dry and treating in the mix.** The reasoning given: an effect only works if it's occupying a specific, intentional space in the arrangement every time it's heard — arrangement space is treated as a fixed container ("a glass you fill up") that has to be emptied (parts dropping out) as much as filled (parts coming in), and committing the effect to tape guarantees the effected version is what's always heard, rather than risking an inconsistent dry/wet decision later.
- **When a part still reads muddy or cloudy after synth-side EQ/filter attention, hand it to the mixing engineer by name, with a specific complaint, rather than continuing to chase it at the source.** Porcaro's habit with engineer Bruce Swedien: naming the exact problem ("my low end's cloudy, I'm having trouble with the sound speaking") got a targeted EQ/compression fix, and — as a secondary effect — invested the engineer personally in getting that part audible in the final mix.
