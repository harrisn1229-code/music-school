# Every type of Cadence EXPLAINED

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SIcFOkMtDJI)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SIcFOkMtDJI)

## Synopsis

A comprehensive, ground-up cadence taxonomy — the four classical named cadences plus their minor-key and voicing-strictness variants, worked with real song examples throughout, and closing with a modern "slang" cadence (the Super Mario cadence) as a case study in how cadence names actually get coined and stick.

## What's taught

- **Perfect/Authentic cadence (V→I)**, UK "perfect" = US "authentic" naming split. In the minor key, only the ROOT chord goes minor — the V chord stays major (a dominant chord, borrowed from harmonic minor) for it to count as a true perfect cadence; a minor-v-to-minor-i movement is still a real resolving cadence but has no formal classical name. Worked example: "Imagine" (John Lennon), G→C.
- **Plagal cadence (IV→I)**, nicknamed the "Amen cadence" from its traditional use setting the word "Amen" in hymns — softer/less final than the perfect cadence, which is why it's common as a way to keep a progression feeling like it can continue rather than fully stopping (a comma vs. a full stop). Explicit lyrical reference: Hozier's "Take Me to Church" sets the word "Amen" to a plagal cadence, switching to a MINOR plagal on its final repetition.
- **Minor plagal cadence** (a chromatic variant IN the major key, not "plagal cadence in a minor key"): iv→I instead of IV→I, introducing a chromatic ♭6 note that pulls strongly down to the 5th of the tonic chord — described as more harmonically driven/resolving than the plain major plagal despite (or because of) its bittersweet color. **Distinct from the actual minor-key plagal cadence**, which is simply iv→i (both chords minor, no chromaticism).
- **Interrupted/deceptive cadence (V→ any chord but I, most commonly vi)** — broader definition: ANY perfect-cadence setup (V) that resolves somewhere other than I counts, not just V→vi specifically. Worked example: Queen's "I Want to Break Free" stacks TWO interrupted cadences (IV-V-vi twice) before finally landing the real perfect cadence (IV-V-I) the third time.
- **Half/imperfect cadence (ends on V, unresolved)** — debated as to whether it even counts as a "cadence" at all since nothing resolves, but classically still classified as one. Especially noticeable ending a whole song rather than mid-phrase: The Cure's "Friday I'm in Love," Owl City's "Fireflies" (emphasized via a sus4-to-V motion possibly modeled on the Beatles' "For No One," which does the same thing).
- **Perfect Authentic Cadence (PAC) vs. the three types of Imperfect Authentic Cadence (IAC)** — the stricter US voicing distinction: a PAC requires BOTH chords in root position AND the tonic note as the highest voice of the I chord. Three ways a V→I cadence can fail to be "perfect" and become an IAC instead: (1) root position but the top voice isn't the tonic note, (2) either chord inverted, (3) the V chord substituted for the leading-tone (vii°) chord — effectively a rootless dominant 7th.
- **The Super Mario cadence, as a case study in how a slang cadence name gets coined and spreads**: ♭VI→♭VII→I (in C: A♭→B♭→C) — not a classical named cadence, but functionally resolves the same way, and the name stuck specifically because referencing a universally-recognized piece of music (the Mario theme) gave people an instant shared reference point for an otherwise nameless progression.
