# The First Songs to use Synth

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NlbUPXlOYSo)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NlbUPXlOYSo)

## Synopsis

A chronological dig backward through pop/rock history for the earliest synth (or synth-adjacent) recording, working from a naive assumption (the Beatles' *Abbey Road*, 1969) back through Moog-era rock (1967–68), the pre-Moog Clavioline/electro-theremin era (1953–66), and finally to the Hammond Novachord on a 1939 hit — landing on a working definition of "synthesizer" (pure electronic-oscillator sound generation, no physical mechanism, no sample playback) along the way.

## Techniques

- **A working definition of "synthesizer" for classification purposes**: an instrument that generates sound purely from one or more electronic oscillators — excludes anything with a physical sound-producing mechanism (rules out electric pianos/organs with real reeds or tonewheels) and excludes sample-playback instruments like the Mellotron (tape loops, not oscillators).
- **Chronological gear lineage, earliest-first**: Hammond Novachord (1939, "We'll Meet Again" — Vera Lynn) — one of the earliest polyphonic electronic instruments, predating even the monophonic early Moog by decades. Clavioline (1961–62, "Runaway" by Del Shannon via Max Crook's self-modified "Musitron," and "Telstar" by the Tornados, played as the lead melody, not background texture, and a US/UK #1). Electro-Theremin/Tannerin (1966, "Good Vibrations" and "I Just Wasn't Made for These Times," Beach Boys) — an easier-to-pitch, slide-oscillator alternative to a true hand-gesture theremin. Moog Modular (1967 onward, via Paul Beaver, Moog's West Coast rep, who demoed it to the Monkees, the Byrds, and George Harrison, and performed on the Zodiac's *Cosmic Sounds* — the first rock album to feature it).
- **A single named individual (Paul Beaver) was the actual vector for how a new instrument entered a genre**: he wasn't just a sales rep — he performed on or assisted most of the early Moog rock sessions cited, and George Harrison secretly recorded one of Beaver's demo sessions and used the recording as source material for an experimental electronic album, rather than learning the instrument from scratch himself.
- **Using an "eerie" sound as a deliberate genre-clash device, in the artist's own words**: Brian Wilson's rationale for the Electro-Theremin on "Good Vibrations" — "as long as we're doing something eerie today, why not get real eerie and put a theremin on it" — explicitly placing a classical/novelty-instrument texture into a pop arrangement for the deliberate friction of it, not to hide it.
