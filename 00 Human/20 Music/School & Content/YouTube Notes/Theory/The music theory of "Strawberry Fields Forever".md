# The music theory of "Strawberry Fields Forever"

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SHDs15pEfl0)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SHDs15pEfl0)

## Synopsis

A focused harmonic/rhythmic/production analysis of one song — the tonal ambiguity in its opening chords, its chorus's erratic meter, and the studio-splice backstory behind two of its most famous "impossible" details (an unplayably-low cello note and the outro's fade-out/fade-in).

## What's taught

- **Microtonal tuning**: the song's actual key center sits at a half-sharp above major (a side effect of the studio vari-speed technique used to splice two takes together — full mechanism in [[Why is Strawberry Fields Forever in A half-sharp major]]) — rounded here to **B♭ major** for analysis.
- **Opening tonal ambiguity**: the intro/verse chord progression plays E natural before E♭ arrives, which briefly makes the key sound like F major (F and B♭ major differ only by E vs. E♭) before the E♭ pulls the tonality to the real key center, B♭.
- **Chorus chord progression**: I → v7 (minor five) → IV(6) → back through IV → I, described as an unusual, distinctive progression.
- **Meter in the chorus**: a one-off 2/4 half-bar, plus a 4/4→6/8 shift under the main hook — 6/8 is compound time (pulse felt as the dotted quarter note vs. 4/4's quarter note), which reads as a momentary slowdown at constant tempo. On the final chorus, John Lennon repeats the closing line three times, and on the last repetition drops a beat entirely, landing on 3/4 instead of 4/4.
- **The two-take splice (~1:00 mark)**: the released master combines a simpler, band-focused Take 7 (electric guitars, bass, no strings/horns) for the first minute with a very different second take — no guitars/bass, built instead around a cello/trumpet arrangement George Martin wrote at Lennon's request. From that splice point on, the harmony is carried almost entirely by Martin's cello/trumpet writing.
- **The "impossible" cello note**: the famous low B♭ in the cello line sits a tone below a cello's actual lowest playable note (C). This isn't an intentional extended-technique choice — Martin wrote that line when the second take was still in C major, where the note was a perfectly playable low C; only after the take was vari-speed slowed down to match the first take's key did that C become an unplayable B♭, baked in permanently by the pitch-shift.
- **The outro fade-out/fade-in**: not a creative decision either — a section of the percussion briefly falls out of time, so the engineers fade the mix out to hide it and fade back in once the band has locked back into the groove.
