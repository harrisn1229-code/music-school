# Scale Degree Ear Training

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Intervals]]. A method (Max Konyi / Mind of Max, creator of the *Sonop* ear-training app) for training the ear on **scale degrees as feeling states relative to a tonic**, rather than on intervals as abstract pitch-distances. The core claim: every scale degree, chord quality, chord position, and rhythm/meter has its own recognizable "feeling state" — a subjective, non-verbal quality that's the same TYPE of experience whether it shows up as a melody note, a chord, or a bass note — and ear training is the practice of consciously labeling those feeling states until recognition becomes instant.

## Why scale degrees over raw intervals

Training melodic intervals in isolation (an app playing two disembodied pitches) teaches a distance, not a musical feeling — and that distance changes character completely once it's placed in a tonal context. A minor 6th floating in free space sounds "dark," but the identical interval as the 3rd-to-root of a major chord (E→G inside C major) sounds nothing like that — it sounds like a major chord. **The brain cannot hear pitches without context**; it will always assign a root/tonic to whatever it's given and hear everything relative to that. This is why scale-degree training (which builds the tonic-relationship in from the start) transfers to real listening far better than pure interval training — a lesson both Max Konyi and David Bennett (interviewed on the channel, see [[David Bennett on Music Theory, Composition & the YouTube Life Sonic Sorcery Podcast No. 04]]) converged on independently: Bennett says explicitly that scale-degree hearing ("that's the fifth moving to the sixth") is the ear-training skill he actually uses day to day, more than named-interval recognition.

## Foundation: pitch matching and tonic recognition, before scale degrees

Two prerequisite skills, worth diagnosing first:
1. **Pitch matching** — can you sing back a note you just heard (any octave)? Find a heard pitch on your instrument? Sing a random pitch and then find it on the instrument? If any of these are shaky, that's the actual starting point — scale-degree/chord work all falls back on "sing it or find it on the instrument" whenever recognition fails, so without this fallback you're stuck when you don't instantly know what you're hearing.
2. **Tonic recognition** — the ear constantly, automatically infers a tonic from the notes it's hearing, the same way vision resolves a blurry/low-res image into a recognizable shape as more information arrives. Every new note either reinforces or breaks the current tonic assumption; enough broken assumptions and the ear drops the old tonic for a new one — that switch IS the subjective experience of a key change/modulation. You don't need to consciously do anything to make this happen — if music sounds pleasurable/structured to you at all, your ear is already doing it; ear training is about becoming consciously aware of what it already knows.

**Three levels of deliberate tonic-recognition practice, easy to hard** (the tonic is "home" — rest, completion, stability, independent of the specific absolute pitch; the same physical note feels completely different depending on its role — C as the tonic of C major feels nothing like the same C as scale degree 5 of F major):
1. **Scale-based** — play a scale up or down and stop one note short of the tonic; let the feeling of near-completion build, then **sing** the resolving note before playing it (producing the feeling, not just perceiving it, is what locks it in).
2. **Chord-progression-based** — play any progression that stays in one key (start with something simple and familiar, e.g. I–IV–I–V), stop on a non-tonic chord, and sing the resolution. Escalate to progressions you don't already know, even improvised ones.
3. **Real-music-based**, two variants: (a) with a song whose chords you already know, consciously flag the tonic chord every time it recurs — including mid-phrase, not just at the start/end; (b) with a song you've **never heard**, stop playback at a random moment and try to sing the resolution note *before* checking — an active test of whether the tonic feeling has actually been internalized, not just recognized in the abstract.

Singing (producing) the tonic trains it more effectively than only listening for it. — Max Konyi, [[The Secret of Ear Training]]

## The 12 scale-degree feeling states

Trained against a drone (the tonic held underneath), each of the 12 chromatic scale degrees has a distinct, describable feeling state. These are one person's subjective labels — offered as a starting vocabulary, not gospel; the point is to find your own words, then discard the words and keep only the felt recognition:

| Degree | Feeling state | Most easily confused with |
|---|---|---|
| **1 (tonic)** | Perfect, complete, whole, resolved, stable — the minimum-energy state | — |
| **5** | Pure, stable, consonant, smooth, but *active* — open, expansive, outward | 1, 4 |
| **4** | Neutral/pure like 5, but *contractive*, inward-curling, "square," flat-in-shape | 1, ♭7 |
| **3** | Sweetness, warmth, glow, vitality — a whole extra dimension 5 doesn't have | 6, 7 |
| **2** | Clean, crystalline, placid, "5 multiplied" — sharper/more amped than 5 | 5, 6 |
| **6** | 3's warmth mixed with 4's neutrality — softer, more pastel/muted than 3 | 2, 3 |
| **7** | Melancholy/bittersweet — combines the 5-feeling and 3-feeling, sharpened by the half-step dissonance against the tonic | 3, ♯4 |
| **♯4** | One of the strongest/most energized states — bright, strange, "alien," spiky; maximally opposite of 1 on the circle | 7, ♭2 |
| **♭7** | Lightest of the "flat"/minor-leaning degrees — powerful, playful, "fun minor," not dark | 4, ♭3 |
| **♭3** | Defines "minorness" itself — dark, serious, gloomy; a bit of sorrow | ♭7, ♭6 |
| **♭6** | More intense than ♭3, less than ♭2 — desperate, hopeless, no-joy, high negative emotional valence | ♭3, ♭2 |
| **♭2** | The most extreme/dissonant — in-your-face, bitter, "the world's going to end," strongest tension | ♭6, ♯4 |

Practical notes on the table:
- **Circle-adjacency predicts confusions.** Arrange the 12 degrees in a circle by feeling-similarity (not pitch order) and degrees next to each other are the ones people mistake for one another (3/6, 3/7) — degrees a half-step apart *melodically* (5 and ♯4) are usually maximally far apart on this feeling-circle, which is the whole point: close in pitch ≠ close in feeling.
- **Two "worlds": the 5-axis and the 3-axis.** 1-4-5-2 share a clean/neutral/pure quality (the "5 world"); 3-6-7 share a warm/glowing/sweet quality (the "3 world"). Most confusions happen within one of these two families, not across them.
- **Drone practice, not free-floating.** Always train against a sustained tonic drone — it's the reference the brain uses to build these relationships explicitly while learning; in real listening you don't need the drone because the mind holds a "virtual tonic" automatically once it's familiar with a piece's key.
- **The transfer test:** play a descending major scale and just focus attention on landing on 1 — that felt sense of arrival/completion IS the degree-1 feeling state, and it's the easiest one to bootstrap the whole method from.

## Bridging from drone training to real music

The single biggest gap ear-training apps leave: they train degrees against an isolated drone or a single chord, but real music has a constantly shifting harmonic context underneath the melody, which recontextualizes what a melody note "sounds like" (a C over a C chord sounds like 1; the same C over an F chord sounds like 5; over a Dm chord it sounds like ♭7). Two skills, and they're different:
- **Chord-relative hearing** — the melody note's feeling relative to the CURRENT chord underneath it (useful for voicings/comping).
- **Key-relative hearing** — the melody note's feeling relative to the overall TONIC of the song, independent of which chord is under it at the moment (this is the scale-degree skill, and it transfers to being able to play back what you hear).
- **The bridging drill:** take a song you know, try to hear/label the scale degrees of the melody as they go by from the key-center perspective, stop and check by singing/playing it out to confirm, then relisten with the confirmed answer in mind. Repeat on new material. Over time this becomes simultaneous — you can hear that a note is "the 7 of the current chord AND the 3 of the key" at once — but it starts as this slow stop-check-relisten loop.
- Non-diatonic/modulating passages will always be harder — there's no shortcut past more repetition once the harmonic context gets genuinely ambiguous (key changes, non-diatonic chords).

## Chords, chord qualities, and progressions as "meta feeling states"

- **Chords are not the sum of their component scale-degree feelings** — combining feeling-states produces a genuinely NEW, higher-level ("meta") experience, the same way syllables combine into a word you hear as a whole rather than as separate sounds. Practical implication: don't try to consciously decompose every chord into its individual scale degrees in real time (unless you specifically need to, e.g. transcribing a tricky voicing) — instead train each **chord type/quality/position combination as its own feeling state directly** (major-on-4, minor-on-6, dominant-7-on-♭7, etc.), the same way you trained the 12 single-degree feelings.
- **This stacks recursively**: individual degrees → chord (quality × position) → progression → whole-song feeling, each level its own holistic "meta" experience, recognized as a gestalt rather than reconstructed from parts (same as the harmonic-series point below: even a single note is technically a "chord" of overtones, and you don't consciously parse that either).
- **Chord inversions are their own feeling states, not calculated from the chord + a formula.** Root position has a distinct "bang, solid, this is IT" landed feeling; each inversion has its own equally-distinct-but-different feeling, driven by the bass note's own scale-degree feeling (a 3rd or 5th or 7th in the bass doesn't feel like "1," so an inverted chord never feels like a landed root-position chord even before you consciously identify which inversion it is). See [[Chord Inversions]].
- **Extended chords (9/11/13) blur major/minor identity** and read as their own distinct "pastel," softened meta-experience rather than as [base chord] + [extension] stacked consciously — train major-9, major-add9, major-6/9, major-9-♯11 etc. as individually memorized flavors the same way, rather than trying to hear the 9th/11th/13th as a separately-identified scale degree layered on top. See [[Extended Chords]].
- **Hearing inner voices (the notes buried inside a chord) works against a natural integrating instinct** — the ear/brain actively fuses simultaneous notes into one gestalt (same mechanism that fuses a word's syllables, or that fuses a single note's own harmonic overtones into "one sound"). Pulling a chord apart into its individual voices is going against that fusion instinct, not extending it — it CAN be trained (with enough repetition it gets much faster), but for most people it needs deliberate, repeated, conscious effort, and even then it stays effortful compared to top/bottom-note recognition. Practical fallback for transcribing a genuinely hard voicing: brute-force it — play it back repeatedly, match individual pitches on the instrument/voice one at a time, then name what you found.
- **Telling root position from an inversion by ear** = recognizing the bass note's own scale-degree feeling. A root-position bass note has the "1" feeling (solid, landed); an inverted bass note has whatever THAT degree's feeling is (a 3rd sounds like "3" in the bass, a 5th like "5") — you can know a chord is inverted without yet knowing the full chord, purely from the bass note's feeling not matching "1."

## Practice routine, roughly in order

1. **Pitch matching** (sing back / find on instrument) if that's not already solid.
2. **Tonic recognition** — learn to consciously notice what your ear is already doing automatically.
3. **Scale degrees against a drone** — one degree at a time, compare/contrast against its most-confused neighbors (the table above), then in short melodies.
4. **Melodic intervals** — worth doing, but Konyi ranks this below scale-degree training in priority; do both, they reinforce each other in real playing (a leap "up a 5th" and "landing on scale degree 3" can be recognized simultaneously, not sequentially).
5. **Chord qualities and positions** — same drone-and-drill method, chord by chord, extending into progressions.
6. **Real-music bridging drill** — the stop/sing/confirm/relisten loop on songs you know, moving from drone-isolated training into full harmonic context.
7. **Extensions and altered chords** — last, since they take the longest and blur identity the most; isolate one extension type at a time on one chord type at a time before mixing them.

This is explicitly a **years-long, not weeks-long** process — Konyi is candid that even after extensive training he still regularly hits sounds he can't immediately identify and has to brute-force. Progress is measured by how often that happens, not by ever reaching zero.

## Listen-along hours (Max Konyi)

Three 68-minute drills that run this note's method as continuous audio, from the same source the method itself came from. Each plays a tone against a drone, **speaks the correct scale degree, then replays the tone** so the feeling and the number bind on the second hearing. The key changes every few minutes, so degrees are learned rather than pitches. Recorded from his app **Sonofield** in "Pocket Mode" — built for walking and commuting, and explicitly *not* something to sit and concentrate on.

His own instruction for beginners is worth repeating: **do not try to guess.** Just listen and relax. Guessing wrong for an hour trains anxiety, not recognition.
