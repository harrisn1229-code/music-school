# Behind the Track｜'Messy' by Lola Young

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=02QKFlkbmYY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=02QKFlkbmYY)

## Synopsis

A **Mix with the Masters** "Behind the Track" piece on Lola Young's "Messy," with Young joined by producer Jared Solomon (Solomonophonic) and band members Manuka, Will Brown, and Conor Dickinson. Like its sibling piece on 070 Shake's "Elephant" (same channel/series, see [[Behind the Track｜'Elephant' by 070 Shake]]), it's a process/philosophy piece rather than a plugin-by-plugin breakdown — but this one carries noticeably more concrete production detail: a real full-band live-tracking session, specific instruments (Korg MS-20 through a spring reverb, a Wurlitzer, a Juno-106), and one genuinely unusual signal-chain accident. The song began as a two-chord bedroom demo Young wrote alone (a single "weird" C-shape moved up the fretboard, carrying both verse and chorus), written slow and folky. At 64 Sound in Highland Park, the band's default process is to jam as a live group and let songs emerge from that — but on the last day of a week-long session, after three attempted group-written songs failed, they pivoted to Young's already-finished solo song instead, and Solomon proposed speeding up the sad/folky original and adding "70s drums" for energy. The core take (drums, acoustic guitar, bass, Scott's MS-20/CS-60, vocals) was tracked live as a full band, and the vocal — recorded on the tail end of Young losing her voice — was kept whole, imperfections included, because the imperfection reinforced the song's theme (the tension between being "too messy" and "too perfect"). The video's most interesting technical anecdote is a genuine Juno-106 accident: the synth has no built-in arpeggiator, so the engineer clock-synced it to Pro Tools, but because the synth is polyphonic, each arp step fired the whole held chord instead of one note — an unintended "chord within each note of the arp" effect they kept because it sounded great. A second small anecdote: an electric guitar lick that landed close to "I Want It That Way" (Backstreet Boys) was deliberately left incomplete — they never played the melodic "...way" resolution — specifically to avoid an interpolation clearance cost.

## Techniques

- **Two-chord songwriting bed built from one moved chord shape.** The entire song (verse and chorus both) is built on two chords: a "weird" C chord shape played, then moved up the fretboard for the second chord — no new voicings, just the one shape relocated. A minimal harmonic bed by design, not a placeholder later expanded.
- **Deliberately sped up and re-genred a slow, sad, folky acoustic demo for energy, while protecting the original lyric density.** Young's original "Messy" demo was slow and folky/sad; producer Jared Solomon's move was to take that same song, speed the tempo up, and add "70s drums" to give it energy — explicitly finding the fastest tempo the wordy, dense original lyric could still support ("the lyrics are really wordy, so it was like, how fast can we get away with it?"). The melody, chords, and lyric stayed the same; only tempo and drum genre-coding changed. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **Pulled-and-pushed, speech-adjacent vocal phrasing instead of a straight-on-the-beat delivery.** Young describes the melody as "meandering," pulling back and forward against the beat rather than landing squarely — named directly on a specific line ("It was like minus four degrees and I...") as a deliberate rhythmic push-pull rather than a "more straight" phrasing choice.
- **A full band tracked live together as the foundational take — drums, acoustic guitar, bass, synth, and lead vocal simultaneously, whole-take kept, no comping.** Will on drums, Conor on acoustic guitar, Solomon on bass, Scott on the CS-60 (and separately the MS-20, below), Young in the vocal booth — "we just kept doing takes over and over," and the entire live take (not a comp) was used: "I think we used the whole take... the imperfections were kind of part of it." The band names this explicitly as a "raw, organic way of working," with attention placed on ensemble dynamics rather than individual polish, because a two-chord song has to earn its interest from performance dynamics ("it was important to have the ups and downs, because that's where the interesting... keeping the attention comes from") rather than harmonic movement. → [[Vocal Comping and Takes]], [[Compression Staging]]
- **Kept an audibly fatigued, imperfect lead vocal take on purpose, because the imperfection reinforced the song's theme.** Young was losing her voice by the time "Messy" was recorded and eventually said she couldn't keep singing; the verses were kept from that fatigued take. The "slight imperfections" in the take are described as "massively" making the song what it is, and tied directly to the song's own lyric ("I'm too messy... I'm too perfect") — imperfection as more "enticing and interesting" than something clean-cut, so the flawed take stayed rather than being redone or comped around. → [[Vocal Comping and Takes]]
- **A single added harmony on the last chorus, singled out as the emotional high point of the whole arrangement.** BVs and harmonies were layered on afterward; one harmony specifically on the final chorus is called out by both collaborators as "a really sad moment" and the standout addition — one layer doing outsized emotional work rather than a dense stack.
- **A buried, genre-incongruous acoustic guitar lick, deliberately mixed low rather than brought forward, discovered only because the whole track was played through rather than looped.** A "kind of country"-flavored acoustic part sits quietly in the background — a texture Young "would never really put in her song" on purpose, but the band loves it precisely because it's a little buried. Because the take was a full live performance rather than one part looped throughout the song, small unplanned licks and riffs "poke out" at different moments from different players rather than the same phrase repeating identically. → [[Ear Candy and Hiding Synths]]
- **A Korg MS-20 part chopped and run through a physical spring reverb tank, producing a guitar-like tone purely from the spring's coloration.** "This was played on the MS-20 and then me and Scott chopped this out and ran it through a spring" — the result reads so much like a real guitar that everyone in the room comments on it; framed as a general rule of thumb, not a fluke ("honestly, spring makes anything sound like a guitar"). A synth source run through spring reverb is a repeatable way to fake a stringed-instrument texture. → [[Sound Selection Guide]], [[Reverb]] (effect), [[Vintage Gear Worklist]]
- **A Wurlitzer electric piano reserved specifically for the bridge, valued purely for warmth and presence.** "So this is just the wurly that comes in in the bridge, it's just really warm, it's a really nice part" — singled out by name as the moment both collaborators say they listen for on replay, for the warmth/presence it adds rather than any processing. → [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **An electric guitar lick deliberately left short of a recognizable melodic quote, to dodge an interpolation clearance cost.** A guitar phrase landed close to the Backstreet Boys' "I Want It That Way" melody; rather than cut the take, they kept the near-quote but never played the resolving "...way" phrase, explicitly because completing it would mean "you gotta pay them a lot of money" — the interpolation stayed a "cool little Easter egg" specifically by staying incomplete. → [[Sample Clearance]]
- **A Juno-106 arpeggiator hack via Pro Tools MIDI clock sync produced an unintended polyphonic arp — a full chord fired on every step instead of one note.** The Juno-106 has no built-in arpeggiator; near the end of a session day, their engineer (Tyler) clock-synced the hardware to Pro Tools to drive it as an external arpeggiator while Will played chords. Because the synth is genuinely polyphonic, each arp step played the WHOLE held chord (arpeggiated up octaves) rather than a single note at a time — described as "making a chord within each note of the arp," audible especially as the filter cutoff was opened. Kept as a happy accident/"Easter egg" purely because it sounded great, not because it was the intended arpeggiator behavior. → [[Roland Juno-106]], [[Vintage Gear Worklist]]
- **Trust in a collaborator's already-finished song, over pushing a group-written idea, is what actually saved the session.** After three failed group-jam attempts on the last day of the studio week, the band pivoted to Young's already-written solo song (with light help from Conor) instead of continuing to force a new group idea — Solomon frames having someone he trusts to bounce ideas off, and believing in that person's song, as the single most important factor in the session landing, more than any technical choice made afterward.
- **Studio-level context (not a reusable technique, logged for completeness):** tracked over a week at 64 Sound in Highland Park, called out specifically for having "so much cool vintage gear" on hand — the Juno-106, MS-20, CS-60, and Wurlitzer used on the record likely came from the studio's own collection rather than being brought in.
- **Perfectionism/release-anxiety closer, stated as a general philosophy rather than a technique:** Solomon recounts being so insecure about an unfinished mix that he shut the song off halfway through playing it for his own parents ("the mix is not right!"); the video closes on the stated belief that in the social-media era you don't need to be a "super perfectionist" before releasing — you can put songs out and see what happens, and trust your gut over chasing a flawless mix.
