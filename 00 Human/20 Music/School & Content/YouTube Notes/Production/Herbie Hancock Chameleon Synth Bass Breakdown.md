# Herbie Hancock "Chameleon" Synth Bass Breakdown

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=p7q3xX2Adxs)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=p7q3xX2Adxs)

## Synopsis

A patch-recreation of the ARP Odyssey bass from Herbie Hancock's "Chameleon" (1973, Head Hunters), explicitly framed as a sound Anthony had chased unsuccessfully for years — until identifying the one missing ingredient: outboard preamp overdrive, not anything in the patch itself.

## Techniques

- **Dual-oscillator PWM "chorusing," both oscillators starting narrow and widening via envelope, deliberately offset in phase.** Both oscillators start on a narrow pulse width; the ADSR envelope is routed to modulate pulse width on each, sweeping from narrow toward "fat" over the envelope's shape — with the two envelopes' starting points offset slightly against each other so the pulse-width sweeps land out of phase. The resulting beating between two independently-widening PWM oscillators (kept in unison, not detuned) is what reads as "chorusing," not an actual chorus effect.
- **No keyboard tracking on the filter at all, and no portamento** — demonstrated A/B: adding tracking makes upper-register notes read "bright, just not a bass," confirming the standard rule of leaving bass filters untracked so the tone stays consistent (or slightly duller, mirroring a real bass) as you play higher.
- **Envelope shape: light attack, fast decay, moderate sustain, and a deliberate SHORT-but-nonzero release** — zero release would clip every note; a short release gives just enough "ring out" for the part to breathe on a single-trigger instrument.
- **The load-bearing ingredient: driving the synth into an outboard mic preamp's overdrive by riding the SYNTH's own output level, not a preamp gain knob.** A Great River preamp, pushed into distortion by the synth's own output fader level — described as needing to "find the right spot" where it starts "ripping." Without this stage, the patch is recognizably not the reference sound no matter how precisely everything else is dialed in — the distortion is part of the instrument's character, not optional coloring on top.
- **Phrasing has to be played, not just patched**: the actual part is a repeating long-long-long-SHORT rhythmic pattern (three held notes, the fourth deliberately clipped), and getting that phrasing right (on a single-trigger instrument that must be re-articulated per note) is as much a part of the sound as the settings.
