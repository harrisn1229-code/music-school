## Synopsis

Hen and Lou (Joy Anonymous) walk through three tracks — **Nobody Ever** (feat. Tamaraby), **Like You Used To** (feat. Motherboard/Kyle Kidd, with Tommy Champion on modular synth), and **Situation** (feat. Ezra Collective & Stush) — plus an outro clip of "She Made Me Feel." The through-line is a deliberately layered release workflow (source/main/7-inch/club versions of the same song, all sampled from their own raw jams rather than old records) and a house style built on capturing raw, imperfect, often phone-recorded material and keeping it mostly untouched rather than over-produced. Two genuinely unusual production moves stand out: using AI (Suno) to turn a sung voice-note into a placeholder reference track for live horn players to play along to, and releasing a spoken-word intro captured live at a gig within 48 hours of the show. Companion piece to [[TN 186 Joy Anonymous - Gear List]].

## Techniques

### Workflow: source/main/7-inch/club versions, sampling only themselves
- **Their whole creative pipeline starts from "Joy Journals" — loose, no-intention live jams recorded to a loop station, later mined as a private sample bank.** During a week-long writing retreat (Stornoway, Scotland), they'd record ~100+ unstructured loop-station jams with one mic in the room and no song goal, then over following years sample and reshape those jams into actual songs — explicitly framed as "creating our own sample bank of source material" specifically to sidestep the clearance/cost problems of sampling other people's records, while keeping "our own initial essence" in every track.
- **Every song gets released in up to four versions from the same source material: a "source version" (the rawest/most acoustic form), a "main version" (the polished single), a "7-inch version," and a "club version" (extra weight/techno elements added).** Not a remix afterthought — the source version is sometimes recorded *before* the main version (reverse-engineered from the polished version back to something rawer) and sometimes after. Framed as an intentional "show our workings" philosophy, and as extra content that's already release-ready without new writing.
- **A blind listening test for whether a new idea holds up: slip an unfinished sketch into a Bluetooth-speaker playlist of already-released music from other artists, without announcing it, and watch whether anyone reacts or asks about it.** Used specifically to get a reaction from "the media ecosystem" quickly, without the bias of people knowing it's theirs or unfinished.
- **General creative-process advice (from Q&A):** don't let "the pedestal" of what a release has to achieve keep growing — the fix is getting music in front of real people (a trusted friend, a small crowd, an open mic) as fast as possible rather than polishing in isolation; a song that gets a strong live reaction is a better signal than a room of producers nodding along in the studio. → **[[Creative Process and Idea Capture]]**.

### Nobody Ever (feat. Tamaraby)
- **Built around a barely-processed raw iPhone video of a collaborator singing solo in his garden, playing keys with one hand while holding the phone/mic in the other.** The vocal take is close to entirely untreated — "a slight bit of EQ, almost non-existent... just to give it a bit of gloss" — deliberately kept because the phone-recording's natural compression and room tone already had the character they wanted; chords were re-harmonized underneath the existing vocal to shift its emotional read, rather than reworking the vocal itself.
- **Drum philosophy: chase the *feeling* of "world-class huge drums" (their reference point: a Stardust-style dance record) through minimal, layered elements rather than a single big sample** — a restrained kick/groove pattern layered with a ringing-glass hit (a literal glass, mic'd) for a lo-fi "early Michael Jackson demo" character, deliberately not overpowering the vocal. Subtle techno-flavored drums are introduced later in the arrangement specifically to add weight for a "drop" section, contrasting against the sparse opening. → **[[Layering and Stacking]]**.
- **Deliberate phone-mic recording as a stylistic choice, not a workaround.** Both this and the *Like You Used To* source jam were tracked largely on iPhone mics on purpose — "the compressor on the iPhone is phenomenal," and the captured room noise/imperfection (footsteps, table-knock texture) is treated as a feature they actively lean into rather than clean up. → **[[Ambient Texture and Field Recording]]**.

### Like You Used To (feat. Motherboard/Kyle Kidd, Tommy Champion)
- **Drums built almost entirely from Forever 89 Visco (VSCO), used across "three layers" for the main kit.** Praised specifically for its hands-on character (record a long, loose take and find the usable bit afterward, same "long take → find the hook" philosophy applied to drums as to vocals), a strong built-in filter, time-stretching, and a full-randomize function used deliberately to generate "happy accidents." → [[Plugin Wishlist]].
- **Vocal chain kept deliberately minimal/untreated, and Auto-Tune avoided on original takes on principle** — "we tried to keep auto-tune off original takes as much as possible... you wanted to put your heart into it," reserving correction for later if genuinely needed rather than defaulting to it while singing. Light touch of [[Decapitator]] ("dirty dog crystallizer" in the auto-caption — likely a blend of Decapitator + the Safari Pedals Dirty Dog plugin) plus [[MicroShift]] for width — "nothing crazy, really wanted to let their voice sit on top."
- **"The Hentai-cizer" — their own voice, heavily pitched and layered with an octave doubler, used as a substitute for a real horn section.** Built from a clean, dry vocal take run through a chain they describe as "a combination of Safari Pedals and some Soundtoys stuff" — [[MicroShift]] specifically named ("I love"), plus the Safari Pedals Dirty Dog plugin for "great distortion" that makes it "start feeling otherworldly," then doubled with an octave layer. The explicit reasoning: a synth patch can be copied by anyone, but a processed human voice "can't be copied" — used as a deliberate signature/ownership device, not just a sound-design shortcut. → **[[Layering and Stacking]]**, [[Sound Selection Guide]].
- **A genuine happy-accident bass patch: a Logic "Session Player" preset meant for auto-generating acoustic drum fills ("Acoustic Drama Pop"), used by mistake as a bass sound instead of a drum part**, discovered by varying the fill-amount/complexity controls on something that was never meant to produce a bass tone at all — kept because it worked, not reverse-engineered afterward.
- **Coda Labs Szechuan Saturator (free) used on a modular-synth bass/chord part specifically to "chop everything up nicely."** The part itself: Tommy Champion playing live through a Moog Matriarch feeding a larger modular rig, tracked as a genuine jam rather than programmed — his modular rig was later brought on stage for a live performance of the song. → [[Plugin Wishlist]].
- **A hardware 4-pad dub siren used as their main source for arpeggiated 808-style bass sounds, run through what's described as "an amazing filter."** Used both in the studio and live; credited as a favorite recent discovery, distinct from a software 808/bass patch.
- **Live rig built around a 10-year-old Native Instruments Traktor Kontrol S8, used specifically for its stem-separation faders — treated as a DJ mixer for their OWN back-catalog stems rather than a DJ tool for other people's tracks.** This lets them mix/loop/re-key stems of their own songs live instead of playing a static backing track, described as "a real breakthrough" that let them communicate and improvise on stage the way a full band would, rather than singing over a fixed track. → **[[Live Performance Setup]]**.
- **Live setup also includes a Roland TR-1000 drum machine** (their studio owned/used version, praised as "a very alive machine" they're still "digging deeply into" — see [[TN 187 Overmono - Gear List]] for the same unit) **and a bandmate (Daniel/"Danio") live-sampling vinyl records into an MPC on stage** to build ambient beds and needle-drop "easter eggs" mid-set, plus the RC-505 loop station for building live ambience. → **[[Live Performance Setup]]**.

### Situation (feat. Ezra Collective & Stush)
- **An AI-generated placeholder track (Suno) used as a rehearsal reference for live horn players — sung a rough voice-note melody idea, ran it through Suno to generate a synth "AI horn" version, then had Ezra Collective's horn section play chords/parts along to that synth reference in the studio rather than reading sheet music.** Framed with visible discomfort about the tool ("the scary time of trying this... had to look at this head-on"), used strictly as a disposable pre-production reference, not as a source that ends up on the record — the released horns are 100% the live players. → **[[Creative Process and Idea Capture]]**.
- **Horn section recorded live at Miloko Studio (Bermondsey) using a matched pair of Coles 4038 ribbon mics plus a vintage Telefunken-style omni (recalled as a U87) from an earlier session — multiple mic types on the same live horn take, different players layered as harmonies rather than doubled on one part.** → **[[Vintage Gear Worklist]]**.
- **A full, real drum take (from Ezra Collective's drummer + a second player on bass/drums) deliberately thinned down with a transient shaper to sit as texture/energy rather than a competing full kit.** Recorded as a complete drum performance, then processed with the LHI Audio ST4B transient shaper to strip out low end and "frequency shift" the drums for an "alien"/slightly phased character — the goal was to keep the *feel* and top-end energy of a real player without the recording taking up the low-end space the main programmed drums needed. Explicitly signed off by the drummer himself once he heard the intent ("you might not even need my drums here" / "I like hearing your energy in there"). → **[[Compression Staging]]** (transient-shaping-as-arrangement-tool angle), [[Plugin Wishlist]].
- **A viral, gig-captured spoken-word intro, turned around from live performance to release in under 48 hours.** Femi (Ezra Collective) improvised a spoken intro live on stage at a festival show; their sound engineer happened to be recording, the clip was licensed/pulled the next day, and the finished track was out two days after the performance — a deliberate "capture the moment and ship immediately" workflow, explicitly reasoned as a way to give a released track scarcity/authenticity value ("this is my song, I was there") in a landscape where music can feel disposable. → **[[Creative Process and Idea Capture]]**.
- **General collaboration technique: send session players a sung voice-note reference/guide rather than sheet music or a demo mix**, and let them interpret it in one take — repeated as their default way of briefing outside musicians (also used for the Tamaraby/Kyle Kidd vocal collaborations).
