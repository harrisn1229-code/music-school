# Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords

Part of [[Jazz Harmony]] and [[Seventh Chords]]. Distinct from — but related to — [[Upper Structure Chords]]: that page's "upper structure" is a *different* chord dropped over a foreign bass note
(a reharmonization/polychord device). This page's upper structure is narrower and more literal: the
**triad already sitting inside a seventh chord's own 3rd, 5th, and 7th**, extracted and played as a
substitute/simplification for the full four-note chord. Same word, two different jazz devices — don't
conflate them.

## The derivation

Any seventh chord = a root-3rd-5th triad plus a 7th stacked on top. Instead of reading the chord as
root+triad+7th, read the **top three notes** (3rd, 5th, 7th) as their own triad — that triad is playable
on its own, on a completely different part of the neck from the root, as a lighter-weight stand-in for
the full seventh chord. Worked example: **C minor 7** = C-E♭-G-B♭; the 3-5-7 stack (E♭-G-B♭) is an
**E♭ major triad** — so any E♭ major triad shape already known on the fretboard is fair game to play
over Cm7, comping or soloing, with no need to grip the full four-note chord.

## The four chord-quality rules

Each seventh-chord quality produces a fixed upper-structure triad quality, independent of key — learn
these four once and they transfer to any tune, any key:

| Seventh-chord quality | Upper structure triad | Relationship to root |
|---|---|---|
| Minor 7 | Major triad | Minor 3rd above the root |
| Dominant 7 | Diminished triad | Major 3rd above the root |
| Major 7 | Minor triad | Major 3rd above the root |
| Minor 7♭5 (half-diminished) | Minor triad | Minor 3rd above the root |

*(general note: not stated as a formal rule in the source video, but implied by all four worked
examples — the pattern holds because the upper-structure triad is built from exactly the two notes that
define seventh-chord quality, the 3rd and 7th, plus the 5th; cross-reference [[Guide Tones]].)*

## One level up: a 7th chord as the upper structure of a rootless 9 chord

The same derivation generalizes one chord size up: stack a key's tonic **9 chord** as 1-3-5-7-9, drop the root, and the remaining 3-5-7-9 are exactly the four notes of that key's **iii chord** — a minor 7 chord. So any minor-7 shape you already know doubles as a **rootless major 9** voicing of the I chord a major 3rd below its own root. Worked example: **E minor 7** = the 3-5-7-9 of **C major 9** (E-G-B-D against an implied/absent C) — play an Em7 grip over (or without) a C bass and it reads as Cmaj9 with no root. **Soloing payoff, stated directly:** arpeggiate the iii-minor-7 (Em7) over the I chord (Cmaj7) instead of the plain 1-3-5-7 arpeggio of the I chord itself, for a "fresher" sound — the same target-tone-substitution logic as this page's own upper-structure-triad soloing application, just one chord size bigger. — Jared Borkowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared), [[Rootless Voicings - WOW!]]

## Beyond the fixed rule — searching all diatonic triads for options

The four-row rule table above gives *one* answer per chord quality (built from the 3rd-5th-7th
specifically), but it's a special case of a broader search: relate **every** diatonic triad in the
chord's parent scale back to the chord's root in scale-degree terms, then keep whichever ones avoid an
unwanted tension — this is how you find the *other* usable upper structures beyond the one the rule
table hands you, and it's the only workable method on non-tertian scales (diminished, altered) where
"just take the 3-5-7" doesn't apply. Worked both ways below.

**Dm7, all seven diatonic triads of its parent scale related to D as root:**

| Triad | Relationship to D | Usable? |
|---|---|---|
| C major | 7-9-11 | ✅ |
| D minor | root-♭3-5 | — it's just the chord itself, not a useful "upper structure" |
| E minor | 9-11-13 | ❌ contains the 13th |
| **F major** | **♭3-5-7** | ✅ — this is the fixed rule-table answer (minor 3rd above root) |
| G major | 11-13-root | ❌ contains the 13th |
| **A minor** | **5-7-9** | ✅ |
| B diminished | 13-root-♭3 | ❌ contains the 13th |

Filtering out every triad containing an unwanted 13th leaves **three** usable upper structures for
Dm7 — C major (7-9-11), F major (the rule-table one, ♭3-5-7), and A minor (5-7-9) — not just the one
the fixed rule predicts.

**Cmaj7, same method, filtering out the 11th (F) as an avoid-note against a major 7 chord:**

| Triad | Relationship to C | Usable? |
|---|---|---|
| C major | root-3-5 | — the chord itself |
| D minor | 9-11-13 | ❌ contains the 11th (F) |
| **E minor** | **3-5-7** | ✅ |
| F major | 11-13-root | ❌ contains the 11th (F) |
| **G major** | **5-7-9** | ✅ |
| **A minor** | **13-root-3** | ✅ |
| B diminished | 7-9-11 | ❌ contains the 11th (F) |

**Scale-choice matters as much as the filter:** swapping the parent scale from C major (Ionian) to
**C Lydian** (♯4/♯11 instead of natural F) removes the F avoid-note constraint entirely, which reopens
D minor and F major as usable Cmaj7 upper structures too — the same filtering method, a different scale
input, a bigger output set. This is why [[Diatonic Modes]] and [[Scale Modes Reference]] matter for this
technique, not just the chord-quality rule table.

**Altered dominant (G7alt), diminished-scale derivation — no diatonic stack, so relate-to-root is the
only method:** the (half-whole) diminished/octatonic scale doesn't stack into thirds the normal way
because it's symmetrical rather than tertian, so [[Diminished Chords]]' scale is searched by direct
relation instead. Because the scale repeats every minor 3rd, its usable major/minor triad pairs also
repeat every minor 3rd — **E major/E minor, G major/G minor, B♭ major/B♭ minor, D♭ major/D♭ minor**,
eight triads total. The triad built on the dominant's own root (G major/G minor) is skipped since it
adds no color beyond the plain chord tone; the other three pairs (six triads) are the real menu, giving
a wide choice of ♭9/♯9/♭13-flavored upper structures over one altered dominant — see [[Altered Chords]]
for the tension vocabulary these triads imply.

## Finding voicings on the neck (mechanical / fretboard method)

Once a triad choice is settled theoretically, there's a second, purely physical way to generate more
voicings from it without re-deriving anything: take a triad shape you already know in one fretboard
position, **invert it up the neck into a new position**, then find the nearest available guide tone
(root/3rd/7th) to complete the chord at that new position. Example: the Dm7 voicing above (C major
triad + added F) — invert the C major triad shape to the next position up the neck, then look for the
closest F to add underneath; repeat at further positions to build a full fretboard map of the same
Dm11(9) sound using one triad and several completions. This is the same three-zone logic as
[[Triads as a Fretboard Map]], just run in the opposite direction — start from a known shape and search
for its next position, rather than starting from a scale degree and deriving the shape. Useful once the
theory-driven search above gets slow to run live.

## Worked example — Autumn Leaves (all seven chords)

| Chord | Quality | Upper structure triad |
|---|---|---|
| C minor 7 | minor 7 | E♭ major |
| F dominant 7 | dominant 7 | A diminished |
| B♭ major 7 | major 7 | D minor |
| E♭ major 7 | major 7 | G minor |
| A minor 7♭5 | half-diminished | C minor |
| D7 | dominant 7 | F♯ diminished |
| G minor 7 | minor 7 | B♭ major |

Chosen deliberately as a teaching progression because it touches all four seventh-chord qualities in
one eight-bar run, making it a compact drill for all four rules at once.

## Prerequisite — know the triad shapes across the fretboard first

The technique is only usable once each resulting triad is mapped across the neck — a triad is a
three-note shape that repeats in **three close-position zones**, exactly the system taught in
[[Triads as a Fretboard Map]] (worked there in E major for blues soloing; the positional method is
identical, just applied here to jazz upper-structure triads instead of blues major triads). Without the
fretboard map, "Cm7's upper structure is E♭ major" is trivia, not a playable idea — map the triad first,
then apply it.

## Two applications: comping and soloing are the same shapes at two speeds

- **Comping** — string the upper-structure triads together as the comping voicing in place of full
  seventh-chord grips. Start on one string set (demonstrated on D-G-B) playing straight through the
  progression, one upper-structure triad per chord; once comfortable, move freely between fretboard
  zones and string sets chord to chord rather than staying pinned to one set/position.
- **Soloing** — the same triad shapes double as a chord-tone target map for single-note lines, outlining
  each chord almost entirely from its upper-structure triad's three notes as the progression moves —
  the same "target the chord tones" logic as [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]'s Step 4 (outline every chord's
  chord tones), narrowed specifically to the upper-structure triad rather than the full four-note chord.
  Build toward a fuller solo by adding connective/passing tones once the triad skeleton is solid, and by
  moving across fretboard zones and string sets instead of staying in one spot — the triad tones are the
  skeleton, not the whole vocabulary.

## Practice sequence

1. Derive the upper-structure triad for each chord in the target progression using the four rules
   above.
2. Map each resulting triad across the three fretboard zones ([[Triads as a Fretboard Map]]'s method).
3. Comp through the progression on one string set, then vary string sets/zones.
4. Solo using the same triads as a target map — one string set first, then with movement and connective
   tones layered on.
