# these 1970's soul secrets will change how you make melodies!?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oRLzsshf6yg)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oRLzsshf6yg)

## Synopsis

loner dissects two Marvin Gaye records — "Ain't No Mountain High Enough" and "Your Precious Love" — element by element (drums, chords, vibraphone, bass, electric guitar, vocals, strings), then rebuilds a full 70s-soul-style sample from scratch using the findings. The most valuable material is the recurring theme that the "vintage" character isn't a plugin preset, it's a set of period *recording limitations* deliberately reimposed: hand-played (unquantized) drums for real swing, heavy bus compression that levels every peak, dead-simple amp-only processing on bass/guitar instead of a modern multi-stage chain, unprocessed vocal samples (no auto-tune, no delay — those didn't exist yet for this era), and — the standout technique — physically recreating cross-mic bleed by printing a reel-to-reel tape pass with the noise/mic circuit engaged, pitching that hiss up an octave, and burying it quietly under the mix so the whole sample reads as one live room take rather than a stack of separately-recorded VST layers. Two structural/arrangement lessons carry independently of the era: the bass is written as a genuinely melodic line rather than root-note support (a 70s/80s convention that inverted in modern production, where bass mostly just hits), and doubling the bass line note-for-note on electric guitar is used deliberately as a fullness/thickness trick, not a mistake to avoid.

## Techniques

**Drums:**
- **Play drums in by hand, don't quantize, when recreating a swung acoustic-era feel.** To match "Ain't No Mountain High Enough"'s kick/hi-hat/perc pattern, the part was performed live rather than programmed-then-quantized — the swing IS the character, and grid-snapping it away loses the thing being recreated. → [[Pocket and Microtiming]], [[Rhythm and Groove]]
- **Addictive Drums 2, "the gayest preset" — a kit voiced specifically to Marvin Gaye's sound** — the drum source for the whole flip; three audible elements on the reference (kick, hi-hat, perc) kept the arrangement deliberately sparse. Unowned — see [[Plugin Wishlist]]. → [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **Heavy bus compression to level every peak, not just glue the transients.** The reference track's drum waveform shows peaks sitting at nearly the same level across the whole performance — a much harder compression than a typical modern glue pass; a compressor was run across the recreated drums to match. *(general starting point — no ratio/threshold given in the source)* → [[Compression]], [[Compression Staging]]

**Chords / piano:**
- **A vintage/warm upright piano tone, then compensate with EQ + compression rather than fighting the source tone.** loner loaded "SSS Vintage Upright" (name as heard in captions — likely the same vendor's, XLN Audio, given Addictive Drums was already in use this session; not confidently identified, flag before buying) specifically for its tone, then added a compressor to bring the level back up (the vintage voicing reads quieter/softer than a modern piano patch) and an EQ cutting some low end (the source tone runs boomy). → [[EQ]], [[Compression]], [[Plugin Wishlist]], [[BED Slot]]

**Vibraphone:**
- **A vibraphone carrying a catchy top-line, written with a percussive, repeated-note rhythm rather than a sustained melodic phrase.** Sits alongside the piano; the deliberate choice to repeat notes in an almost-percussive pattern is what gives the part its "bounce," distinct from a legato melodic read. → [[Melody Writing]], [[Layering and Stacking]]

**Bass:**
- **Write the bassline as a genuinely melodic part, not root-note support — a 70s/80s convention, inverted from how most modern songs use bass.** Called out directly: most modern songs use the bass to hit hard (rhythmic/low-end role); in 70s/80s soul the bass was a second melodic voice with real movement, second only to the vibraphone in "catchiness." → [[Bassline Writing]], [[Bass Design]], [[BASS Slot]]

**Electric guitar:**
- **Double the bass line note-for-note on electric guitar, as a deliberate fullness trick.** "Your Precious Love"'s guitar plays the exact same notes as the bass — having two different-timbre instruments carry the identical melody line is called out explicitly as a technique for "a really full sound," not doubling by accident. → [[Layering and Stacking]], [[Bassline Writing]]

**Simple period-accurate amp processing (bass + guitar):**
- **Run both bass and guitar through a single amp-sim stage instead of a modern multi-processor chain — because 70s studios had a much narrower range of equipment and mostly just ran things through amps.** AmpliTube used on both the bass and the electric guitar for this reason — one simple coloring stage each, not stacked saturation/EQ/comp like a modern chain. Unowned — see [[Plugin Wishlist]]. → [[Saturation and Distortion]]

**Vocals:**
- **Source period-appropriate vocal one-shots by explicitly searching for MINIMAL processing, and reject anything with auto-tune or delay on it.** Splice search: "Soul vocals," filtered by ear for samples with little/no baked-in processing — auto-tune and delay are period-inaccurate for a 70s soul flip and were deliberately screened out. → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]], [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **A vintage-character mic plugin on the vocal layers, for period color.** Each vocal sample was run through a plugin heard/captioned as "King's Microphone" (name uncertain — auto-captions; vendor unconfirmed, flag before vetting) specifically to add "a bit more of a vintage sound." → [[Plugin Wishlist]], [[Vocal Mic Technique]]

**Room bleed / mic-bleed simulation — the headline technique:**
- **Recreate cross-mic bleed (the sound of every instrument recorded together in one room, leaking into every other mic) by printing a physical-tape-style pass with the mic/hiss circuit engaged, then pitching it up and burying it under the mix.** Noticed on every stem of the reference Marvin Gaye songs: the other instruments audibly bleed into each individual stem, a direct byproduct of tracking everything live together. Recipe: put a tape-emulation plugin ("Reels," used elsewhere on this vault as a tape/noise-envelope tool — see [[Plugin Wishlist]]) on the master bus, turn ON its mic-noise button, turn UP the hiss, bounce that pass to audio, pitch the resulting bounce UP an octave, then lay it quietly in the background on top of the full sample — the hiss-and-bleed texture reads as "recorded together" glue rather than a stack of isolated VST layers. → [[Ambient Texture and Field Recording]], [[Layering and Stacking]], [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **Add a separate crowd-noise layer (sourced from Splice) alongside the tape-bleed layer, to finish the "live room" illusion.** A second, distinct ambience layer from a crowd-noise sample rather than relying on the tape-hiss layer alone. → [[Ambient Texture and Field Recording]]

**Strings:**
- **A staccato string layer following the bassline's melody, not the chord progression, chosen because it read as "more catchy."** On "Ain't No Mountain High Enough" the reference staccato string follows a different line; in the rebuild, loner deliberately chose to have the staccato strings shadow the bass melody instead, judged by ear as the catchier of the two options. → [[Melody Writing]], [[Bassline Writing]]
- **A second, sustained string layer following the chord progression** — a plain legato pad-like string part just tracking the chords, layered under/against the staccato line above for contrast (short/rhythmic vs. long/sustained doing two different jobs). → [[Melody Writing]], [[Layering and Stacking]]

**Final mix/finishing moves:**
- **Pitch the entire finished sample DOWN by four semitones as the very last step, specifically so the vocal layers land back at their original recorded pitch.** The vocals were tracked/chosen at their natural pitch, then the whole composite sample (drums, chords, vibraphone, bass, guitar, strings, bleed layer) was built a few semitones up from that target key; pitching everything down 4 semitones at the end re-aligns the vocal to its natural, undetuned pitch while the rest of the arrangement lands in the target key. → [[Pitch and Formant]], [[Sampling and Sound Selection]]
- **A second tape pass (Reels) across the whole finished sample after the pitch-down, purely for a final vintage-tape character on top of everything else.** Distinct from the earlier bleed-simulation tape pass (which built a background ambience layer) — this is a straight color pass over the finished mix. → [[Plugin Wishlist]], [[Saturation and Distortion]]
