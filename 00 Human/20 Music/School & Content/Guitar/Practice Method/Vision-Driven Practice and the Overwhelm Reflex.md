# Vision-Driven Practice and the Overwhelm Reflex

Part of [[Song Practice]]'s practice-methodology material — Jared Borkowski's answer, spread across four separate Q&A answers in the same video, to "how do I stay organized when there's infinite guitar material to practice." The throughline: **there is no objectively required practice material — only material in service of a genuinely felt, specific goal ("vision")** — and managing the resulting overwhelm is itself a skill to deliberately train, not a one-time organizational fix. Complements [[Interleaved Practice, Chunking, and Deliberate-Practice Drills]] (Daniel Seriff's session-structuring mechanics) and [[The Five Essentials of Guitar Practice]] (Seriff's what-to-cover framework) — this note is closer to the *why* layer underneath those *how* layers: it doesn't prescribe a session structure, it prescribes a way of choosing what belongs in one at all.

## Nothing is objectively required — only conditional on a stated goal

**Core rule, stated directly: "there's nothing you need to practice, unless you have a big if."** No single skill — reading music, playing chords, single-note lines, sight-reading — is mandatory to be a great or successful musician; cites Paul McCartney (doesn't read music, arguably as successful as a musician gets) as the counter-proof to "you have to learn to read." The "if" is always a specific, named goal: *if* you want to join a guitar quartet that requires reading, *then* practice reading. Before adding anything to a practice list, **name the "if" it's actually in service of** — a piece of material with no attached goal is exactly the kind of thing that produces the "lots of jazz chords, but I'm not making music" disorganized feeling this note starts from.

## Disorganized practice is usually a vision problem, not a discipline problem

Diagnosed directly against a self-described case (lots of teachers, lots of Santana riffs, lots of jazz chords, but "not making music," feeling disorganized): the fix isn't more material or more discipline, it's a **genuinely clear end point.** Concrete framing questions to actually answer, not just consider:
- What kind of music do you want to play? What inspires you the most?
- What's a specific, finished, start-to-finish piece of music you want to be able to play?
- What does "done" mean for it — comfortable performance level? A rough take recorded on your phone? Just understanding the theory of it? All are legitimate, but pick one *on purpose*.

Once the goal is rock-solid clear, the steps in between either become obvious or can be deliberately invented. Practicing whatever material an outside source (a teacher, a video, "someone online") recommended, with no felt personal connection to *why*, is what produces the hectic/disorganized feeling — the fix is reconnecting each practice item to a real, personally-held goal, not abandoning structure altogether.

## "Directness" — learn what you can apply right now, not what seems generically useful

**Named concept, credited to Scott Young's book *Ultra Learning*: directness.** Don't learn something because it seems like a good thing to know in the abstract — learn something you have a **direct, immediate application for.** The mechanism: applying a skill right away is what makes it actually stick as learning rather than sitting as inert, stockpiled information. Practical filter for any new practice item: can I use this for something I actually want to do soon? If not, it may not be worth the practice slot right now even if it's "good to know" in general.

## Start the hard goal now, adapted down — don't wait to "be ready"

Answering whether to chase an ambitious goal immediately (a full solo chord-melody arrangement of a jazz standard) or wait until finishing prerequisite material first: **go toward the exciting, inspiring goal right away — don't wait.** The one caveat is to **adapt it down to your current skill level rather than attempting the full difficult version cold**: play just the melody, play it much slower, learn just the roots of the chords running through it, and build up from there. "You can manipulate anything difficult to make it easier" — and getting practice specifically at *doing that adaptation* (watering a hard goal down into something currently playable) is a transferable skill in its own right, separate from the piece itself. The trap this avoids: practicing prerequisite material for a long stretch ("a year") before ever touching the actual goal, by which point the goal-connection and motivation that made it worth doing in the first place has usually faded.

## Rotate practice material — and come back right before you'd forget it

A second, independent piece of the same answer: **rotate through multiple practice items rather than finishing one before starting the next.** Could be as few as three things or as many as a hundred — your call, and it can be either loosely inspiration-driven (jump around by feel) or strict (a set rotation order). The discipline that actually matters: **come back to each item right before you'd have forgotten it** — that's framed as the single highest-leverage timing for a return rep, far better than either over-massing one item to full mastery before moving on, or leaving something so long between touches that it has to be relearned from scratch. The alternative (nail something once, then don't touch it again for a long stretch) reliably loses the material. *(This is the same underlying spaced-return mechanism as [[Interleaved Practice, Chunking, and Deliberate-Practice Drills]]'s interleaving/retrieval-practice logic from a different channel — two teachers converging on "rotate and return before forgetting" as the core lever, independent of session-length or timer mechanics.)*

## Overwhelm itself is a skill to practice, not a problem to solve once

The final, capstone answer in the video, framed as the big question saved for last on purpose: **the amount of available guitar material is functionally infinite, so feeling overwhelmed by it is the default state, not a sign something is wrong with your organization.** "You're never going to practice everything ever" — the reframe on offer is to **treat not-getting-overwhelmed as its own muscle to deliberately train**, the same way you'd train a scale or a technique, rather than as a one-time checklist or planning problem to be solved and closed out.

- **Practical, moment-to-moment gauge (not a comprehensive plan):** if a practice session — even a short one, 20 minutes — makes time slip away and leaves you feeling nourished/excited rather than dutiful, that's the right material for right now. This is a felt check, run per-session, not a long-range curriculum decision.
- **Framing:** close to a meditative practice — quiet the noise of "everything there is to do" and narrow to the one thing in front of you. The target feeling is described as "this is exactly where I'm supposed to be right now," explicitly contrasted with the stress-feeling of being pulled in many directions simultaneously; landing on the former is treated as evidence the overwhelm-management is working, independent of how much material got covered.
- **Standing personal habit as an anchor:** Jared runs the identical specific warm-up every single time he sits down to play, without skipping it — offered as one concrete way to give a session a stable, low-decision starting point before the "what should I even work on" question has to be answered at all.
