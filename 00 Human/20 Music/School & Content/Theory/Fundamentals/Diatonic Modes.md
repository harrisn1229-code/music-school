# Diatonic Modes

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Scales]]. The 7 modes generated by starting the major scale on each of its own scale degrees — same 7 pitches, different tonic, different mood. For the modes of melodic/harmonic minor and other non-diatonic scales, see [[Scale Modes Reference]] (that page assumes these seven as the baseline "anchor" set).

## The seven modes (built from C major / the white keys)

| # | Mode | Starts on (of C major) | Formula vs. major | Character |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ionian | C | 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 (= the major scale) | Happy, bright, resolved — the "default" major sound |
| 2 | Dorian | D | 1 2 ♭3 4 5 6 ♭7 | Melancholy but hopeful — minor with a brighter natural 6th |
| 3 | Phrygian | E | 1 ♭2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7 | Dark, tense, exotic — the ♭2 gives a flamenco/metal edge |
| 4 | Lydian | F | 1 2 3 ♯4 5 6 7 | Dreamy, floating, fantastical — the raised 4th is the signature note |
| 5 | Mixolydian | G | 1 2 3 4 5 6 ♭7 | Bright but unresolved — major with a bluesy/rock ♭7; reads as dreamy/romantic |
| 6 | Aeolian | A | 1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7 (= natural minor) | Sad, serious, cinematic — the default "minor" sound |
| 7 | Locrian | B | 1 ♭2 ♭3 4 ♭5 ♭6 ♭7 | Unstable, unresolved — the only mode with a diminished tonic triad; rarely used as a true home key |

## Modal ambiguity — when the tonic itself is genuinely disputed

All 7 modes above share the exact same 7 notes (the white keys) — they differ only in which of those notes is treated as the tonic. That means **the same seven-note collection can plausibly be heard as any of its 7 modes**, and real songs sometimes leave the choice genuinely unresolved rather than settled: different listeners latch onto different notes as "home," and there's no single objectively correct answer — it isn't listener error on either side.

**Three factors that create or resolve the ambiguity:**

1. **A repeating chord loop with no resolution.** A song built on a 4-bar (or similar) rotating chord cycle that never breaks out into a resolving cadence gives the ear no moment of arrival to lock onto — different chords in the loop can each sound like "home" to different listeners, because the loop just keeps resetting instead of concluding.
2. **The melody's own tonic pull, independent of the chords.** A melody has its own implied tonic even unaccompanied — and the SAME chord loop, set to two different melodies, can point the ear toward two different tonic notes, because it's the melody, not just the harmony, doing a large share of the work.
3. **Whether all 7 notes of the shared collection actually get used.** Some of the 7 candidate modes differ from each other by only one note (e.g. E♭ major vs. A♭ major differ only in D natural vs. D♭) — if a song's melody happens to avoid that one distinguishing note entirely, there's no evidence left to settle which of two neighboring modes is "really" the key.

**The first chord has outsized influence, independent of all three factors above**: whatever chord opens the loop gets a "home-field advantage" simply by being the first thing the ear hears with zero prior context — even when it isn't the strongest candidate by the other criteria.

**A resolvability test, generalizable:** check whether any passage in the song states the ONE note that would distinguish the competing readings. If it does, the ambiguity resolves; if the song systematically avoids that note (deliberately or not), the dispute stays genuinely open. A companion song by the same artist, on the same chord loop, that eventually breaks out and resolves demonstrates the mechanism directly — the resolving version removes the ambiguity the looping version has.

**This is a perceptual switch, not a blend — like an optical illusion (duck/rabbit).** A trained listener can consciously shift which note they hear as tonic, but can't hear two readings simultaneously; recontextualizing lets you flip between them, not hold both at once.

## Two ways to find/build any mode

- **Relative approach** — play the same 7 notes as a known major scale, just start (and center the phrase) on a different degree: e.g. D Dorian = the white keys, but centered on D instead of C. Same pitch collection as [[Keys|C major]], different tonic → completely different mood. Fastest way to answer "what mode am I already implying with these notes?"
- **Parallel/formula approach** — build the mode off any root directly, by altering the major-scale formula on that root: e.g. Aeolian = major scale with ♭3 ♭6 ♭7 (exactly the natural minor formula in [[Scales]]). Fastest way to answer "I want THIS mood, starting on THIS specific note."

## Aeolian in depth (it's the one you'll use most)

- **Build it from its parallel Ionian**: take the major scale on the same root and flatten scale degrees 3, 6, and 7 a half-step (C Ionian → C Aeolian: 1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7). This is the [[Scales|natural minor]] formula.
- **Find it from a relative Ionian**: every major scale's Aeolian relative starts on that scale's own 6th degree (G Ionian → E Aeolian; C Ionian → A Aeolian) — same notes as finding the [[Keys|relative minor]].
- **The i–♭VI move is the essence of the sound.** Build the full diatonic harmony off any Aeolian tonic — i ii° ♭III iv v ♭VI ♭VII (the natural-minor pattern in [[Chords by Key]]) — but the two-chord fragment that instantly reads as "Aeolian" regardless of genre is the tonic moving to its ♭VI (often voiced as an inversion for a smoother top line): i → ♭VI. Cited as the backbone of somber cinematic scoring and also common in EDM as ♭VI–♭VII–i. → [[Chord Progressions]]
- **Ear-training the minor scale by drone: drill exactly the 3 degrees that differ from major (♭3, ♭6, ♭7)**, one at a time against a sustained tonic drone, since those are the only new feeling-states minor adds beyond what [[Scale Degree Ear Training|major-scale degree training]] already covers. Comparing adjacent pairs (5 vs. ♭6, 6 vs. ♭6, ♭3 vs. ♭6) sharpens the contrast — ♭3 reads as the degree that most defines "minorness" itself, while ♭6 and ♭7 are each their own distinct flavor rather than interchangeable "flat" degrees. The feeling-state of any given degree is octave-invariant and (for most listeners without perfect pitch) transfers unchanged to a new key. → [[Scale Degree Ear Training]] — Max Konyi, [[Essential Ear Training - Feeling the Minor Scale]]

## Telling natural minor (Aeolian) apart from harmonic minor by ear/eye

The two most common minor scales in pop/rock differ by exactly one degree — the 7th — but that one note changes the whole character and gives two reliable tests:
- **Check the v chord**: in natural minor (Aeolian) the 5th-degree chord is minor (v); in harmonic minor it's major (V) — because the raised 7th becomes that chord's 3rd. "Ain't No Sunshine" (Bill Withers, A minor) uses Em (v) → confirms Aeolian.
- **Check the 7th scale degree directly** in the melody: unaltered (G♮ in A minor) → Aeolian; raised (G♯) → harmonic minor. "Jolene" (Dolly Parton, C♯ minor) uses an unaltered 7th (B♮) → Aeolian. "Back to Black" (Amy Winehouse, D minor) uses a raised 7th (C♯), both in the melody and via major-A chords → harmonic minor.
- **A song can genuinely use both within itself** — "Sultans of Swing" (Dire Straits) switches between the raised and unaltered 7th at different points, and further mixing (Dorian, melodic minor) is common enough that "just describe it as being in the minor key" without committing to one variant is sometimes the more honest label than forcing a single scale name onto the whole song.
- **Character difference**: harmonic minor's raised 7th (a semitone from the octave) creates strong upward pull/resolution — directional, "wants to move." Aeolian's unaltered 7th has much weaker pull — laid-back, less resolved, more purely "sorrowful" than harmonic minor's brighter/stricter/more dramatic color. A/B comparison: "Losing My Religion" (R.E.M.) recomposed in harmonic minor loses its laid-back sorrow and gains a stricter, almost sinister edge.

## Dorian in depth

- **Build it from the parallel natural minor**: Dorian = natural minor (Aeolian) with the 6th degree raised a half-step (E Aeolian ♭6 = C natural → E Dorian ♮6 = C♯). That single raised 6th is the ONE note that distinguishes Dorian from plain natural minor — everything else about the two scales is identical, which makes it the note to listen/write for when deliberately invoking Dorian over Aeolian. Described as "a less sad minor scale" — brighter, more mystical/uplifting than Aeolian, without becoming major.
- **The Dorian vamp — i–IV** — a minor tonic moving to a *major* IV chord (the major IV is only possible in Dorian; Aeolian's iv is minor) is the fastest way to state Dorian in two chords: the minor i sets a minor key, the major IV introduces the raised 6th. Extremely common in funk (arguably more common there than plain Aeolian) and vamped for extended stretches in Pink Floyd. An alternative to the major IV: a **minor ii chord** built on the 2nd degree also carries the raised 6th (it's minor rather than diminished specifically *because* of that raised 6th) and gives the same Dorian color from a different chord. → [[Chord Progressions]]
- **Placement of the raised 6th signals intent.** Several songs deliberately withhold Dorian's 6th from the melody until a structurally important moment (the chorus) even when the chords have carried it from the start — "Blue Jeans" (Lana Del Rey), "Mad World" (Tears for Fears/Gary Jules, chords Dorian from the verse, vocal only touches the raised 6th in the chorus) — the delayed payoff lifts the chorus and sets it apart from the verse. Some songs never touch the 6th in the melody at all and stay Dorian purely via the chords ("Drive," R.E.M. — a major IV chord recontextualizes an otherwise ambiguous minor vocal melody as Dorian for the whole song). And some songs sit on an ambiguous pentatonic minor (touching neither Dorian's raised 6th nor Aeolian's flat 6th) until a specific structural moment resolves the ambiguity — "Woodstock" (Joni Mitchell) stays pentatonic until the end of the first chorus, when the vocal finally lands on the Dorian 6th.
- **Direct Dorian↔Aeolian contrast within one song is a deliberate mood device**, not just modal drift: "Eleanor Rigby" shifts from E Dorian (raised 6th) to E Aeolian (natural 6th) line to line, contributing to its unsettled, eerie mood; "Thriller" (Michael Jackson, written by Rod Temperton) sits mostly in C♯ Dorian but briefly dips a chord to the Aeolian minor iv for the same bright/bleak contrast effect.
- **Modal jazz vs. functional harmony**: "So What" (Miles Davis) doesn't use a chord progression at all — 16 bars of D Dorian, 8 bars of E♭ Dorian, 8 bars back to D Dorian. Even though the underlying chords could be spelled Dm7→E♭m7→Dm7, these are genuinely **mode changes, not chord changes** — closer to a key change than a ii-related chord substitution — with the soloist exploring each Dorian scale over a static tonal center rather than resolving through functional harmony. → [[Modal Jazz]]
- Other Dorian song examples: "Riders on the Storm" (The Doors, textbook intro — the descending keyboard line walks straight down E Dorian), "Scarborough Fair" (traditional, the "rosemary and thyme" melodic line lands on the Dorian raised 6th).

## Phrygian in depth

- **Build it from the parallel natural minor**: Phrygian = natural minor (Aeolian) with the 2nd degree lowered a half-step. That's the one note that separates it from Aeolian, and it's what gives Phrygian its dark, unnerving, exotic edge on top of Aeolian's plain minor bleakness — described as "all the bleakness of the minor scale, plus an extra streak of darkness." Doesn't suit typical pop music, but is a natural home in metal (Metallica's "Wherever I May Roam," "Harvester of Sorrow" — both feature the lowered 2nd directly in the riff) and hip-hop (Snoop Dogg's "Gin and Juice" — a two-chord i–♭II Phrygian vamp between the minor tonic and a chord built on the lowered 2nd; Kendrick Lamar's "Humble" — a single altered scale degree in an otherwise minimal piano riff carries the whole dark quality).
- **A song can state Phrygian in the chords/melody without the tonic note being unambiguous — because modal ambiguity is about which note reads as "home," not which notes are used.** Modes sharing a parent scale share every note; "New Person, Same Old Mistakes" (Tame Impala) uses only the notes of A♭ major, but different listeners genuinely hear different tonics (C, giving C Phrygian, vs. A♭, giving A♭ major) — the song sounds identical either way, only the felt tonal context changes, and the ambiguity itself is part of what reads as "modal."
- **A i(Phrygian)→major-V chord substitution resolves a Phrygian cadence far more strongly than staying diatonic** — "London Calling" (The Clash) sits mostly in E Phrygian, but resolves its chorus hook via a **D major** chord (borrowed from outside Phrygian, which would diatonically give D minor) moving to E minor. The borrowed major D's F♯ pulls up a single semitone to the E minor tonic's 3rd (G); the diatonic D minor's F♮ would have to travel a whole step to reach the same target, giving a visibly weaker pull. Generalizes: substituting a major chord for the mode's own diatonic minor chord one step below the tonic strengthens the cadence, at the cost of stepping outside the pure mode for that one chord.
- **Not every "exotic ♭2" scale outside the West is really Phrygian** — in Arabic/Middle Eastern music the equivalent lowered 2nd is often a genuine quarter-tone (roughly half a semitone, a "half-flat"), not the West's fixed semitone-lowered 2nd, so mapping it onto 12-tone Phrygian is an approximation, not a match (example: "Hadi Kedra" by Nass El Ghiwane, later sampled by The Chemical Brothers for "Galvanize"). See [[Harmonic Minor Modes]] § *From videos* for the closely-related-but-distinct **Phrygian dominant** (major 3rd) and **double harmonic/Hijaz** (major 3rd + raised 7th) scales, both frequently confused with plain Phrygian.

## Lydian in depth

- **Build it from the parallel major**: Lydian = major (Ionian) with the 4th degree raised a half-step. That raised 4th gives Lydian its bright, ethereal, "magical" quality — the standard color for film-score wonder/magic (Yoda's Theme, John Williams; the flying theme from *E.T.*, John Williams — both introduce the raised 4th mid-theme as the moment magic enters the music; *Back to the Future*, Alan Silvestri, transposes a Lydian motif upward for escalating awe). Rare in pop/rock but real: "Waltz No. 2" (Elliott Smith), "When We Dance" (Sting), "Possibly Maybe" (Björk), "Dancing Days" (Led Zeppelin — Lydian with an added blues ♭3, "a Lydian blues scale"), "Oceans" (Pearl Jam, shifts between two different Lydian keys section to section).
- **The i–II vamp** — tonic chord to a *major* II chord (only possible in Lydian; Ionian's ii is minor) is the efficient two-chord way to state Lydian: the tonic establishes the key center, the major II supplies the raised 4th. Seen in Yoda's Theme and other film-score Lydian passages.
- **Lydian is harmonically the LEAST stable mode, despite being called the "most stable" in some contexts** — the apparent contradiction resolves once you separate melodic stability (George Russell's Lydian Chromatic Concept, which the video sets aside) from harmonic stability (the concern here). Every major-scale mode contains one tritone; in Ionian it sits between the 4th and 7th degrees and resolves outward onto the two most consonant scale tones (3 and 1), which is exactly what makes the major scale feel so resolved. **In Lydian the tritone sits between the root and the 4th degree itself** — its most natural resolution (root held, 4th rising to 5th) only weakly implies the Lydian tonic; in practice a I→V move in Lydian tends to get re-heard as a IV→I move in the *plain major scale a 4th below*, collapsing the Lydian color back into ordinary Ionian. This is why almost no song commits to Lydian for its entire duration — it usually surfaces for one section, then resolves back to major.
- **Tonic ambiguity is common and demonstrable mid-song** — "Here Comes My Girl" (Tom Petty): verse vamps A(I)–B(II), A unambiguously reads as tonic (A Lydian); the chorus uses the identical seven notes but now E reads as tonic, since A Lydian and E major share every note — the song audibly shifts from Lydian to plain major without changing a single pitch, only which note the ear crowns "home." "Man on the Moon" (R.E.M.) does the same shift, C Lydian in the verse to G major in the chorus.
- **Pedaling the tonic note stabilizes an otherwise-unstable Lydian passage** — sustaining/repeating the root underneath the chords keeps the ear anchored despite Lydian's weak tritone resolution. "Sara" (Fleetwood Mac, Stevie Nicks) uses the *identical* chord progression to "Dreams" but pedals F throughout, which is what actually makes it F Lydian — whereas "Dreams" itself is commonly misidentified as F Lydian from its F–G vamp, but the melody clearly resolves to A, making it A Aeolian (chords vi–♭VII of C, not I–II of F Lydian).
- **Lydian dominant is a distinct, closely related scale — Lydian's raised 4th plus a *lowered* 7th** (plain Lydian keeps the natural 7th). Commonly confused with plain Lydian: The Simpsons theme (Danny Elfman) is often cited as Lydian for its raised 4th but is actually Lydian dominant, confirmed by its lowered 7th; "Pretty Ballerina" (The Left Banke) is a second real example.
- **A borderline case, useful as a "how strict is the label" test**: "Blue Jay Way" (The Beatles) uses all of C Lydian's notes but routinely swaps in a minor 3rd alongside the major 3rd — since the major 3rd is essential to Lydian's identity, the song only reads as Lydian in the moments it uses it, and isn't a clean single-scale example.

## Mixolydian in depth

- **Build it from the parallel major**: Mixolydian = major (Ionian) with the 7th degree lowered a half-step — "the major scale's cooler younger brother." Mellow/relaxed but still bold; probably MORE common than plain major in rock. Fully-committed examples: "Clocks" (Coldplay), "Bittersweet Symphony" (The Verve), "Royals" (Lorde). Chord-only (melody stays diatonic, the ♭7 chord alone carries the color): "Sweet Child O' Mine" (Guns N' Roses — a ♭VII chord does the work even though neither riff nor vocal touches the ♭7 note itself).
- **Why the lowered 7th softens resolution**: the major scale's 7th degree is a leading tone (a half-step below the tonic) that strongly pulls upward — the mechanism behind a perfect cadence's tension. Mixolydian's lowered 7th is now a whole step from the tonic (closer to the 6th degree than the root), so that pull weakens dramatically — tension/release still exist, but read as patient and laid-back rather than urgent. This is the harmonic reason Mixolydian riffs can loop indefinitely without feeling like they need to resolve (Gary Numan's "Cars" — the ♭7 riff never completes a cadence, so it "can just continue rolling forward forever"; the equivalent major-scale version of the same riff sounds finished after one pass).
- **The i–♭VII–IV–i vamp** — the signature efficient Mixolydian progression: tonic establishes the key, ♭VII makes the lowered-7th color unambiguous, then a perfect-4th descent to IV and back home to I. Heard in "Royals," "Sweet Child O' Mine," "It's a Long Way to the Top (If You Wanna Rock 'n' Roll)" (AC/DC). → [[Chord Progressions]]
- **Mixolydian sits halfway between major and the blues scale** — it shares the blues scale's lowered 7th but keeps major's natural 3rd and 5th (no minor 3rd, no ♭5), giving blues-adjacent songs a brighter edge than a full minor-blues scale would. "All Blues" (Miles Davis, *Kind of Blue*) is a modal-jazz example built on G Mixolydian.
- **Key-signature confusion is common because Mixolydian shares a key signature with the major scale a 4th below it** — "All Night Long" (Lionel Richie, fully A♭ Mixolydian) is frequently mislabeled D♭ major on sheet-music sites because both use the same 5-flat key signature; the signature only fixes which *notes* are in play, never which one is the tonic.
- **Mixing Mixolydian with a nearby mode mid-song as a light↔dark shift**: "Norwegian Wood" (Beatles) opens in E Mixolydian, then shifts to E Dorian (one degree darker — only the 3rd flattens) for the middle eight. "Burn the Witch" (Radiohead) opens vamping F♯ Mixolydian (tonic to ♭VII), then shifts at the chorus to F♯ Aeolian/minor (two degrees darker — both the 3rd and 6th flatten) for a much more pronounced bright-to-dark contrast than the Mixolydian→Dorian shift.

## Ionian in depth: triad quality per degree

Stacking every-other note of any Ionian scale (root, skip a note, add a note, skip, add) gives the same quality pattern in every major key:

| Degree | I | ii | iii | IV | V | vi | vii° |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quality | Major | minor | minor | Major | Major | minor | diminished |

- The quality is decided purely by counting half-steps inside the stacked triad: **4 + 3 half-steps = major**, **3 + 4 = minor**, **3 + 3 = diminished** (major/minor both total 7 half-steps root-to-top; diminished totals only 6 — the "odd one out," which is why vii° sounds unresolved and gets avoided/used only as a passing chord). Matches [[Chords]]; the full per-key table already lives in [[Chords by Key]].

## Writing a progression in any mode: direction, distance, quality

Three independent levers change how a chord-to-chord move feels, regardless of which mode you're in:
- **Direction** — moving *up* to the next scale-degree chord reads as uplift; moving *down* reads as a pull-back/held-in-place feeling.
- **Distance** — a small scale-degree jump (I→ii) is a gentle move; a large jump (I→vii°) is dramatic.
- **Quality** — the destination chord's own major/minor/diminished color changes the move independently of direction and distance (major→major feels different from major→minor even over the same distance).
Start a progression on the mode's own root chord to establish the tonic, then vary these three levers deliberately rather than picking the next chord at random. → [[Chord Progressions]]

## Mode per scale degree — the chord-quality view

The table at the top organises modes by *character*. This is the other way in: for a given key, which mode sits on each scale degree, and what chord quality that degree carries. Useful when you're already holding a progression and want to know which mode to solo in over each chord.

![[Modes by Scale Degree (handwritten).jpeg]]

| Degree | Major key | Mode | | Degree | Minor key | Mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | I△ | Ionian | | 1 | im7 | Aeolian |
| 2 | iim7 | Dorian | | 2 | ii⌀ | Locrian |
| 3 | iiim7 | Phrygian | | 3 | ♭III△ | Ionian |
| 4 | IV△ | Lydian | | 4 | ivm7 | Dorian |
| 5 | V7 | Mixolydian | | 5 | vm7 | Phrygian |
| 6 | vim7 | Aeolian | | 6 | ♭VI△ | Lydian |
| 7 | vii⌀ | Locrian | | 7 | ♭VII7 | Mixolydian |

Two things fall out of reading it this way:

- **The minor column is the major column rotated.** Natural minor is the major scale started on its 6th degree, so the minor key's mode sequence is the major key's sequence shifted by five — Aeolian, Locrian, Ionian, Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian. Same seven modes, same order, different entry point. This is the relative-minor relationship stated as a mode list.
- **Only one degree in each key carries a dominant 7th** — V7 in major, ♭VII7 in minor — and in both cases it's the Mixolydian degree. That's the same fact as "Mixolydian is major with a lowered 7th," seen from the chord side.

## Color tones — the fast ID shortcut
Each mode differs from its major/minor baseline by exactly ONE distinguishing scale degree — memorize that one note instead of the whole formula: **Ionian** = major baseline · **Mixolydian** = major + lowered 7th · **Lydian** = major + raised 4th · **Aeolian** = minor baseline (natural minor) · **Dorian** = minor + raised 6th · **Phrygian** = minor + lowered 2nd · **Locrian** = theoretical-only (diminished tonic triad, essentially never used as an actual home key). — Toby W. Rush, [[Music Theory for Musicians and Normal People - Enrichment|Music Theory for Musicians and Normal People]], p.55

## From videos

- **"Brightness," not "happiness," is the accurate term for the major/minor contrast — worked counterexamples in both directions.** Happy-sounding minor: "Move On Up" (Curtis Mayfield, built entirely from minor 7th chords) and the Tetris theme (upbeat despite minor tonality, credited mainly to its brisk staccato tempo). Sad-sounding major: "Everybody Hurts" (R.E.M.) and "When Somebody Loved Me" (Toy Story 2) — a distinct *tender/heartbreaking* sadness (brightness + warmth) vs. minor's more *mournful/hopeless* sadness. **Tempo/rhythmic feel demonstrated as a bigger mood driver than tonality itself**: identical notes played fast+staccato read as upbeat, the same notes slow+legato read as reflective — mood often comes from rhythm more than from major-vs-minor. **Cleanest A/B brightness comparison**: a parallel-key shift, same tonic directly contrasted in both modes back to back — "I'm Still Standing" (Elton John, B♭ minor verse → B♭ major chorus). → David Bennett Music Theory, [[Major isn't happy, Minor isn't sad]]
- **A second full "one piece per mode" real-composition example — different compositional choices from the King Gizzard album below, useful as a contrast set.** David Bennett recorded a 7-track solo-piano album live, one mode per track: **Dorian** (D) via the "Dorian vamp" i→IV with IV as a MAJOR chord, melody emphasizing the 2nd degree. **Phrygian** (E) via pedaling the ♭2 on the offbeat. **Lydian** (F) opens directly on the raised 4th to state the character note immediately, set in 3/4 specifically to keep it from blurring into the other (4/4) tracks. **Mixolydian** (G) set in 12/8 shuffle, reasoning that the ♭7's "bluesy edge" pairs naturally with a swung groove. **Aeolian** (A) built from Am7/Dm7 (not plain triads) for a Latin-inspired "jazzy, lush" read rather than a flatly sad one. **Locrian** (B) — a different fix from King Gizzard's chord-tone swap below: rather than altering the tonic chord, the approach here is melodic — since Locrian gives a melody nowhere satisfying to resolve, the only workable move is to stop expecting resolution and fully commit to the mode's unsettled character ("go full Halloween"); cites "Dust to Dust" (see [[A Song That Actually Uses Locrian]]) as the reference for that deliberate-instability use. → [[King Gizzard used EVERY mode on this album]] for the companion album and its different per-mode techniques — David Bennett Music Theory, [[I made an album in a day using ALL the modes]]
- **Why the white keys spell out all 7 modes for free, traced back to the Greek tetrachord.** Reframe: the white-key layout isn't built around C major specifically — it's built around the diatonic scale in general, and C major, A Aeolian, D Dorian, E Phrygian, F Lydian, G Mixolydian, and B Locrian all live on the same seven white keys with zero accidentals. Historical root: a **tetrachord** is 4 notes spanning a fixed perfect 4th with two locked outer notes and two movable inner notes; the **diatonic tetrachord** (tone-tone-semitone) is the type that produces our modern diatonic scale, and stacking four overlapping diatonic tetrachords built the ancient Greek "Greater Perfect System." **Practical takeaway**: since all 7 modes already exist on an unaltered keyboard, any mode can be explored just by starting/centering on a different white key — no new fingering or key signature required, only a different tonal center. → David Bennett Music Theory, [[Why does the piano begin on C not A]]
- **A full album, one track per mode — the richest real-arrangement source in the vault for how to actually establish each mode's character, not just define it on paper.** King Gizzard & the Lizard Wizard's *Ice, Death, Planets, Lungs, Mushrooms and Lava* (2022) dedicates one track to each of the 7 modes: **Ionian** (I–IV–V with a deliberately non-root-following bass, to avoid sounding too plain/"Disney"). **Dorian** — vamp between i and ii (Gm–Am); the ii chord is what supplies the defining raised 6th, locking the ear into Dorian rather than plain minor. **Phrygian** — drones on the tonic with occasional bass turnarounds, no real chord progression (the standard way to present modal, as opposed to tonal, music — sit still on a center rather than navigate between chords). **Lydian** — tonic drone, then a I→"II/I" vamp (G/F over F) dubbed "the Lydian chord," since a II triad over the tonic bass puts the raised 4th directly in the chord while keeping the true tonic anchored underneath — anchoring the tonic bass matters more here than in other modes because the sharp 4th constantly pulls the ear toward hearing the parent major key instead. **Mixolydian** — a I7–vi7–IIadd9–I loop, leaned into for the whole 13-minute track rather than ever cadencing away, since the lowered 7th removes the leading-tone pull a major scale would have. **Aeolian** — plain natural minor, vi–i opening vamp; - **Two valid-but-different ways modes get taught, named explicitly as the source of most mode confusion: scalar methodology vs. tonal center methodology.** Scalar = a mode is a scale shape starting on a different fretboard position (the "relative approach" above) — flagged as *incomplete* on its own because a shape without a grounding chord progression underneath doesn't establish which note actually feels like home. Tonal center = the mode you actually hear is decided by which note the underlying chord progression makes feel like "home," independent of which notes you play over it. **Demonstrated by direct audio A/B**: the identical G major scale, played note-for-note the same way, was played over three different backing tracks — over a G-rooted track it read as G Ionian, over an E-rooted track (same 7 notes) it read as E Aeolian, over an A-rooted track it read as A Dorian. Practical diagnostic: to identify a mode by ear inside a progression, find which chord functions as the tonal center — that chord's root names the mode (e.g. a D–C–G progression, all diatonic to G major, reads as D Mixolydian specifically because D is the one functioning as home, not because of the key signature). — Brandon D'Eon, [Best Guitar Modes Lesson on the Internet](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zrNJ9-jHM6k)

- **Second source extending the brightness ladder above in two directions, plus the build method for it: sharpen one scale degree to brighten a mode, flatten one to darken it — the whole ladder is just repeated single-note edits from the major scale.** Lydian → Lydian augmented (sharpen Lydian's 5th too) is brighter than plain Lydian — the brightest mode on the extended ladder. Locrian → Super Locrian / the altered scale (flatten Locrian's 4th too) is darker than plain Locrian — the darkest mode on the extended ladder. **Framing point**: describing modes as "happy/sad" is a category error once you're past major/minor — Mixolydian has a major 3rd but doesn't read as simply "happy," it reads as major's "edgier" cousin; bright/dark is the more accurate axis. **New examples**: Mixolydian's weakened 7th-to-octave pull (vs. major's strong pull) makes it common in rock — "You Really Got Me" (The Kinks) is pure Mixolydian throughout, cited via a Leonard Bernstein clip. Dorian vamp (i–IV, natural 6 instead of minor's ♭6) — "Uptown Funk" (Bruno Mars) is built on the same two-chord Dorian vamp as *Mad World*, just in a different key (F); the Force/Binary Sunset theme (John Williams, *Star Wars*) briefly shifts from minor into Dorian for one bar as a deliberate moment of brightening within an otherwise minor piece. → [[The Axis Progression]] (Dorian vamp overlap) — David Bennett Music Theory, [[The Modes Ranked by Brightness]]
- **The modes group into "relative pairs" — a second, complementary way to organize them beyond the brightness ladder above**: each major mode paired with its relative minor counterpart (same key signature, different tonic — same relationship as [[Keys]]'s relative major/minor, generalized to all 7 modes) — **Ionian ↔ Aeolian** (the middle pair, plain major/plain minor), **Lydian ↔ Dorian** (brightest major paired with brightest minor), **Mixolydian ↔ Phrygian** (darkest major paired with darkest minor). Comparing a composition through these pairs in sequence (rather than straight down the brightness ladder) contrasts major against minor repeatedly, which reads as more ear-friendly than a slow bright-to-dark drift. **Locrian confirmed unusable as a true home key by direct compositional test** — its diminished tonic triad (no perfect 5th) never actually reads as "home" even when a full arrangement is built around it; it instead reads as hearing the same material in a relative Aeolian or Phrygian. → [[Modal Pop]] — Max Konyi, [Explained!
