# Songs that use Modal Mixture

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=r0rawEpjAcA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=r0rawEpjAcA)

## Synopsis

David Bennett catalogs modal interchange (a.k.a. modal mixture) — chords borrowed from a song's parallel key — starting with the most common move (minor iv borrowed into a major song) and building up to songs so thoroughly blended that "major or minor" stops being answerable, plus a genuine disagreement about how to analyze one chord in "Waterloo" (modal interchange vs. secondary dominant).

## Techniques

- **The minor iv borrow (most common modal-interchange move)**: "In My Life" (Beatles, A major) swaps D minor for the diatonic D major. Same device: "Wake Me Up When September Ends" (Green Day), "Every Time We Say Goodbye" (Cole Porter, whose lyric narrates "from major to minor" at the exact harmonic shift).
- **Reverse direction (major vi borrowed into a minor song)**: "Light My Fire" (Doors, A minor) uses F♯ minor (A major's vi) instead of the diatonic F major.
- **A song where "major or minor" stops being answerable**: "Are You Gonna Be My Girl" (Jet) draws roughly half its chords from A major and half from A minor, while the melody sits in A minor — honestly described as just "in the key of A."
- **A disputed tonic chord**: "Give Me Shelter" (Rolling Stones) sits almost entirely in C♯ minor except its tonic chord, which is C♯ major — describable either as major-key-with-interchange or minor-key-with-interchange-on-the-tonic, and the second framing is awkward since a minor key's own tonic "should" be minor. Same pattern in "Lola" (The Kinks).
- **Ionian↔Lydian blend with a genuine two-valid-readings caveat**: "Waterloo" (ABBA, D major) uses a major E chord (Lydian's raised-4th-flavored ii) instead of diatonic E minor, reinforced by a G♯ in the melody — but this exact chord shape is also analyzable as a secondary dominant. Both readings are correct; music theory terminology developed piecemeal, not as one unified system.
