# Songs that use 2 5 1 chord progressions

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6y-LoytFckI)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6y-LoytFckI)

## Synopsis

A deep survey of the ii–V–I progression's specific use as a **modulation tool** — not just a cadence, but the standard way pop, rock, and jazz songs pivot smoothly into a new key and (often) back out again — with a dozen worked examples spanning The Beatles, Carole King, Stevie Wonder, and jazz standards.

## Techniques

- **Core mechanic**: to move from any current key to a destination key, play the ii–V–I of the *destination* key — the ear reinterprets those three chords as belonging to the new key, completing the modulation. Demonstrated live: C major → play ii–V–I of F major → now in F; play ii–V–I of C major → back in C.
- **Flexibility of the ii and I chords**: the ii chord can be major, minor, dominant, or diminished and still serve the modulation function; the I chord (destination tonic) can be major or minor. **Only the V chord is fixed** — it must be major/dominant (ideally a dominant 7th) to supply the leading-tone pull that makes the key change land.
- **Worked examples**: "From Me to You" (The Beatles) — verse in C major, middle eight uses Gm–C–F (ii–V–I of F) to modulate, then D7–G–C (ii–V–I of C, with a dominant-quality ii this time) to return. "You've Got a Friend" (Carole King) — Gm–C–Fm, a ii–V–**i** (minor one) moving from A♭ major to its relative minor F minor. "If I Fell" (The Beatles) — intro in D♭ major, a ii–V–I in D major smoothly modulates up a semitone into the verse. "Summer in the City" (The Lovin' Spoonful) — chorus starts in F major, then D minor/G alternate (functioning first as vi/II in F, then reinterpreted as ii/V of C minor) — a "dragged-out" pivot-chord modulation where the same two chords serve double duty in both keys simultaneously. "Sunshine of My Life" (Stevie Wonder) — opens in B major, shifts to A♭ major for the middle eight via ii–V–I, then back to B major the same way.
- **Jazz usage**: standards routinely tour through several keys via chained ii–V–I's — "Satin Doll" moves from its C major A-section to F major for the B-section via a ii–V–I, giving the bridge "its own distinct character." "Giant Steps" (John Coltrane) uses a rapid chain of ii–V–I's across widely separated keys as its core structural device.
- **Turnarounds**: a ii–V–I placed at the end of a jazz chart's form, in brackets, as an *optional* device to loop back to the top — "optional" is why it's bracketed; skip it if the tune is actually ending there.
- **Why it works for modulating, mechanically**: the strongest way to establish a new tonic is a **perfect cadence** (V→I) into it — that's the "I" landing. The "ii" beforehand exists because the ii chord IS the V's own subdominant-function setup (a ii–V is itself a mini cadential run into the V), so stacking ii before V just extends and strengthens the same approach.
