# How The Beatles wrote A Day In The Life

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RnZ-pF2S-Sc)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RnZ-pF2S-Sc)

## Synopsis

A songwriting-history and structural breakdown of "A Day in the Life" (Sgt. Pepper's closer) — one of the few Beatles songs genuinely co-written roughly 50/50 by Lennon and McCartney rather than credited-jointly-but-written-solo. Covers the two songwriters' separately-written sections, the deliberate key/tempo contrast between them, the aleatoric orchestral crescendo (and George Martin's fix for getting a classically-trained orchestra to play something "random"), and the circle-of-fifths bridge chord progression. Companion piece to [[I recreated A Day In The Life from scratch]], which covers the recording process in more depth.

## Techniques

- **Genuinely joint authorship, not just joint credit**: John Lennon wrote the opening two verses (inspired by a newspaper report on the death of Tara Browne), got stuck on where to take the song, and brought it to Paul McCartney, who contributed the "Woke up, fell out of bed" bridge section plus both orchestral crescendos and the closing "I'd love to turn you on" line — a rare case where the Lennon-McCartney credit reflects near-equal actual authorship rather than one writer's song credited to both by convention.
- **Deliberately irregular verse lengths, verse to verse**: Lennon's three opening verses are 20, 18, and 19 bars respectively — each subtly different in length rather than a repeating fixed form.
- **A hard key/tempo contrast between sections, deliberately masked by the transition rather than smoothed**: the verses sit in G major at ~79 BPM; McCartney's bridge is in E major at ~163 BPM (effectively double time). The two sections don't share key or tempo, but the orchestral crescendo transition is dramatic and disorienting enough that the mismatch reads as intentional drama rather than a seam.
- **Constrained randomness for an orchestra — giving trained musicians a controlled instruction instead of true chaos**: McCartney initially asked the orchestra to start on the lowest note their instrument could play and work up to the highest "at your own pace," fully improvised — but classically trained players, used to fully notated parts, found that genuinely confusing and unworkable. George Martin's fix kept the aleatoric feel but added just enough structure to make it playable: musicians were told approximately which note to be at, at which point in the crescendo, while still moving at their own pace between those checkpoints — random enough to sound chaotic, structured enough for a real orchestra to actually execute it. Stockhausen's avant-garde influence is credited directly (and acknowledged on the *Sgt. Pepper* album cover, where Stockhausen's face appears among the crowd).
- **A circle-of-fifths bridge chord progression, functioning outside the home key**: McCartney's bridge section opens with a 4-bar piano intro on a repeating E major chord (establishing the new key center), moves into a 10-bar loop ("across my head... downstairs"), then a different 10-bar passage built on five chords related to each other by the interval of a fifth — the chords don't individually belong to the song's home key, but the smooth root motion of a perfect fifth between each keeps the sequence feeling functional rather than random. The section then modulates back to G major via a 5-1 perfect cadence to set up Lennon's fourth and final verse.
