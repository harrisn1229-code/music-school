# Continuous Scale Exercise

Part of [[Guitar Positions]] and [[Modulation (Key Change)]] — a drilled improvisation method for tunes that **change keys often** (jazz standards especially): keep a scale running continuously and, right at the moment the harmony changes, find the physically closest correct note in the new key's scale and keep going in the same direction. The point isn't playing scales — it's rehearsing the *split-second decision* at each key change until it's reflexive, so real improvising over moving changes stops being a memorized path and becomes a live, in-the-moment choice.

Demonstrated over **"Solar" (Miles Davis)**, worked out as five scale/key zones mapped as close together as possible in one fretboard position: 2 measures **C melodic minor** → 4 measures **F major** → 3 measures **E♭ major** → 2 measures **D♭ major** → 1 measure **C harmonic minor** (12 bars total, then the cycle repeats). The method generalizes to any tune with a known chord-scale map — Solar is just the vehicle. → [[🎵 MILES DAVIS - Solar]]

## Prerequisites (mapped out before this drill starts)
1. **Know the melody** at a "service level" first — enough to recognize it and hum it, not a full transcription yet. Coming back to the melody *after* this drill (see Exercise 10 below) is where it clicks.
2. **Determine the scale for each chord/zone of the tune** (sometimes more than one scale option exists for a chord — pick one).
3. **Position-map all the zones as close together as possible on the fretboard**, ideally within a single 4-fret position, so changing key means finding the nearest note rather than jumping to a memorized shape somewhere else on the neck. On Solar, four of the five zones (C melodic minor, F major, E♭ major, C harmonic minor) sit in the same position; only D♭ major needs a small shift.
4. **Play each scale up and down in time, flawlessly, 10 times in a row, one at a time** — a mastery gate before combining them. Skipping this and going straight to the key-change drilling below just papers over a shaky scale.
5. **Play the scales back-to-back in the tune's actual zone order** (still out of time) to start hearing/feeling the progression's shape before adding the real-time constraint.

## The rules ("rules of the game")
A fixed constraint set — Jared calls the underlying idea **the continuous scale exercise** — that turns "just noodle over the changes" into something with a clear pass/fail, which is what makes it practicable and keeps it from getting boring:

1. **Constant notes, no rests, no phrasing.** Keep playing continuously. Durations can vary (quarter, eighth, triplet, sixteenth, or a mix) — an evolution from an earlier, stricter "eighth notes only" version that he found less musical and less fun to drill.
2. **Move by step only** — whole step or half step, both within a scale and across a key-change boundary. No leaps.
3. **Continuous direction** — keep going one direction (up or down) until you hit the edge of the mapped scale shape/position; only then turn around.
4. **Optional half-step-back exception:** if, right at a key change, the closest correct note in the new scale is a half step *behind* the direction you were traveling, you're allowed to step back that half step and then resume the original direction — it sounds good and is explicitly sanctioned, unlike any other backward move.
5. **Don't look at the chord chart** — even an app that highlights the current chord for you (he uses iReal Pro) — while playing this. Watch your hands/instrument, not the changes. The whole point is knowing where you are without a visual crutch.
6. **On a mistake:** stop, restart the backing track, and begin again on a **different note** than last time — never restart from the same spot.
7. **Grace notes and mordents are allowed** as pure ornament — a quick chromatic slide into a note, or a fast alternation with the neighbor tone above and back — as long as the chromatic note doesn't get its own rhythmic value. They don't change the underlying note path.
8. **Mastery bar: 10 clean passes through the entire tune, back to back.** Called "the hardest exercise in this whole thing but 100% worth it" — can take weeks to months. Any mistake resets the count to zero.

### Avoid "getting caught in a rut"
If you land back on the exact same note you started on when the form repeats, you've drifted into a memorized, repeatable path instead of practicing the live decision — the actual skill being trained. **Fix:** the moment you notice it, deliberately switch up the rhythm (throw in a triplet, change note duration) or the starting note next time through, so the key-change moment is never the same twice. This is flagged twice in the lesson — once as a rule, once as **Bonus tip 1** — because it's the most common failure mode he sees from private students who drill hard enough to accidentally memorize "one giant lick" instead of the underlying skill.

## Exercises 5–10 (continuing a numbering that starts at 1 in the prerequisite/part-1 video)

**5 — Out-of-time eighth notes.** Treat the whole tune as steady counted eighth notes ("1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and…") but out of tempo — free to pause, slow down, or speed up. Count out the right number of 8th-note beats for each zone's bar count (e.g. 2 measures of C melodic minor = two counted measures), and deliberately **pause at each key change** to consciously solve "what's the closest correct note in the new scale, continuing the same direction." That pause-and-solve moment is the actual practice target, not the scale-running in between. Introduces the **"money note"** — the note right at a key change that is *not* shared between the outgoing and incoming scale (a genuinely new pitch), which "sounds amazing" when you land on it; not every key-change note is a money note — sometimes the connecting note is common to both scales, which is fine too, just less dramatic. 10 clean passes is the target, same as the rules above.

**6 — In time, over a backing track, any tempo.** Same rules, now played to tempo (demoed at 120 BPM via iReal Pro looping the form 10 times so there's no need to watch a counter — also lets rule 5, don't look at the chart, be followed for real). This is the hard jump from "solved intellectually, out of time" to "solved live, at tempo" — described as "a beast."

**7 — "Try this": free phrasing.** Same scale-connecting logic and key-change accuracy, but now any rhythm/phrasing is allowed instead of constant notes. The payoff moment: once you truly know where you are at each key change, breaking up the phrasing is what turns "audibly running scales" into "sounding like music." A lighter, non-drilled variation — no 10-in-a-row requirement.

**8 — Solo over each zone in isolation, looped.** Loop just one key zone at a time (e.g. the 2 bars of C melodic minor alone) and make sure it sounds musical entirely on its own — phrasing, note choice, whatever — before worrying about the connections. Deepens per-chord vocabulary independent of the key-change skill; a separate 12-video series on improvising over specific chord *types* is the referenced deep-dive for this piece.

**9 — "You're doing it": free playing over the whole form.** Any phrasing, any note order, the only constraint is playing the correct scale/key at the correct time as the changes go by. Framed as the actual arrival point — this is what real improvising over the tune's changes looks like, built entirely out of the constrained drills above.

**10 — Revisit the melody.** After all the scale/connection work, go back and play the tune's actual melody in the same mapped scale position. The claim, demonstrated live on Solar: the melody suddenly reads with total clarity because every melody note is now recognizable as coming from a specific, just-drilled scale/key-zone — which cements the melody in memory and sharpens phrasing and singing it. Closes the loop back to prerequisite #1 above.

## Bonus tip: baby-step entry point
If the full 6-string, whole-position version is too hard to see/hear cleanly, narrow it down to **two strings at a time** (the middle two strings — on guitar, the G and D strings — are the recommended starting pair), or even one string, and run the *entire* exercise progression (up/down scales, out-of-time eighths, in-time, free phrasing…) restricted to that narrow string set. Full skill transfer at much lower physical difficulty — a genuine on-ramp, not a watered-down version, and something anyone comfortable with all six strings can skip.
