## Synopsis
A 2-minute rapid-fire visual explainer of secondary dominants, built entirely around one idea: "put a dominant 7th chord on the fifth of whatever note you're trying to go to." Max walks the same logic across four different targets (C, A minor, F, G) inside a single key-of-C progression, color-codes the chord chart on screen (purple = secondary dominants, red = "extra fancy" chords for a future video), and demonstrates that the secondary dominant doesn't have to sit in root position — putting its 3rd in the bass still pulls into the target chord.

## Techniques
- **The core recipe: dominant 7th built on the 5th of your target = a secondary dominant that pulls into it** — worked four times: G7 → C (V7/I, the plain case), E7 → Am (V7/vi), C9 (C7 with an added 9) over G in the bass → F (V9/IV in first inversion), D9 over F♯ (its 3rd) → G (V9/V in first inversion). Matches the V7/x notation and full example set already on [[Secondary Dominants]]. → [[Secondary Dominants]], [[Chord Progressions]]
- **A secondary dominant doesn't need root position to do its job** — C9/G (root C9, but G — its own 5th — in the bass) still resolves to F just as strongly as root-position C9 would; same with D9/F♯ (3rd in the bass) resolving to G. Confirms the general [[Chord Inversions]] principle (inverting doesn't change a chord's identity/function) applies to secondary dominants specifically, not just plain diatonic chords.
- **Adding a 9th onto a secondary dominant is a free color upgrade** — both worked examples past the first (C9/G → F, D9/F♯ → G) stack a 9th on top of the dominant 7th rather than leaving it a plain 7, with no change to the resolution logic. → [[Extended Chords]]
- **Color-coding a chord chart by function speeds up hearing it** — on-screen, purple marks every secondary dominant and its target gets highlighted as it resolves; a quick visual habit worth stealing for analyzing/annotating a progression while learning it.
