# Altered Chords

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Chords]]. Dominant-family chords with the 5th and/or 9th (sometimes 11th) shifted a half-step. Notation: ♭/♯ and −/+ are interchangeable (C7♭5 = C7-5).

## Single alterations

| Chord | Notes |
|---|---|
| C7♭5 | C E G♭ B♭ |
| C7♯5 (= Caug7) | C E G♯ B♭ |
| C7♭9 | C E G B♭ D♭ |
| C7♯9 ("Hendrix chord") | C E G B♭ D♯ |
| C7♯11 | C E G B♭ F♯ |

## Bigger / multiple alterations

| Chord | Notes |
|---|---|
| C9♯11 | C E G B♭ D F♯ |
| C13♭9 | C E G B♭ D♭ F A |
| C13♯11 | C E G B♭ D F♯ A |
| C7(♯5♯9) | C E G♯ B♭ D♯ |
| C13(♭9♯11) | C E G B♭ D♭ F♯ A |

Non-dominant alterations exist too: maj7♭5 (C E G♭ B), maj7♯5 (C E G♯ B) — see [[Seventh Chords]].

## Usage
- Jazz: free substitutions for any dominant 7th, especially the V7 resolving to I ([[Chord Progressions]]) — extra tension → stronger resolution
- Rule of thumb: don't alter a note the melody is sitting on (the clash)
- ♯9 in funk/rock: E7♯9 is the James Brown / Hendrix stab
- Chord-scale source for fully-altered sounds: the altered scale (melodic minor mode) — [[Scale Modes Reference]]

- **Two valid scale choices for a dominant 7♯5, chosen by ear rather than a fixed rule: whole tone vs. the altered scale.** Both contain the chord's tones (root, major 3rd, ♯5, ♭7); the whole-tone scale stops there (no ♭9/♯9, sounds more open/"ungrounded"), while the altered scale (7th mode of melodic minor, "super Locrian") additionally supplies a ♭9 and ♯9. - **A dominant 7♭9 chord symbol and a dominant 7♭13 chord symbol mean the exact same thing — ♭13, not ♯5, is the correct name for the note a half step above the 5th on an unaltered-5th dominant chord.** Answering a viewer who flagged a "wrong chord" (played G7♯5 instead of the written G7♭9): a dominant-7-family chord symbol represents a **scale**, not a fixed literal note collection — G7♭9 is telling you "this is the V chord of a minor key," and that key's harmonic-minor-derived dominant scale (1, ♭9, major 3, 4, 5, ♭13, ♭7) contains **♭13**, not ♯5 — the same enharmonic pitch, but ♭13 is the name that matches what scale is actually being represented (same naming test as this page's existing "check whether the plain 5th is still sounding" bullet below, reached here from the theory side instead of the voicing side). - **The altered dominant sustained straight across the barline, with no substitute chord at all — the whole "suspension" is simply not resolving the V — plus a form-based rule for exactly where this reads as effective versus flat.** In a Dm7 → G7 ii–V, don't resolve into Cmaj7 at all: keep playing G7(altered) for a half bar, one bar, two bars, or an entire section, depending on the song. **Comping-viability rule: soloing-only** — "hard to fit with the melody most of the time," so this is used almost exclusively while improvising, not while comping under someone else. **Placement rule, tied to song FORM rather than a fixed duration: works best where a strong resolution is genuinely expected** — e.g. the cadence at the end of an AABA form's first half, back to the top — and is comparatively ineffective in a spot like a rhythm-changes turnaround, where the dominant not resolving between bars 2–3 "isn't going to be that effective" because nothing in the form was demanding a hard resolution there in the first place. Reference point for pushing this device the furthest: **Coltrane**, cited as the most famous example of playing across the bar line "pretty out there" by simply refusing to let the harmony resolve. Note: **sustained-unresolved-dominant suspension is common in classical music but comparatively rare in jazz standards** — where a standard's written harmony looks like a dominant suspension, the chord underneath has usually actually changed rather than merely delayed resolving (contrast with a real substitute chord, e.g. [[Diminished Chords]]'s tonic-diminished device in the same source lesson). → [[The ii-V-I]] § *When the I doesn't arrive* — Jens Larsen, [[II V What!? - How not to resolve a II V I on purpose - Jazz Guitar Lesson]]

- **Derive all four altered-dominant extensions directly from the altered scale, rather than memorizing them as unrelated chord symbols.** The altered scale (e.g. E altered = E F G G♯ A♯ C D, identical to F melodic minor) contains a plain dominant-7 shell (root, major 3rd, ♭7) plus four leftover notes, each of which is one alteration: **F = ♭9, G = ♯9, A♯ = ♯11, C = ♭13** (spelled relative to an E7). - **Altered dominants only earn their tension over a *functioning* dominant (the V of a real ii–V–I), and each of the four color tones (♭9/♯9/♯11/♭13) can be used as a moving chromatic voice that walks through the whole progression, resolving one half-step at a time into the I chord.** Worked in a major 2-5-1 (Bm7–E7alt–Amaj7): ♭9 gives a descending top-note line F♯→F→E; ♯9 gives an ascending line F♯→G→G♯; ♯11 gives a descending line B→A♯→A; ♭13 (voiced down an octave, inside the chord rather than on top) gives an ascending line B→C→C♯. The mechanism: find where the color tone sits a half-step from a note in the ii chord and a note in the I chord, and the progression grows a built-in inner chromatic voice — see [[Guide Tones]] for the same "track one line through the changes" logic applied to 3rds/7ths. **A dense stacked-alteration chord (e.g. E7 with ♭9 and ♯11 together) sounds dissonant and ugly in isolation — the payoff is entirely the chromatic pull into the next chord**, which is why these voicings should be evaluated in context, never as an isolated stab. → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Guide Tones]], [[Chord Voicings]] — Jack Ruch, [[Learn These Beautiful Altered Chords!]]

- **Reach for ♯11 deliberately on approach chords, not just the more familiar ♭9/♯9 — it's a major source of a "magical, mysterious" harmonic color.** Demonstrated as part of a target/approach reharmonization scheme: keep the stable, diatonic "target" chords lush with extensions (9/11/13) and make every "approach" chord an altered dominant leading into the next target — a plain, unaltered dominant in the same slot doesn't produce the effect; the alteration is doing the work. - **Build up a dominant's altered-scale sound gradually rather than jumping straight to the "kitchen sink" version** — starting from a plain G7 scale, add ♭9/♯9 first, then ♭5/♯5, before combining all four into a full G7(♭5,♯5,♭9,♯9) altered sound; every stop along the way still reduces back to "basically a G7 chord," which is the practical value of the reduce-to-basic-form habit (Cma7/C6/C6-9 → C major; C9/C11/C13 → C7; Cm7/Cm9/Cm7♭5 → Cm) — a quick way to identify which family a dense-looking chord symbol actually belongs to before deciding how to voice or substitute it. If an altered sound reads as genuinely "strange" rather than just tense, that's the cue to work ear-training drills first and come back to it, not force it. The ♭9/♯9 additions overlap directly with whole-tone passages, which fit any augmented or 7♯5 chord. → [[Extended Chords]] — [[Joe Pass Guitar Style - Enrichment|Joe Pass Guitar Style]], p.2, 15–16, 22–23
- **Raise the 5th a half-step (♯5) on a plain dominant/7th chord for a quick extra hit of tension/color** — applied as a late finishing touch on an already-built progression (after extending to 9ths, doubling roots, and holding common tones), sharpening the 5th of one chord adds noticeably more bite without changing the chord's overall function. → [[Seventh Chords]] — You Suck at Producing, [How to write lofi hip hop chords WITHOUT samples](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=djQlIr1ZYt8)
- **Build an upper-structure diminished-7 arpeggio off the 3rd, 5th, ♭7th, and ♭9th of an altered dominant chord for the classic bebop "outside" sound** — a signature device: stack a diminished 7th starting from the dominant chord's 3rd (e.g. over C♯7alt, a diminished 7 off F/E♯) and it outlines the chord's altered upper tensions (♭9, ♯9-adjacent, ♭5/♯5 territory) at once. Because a diminished 7 chord is symmetrical, **the same shape slides freely in minor thirds** and still fits the same dominant chord — one fingering, four usable positions. Demonstrated as one "concept" pulled from a transcribed solo, then deliberately relocated/reused rather than kept as a fixed lick. → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Scale Modes Reference]], [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]] — Jack Ruch, [[How I Approach Practicing]]
- **Reach for the altered scale (7th mode of melodic minor) to deliberately push a dominant chord "outside"** — used across two consecutive passes through the same V chord in a transcribed solo, contrasted directly against a more diatonic upper-structure-diminished treatment of the identical chord the first time through, showing the altered scale as the more extreme/outside option on the same harmonic menu. → [[Scale Modes Reference]] — Jack Ruch, [[How I Approach Practicing]]
