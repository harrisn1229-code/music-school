# You Suck at Mixing No. 3: True vs. Fake Panning & Mono Compatability

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8DQeE3WUKEI)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8DQeE3WUKEI)

## Synopsis
Two linked myths get debunked in one short video. First: a DAW's "pan" knob is really a balance knob — it can only turn a channel's existing left/right volume up or down, it can never move content from one side to the other, proven by soloing a track's left vs right channel via Utility and watching a hard-panned element refuse to cross over no matter how far the "pan" knob turns. Real panning needs either a dedicated plugin or Ableton's native Split Stereo Pan mode. Second: phase — demonstrating with an inverted-channel sine wave how out-of-phase content reads as huge stereo width in headphones but cancels to near-silence in mono, then introducing Ozone Imager's correlation "pizza" display (the 45-degree lines) as the way to see how much of a mix is living in that dangerous out-of-phase zone.

## Techniques
- **Prove a "pan" knob is actually a balance knob, and diagnose real channel content with Utility** — put Utility on a stereo track and use its L/R-to-both-channels dropdown to solo just the left or just the right channel; this can reveal, for instance, that the left channel is pure reverb tail while the actual source lives entirely on the right. Twisting the track's regular pan control never moves that source across — it only turns the existing L/R balance up or down, proving it can't relocate content between channels. → [[Width and Stereo]]
- **Two ways to get TRUE panning in Ableton (actually move content, not just balance it)** — a free Max for Live plugin that converts the fake balance-pan into a real one; or, without any extra plugin, right-click the track's pan knob and choose **Split Stereo Pan mode**, which lets you pan the left and right channels of a stereo track independently, as if they were two separate mono sources (e.g. pan a dry source to center while pushing its reverb channel hard right). → [[Width and Stereo]]
- **Inverting one channel's phase creates huge apparent width that collapses to near-nothing in mono** — flipping the phase of a mono sound's left channel makes left and right equal-and-opposite, reading as huge, spacious width in stereo headphones — but summing to mono cancels the two channels against each other almost entirely, since they're now mirror images. Most real-world playback (club systems, bars, many venues) is mono or mono-summed, so extreme out-of-phase width tricks are a translation trap, not a free win. → [[Width and Stereo]], [[Referencing and Translation]]
- **Read a mix's mono-safety on a correlation/imaging display, not just by ear** — a free tool like Ozone Imager visualizes a sound's stereo field on a "pizza slice" with 45-degree lines marking the phase boundary; content that swings past those 45-degree lines is out of phase and at risk in mono. The goal isn't perfectly mono/in-phase everywhere — it's keeping most content inside the safe wedge, then confirming by ear that a mono fold-down still holds up. → [[Width and Stereo]]
