# How to Slap Bass (7 Stupidly Simple Steps)

## What's taught

### 1. The forearm-rotation mechanic — proven by comparison, not just asserted

Slapping isn't about the thumb muscle and isn't about the wrist — it's a rotation of the **entire forearm at the elbow joint**. The video is explicit that the common "like turning a doorknob" analogy other bass teachers use isn't actually the same motion. **Drill 1 — Slap the Table:** on a tabletop, hold the hand with the thumb pointed up and fingers curled in, then twist the forearm down so the thumb hits the table. Done three ways for comparison — thumb-muscle wiggle alone (wimpy, quiet), wrist rotation alone (doesn't work at all; the fingers get in the way and can't even reach the table), and forearm rotation (loud, table visibly vibrates) — the forearm wins decisively on the table, and the same three-way comparison repeated on the bass gives the same result: thumb muscle = wimpy, wrist = just banging the strings with the fingers, forearm rotation = "boom, nice, juicy slap tone." *(General mechanic, not a specific settable number — the actionable instruction is the comparison test itself: try all three ways and feel/hear the difference.)*

### 2. Where on the thumb to strike

The same Slap the Table drill reveals the strike point: the **outside edge of the thumb, just past the knuckle** — not the pad (wrong angle, hard to control) and not further back toward the palm (the palm itself starts touching the string and muting it). Confirmed directly on bass with the same forearm-rotation motion: rotating the forearm naturally brings the outside-edge/near-knuckle area of the thumb into the string; going further back on the thumb produces nothing because the palm gets in the way.

### 3. Thumb-up vs. thumb-down positioning — the great up-down debate

Two basic thumb orientations for slapping, and the physical bass setup differs for each:

- **Thumb down** — thumb points below the string. **Setup:** lower the strap so the bass sits low enough to comfortably point the thumb down somewhere between the fingerboard and neck pickup — roughly waist height or a little lower/higher. **Real-player examples:** Flea (Red Hot Chili Peppers), bass strapped a little below the waist, slapping right past the end of the fingerboard; Kiyoshi, bass around waist height, same thumb-down angle and strike zone.
- **Thumb up** — thumb points above the string, at roughly **11 o'clock** if the string itself is 12 o'clock. This is the teacher's own preference and what he teaches, for two stated reasons: (1) easier to hit accurately, one string at a time; (2) sets up learning **double thumbing** (down-and-up thumb strokes) more easily later. **Setup — three adjustable variables:** (1) **strap height** — needs to be higher than waist height, maybe just below the right chest/ribcage; (2) **neck-to-floor angle** — the more parallel the neck is to the floor, the easier the thumb-up angle is to get; (3) **horizontal bass position** — more in front of the body vs. more off to the side, which shifts where the hand naturally lands on the strings and can be adjusted for torso comfort. **Real-player examples:** Ida Neilsen, bass strapped over the ribcage with the neck pointed up slightly, thumb just above parallel, slapping past the end of the fingerboard; Marcus Miller, same ribcage strap position and slightly-up neck angle, slapping between the end of the fingerboard and the neck pickup.
- **Either orientation works with the rest of this lesson** — the fundamentals (forearm rotation, strike point, diagnostics, drills) apply to both thumb-up and thumb-down players.

### 4. The note/percussion diagnostic — troubleshooting a bad slap sound

A good slap has two components — **note** (sounds the same as if the string were plucked normally, the pitch itself) and **percussion** (the strings smacking against the frets from the collision of the slap). A bad slap is missing one of the two:

- **Scenario A — note only, no percussion.** Reproduced by hitting too softly. Fix: hit harder, and confirm it's coming from the forearm rotation, not the thumb muscle. A second possible cause: **action set too high** — if there's more than roughly **5 mm of clearance between the E string and the 12th fret**, the bass may need a setup (truss rod tightening, or lowering the bridge saddles); the video's advice for anyone unsure how to do this themselves is to get a professional to do it rather than guess.
- **Scenario B — percussion only, no note.** Reproduced by holding the thumb too tense and leaving it resting on the string after the smack. Fix: relax the thumb (see Drill 2 below). A second possible cause: the **palm touching the string** — confirms the strike point should be the outside edge of the thumb past the knuckle (not further back toward the palm), plus a small angle adjustment to leave a little space between the strings and the palm/wrist.

### 5. Drill 2 — Relax the Thumb (away from the bass)

The single hardest part of slap technique, per the video: getting genuinely relaxed under speed, since the natural tendency is to tense up as tempo and complexity increase — which is exactly backwards from what works. **The drill:** let the arm hang loosely at the side, then flail it with the same rotational motion used to slap, but with the thumb and fingers left completely relaxed/hanging. Constrain the flailing to **one rotational plane only** — not wrist-flailing, not side-to-side — pure forearm rotation, though it's fine if the upper arm rotates along with it. Bring the hand back to the bass right out of that flailing motion and the resulting slap has a noticeably better tone. **The mechanical payoff of real relaxation:** a sufficiently relaxed thumb bounces off the string naturally from the momentum of the strike itself — no separate "twist back up" motion needed to clear it, which is what breaks down entirely if the thumb re-tenses (produces exactly Scenario B above).

### 6. Drill 3 — Nail the Accuracy (single-string isolation)

Once tone and relaxation are solid, the next layer is accuracy: slap–mute (touch the strings with the fretting hand right after) repeated on a single string, listening for all three things every time — note, percussion, and whether the intended string (and only that string) actually got hit. Drilled on **E** and **A** first (easier), then **D** and **G** — flagged as genuinely harder to slap and thinner-sounding no matter how well it's executed, but with **no technical reason to avoid them**; maintaining the same thumb angle as strings get thinner (11 o'clock, for thumb-up) is the key consistency check moving across the string set. Beginners can focus on E/A first and bring in D/G once those are solid.

### 7. Drill 4 — Cross the Strings

Once single-string accuracy holds, add string crossing: E→A→D→G→G→D→A→E, four slaps per string to start (fewer string changes to manage while still muting between every slap and listening for note/percussion/accuracy on each one). Progression once that's easy: cross on every single slap (E, A, D, G, one slap each), then break up the order entirely (e.g. E, G, A, D, E, D, A, G) rather than always moving sequentially.

### 8. Drill 5 — Mute the Notes (note length: short vs. long)

Controlling whether a note rings short or long is what makes slap fit into real musical parts rather than just sounding percussive. **Open-string notes:** touch the string anywhere with the fretting hand to kill it. **Fretted notes:** lift the fretting finger but stay in contact with the string (mutes it); if that produces unwanted harmonic ringing, rest additional fingers on the string at the same time to kill it completely. **The drill:** on a G (3rd fret, E string), play four short notes, then four long notes, slow. Once comfortable, work toward alternating short–long–short–long–short–long as one continuous pattern rather than four-then-four.

### 9. Song applications woven through the drills

- **"We Care A Lot" (Faith No More)** — a simple slap tune on largely one string, applied right after the basic tone/relaxation drills, muting some notes between slaps to get the part sounding right.
- **"Smoke on the Water" (Deep Purple)** — both the familiar guitar riff (played on bass, everything jiggled onto the E string, deliberately in the "wrong" key many bassists default to) and the actual bass part in its correct key of G (also compressed onto the E string) — used as single-string accuracy application, right after Drill 3.
- **"Smells Like Teen Spirit" (Nirvana)** — used to apply string-crossing (Drill 4); simplification offered for beginners — drop the small slide at the end of bar one, and hold the Db (4th fret) through the end of bar two instead of moving to C.
- **"Everyday People" (Sly and the Family Stone, Larry Graham)** — the payoff reveal for Drill 5: the short-long-short-long note-length exercise, done purely as mechanics practice, turns out to *be* Larry Graham's actual classic bassline on the song — a "wax on, wax off" drill that's secretly the real thing.

### 10. Closing advice — tempo discipline (Mark King, Level 42)

Getting genuinely fast at slap takes years, on top of everything covered in the video (forearm mechanic, strike point, position, diagnostics, accuracy, muting) plus other techniques not covered here (pops, double thumbing). The video's closing framing, credited to Mark King: **"the trick to playing fast is to start slow and get faster"** — find a tempo the accuracy drills above can be played cleanly at, and bump it up gradually without sacrificing the technique, rather than rushing straight to speed.
