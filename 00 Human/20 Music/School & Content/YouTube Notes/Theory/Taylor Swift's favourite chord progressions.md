# Taylor Swift's favourite chord progressions

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AbW3sES2zCw)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AbW3sES2zCw)

## Synopsis

Bennett went through Taylor Swift's entire discography and ranked her five most-reused chord progressions by frequency — every one turns out to be a member of the Axis-Progression family (the same four chords, I–V–vi–IV, just started at a different point in the loop), making this a useful single-artist case study in how reordering a loop's starting chord changes its character while keeping the same underlying harmonic content.

## Techniques

- **#5 — IV–V–vi (9 uses)**: starts on the unresolved subdominant for immediate momentum, through the tense dominant, resolving to the "softer," minor-flavored tonic-function vi rather than the full tonic — deliberately not fully resolved so the loop keeps wanting to continue. Song cited: "Fearless."
- **#4 — vi–IV–I–V (9 uses)**: starts in minor-key-flavored vi (Aeolian color), through the tense subdominant IV, resolves to the true tonic I, then straight back into dominant V tension to keep the loop moving. Song cited: "I'm Only Me When I'm With You."
- **#3 — I–vi–IV–V, "doo-wop"/"50s" changes (17 uses)**: the classic loop, historically tied to '50s doo-wop but far older and more widespread in practice. Full T→(T)→SD→D functional cycle. Song cited: "The Way I Loved You."
- **#2 — I–V–ii–IV, Bennett's "Believe" progression, named after the Cher song (20 uses)**: fully diatonic, loops cleanly — I (full rest) → V (tension) → ii (subdominant-function, softer than IV) → IV (subdominant again) → back to I. Song cited: "The Story of Us" ("You Belong With Me" era-adjacent).
- **#1 — I–V–vi–IV, the canonical Axis Progression itself (21 uses)** — the single most reused progression in her catalog by a clear margin. Bennett explicitly frames this as the correction to a viewer comment (from his prior Axis-progression video) suggesting Taylor Swift's songwriting is lyrics-led rather than harmony-led — i.e., the chords function as a stable, familiar emotional bed specifically so the lyric can carry the song, rather than the harmony being the point of interest. Cited as the sole harmonic basis of "All Too Well," including its extended 10-minute re-recording.
- **Framing point (worth preserving as a general songwriting-craft lesson, not just a Swift-specific note)**: reusing a chord progression across many songs is not inherently lazy songwriting — it can be a deliberate genre/artist signature (compare 12-bar blues' ubiquity across an entire genre), and a chord progression alone "is not a song"; it's a color palette the rest of the song (melody, lyric, arrangement, production) differentiates from track to track.
