## What's taught

**The question (from viewer Pat/David Patrick):** how to pick a set daily time window (he proposed 8–10, i.e. 2 hours/day) and stick to it — acknowledging "the consistency concept is great" but not knowing how to actually plot/execute it.

**Jared's three-part answer, in the order he gives it:**

1. **A time window helps, but the *order* matters more than the exact clock time.** Put practice in the calendar at a specific time, but don't stress when life bumps it — what he actually preserves day to day is the **position of practice relative to his other commitments** ("I tend to do it in the order of when I plan to do it"), not the literal hour. Practice earlier in the day when possible — not because evening practice is lower quality (he's "kind of an evening person" and plays well then too), but purely because early practice is less likely to get bumped as the day goes sideways. Cites the framing "the best time to exercise is whenever you actually do it" — same logic applies to guitar.
2. **THE SURPRISING ANSWER — the most important of the three, and the headline takeaway:** take whatever amount of time you *think* you want to practice (Pat's number: 2 hours/day) and **cut it in half**, then commit to *that* as your daily minimum. Concretely: want 2 hrs → commit to 1. Want 1 hr → commit to 30 min. Want 30 min → commit to 15 min. **Why it works (his reasoning, stated directly):** an ambitious target is easy to blow past on a good day but easy to break on a bad/busy one, and *breaking the streak* is the actual cost — since consistency (Stage 1 of [[The Practice Priority Pyramid]]) outranks everything else, protecting the streak matters more than hitting an ambitious number. A low minimum is achievable on hard days too, builds a visible "string" of shown-up days, reduces resistance/dread going in, and — the safety valve — **you can always do more than the minimum when you have time or energy; the minimum is a floor, not a ceiling.** He frames it explicitly as "a psychological trick to commit to a minimum that is easy and doable," not a limit on ambition.
3. **Bonus/perspective framing (given after the numbered answer, but presented as the piece that puts everything else in context):** the whole practice-scheduling system is "a construct" — self-invented, not objectively correct, and **will eventually break** (life circumstances change, a phase ends, a routine stops working). That's fine and expected. The recovery move when it breaks: **don't just resume the identical plan** — ask specifically *why* it fell apart, and adjust the system rather than re-running the same plan that already failed. He's iterated his own system "for years and years," changing it each time something stopped working, and expects to change it again.

**Jared's own current routine (concrete, as of this recording):**
- **Minimum commitment: 1 hour/day of jazz guitar**, deliberately kept low — he trained at a "competitive" conservatory-style program where 8 hrs/day was normal, and still keeps his real commitment at 1 hour because it's what he can *actually* sustain; he does more "if I'm inspired," often doubling it (mentions playing 2 hours the morning he recorded this), but the commitment stays at 1 hour.
- **Tracking:** the practice slot lives in his calendar (so order/position is preserved even when the clock time shifts), plus a separate **habit-tracker app** where he checks off each day he practiced, giving him a visible streak.
- **Missed-day "quota" system:** if he misses a day, he treats the missed session as a quota owed and **makes it up by doing roughly double on a later day** — explicitly flagged as "a little intense/hardcore," offered as an option rather than a rule everyone needs.
- **Fixed daily warm-up, no exceptions:** does the same specific warm-up "every single day, every time I practice" (~5–10 min) — he calls it "the best warm-up," it's tab + notation, and it's a **free PDF** at `soundguitarlessons.com/warmup` (linked in his own video description). Skipping it causes physical issues ("my arm can start to have issues"); doing it, "everything feels great." *(Distinct resource, not detailed here beyond the name/URL — no exercise content pulled from the transcript since he doesn't walk through it on camera in this video.)*
- **"Runner's itch" analogy:** sustained consistency eventually produces the same restlessness runners describe when they skip a run — it starts to feel *wrong* not to practice. He treats that felt discomfort as the signal that the habit has actually taken hold, distinct from willpower.
