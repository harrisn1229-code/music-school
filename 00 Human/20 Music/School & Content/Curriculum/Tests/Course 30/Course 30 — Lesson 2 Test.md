> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **Move one note of the shape up or down an octave, then repeat from whatever you land on**
> Start from a shape you already have memorised, usually root position. There is no target voicing and no fixed order — that is the point.
>
> **Q2.** **Move the newly-colliding note to its own nearest octave, cascading until every note has a string**
> The note that moves is the one that was already there, not the one you were shifting. Repeat if the fix causes another collision.
>
> **Q3.** **Root up an octave gives another close inversion · middle note up gives the open 1-5-3**
> The second one is the definition of an open or spread voicing: the same three notes with real space between the C and the G.
>
> **Q4.** **No — discarding unplayable results is a normal, expected outcome**
> Moving notes around blind is meant to produce misses. Drop them and shift a different note.
>
> **Q5.** **E-A-D, A-D-G, D-G-B, G-B-E — three inversions each, so twelve locations**
> Root on the bottom, 3rd on the bottom, 5th on the bottom, at three different octave locations of the root.
>
> **Q6.** **The two groups crossing the G–B pair need every note above it shifted up a fret**
> In D-G-B that is the top note; in G-B-E it is the top two. The guitar is tuned in fourths except between G and B, which is a major 3rd, so anything fretted on the B or high E string sits a semitone low against an all-fourths shape. The shift compensates for it, so the shapes are reused rather than relearned.
>
> **Q7.** **You pick each chord's voicing by proximity to the previous chord instead of defaulting to root position**
> Less hand travel between chords, so a higher clean tempo, and non-root-position voicings of the same notes sound less generic.
>
> **Q8.** **Being able to find the root in every octave position on the neck**
> Use the octave shapes rather than searching blind — that is the prerequisite step, not an optional extra.
>
> **Q9.** **One memorised shape played off a different chord tone of the target each time**
> The fingering never changes. Only which note you mentally treat as the root does, and that is what changes which chord the shape is.
>
> **Q10.** **An altered voicing of that dominant**
> Every note of the min7♭5 built on the dominant's ♭7 is a legitimate altered tone of it, so the grip reads as a rootless dominant-altered sound.
>
> **Q11.** **A dominant 9 voicing**
> *Also accepted:* a dominant 9 · a 9 chord
> Same shape, different chord tone taken as the anchor, different resulting chord.
>
> **Q12.** **Fewer shapes memorised, more chords covered — one grip re-pointed instead of a new grip found**
> *Rubric (5):* states the efficiency argument, that harmonic mileage per shape beats shape count (2) · gives a correct worked example of one shape serving two functions (2) · notes that the shape is unchanged and only the assumed root moves (1)
> The realistic constraint mid-tune is not knowing enough chords, it is finding one fast enough. A shape you already own, re-pointed at a different chord tone, is available at speed in a way a newly learned grip is not. Worked: a half-diminished shape played from its own root is a half-diminished chord; played from the ♭7 of a dominant it is that dominant, altered, and resolves to I. A dominant 9 grip moved to the root a tritone away voices the altered form of the original dominant, because the two share their guide tones. In every case the fingers are doing something they already knew and the ear is being told a different story about which note is home.
