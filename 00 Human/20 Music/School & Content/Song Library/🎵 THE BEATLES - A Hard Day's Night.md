# A Hard Day's Night — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

Pollack hears this as the record where the blues finally recedes. Compared with "Can't Buy Me Love" (blues in form, changes and tune) and "You Can't Do That" (blues form, wavering third), what survives here is only the 12-bar length, the AAB phrasing and a trace of minor-third flavour — the rest is Lennon and McCartney ([[BLU-C08 — Blues into rock and hip hop]]).

**Form.** A 2-bar intro in tempo; a 12-bar verse that borrows the blues' proportions (three equal 4-bar phrases) but not its progression; an 8-bar bridge in two parallel phrases; a half-instrumental "semi-solo" verse that hands the last phrase back to the voice; and an outro that triple-repeats the closing half-phrase before fading on the opening chord.

**Harmony.** Solidly G, with the verse's ♭VII an early instance of a habit that becomes theirs ([[POP-C09 — Mixolydian ♭VII]]). The verse withholds a formal V–I until its third phrase, having already confirmed the tonic by the gentler route of IV and ♭VII — closed-sounding without ever cadencing. The bridge feints at B minor for a phrase but never supplies its dominant, so it stays tonicization rather than modulation ([[HAR-C10 — Tonicization vs modulation]]).

The famous opening sonority works as a **surrogate V into G**: roughly D minor, F and G stacked, with G sitting as a suspended 4th over a low D and no B anywhere. Beginning *and* ending a rock record on a non-tonic chord was close to unheard of.

**Arrangement.** George's 12-string arrives as an identity here. Ringo plays essentially one figure the whole way with cowbell added in the bridges — the *absence* of fills and texture changes is what makes the track a steamroller.
