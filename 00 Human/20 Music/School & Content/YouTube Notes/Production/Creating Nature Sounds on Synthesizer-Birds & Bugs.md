# Creating Nature Sounds on Synthesizer-Birds & Bugs

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bri8fnMUfaQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bri8fnMUfaQ)

## Synopsis

An 8-track "little aviary" of bird, insect, and frog sounds built entirely from FM synthesis on the Synclavier (from the late 1980s), with a clear case for why FM specifically suits organic nature-sound design better than analog/subtractive synthesis.

## Techniques

- **Two partials, one on a note-length-triggered delay, for expression without velocity/pressure sensitivity**: a bird call is built from 2 FM partials — partial 1 sounds immediately, partial 2 only enters after a short delay. Playing a short note only triggers partial 1; holding the note lets partial 2 (a rising pitch element) join in — a way to get expressive, performable variation from a keyboard with no velocity or aftertouch at all, purely through note duration.
- **Very low FM ratio (4-8 octaves below the carrier) for a "glottal scrape"/raspy organic quality**: a nearly-pure sine wave with just a touch of very-low-ratio FM added gives a subtle rasp reminiscent of a real animal's throat/vocal-tract texture, without turning the tone metallic or obviously synthetic.
- **Pitch-swoop via envelope-modulated pitch on a simple sine + light FM base** — the core mechanism behind most of the bird calls, with the specific melodic shape (up-swoop, down-swoop, or up-then-down) and performance phrasing being what actually sells "bird" over "generic synth blip."
- **Why FM specifically suits this better than analog/subtractive synthesis**: FM can produce pure sidebands with the fundamental largely removed, giving a thin, transparent, non-tonal-sounding texture — analog subtractive synthesis "always has too much fundamental" by comparison, making it harder to avoid an obviously pitched/musical quality when the goal is an unpitched, organic texture.
- **"Simpler is better" as an explicit design rule for this category**: a little bit of odd/off FM goes a long way — the goal is scrapiness and pitch movement, not a rich, developed patch.
- **Playing technique matters as much as patch design**: "if you don't play it like a bird, it doesn't work" — phrasing and timing on the keyboard are as load-bearing to the illusion as the patch itself.
