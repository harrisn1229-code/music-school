# Chromatic Pedal-Tone Blues Turnaround in 3 Levels

Part of [[Guitar Positions]]. A single turnaround lick, taught as **one idea built up in three densities** — a 2-note pedal-tone-over-chromatic-descent lick, then a 3-note contrary-motion (counterpoint) version, then the same shapes reinterpreted as full 4-note chords. Where [[Turnarounds]] documents the **jazz** turnaround family (1-6-2-5 and its relatives) and [[L-Shape Root Map for Blues Chords]] documents **where the hand goes** for I/IV/V, this page is a **plain (non-jazz) blues turnaround lick**, worked in the key of C, that doubles as a compact lesson in how melody, counterpoint, and chords are really one skeleton viewed at different resolutions.

## Where it falls in the form

**Measure 11** of a 12-bar blues — the turnaround occupies the last two bars, leads into the **V chord** in bar 12, and brings the form back to the top. Worked in **C**: I = C7/C9, IV = F7/F9, V = G7/G9.

## Level 1 — Beginner: chromatic descent under a pedal tone

Two notes:
- **Pedal tone (held constant, on top):** **C** — 8th fret, high E string. This is the **root** of the C7 chord.
- **Moving voice (bottom):** starts on **B♭** — 8th fret, D string, the **♭7** of C7 — and walks **chromatically down**: B♭ → A → A♭ → G (8th → 7th → 6th → 5th fret, same string), while the C on top never moves.

**Picking:** either alternate the two notes, or grab both together with a pick-plus-finger "claw" technique (pick takes the D string, a free finger takes the high E). Try a few different rhythmic phrasings once the shape is comfortable — the video notes there are "lots of different phrasing options."

**Drill:** loop the four-note descent (B♭-A-A♭-G) under the held C pedal tone in time, cycling back to B♭ to repeat. Get it clean with the pick-and-finger claw technique specifically before adding Level 2 — the block-picking motion is what Level 2 builds on.

## Level 2 — Intermediate: add a contrary-motion counter-line

Adds a second moving voice, in **contrary motion** to the descending line — this is the video's own use of the word "counterpoint": one voice climbs while the other falls. The new line: **E** (3rd of C major) walking up to **G** (5th of C, and the root of the V chord/G7).

Combined, you now have three moving parts every beat: the **descending** B♭→G line (bottom), the **held pedal** C (middle/top), and the **ascending** E→G line. Picking: the pick grabs the lowest (bottom-string) note, the middle and ring fingers grab the B string and high E string together — a block, piano-like hit where all three notes land at once, rather than a scalar run.

**Drill:** once Level 1's descending-line-plus-pedal is solid, layer the ascending E→G line on top and practice hitting all three notes together as a block on each chromatic step; then experiment with rhythmic placement (the video again notes "different rhythmic things" are open here). *(A different, more generative use of contrary motion — not this exact worked line — is documented on [[Counterpoint Line Generation]]; this page's version is one concrete, fully-worked instance of the same "one voice up, one voice down" idea rather than a generate-your-own-line exercise.)*

## Level 3 — Advanced: reinterpret the same shapes as full chords

**The core idea Ruch states directly:** melody, bass lines, and chords aren't separate things — "everything we play on guitar... single-note ideas really stem from chords, from harmony," and a solo is "really just an extension of what's happening underneath in the chords." Level 3 makes that literal: look at each 3-note cluster from Level 2 and read it as part of a bigger chord voicing already sitting under the fingers.

Reading the Level 2 shapes this way produces a **C9 – F7 – A♭7 – G** progression:

1. **C9** — the first cluster reads as a classic bluesy C9 voicing: **♭7 on the bottom, 9th, major 3rd, root on top.**
2. **F7** — the next cluster is the **top part of an F7 voicing** (a barre-chord-family grip with the root left off the bottom and the **5th** played on top instead) — same three notes, reheard as F7.
3. **A♭7** — the following cluster reads as an **A♭7 voicing** (a D-shape-family grip transposed to A♭, ♭7 included).
4. **G** — finally, a plain **G major** triad — the V chord.

**Why this progression makes sense harmonically:** C9 (I) moving to F7 (IV-ish) is the ordinary I→IV motion; A♭7 (♭VI7) resolving down a half step into G (V) is the classic **♭VI-to-V chromatic pull** heard throughout the blues — the exact same half-step-down device already documented in [[L-Shape Root Map for Blues Chords]]'s minor-blues turnaround (there F7→E7→Am7→A7, i.e. ♭VI7→V7→i7→I7, in A minor) — confirmed here in a **major/dominant-blues** context instead of minor.

**Framing, stated directly:** this is presented as "dipping your toe in the water" of full **chord-melody** playing, the kind of thing associated with players like **Joe Pass** who take a melody and dress it out into a full harmonized chord part (→ [[Chord Melody Arranging]]).

## Full drill — build the lick in layers, then transpose it

1. **Level 1 alone:** loop pedal tone C (high E, 8th fret) against the chromatic descent B♭-A-A♭-G (D string, 8th→5th fret). Get the pick-and-finger claw articulation clean before moving on.
2. **Add Level 2:** layer the ascending E→G counter-line on top, hitting all three notes together as a block on each step. Vary the rhythmic placement once it's clean.
3. **Move to Level 3:** replace the thin 3-note clusters with the full chord voicings — **C9 → F7 (top voicing, root off, 5th on top) → A♭7 → G** — comping the same turnaround as real chords instead of a lick.
4. **Transpose the whole system.** Every shape here is fretboard-relative to the pedal tone's starting fret (8th fret = C on the high E string) — slide the entire 3-level system to a different starting fret to work it in other keys, the same "movable shape" logic used throughout [[Guitar Positions]].
5. **Drop it into a real 12-bar blues**, bars 11–12, and play through a full chorus of the form so the turnaround lands in context rather than being drilled in isolation.

*(Step 4's transposition instruction is a general application of the vault's standard "any movable shape should be drilled in every key" practice principle — the source video only demonstrates the lick in C.)*
