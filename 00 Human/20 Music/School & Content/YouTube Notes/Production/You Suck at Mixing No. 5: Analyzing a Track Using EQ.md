# You Suck at Mixing No. 5: Analyzing a Track Using EQ

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N_KIHqHb_jA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N_KIHqHb_jA)

## Synopsis
A track-analysis exercise (credited to Will Marshall) rather than a mixing move on your own track: using a narrow, soloed EQ band swept slowly up a reference track's whole frequency spectrum to hear exactly which element lives where, and why. Deconstructs a Skrillex & AB (credited as "Scarlets and Abstract" in captions) track this way — kick, bass, their overtones, percussion, vocal, and top-end "air" — and draws out the reasoning behind why the kick and bass were tuned to different notes, why club music leans on a handful of keys, and why the very top of the spectrum still matters even though it's nearly inaudible in isolation.

## Techniques
- **Sweep a narrow, soloed EQ band up a reference track's whole spectrum to map where every element sits** — put EQ Eight on the track, expand the display, narrow one band's Q (hold Option while dragging), and click the headphone solo icon so only that narrow slice of the spectrum plays back. Slowly sweep it up from the bottom (EQ Eight goes down to 10 Hz) while the frequency/note readout in the corner tells you the pitch at each stop, and note what you're hearing at each point. → [[Referencing and Translation]]
- **Kick and bass tuned to different notes so they don't compete for the same sub-100 Hz space** — in the worked example, the kick's fundamental sat around 50 Hz (the note G) with an octave overtone at 100 Hz; the bass fundamental sat lower, around 40 Hz (E), with its own perfect-fifth overtone above it. Tuning them a fifth apart rather than to the same note keeps them from fighting for the same narrow low-end pocket. → [[Masking and Carving]], [[Kick Design]]
- **Why club/EDM tends to sit in G, F, or E** — below roughly 30-40 Hz most speakers can't reproduce a fundamental realistically at all, so that's close to the practical floor for a song's key; anything lower and you start losing power fast.
- **Overtones are what cheap speakers actually reproduce** — most laptop/phone speakers roll off around 150 Hz, well above a bass's true fundamental; including a clean overtone a fifth or octave above the fundamental (rather than relying purely on the sub) is what lets a bass "pop" on small speakers that can't physically reproduce the note itself. → [[Referencing and Translation]]
- **Continuing the sweep reveals the whole arrangement's frequency map** — above the kick/bass region: toms and the low end of the vocal sample in the low-mids, the snare (also tuned to the key of the track) in the mid-range, then progressively brighter percussion, and finally a high "airiness" band that the hi-hats and tiny percussion details occupy.
- **Rolling off the very top of the spectrum makes a track sound dull even though that band is nearly silent in isolation** — the high-hat/percussion "airiness" region barely registers as its own sound when soloed, but removing it entirely noticeably dulls the whole mix — it's doing real, if subtle, work.
- **The takeaway is arrangement, not extra EQ** — the point of the exercise is to notice that a well-arranged track needs *less* EQ because the elements were placed to avoid competing for the same space in the first place; it's better to arrange around collisions than to rely on EQ to un-collide them after the fact. → [[Masking and Carving]]
