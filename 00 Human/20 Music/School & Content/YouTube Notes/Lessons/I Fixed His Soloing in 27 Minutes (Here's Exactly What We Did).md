## What's taught

- **Default ("half-cocked") vibrato is the #1 flagged habit.** When landing on a note at the end of a phrase, the near-universal default is to bend the note slightly out of tune *before* starting vibrato — described as taking "the most solid and stable note" (here, the root at the 7th fret of the G string, the anchor of the classic pentatonic box) and bending it out of tune first, which pulls the rug out from under the listener's sense of landing. The fix: make the landed note clean/in-tune by default, then *intentionally* choose to bend/vibrato it — intention before technique, not the reverse. → [[Blues Lead Phrasing and Touch]]
- **Vibrato-free phrase-ending drill.** Prescribed on the spot as new homework, not previously assigned: play a short line, land on the final note, and hold it completely still — no vibrato at all — even without a backing track. Framed as deceptively hard: the student's hand "immediately wanted to vibrato" even when told explicitly not to, confirming how deeply automatic the habit is. Explicitly named as a good few minutes of dedicated daily practice, separate from regular playing. → [[Blues Lead Phrasing and Touch]]
- **"Swing the pendulum hard the other way" as a retraining method.** Rather than trying to moderate an over-used default (vibrato) directly, temporarily eliminate it entirely (zero vibrato for a few minutes a day) so it can be reintroduced later *with* intention rather than by habit — a general behavior-change technique applicable to any overused default technique, not just vibrato.
- **Practice in small, mindful chunks rather than "get it right, then repeat it 100 times."** Explicit pushback on the common belief that a technique needs to be drilled mindlessly in bulk to become automatic — the stated method is a few mindful reps, switch to something else, come back and remind yourself, repeat — with mindless/automatic execution being the *product* of accumulated mindful reps, not a shortcut to skip them.
- **Reapplying the same lick one string thicker.** As a quick variation exercise mid-lesson, Seriff has Lance take the exact lick just played and relocate it one string lower (thicker) — same pentatonic shape, moved — as a lightweight way to extend a single idea into a second usable phrase.
- **Neck pickup for a "sweet" major-key sound.** For this D-to-G (I–IV) major vamp specifically, the neck pickup is called out as the right tonal choice — "the sweet sound fits this track kind of perfectly." General principle stated alongside it: every track calls for a different tonal approach, not one fixed guitar tone across everything.
- **Rig for this session (gear color, not a technique lesson):** neck pickup → Wampler Tumnus (overdrive) → Fender Vibro Champ ("the small bedroom one"). Settings were dialed in by describing a target tone (a "Warren Haynes" sound) to ChatGPT and using the settings it suggested as a starting point.
- **Mixolydian ♭7 insert over a major I–IV vamp — the "sweet"/jam-band color.** Over this D-to-G one-to-four, inserting the Mixolydian ♭7 (C♮ over D) is called out as a deliberate color choice, tied explicitly to Grateful Dead/jam-band vocabulary — the progression is framed as able to go either direction: the "sweet," major-leaning sound (Mixolydian ♭7) or a bluesier direction (the flat-3/flat-7 blue-note vocabulary below). → [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]]
- **The "Dickey Betts note" — adding the 4th to a pentatonic line.** Named directly after Dickey Betts (Allman Brothers), who is cited as having played this move "many times" in the classic box. Playing pentatonic but inserting the scale's 4th degree as a passing color — framed as a specific, attributable lick rather than generic scale theory, and as a reason not to dismiss the "classic box" as overused/basic. → [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]], [[Blues Lead Phrasing and Touch]]
- **The major pentatonic blue note = the flatted 3rd.** Stated directly as a definition check mid-lesson: inserting the ♭3 into a major pentatonic line is what gives it that "soulful" quality — the same friction [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]] documents as "why the ♭3 isn't wrong." Used here as a deliberate touch/embellishment rather than a wholesale scale switch — the student confirms he uses it as "a touch," not constantly. → [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]]
- **Flat-three-to-two-to-one lick, credited directly to Derek Trucks.** A short descending chromatic-adjacent connector — ♭3 → 2 → 1 (root) — stated as stolen straight from Derek Trucks and used as embellishment vocabulary, not a wholesale key change; usable "unless you're trying to make a statement," in which case it can be leaned on harder. → [[Blues Lead Phrasing and Touch]], [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]]
- **Treat the flat seven the same way you'd treat the flat three — the "diagonal pentatonic" visualization.** Since the student was already inserting the ♭7 as a straight descending passing tone, Seriff reframes it: anything you can do with the ♭3 (the ♭3–2–1 connector above) can be done with the ♭7 by starting the identical shape from the 6th degree instead of the 3rd — because the ♭7 sits the same relative distance from the 6th that the ♭3 sits from the root. A portable pentatonic-visualization trick: once you know one embellishment pattern off one scale degree, the same shape transposed to a different starting degree gives you a second embellishment for free. → [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]]
- **Two-flat-three-two slide-slide-pull-off lick, one pick attack.** A specific fingering drilled live: 2 → ♭3 → 2, executed as slide up, slide down, pull off — all from a single pick attack (the rest articulated by the fretting hand). Assigned to be practiced starting from a different scale degree (the 6th, at the B note on the 9th fret of the D string) as a variation, and specifically with the ring finger. → [[Blues Lead Phrasing and Touch]]
- **Solo dynamics — building the arc, saving the peak, and the "dismount."** A full macro-structure lesson distinct from the note-choice material above: walk register up gradually rather than jumping straight to the highest available note; save the single highest/"high root" note in the position for the actual climax near the end of the solo rather than an early phrase (spending it early "shows your cards" too soon); once you've built up into the higher frets, keep climbing rather than backing off; deliberately execute a "dismount" at the end — repeating notes at the current pitch while descending (rather than a straight scalar walk-down) keeps the energy up even as the pitch drops, landing in a register that reads as a natural resting point. Full extraction (new note, no prior home in the vault) → [[Solo Dynamics and Arc]]
- **Repetition over novelty — "play it again."** The single biggest actionable takeaway condensed at the end: when a phrase lands well, the instinct is to move on to the next idea — instead, restate/elaborate on it, because dwelling on a strong idea creates more meaning for the listener than constantly introducing new material. Same note given to a different student ("Brad") in a separate session, suggesting it's a standing piece of Seriff's coaching vocabulary. → [[Solo Dynamics and Arc]]
- **Steal your own rhythm from an earlier lick as a unifying theme across the solo.** A device for tying a longer solo together: reuse the *rhythmic* cell from an earlier phrase (not necessarily its pitches) later in the solo, so the whole thing reads as thematically connected rather than a sequence of disconnected licks. → [[Solo Dynamics and Arc]]
- **Copy a bend/melodic idea across strings as a lightweight variation device.** A half-step bend (B up to C) used on one string is relocated to a different string later as a "theme" — generating a sense of development without inventing new material from scratch. → [[Solo Dynamics and Arc]]
- **Making mistakes mid-solo is fine as long as you don't get lost.** Explicit framing: hitting an unintended note is a normal, acceptable cost of trying things live in a coaching/practice context — the actual failure mode to avoid is losing your place in the form, not hitting a wrong note.
- **"If you make a mistake, play it again" — endorsed, despite being commonly dismissed as bad advice.** Seriff explicitly defends a piece of advice he says most people consider terrible: if a mistake happens because you don't know the fretboard in that spot, playing through it again (rather than avoiding the area) is how you actually learn it — treated as a necessity, not an excuse to avoid practicing accuracy.
