# Course 15 — Vocal Method

Everything that happens to a vocal **before the chain goes on it** — the mic, the comp, the tuning decision, the stack — plus what changes when the vocal is rapped rather than sung. Processing a vocal is a separate subject; this is how you end up with one worth processing.

**What it assumes**

- Nothing. It starts at where to stand in front of the microphone.

**What this unlocks**

- The 22 vocal chains in the vault. They all assume a finished take and a decided stack; this course is what produces both.
- [[Course 16 — Handoff & Release Prep]] — comping and tuning are on the *commit* side of that course's boundary, so the decisions made here are the ones that go out permanently.
- Practically: the difference between a take that gets fixed in the mix and one that didn't need fixing.

> [!info] Short lessons on purpose
> The lessons here are shorter than elsewhere in the school. Vocals are a subject where most of the knowledge is procedural — the value is in having the procedure stated, in order, so it can be followed the same way twice.

## The lessons

### Lesson 1 — Before the Chain

**Read**
- [ ] [[Vocal Mic Technique]], then [[Vocal Comping and Takes]]

**The material**
- **[[VOX-C01 — Proximity effect|Proximity effect: bass response rises as a directional mic gets closer.]]** It turns distance into a tone control — move in for weight and intimacy, back off to stop a vocal getting boomy.
- That's the same fix as an EQ cut, made at the source where it costs nothing. It only applies to **directional** mics, which is why an omni is sometimes the right call for a singer who moves.
- **[[VOX-C02 — Comp by phrase, not by syllable|Comp by phrase, not by syllable.]]** The criteria, in order, are **delivery, then pitch, then timing** — delivery outranks timing, because timing is fixable afterwards and delivery isn't.
- Watch the seams: cut on a breath, **keep the breaths**, and crossfade every edit. The failure mode is a Frankenstein comp that's technically perfect on every syllable and worse than the best single take.

**Concepts**
- [[VOX-C01 — Proximity effect]]
- [[VOX-C02 — Comp by phrase, not by syllable]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — record the same line at 4 inches and at 12 inches, then name what changed and which EQ move it replaces.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — record four takes of one verse on the TLM 102 at a consistent 6 inches. Comp it twice, once by syllable and once by phrase, then A/B both against the best single take.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 15 — Lesson 1 Test]]

### Lesson 2 — Tuning and Stacks

**Read**
- [ ] [[Vocal Tuning]], then [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]]

**The material**
- **[[VOX-C04 — Retune speed is a stylistic dial|Retune speed is a stylistic dial, not a correctness setting.]]** The hard-tuned end and the transparent end are both deliberate choices. What's easy to miss: **fast retune destroys vibrato**, which matters when the vibrato is the reason the take is good.
- **[[VOX-C03 — When not to tune|Don't tune when the deviation is the expression.]]** That's a sharper rule than "when it sounds bad" — blues and soul phrasing lives between the notes, and a scoop or a note leaned into slightly flat is doing the emotional work.
- Then the rule the lesson exists for:
> **The further a part is from delivering the lyric, the more it can become texture.**
- **[[VOX-C05 — The treatment hierarchy|That's the treatment hierarchy]]:** lead centre, present, least processed. Doubles wide and lower. Harmonies lower still with more processing. Ad-libs widest, furthest back, heaviest effects.
- And the mechanism underneath the stack: **[[VOX-C06 — A copied double doesn't thicken|an identical copy doesn't thicken anything]]** — it just gets louder, or comb-filters if you nudge it. Real doubles work *because* the two performances differ.

**Concepts**
- [[VOX-C03 — When not to tune]]
- [[VOX-C04 — Retune speed is a stylistic dial]]
- [[VOX-C05 — The treatment hierarchy]]
- [[VOX-C06 — A copied double doesn't thicken]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — take one sung phrase with vibrato in it. Retune it fast, then slow, and say what the fast setting cost. Then find one note in the take that should stay untuned and justify it.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — build a lead, double, harmony and ad-lib for one hook in a single session. Set level, pan and processing for each from the hierarchy alone, *then* check it against what instinct would have done.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 15 — Lesson 2 Test]]

### Lesson 3 — Rap

**Read**
- [ ] [[Rap Vocal Production]] in full

**The material**
- **[[VOX-C07 — Rap is rhythm-first|Rap is a rhythmic performance — you're producing pocket, not pitch.]]** Everything in the lesson follows from that one line.
- **Timing edits happen at the syllable level** — deliberately the opposite of Lesson 1's rule, and not a contradiction: you comp *performance* by phrase and fix *timing* by syllable.
- **Punch-ins line by line** are the normal workflow here rather than a fallback for a bad take.
- **Doubles go on bar-ends and key phrases**, not across the whole verse. The double is an accent, not a texture.
- **A dedicated ad-lib pass** is structural, not decoration — it's what fills the space the lead leaves.
- **Compress harder and faster**, choosing presence over beauty, because the lyric has to stay parseable over a loud beat. And **keep the breaths**: in a dense verse they're the punctuation.

**Concepts**
- [[VOX-C07 — Rap is rhythm-first]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — say which edits happen by syllable and which by phrase, and why that isn't a contradiction with Lesson 1.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — take a recorded rap verse, duplicate the track twice, and nudge one copy 15 ms late and one 15 ms early. Play all three against the beat and commit to one, then say what the pocket you chose is doing.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 15 — Lesson 3 Test]]
