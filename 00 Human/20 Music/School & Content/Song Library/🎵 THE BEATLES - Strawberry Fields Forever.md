## Production & Arrangement
The single most influential studio-as-instrument record ever made. Famously, the final master is **two completely different takes — in different keys AND tempos — spliced together** (~1:00 in); George Martin varisped one down and the other up until they *almost* matched, and the seam is audible if you listen (it's part of the magic).

- **Mellotron** flute intro (the wobbly, unstable tape-sampled flutes) — the sound of the whole record.
- **Vari-speed everything:** the vocal was slowed for the dreamy, detached quality; instruments pitched to make the splice work.
- **Backwards tapes, reversed cymbals, Indian swarmandal**, timpani, and heavy tape manipulation throughout — texture over "band."
- The **F♯7** dreamy [[Secondary Dominants|secondary dominant]] + the descending inner lines give the harmony the same unstable, floating quality as the production.

## From videos

- **The outro's cello + trumpet arrangement, which George Martin himself named as his favorite Beatles score.** For the final ~3 minutes, Harrison and McCartney are almost entirely absent — the harmony is carried instead by George Martin's orchestration for cello and trumpet: a triplet-based cello line under a four-part trumpet harmony. Cited by Martin as the piece of writing he's proudest of across all his Beatles arrangement work. → [[27 Beatles songs that George Martin played on]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [27 Beatles songs that George Martin played on](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YDYedISQ2JU)
- **Tonal ambiguity in the intro/verse chords, the chorus's meter changes, and the truth behind the famous splice's "impossible" low cello note.** The song's actual tuning is microtonal (a half-sharp above major), rounded to B♭ major for analysis — but the very opening Mellotron chords are genuinely ambiguous between F major and B♭ major, since those keys differ only by E natural vs. E♭: the intro/verse plays E natural first (sounding like F major) before the E♭ arrives and pulls the ear toward B♭. **Chorus chord progression** (I–v7–IV/vi... as heard, matching this page's own Chords section) plus its own meter quirks: a one-off 2/4 half-bar, and a 4/4→6/8 shift under the main hook ("strawberry fields forever") — 6/8 is compound time (felt pulse = dotted quarter note vs. 4/4's quarter note), which reads as a momentary slowdown even though tempo doesn't change; the **final chorus** repeats that line three times, and on the last repeat drops a beat entirely, landing on 3/4 instead of 4/4. **The splice's "impossible" note**: the famous low B♭ in the cello line (a tone below a cello's actual lowest note, C) isn't a compositional choice — George Martin wrote that cello line for the second take when it was still in C major (its true lowest note, C, was playable); only after the take was slowed down in pitch to match the first take's key did that C become a now-unplayable B♭, baked in by the vari-speed splice itself. **Outro fade-out/fade-in**: also not a creative choice — the percussion briefly falls out of time during that section, so the fade masks the flub and fades back in once the band locks back in. Full breakdown → [[The music theory of "Strawberry Fields Forever"]]. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[The music theory of "Strawberry Fields Forever"]]

## Pollack's analysis

*Extracted from [[AWP — Strawberry Fields Forever]] — Alan W. Pollack, "Notes On…" #105.*

**Form.** Underneath the effects it is a **conservative folk-ballad shape** with one late pop gesture: refrain-first, then strict refrain/verse alternation, no bridge and no instrumental solo, with all three verses carrying different words. That is folk practice, not pop practice. The pop move arrives only at the end, where the last refrain is repeated to the point of elision with the outro. Intro four bars, except the last carries two extra beats. The **refrain is metrically slippery** — a foreshortened half-measure at one point, a single bar of **6/8** at another, with the eighth-note pulse held constant across both, and the vocal entering mid-bar so the felt downbeat is really bar 2. The verse is the stable section: a plain 8 bars in four even 2-bar phrases with noticeably faster harmonic rhythm. The outro fades out, fades back in, and fades out again — novel in 1967.

**Harmony.** The governing device is **cadence avoidance**: V is set up repeatedly and almost never allowed to resolve, and the song settles for **IV–I plagal** closes instead ([[HAR-C03 — Plagal cadence]]). `Intro: F (V) · Cm – A° · B♭ – F · E♭ – B♭` over a stepwise descending bass. On paper that is tame; by ear the opening chord does not sound like V and you expect F to be home until the forced 6/4→5/3 plagal turn corrects it. `Refrain: I (2) · v minor (2) · vii°-of-IV · IV – V · V-of-ii · [6/8] IV – I` — minor v in a major key, a diminished chord that could be aiming at either IV or ii, an approach to ii that never lands ([[HAR-C07 — Secondary dominants]]). `Verse: V · V7 – vii°/vi · vi · IV · IV – V · I – vi · IV – V · IV – I`, whose opening V resolves **deceptively to vi** ([[HAR-C04 — Deceptive cadence]]).

**Notable.** The released master is **two different recordings spliced at 1:00**: take 7 (cut in A) sped up and take 26 (cut in B) slowed down, both landing near B♭ — near enough that the opening reads slightly off against a tuning fork. His angle is that almost nothing here is technically new; it is the Revolver toolkit run all at once and pushed much further.

**Against this page.** Agrees — his "B-flat (more or less)" is this page's sounding pitch (A shapes, capo 1).
