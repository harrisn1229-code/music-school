# Tom Elmhirst breaks down his mix of 'Rolling in the Deep' by Adele

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EHLY5bk2TvY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EHLY5bk2TvY)

## Synopsis

A short, dense Mix with the Masters solo-in-the-room breakdown: Tom Elmhirst (Adele, Amy Winehouse, Beck) walks through four stages of his session for Adele's "Rolling in the Deep" — drum sample reinforcement, an outro-chorus parallel-compression lift, a two-piano arrangement with a spring-reverb chorus entrance, and the full analog vocal chain plus its three-return effects architecture. The real headline isn't any single plugin — it's how deliberately every processing and effects decision is **staged to the song's structure**: the snare sample only exists in the chorus (the verse is a bare stomp with no snare hit at all), the parallel drum compression is held back until the outro-chorus lift, the high piano and its spring reverb are chorus-only, and the vocal's entire effects budget builds in three discrete steps across the song (dry + tape delay in the verse → add reverb at the bridge → add two delays at the chorus). Elmhirst also drops a compact mixing-philosophy line worth remembering: when the multitrack, arrangement, and production already do the work, the mix engineer's job is "just not to [mess] up" — restraint as the whole brief on a record this strong. Settings given are mostly qualitative (console channel/send numbers, not plugin parameters) since this predates his in-the-box rebuild, but the routing/arrangement logic transfers directly.

## Techniques

- **Drum sample reinforcement, staged by song section, not blanket-layered across the whole track.** (0:00–0:40) A bass drum sample runs under the mic'd kit for the entire song, but the snare sample is **chorus-only** — the verse is a bare "stomp" with no snare hit at all to reinforce. Claps get an additional layer bounced up specifically in the chorus. Tambourine gets two separate layers, also chorus-only: a **drier, close/dry tambourine** and a **much wider one carrying effects** (reverb/width). Everything else is one single mic'd drum kit — the reinforcement is a small, surgical set of section-specific samples, not a wholesale drum-replacement pass. → [[Sound Selection Guide]], [[Layering and Stacking]], [[2 Drums Group|Drums Group]]
- **Parallel compression on drums withheld until the outro-chorus lift — run on THREE separate signal groups at once (kit tracks, room mics, stomp tracks), not one drum-bus send.** (0:40–1:20) "Out of the middle and into the last chorus" the parallel-comp path suddenly engages across the kit close mics, the room mics, AND the stomp tracks simultaneously — Elmhirst demos it by simply toggling it in/out to show how audibly it "kicks in." The multi-target routing (three separate destinations getting the same crush treatment at the same moment, not just one bus) is the specific reusable idea — it reads as more drive/excitement precisely because the whole kit's character shifts together at that one structural landmark, not gradually. → [[Parallel Processing]], [[Parallel Comp Send]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]], [[UAD UA 1176LN]]
- **Two-piano arrangement: a low piano runs the whole song as the harmonic "peg," a high piano is reserved for the chorus only, carrying a spring-reverb entrance as its own dimension-add.** (1:40–2:40) The low piano is constant throughout; the high piano is a chorus-exclusive layer, and its defining move is a "shangri-la spring reverb vibe" — heard A/B'd dry vs. with the spring, the spring reads as adding "another dimension in the chorus that wasn't there." Elmhirst names it directly as "a standard spring type, one vintage spring in space" — a plain, unfussy spring reverb rather than an exotic algorithm, used specifically as an arrival cue for the chorus rather than a constant texture. Owned-lane equivalent: [[Ghz DC19 3]]'s built-in Spring section, routed as a send/insert on the high-piano layer and reserved for chorus sections only (automate the send in, or simply don't route the verse-only low piano to it at all). → [[Ghz DC19 3]], [[1 Acoustic Piano Track]], [[Reverb]] (effect), [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **Full analog vocal chain named directly: a channel EQ ("1081") into two compressors in series — a Urei/UREI 1176 then a Tube-Tech CL1B.** (2:40–3:00) "As we know it's a 1081, going into a Yuri, and a Tube-Tech CL1B" — a three-stage analog signal path (console-style channel EQ → fast FET compressor → slow opto compressor) run on the original multitrack. The two-compressor series order (fast/transient-grabbing unit first, slow/leveling opto unit second) matches the same fast-then-slow staging logic already documented elsewhere in this vault ([[Compression Staging]]) — this is a real-world confirmed instance of exactly that pairing on a hit vocal. Owned equivalents for the compressor pair: [[UAD UA 1176LN]] → [[UAD Tube-Tech CL 1B mkII]]. The 1081 (a Neve/SSL-family console channel EQ, not the same unit as the owned [[UAD Neve 1073]]) has no direct owned equivalent — logged to [[Plugin Wishlist]]. → [[UAD UA 1176LN]], [[UAD Tube-Tech CL 1B mkII]], [[Compression Staging]], [[Plugin Wishlist]], [[1 Main Vocal Track]]
- **Three dedicated console return channels for the vocal's effects, each with one job: reverb, a synced short delay for the chorus, and a separate, more atmospheric delay reserved for the outro.** (3:00–3:20) Channel 26 = reverb send. Channel 27 = short/synced delay send, specifically for the chorus. Channel 28 = a more atmospheric, washier delay, used mainly toward the outro. Three purpose-built returns rather than one general-purpose reverb+delay combo — each one is reserved for a specific structural moment rather than running throughout. → [[Reverb Send Long]], [[Delay Send Other]], [[1 Main Vocal Track]], [[2 Main Vocal Group|Main Vocal Group]]
- **The vocal's entire effects budget introduced in three discrete, structurally-timed steps — a clean worked example of the "start dry, build gradually" arrangement principle.** (3:20–5:00) **Verse:** completely dry vocal plus only the *original printed tape delay* ("There's a fire starting" — the same tape delay track carried over from the original demo/writing session) — no reverb, nothing else. **Bridge:** the reverb send (channel 26) is introduced for the first time — "we have the original tape delay for the verse, then I bring in my reverb for the bridge." **Chorus:** "all hell breaks loose" — TWO further delays kick in simultaneously: a standard eighth-note synced delay, and a second, wider eighth-note delay explicitly panned hard/"going off to the canyon" for a panoramic left-right spread, layered together as "a bed of lovely atmospherics behind her vocal" without taking up much room. Each section of the song gets a genuinely different, additive effects state rather than one fixed vocal-space setting — verse (tape delay only) → bridge (+ reverb) → chorus (+ two delays, one wide-panned). This is a second confirmed sighting of the exact "start a lead vocal bone dry, add effects gradually by song section to build intensity" pattern already logged from a different engineer/song (see [[Arrangement and Song Form]], Ben Baptie/Julian Casablancas) — reinforces it as a real, repeatable mix-arrangement device rather than a one-off. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]], [[Reverb]] (effect), [[Delay]] (effect), [[Reverb Send Long]], [[Delay Send Other]], [[1 Main Vocal Track]]
- **Mixing-philosophy line, kept qualitative (not a settings tip):** "When you get a multitrack that's put together like this, and a song and an arrangement and the production's right, this — my job's just not to [mess] up." Restraint framed as the entire brief when the raw elements (performance, arrangement, production) already do the work — a mixer's job on a genuinely great multitrack is to not damage it, not to add more. Mixed on a pair of KRK monitors, noted as "quite old, this record now — seven years" at time of filming. Song-specific color, kept on this page only.
