# Jazz Chord Shapes Guitar Workout

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DqkZomOUpQc)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DqkZomOUpQc)

## Synopsis

A genuinely new practice device not previously in the vault: the **Chord Quality Cycle Exercise** — cycling through every 7th-chord quality that shares a common root, one half-step-moving note at a time, run off three different string-root templates (6th, 5th, 4th string) through all 12 keys via the circle of fourths. Episode 9 of Jared Borkowski's chord-mastery series (episode 8 covered the theory/construction of 7th chords). The stated purpose is ear-training and fretboard-relationship mastery, not a chord vocabulary reference — it deliberately isolates "what one note has to move to turn this chord into the next" rather than teaching chords in the context of a key (that's a separate, complementary exercise the video name-checks as "next week").

## What's taught

- **The chord-quality cycle, in order, each step moving exactly one note by one half step:** major triad → major 7 → dominant 7 → minor 7 → half-diminished (m7♭5) → fully diminished 7 — then reversed back down through the same sequence. The half-step-per-move logic: triad→maj7 moves the root down a half step to the major 7; maj7→dom7 moves that same note down another half step (to the ♭7); dom7→m7 moves the 3rd down a half step; m7→half-diminished moves the 5th down a half step; half-diminished→dim7 moves the (already-flat) 7th down one more half step to the diminished 7th (𝄫7). Every chord quality between the two ends is exactly one moved note away from its neighbor.
- **Three physical templates per root, all previously mapped in an earlier episode in the same series (episode 7, on the six places to play any one major chord)**: the cycle is run starting from a major-triad shape rooted on the 6th string, then again rooted on the 5th string, then again rooted on the 4th string — three separate positions to drill the identical cycle in, not three different exercises.
- **Why this parallel (same-root) framing matters, stated directly**: practicing chords that exist together diatonically within a key (a separate, complementary exercise flagged for the following week) doesn't put chord qualities directly next to each other for comparison. Cycling through qualities off a single shared root forces the player to actually locate and understand *which specific chord tone* differs between neighboring qualities (e.g. "I know minor 7 has to have a flat third, so where is that on this shape") — an ear-training and fretboard-relationship drill, not a harmony-in-context drill.
- **Full 12-key coverage via the circle of fourths** (C → F → B♭ → E♭ → A♭ → D♭ → G♭ → B → E → A → D → G → back to C), each root run through the templates on all three strings, ascending the cycle then reversing back down. Explicit permission to skip shapes that require overreaching (especially relevant on nylon-string/acoustic guitars with less upper-fret access) rather than forcing an uncomfortable stretch.
- **Two brief real-music tie-ins** (not full analyses, just confirming the cycle shows up in fragments): "Something" by The Beatles (George Harrison) opens with exactly the cycle's first three chords off one root (major triad → major 7 → dominant 7); and in jazz, a ii-V-i in a minor key is essentially a half-diminished → fully-diminished → tonic-minor fragment of the same cycle.
- **Companion resource**: a free "any jazz chord" PDF booklet teaching 8 moveable shapes that cover altered/extended chord types beyond what a plain 7th-chord cycle reaches (referenced, not itself extracted here — a promotional resource pointer, not lesson content).
- **Practice framing, stated directly and consistent with the channel's general philosophy**: this is a deliberately dry, methodical "toolbox" exercise for specific goals (ear training on chord-quality differences, fretboard technique, internalizing chord construction) — not a daily-practice mandate, and not the exercise to reach for when the actual goal is learning how chords function together in real progressions (that's next week's diatonic-chords-in-a-key version).
