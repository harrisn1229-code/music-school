# Just Practice Like THIS Every Day (For Real Results)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d4fgvSyvv1g)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d4fgvSyvv1g)

## Synopsis

Nathan Borton lays out the practice framework he says got him to a professional level as a jazz guitarist — not a specific lick or tune, but the whole system for deciding *what* to practice and how to structure a session. The core argument: musical integration is slow (6 months to a year for a concept to emerge freely in playing), so most players' frustration comes from an unrealistic timeline and a lack of structure, not a lack of talent. He names **five pillars** a complete jazz-guitar practice system must cover — ear (oral skills/transcription), vocabulary (internalized language), technique (the physical means to execute ideas), theory/harmony (the framework for identifying sounds), and repertoire (tunes you can call at a jam) — and walks through concrete sub-methods for each: how to transcribe licks vs. whole solos, three ways to acquire vocabulary (aural transcription, written transcription study, improv-language books), how to match picking style to a line and refinger for ease, how to learn a chart-free harmonic ear (find the tonic, then the tonality, then check theory-based movement), and a five-step process for learning a tune entirely by ear before ever checking a chart. He closes with a concrete daily-practice template: pick two of the five pillars per day, split an hour into two 30-minute timed blocks, and let the timer force the transition even mid-idea. The most transferable, non-genre-specific material is the ear-training/transcription workflow and the daily-cycling practice template; the vocabulary-acquisition and theory sections are jazz-specific but generalize to any style with its own "common language."

## What's taught

**Framing — the long-term mindset (the precondition for everything else)**
- Practicing one concept for a month usually will **not** show results — real integration into free playing takes roughly **6 months to a year**. That "it's not working" feeling is normal and is not a signal to abandon the concept; the fix is consistency plus managed expectations, not more hours crammed in.
- Set small, achievable goals along the way to stay motivated while waiting for the long-term payoff.

**The five pillars of a jazz guitar practice system**
1. **Oral (ear) skills** — the connection between ears and fretboard.
2. **Vocabulary** — a well of licks/patterns/language to communicate with other musicians.
3. **Technique** — the physical skill to execute ideas without the guitar limiting them.
4. **Theory and harmony** — the framework for identifying the sounds heard in jazz.
5. **Repertoire** — a working set of common tunes to actually play with other musicians.
- All five need dedicated time; none substitutes for another (knowing theory alone doesn't make you a better improviser, for example).

**Pillar 1 — oral skills (ear training via transcription)**
- Ear skill is trainable, not innate — Borton says he "always struggled" with his ear and it improved through consistent, focused work over years.
- Two starting disciplines: (1) **transcribing short licks** heard on records, and (2) **transcribing whole solos or multiple choruses**, start to finish — write both down on paper.
- Concrete starter goals: **5 new licks over 2 months**; commit to **one full solo**, however long it takes — "commit and finish what you start."
- Purely a listen → replicate → perform-along process for now — the point of this stage is training the ear/fretboard connection, **not** yet folding transcribed material into your own solos; that comes later (see vocabulary pillar). Don't judge this stage by whether it shows up in your playing yet.
- Byproduct: consistent transcription also builds interval/scale/arpeggio recognition **and** time feel/rhythm, not just pitch.

**Pillar 2 — vocabulary (language acquisition)**
- Every high-level jazz player has spent real time learning jazz's common melodic/rhythmic language — Borton pushes back on the idea that pure pattern-generation alone gets you there; shared communication comes from a shared vocabulary other musicians also know.
- **Three methods to acquire language:**
  1. **Aural transcription of canon solos** over common progressions (he names ii–V–I specifically) — examples given: Charlie Parker on "Donna Lee," Miles Davis on "So What," Grant Green on "Cool Blues."
  2. **Written transcription-study resources** — Joe Pass's *Guitar Style* book, Barry Galbraith's solo-method book, and "Omnibook"-style collections of written-out solos — used to visually study how players tackle common progressions.
  3. **Jazz-improv books that explain the *why*** — names Jerry Coker's *Elements of Jazz Language* as an example of a book that explains the how/why of the vocabulary, not just notation.
- **Internalizing acquired language, two methods:** (a) the **"plug and chug" method** — take one new idea/lick and play it over an *entire* tune/form everywhere it applies; (b) **write your own short solos/etudes using only the newly gathered language** — both force the brain to draw on known material rather than improvise from nothing.
- **Quality over quantity, explicitly:** truly internalizing just **5 licks** with a long-term mindset can generate hundreds of improvised lines (cites Grant Green as proof); half-learning and dropping 20 phrases wastes practice time.
- Patterns from scales/arpeggios are a valid third vocabulary source but should be used with the same intentionality as transcribed language, not as a shortcut around it.

**Pillar 3 — technique (physical execution)**
- The reason vocabulary fails to come out in playing is often **not** lack of ideas but lack of the physical means to execute them, or a line that just doesn't sit well on the instrument — Borton says the ideas he actually uses are the ones he can play "without tons of physical effort."
- For guitar this reduces to two things: **picking** and **fretboard fingering**.
- **Match picking style to the line** — is a given phrase best played with Benson-style picking, strict alternate picking, or hammer-ons/pull-offs? Also know the picking style of whoever you're transcribing — it makes learning their lines far more intuitive.
- Practice **string-changing mechanics** in isolation — sweep picking, string skipping, and different ways of executing arpeggios — as a distinct skill from the line itself.
- **Method: isolate the physical skill first, then attach vocabulary to it** — e.g. drill small alternate-picking or sweep-picking fragments in a "black and white" way, then fit language/rearranged existing vocabulary into those isolated shapes, rather than forcing an unpracticed line to happen through repetition alone.
- **Actively re-examine fingerings, don't keep the first one you land on** — students commonly transcribe a line, land on whatever fingering came out first, and never revisit it even when it's awkward. A deliberately re-fingered line "can go from awkward to easy and fluid," and correct fingering should align with the chosen picking style for that section.

**Pillar 4 — theory and harmony**
- High-level improvisers don't just "know scales" — they directly associate specific sound-types (altered, ii–V–I, etc.) with the language that goes over them; theory alone doesn't make you a better improviser, but it's the framework that lets you *identify* which sounds are available so the transcribed/pattern vocabulary can be applied and internalized correctly.
- Analyze a tune as **harmonic devices and progressions** (ii–V–I, backdoor dominant, tritone substitution, ♯IV walkdowns, etc.), not as a flat sequence of isolated chords — this both tells you which vocabulary is idiomatic over that passage and makes tunes far easier to memorize.
- **How to build this framework:** (1) find a teacher — a local jam-session pro or an online teacher who does harmonic analysis; (2) theory books depending on the angle wanted — a **language-based** approach (Jerry Coker's *Elements of Jazz Improvisation*) vs. a **tune-based** approach (*Hearing the Changes*).
- Goal: learn a tune's actual sounds and the vocabulary required over them, then apply transcribed language or chord-scale/pattern theory to generate ideas that fit.

**Pillar 5 — repertoire (learning tunes)**
- Most students under-invest here because online learners don't get out to play with others enough — but knowing a tune by heart is "your license to play with others"; it frees attention from getting the chords right onto actually making music with the band.
- **Find your "core 30"** — roughly 30 tunes that get called at jam sessions everywhere; not mastery, just familiarity, is the bar to be able to sit in almost anywhere (Borton points to a list in the video description, not detailed in the transcript itself).
- **Five-step process for learning a tune, always by ear first:**
  1. **Learn the melody by ear** from the original recording, or an early version where the players are likely reading the actual score — this connects the melody directly to your ear (rather than to a chart) and gives you freedom to play around it later since you know the "vanilla" version.
  2. **Work out the chords from a great pianist's recorded version** (names Bill Evans, Oscar Peterson, Wynton Kelly) — pianists tend to state harmony most clearly since it's literally their job to delineate the chords.
  3. **Listen closely to the bass, especially on downbeats where the harmony shifts** — the bass is usually playing the chord's root while the piano voices its quality.
  4. **A mentor's trick (Steve Kabalch): pitch-shift the recording up an octave** so the bass line sits in the guitar's own register and becomes much easier to hear directly.
  5. **Check your work last** — only after doing the ear-work, cross-check findings against a chord chart (online or iReal Pro); doing the "heavy lifting" by ear first, before checking, both makes the tune easier to actually play and makes it stick in memory longer.
- Chordal/comping practice over real tunes is mentioned as a related, separate discipline Borton has covered elsewhere and deliberately left out of this video since it's soloing-focused.

**Daily practice-cycling template**
- For a **1-hour** session: don't try to touch all five pillars every day — instead **pair two related pillars per day**, split into two 30-minute timed blocks, and look for a natural "carryover" between the pair.
- **Use a hard timer** — when it goes off, move on immediately even if the current exercise isn't finished. The rationale: leaving something incomplete creates a natural pull to come back to it, which keeps motivation up over the long run (rather than exhausting a topic in one sitting).
- **Example day pairings he gives:**
  - Day 1 — 30 min oral skills + 30 min repertoire, carryover = linking what you hear to what you actually need to play.
  - Day 2 — 30 min vocabulary + 30 min technique, carryover = applying physical skill directly to the language you're learning.
  - Day 3 — 30 min theory/harmony + 30 min repertoire, carryover = deepening understanding of the tunes you're learning.
- The rotation, time split, and even single-pillar-only days (e.g. an all-technique day) are all adjustable based on where you feel weak — the one constant is the long-term mindset: consistent daily/weekly reps over many months, not occasional 5-hour cram sessions.
- Borton's own long-run pattern: he cycles focus across months (language for a couple months, then chords, then transcribing) rather than trying to hold every pillar at once — always with the long view.
