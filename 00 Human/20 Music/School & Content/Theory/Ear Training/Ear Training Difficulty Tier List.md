# Ear Training Difficulty Tier List

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Transcribing and Playing by Ear]]. Brandon D'Eon's S-to-F difficulty ranking of 18 distinct ear-training skills, useful as a **study-sequencing roadmap** — start at F tier and work up rather than jumping straight to S-tier skills like perfect pitch. Complements the vault's other ear-training notes ([[Scale Degree Ear Training]], [[Harmonic Ear Training]], [[Identifying Chord Progressions by Ear]]), which each teach one skill in depth; this note is the map of how those skills compare in difficulty and what else exists beyond them.

## The ranking, easiest to hardest

| Tier | Skill | Why |
|---|---|---|
| F− | Dynamics | Loud vs. quiet — the easiest skill on the list. |
| F | Timbre | Tone-color difference between the same pitch on different sources (guitar vs. piano vs. trumpet vs. voice). |
| D | Tonal center / key ID | "The note that sounds like home" — a prerequisite for functional-progression hearing. |
| D | Baseline / root-motion recognition | Hearing the bass notes (chord roots) of a progression — distinct from a melodic bassline. |
| D | Articulation | Staccato/legato/palm-muted/pizzicato — how a note is played. |
| C | Ascending intervals | Standard interval training, upward. |
| C | Descending intervals | Slightly harder than ascending — no intuitive "up" reference. |
| C | Scales by ear | Easier than reputed once you know the scale's interval structure — the struggle is usually a knowledge gap, not an ear gap. See [[Scale Degree Ear Training]]. |
| B | Harmonic intervals | Both notes sound simultaneously — no sequential "landing" note to anchor against. See [[Harmonic Ear Training]]. |
| B | Melodic dictation | Learning a melody by ear — eased in practice by having your instrument in hand. |
| B | Rhythm by ear | Counterintuitively harder than melody — no pitch content to hang the rhythm on; much easier once paired with its melody. |
| A | Microtonal recognition | Rare in Western tuning but load-bearing in string bending — hitting a bent pitch by ear gets much harder in a loud mix. |
| A | Complex chord-quality ID | Dense extended/altered chords (7sus♭13, dominant 11♭9, dominant 7♯5♯9, major 9♯11, minor 6/9) — as opposed to major/minor/diminished, which are easy. |
| A | Chord progression functionality | Hearing how each chord relates to the tonic (I–V–vi–IV, ii–V–I). Gated behind tonal-center ID. See [[Identifying Chord Progressions by Ear]]. |
| A | Time signature by ear | Demonstrated on 7/4 and 5/4; a 13/8 example (Plini, "Electric Sunrise") flagged as genuinely hard without slowing the track down. |
| S | Individual note recognition (no reference) | = perfect pitch. |
| S | Individual pitches within a chord | Harder than single-note perfect pitch; reportedly much easier to build in childhood than as an adult. |
| S | Comping by ear | Hearing a comping instrument's full chords *and* rhythm — hard partly because comping usually sits under the melody/vocal in a mix, not at the surface. Benchmark cited: transcribing Oscar Peterson's comping. |
| S+ | All individual notes of a chord, by ear, no instrument | Promoted as the single hardest skill on the list — perfect pitch plus harmonic-interval hearing combined. |

## Caveat

No single form here is essential on its own — the biggest lever for real-world ear development named in the source is simply **spending time playing with real musicians**, with this ranking meant as a sequencing guide, not a checklist to complete in full.
