# Ableton Sound Design | Glitch Delay Audio Effect Rack

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=95e5bfB8th8)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=95e5bfB8th8)

## Synopsis

A from-scratch build tutorial (2017, so "Simple Delay" and "Ping Pong Delay" are named as separate stock devices — Live 11+ later folded both into the single [[Delay]] device that's in the vault today) for "Glitch Delay," a custom audio effect rack made from only two delays and one Max for Live [[LFO]]. The throughline is that nearly all of the rack's character comes from mapping an LFO to a delay-time macro rather than from any exotic device — a deliberately minimal, three-device build that produces a genuinely wide range of results (subtle stutter through full deconstruction) purely through macro mapping, LFO wave-shape choice, and nested chain-selector tricks. The build proceeds in clean stages: (1) prove the concept manually — drag a delay's time slider around by hand while a loop plays, hear that this IS the effect; (2) automate that manual drag with an LFO mapped to a macro at Control=0.5/Amount=0.5 (the "full modulation" sweet spot), Random wave, ~5 Hz; (3) add a Feedback macro for a metallic-to-more-metallic range; (4) build a genuine dry/wet macro using the **chain-volume-fade** rack trick (two chains, zones stretched full-width, faded past each other) rather than a Dry/Wet knob on the delay itself — done specifically to leave room to nest a second device in later; (5) nest a Ping Pong Delay inside its own mono/stereo sub-rack using the identical two-chain technique, mapped to the same macros so both delays move together; (6) two finishing macros — LFO Rate range (5–30 Hz) and LFO Curve (skews output toward low or high values) — turn the LFO itself into a performable instrument, not just a fixed modulation source. The video closes by demonstrating the three delay-mode behaviors (Beat sync's rhythmically "locked" glitching vs. **Re-Pitch** mode's tape-throw pitch-bend vs. **Jump** mode's hard-click glitch/distortion) and by running the finished rack across three source types — a percussion loop (obvious, foundational result), a melodic piano comp (subtler, but "busy" in stereo/Ping Pong mode), and a full song with both rhythmic and harmonic content (described as producing the wildest results of the three). A limiter goes at the end of the chain because Jump mode and high feedback can get "really loud."

## Techniques

- **~2:00 — Prove the concept manually before automating it.** Loop a percussion-based sample (recommended source for this kind of demo — busy transient content shows the effect clearly), drop [[Delay]] (era-specific note: video calls it "Simple Delay," the pre-Live-11 name for the same device family now merged into [[Delay]]) on the track, turn on **Stereo Link** (bypasses the right channel's own time setting and applies the left channel's setting to both sides), switch **Beat sync → Time** mode, and push **Dry/Wet to 100%**. While the loop plays, manually drag the delay-time slider around by hand and listen — this manual drag, not any single setting, IS the foundation of the whole Glitch Delay rack; everything after this step exists to automate that one gesture. *(general starting point — no exact BPM/time-range numbers given)* → [[Delay]], [[TEXTURE Slot]]
- **~3:00 — Map delay time (and Beat Division) to a macro, delay mode to a separate macro.** Group the delay into an [[Audio Effect Rack]] and map **Delay Time** to one macro; also map **Beat Division** to that *same* macro (so turning the one knob moves both the free-time and synced-division behavior together depending on mode); map the **delay-mode button** (Beat sync / Time) to its own separate macro so the mode itself becomes a performable toggle rather than a fixed choice. → [[Delay]], [[Randomized Rack Design]]
- **~3:30 — LFO → macro at Control=0.5, Amount=0.5 is the "full modulation" sweet spot.** Map [[LFO]] to the delay-time macro via Map → click macro. **Control** (0–1 range) sets the macro's default/center value; **Amount** (0–1 range) sets how far the LFO swings the macro from that center. Both parameters land at exactly **0.5** for "full modulation" — center-parked with maximum swing room in both directions, the starting point worth trying first on any LFO→macro map before hand-tuning further. → [[LFO]], [[Randomized Rack Design]]
- **~4:00 — Random wave + ~5 Hz rate is what turns modulation into "glitch."** Switch the LFO's **Wave** menu to **Random** (stepped, unpredictable values each cycle — not a smooth sweep) and set **Rate to ~5 Hz** — this combination is what produces the rhythmically-relevant, crude "beat-shuffling" character the rack is named for, especially in **Beat sync** delay mode (the sync'd mode reads as more rhythmically locked-in than free Time mode with the same LFO settings). → [[LFO]], [[Rhythm and Groove]]
- **~4:45 — A Feedback macro for a metal-to-more-metal control.** Map the delay's **Feedback** to its own macro; higher feedback reads as an increasingly metallic character on top of the existing glitch modulation — a one-knob "how extreme" control layered on top of the LFO's rhythmic chaos. → [[Delay]]
- **~5:00 — Build the dry/wet macro as a two-chain crossfade, not a Dry/Wet knob — specifically to leave room for a second device later.** Rather than mapping the delay's own Dry/Wet knob to a macro, create a **dry chain** (empty passthrough) and a **wet chain** (the delay) inside the rack, open the **Chain Select editor**, stretch both chains' zones to span the full 0–127 range, then adjust each chain's **fade range** and **zone range** so 0 = fully dry and 127 = fully wet, with a smooth crossfade in between — then map that **chain selector** itself to a macro. Stated reason for choosing the harder two-chain route over the simple knob: it leaves the rack able to later accommodate a second delay (the Ping Pong sub-rack) inside the same wet path. This is the identical mechanism documented independently on [[Randomized Rack Design]] ("the chain-volume-fade dry/wet macro," sourced from a different, later dnksaus video) — this Glitch Delay build (2017) is an earlier demonstration of the same core Ableton rack trick. → [[Randomized Rack Design]], [[Delay]]
- **~6:30 — Nest a second rack (mono/stereo split) inside the wet chain for a Ping Pong Delay.** To add stereo width without disturbing the existing macro mapping: group the Simple Delay into **its own** audio effect rack (a rack nested inside the outer Glitch Delay rack), then build a **mono chain** and a **stereo chain** inside that inner rack using the *same* two-chain zone-stretch technique as the dry/wet macro above. Drop [[Delay]] (Ping Pong mode, the era's separate "Ping Pong Delay" device) into the stereo chain, map its corresponding parameters (time/feedback/mode) to the **same macros** already driving the mono Simple Delay so both delays track together rather than needing independent control, fully expand Ping Pong Delay's **band-pass filter** section, and set its own **Dry/Wet to 100%** (since the outer rack's dry/wet macro already handles the overall blend). Nested racks + the shared-macro mapping is the reusable idea — not specific to delay. → [[Delay]], [[Width and Stereo]], [[Randomized Rack Design]]
- **~9:00 — Two finishing macros make the LFO itself a performable instrument: Rate range and Curve.** Map the LFO's **Frequency/Rate** knob to its own macro, ranged **5–30 Hz** in this build (arbitrary — "set it however you'd like") so the glitch speed becomes a live-turnable knob. Map the LFO's **Curve** parameter to a second macro, ranged roughly **0.3–(unclear upper bound)** — Curve skews the LFO's *output value distribution*: at Curve = 1 the output is unaffected (even spread), below 1 the LFO outputs more low values more often, above 1 it outputs more high values more often. Net effect: Rate controls how fast the glitching happens, Curve controls whether the randomization leans toward subtle (low-value-biased) or extreme (high-value-biased) results — two independent performance axes on top of the base rack. → [[LFO]], [[Randomized Rack Design]]
- **~9:45 — Delay-mode behavior is a creative choice, not just a technical setting; add a limiter before pushing extremes.** Right-click the delay's title bar to pick a transition mode: **Re-Pitch** makes the delay behave like a tape delay — changes in delay time bend the pitch of the repeats (smoother, more musical glitch). **Jump** causes audible clicks on every delay-time change — "mangles and distorts" the output much harder, a genuinely different, more aggressive character than Re-Pitch. Because Jump mode + high feedback can get "really loud," a **limiter** goes at the very end of the effects chain before pushing the rack to its extremes. → [[Delay]], [[Limiter]]
- **~10:30 — Source material changes what the rack sounds like: percussive vs. melodic vs. full mix.** On a **percussive loop**, the effect is obvious and foundational (what the whole build was designed/demoed around). On a **melodic piano comp**, the effect reads as noticeably subtler — less obviously "glitchy" — but switching to the Ping Pong/stereo path specifically creates "a lot of interesting and busy" movement even on melodic material. On a **full song mix** carrying both rhythmic and harmonic elements together, the combination is called out as producing the wildest, most extreme results of the three source types tested. Worth choosing source material deliberately based on which of these three results is the goal (subtle texture vs. busy stereo movement vs. full deconstruction). → [[Sound Palette]], [[TEXTURE Slot]]
