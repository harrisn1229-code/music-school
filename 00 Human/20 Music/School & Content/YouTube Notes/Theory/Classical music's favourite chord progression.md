# Classical music's favourite chord progression

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9vWyS_JGwxQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9vWyS_JGwxQ)

## Synopsis

Covers "La Folia," described as "the 12-bar blues of classical music" — a minor-key ground-bass template used across centuries of Baroque, Classical, and film/TV scoring, and (per Bennett) only one confirmed pop-song use.

## Techniques

- **The progression**: minor key, i–V–i–♭VII–i–V, looping — a ground-bass (baseline) pattern rather than a "chord progression" in the modern sense; harmonizing that repeating bass line in traditional minor-key harmony is what produces the chord sequence. Compare: Pachelbel's Canon in D is the major-key equivalent of a famous ground-bass work.
- **Origin and spread**: a Portuguese folk dance from the Renaissance era that spread across Europe and became one of the most reused templates in Baroque music — many pieces literally named "La Folia" or "Folia" because the form was explicitly treated as a compositional theme/template to base new works on, not just a passing similarity.
- **Beyond Baroque**: also found in Beethoven and in Liszt's "Rhapsodie espagnole" (named "Spanish" specifically because of La Folia's association with the Iberian peninsula — composers reached for it to evoke a "Spanish" sound even though the tune originated in Portugal).
- **Modern film/TV usage as a genre signifier**: Vangelis used it in his score for *1492: Conquest of Paradise* (Renaissance-era setting); Nicholas Britell used it in *Succession*; Adam Crystal in *Bonding*; James Newton Howard in *Restoration* (explicitly set in the 17th century, the form's period of peak popularity).
- **Meter association, not a hard rule**: virtually every historical example is in 3/4 time, tied to La Folia's association with the sarabande dance — but Bennett notes this is tradition, not a requirement of the harmonic form itself.
- **The one pop example found**: "Oops!... I Did It Again" (Britney Spears) — the only pop song Bennett could find using this progression, despite its huge classical footprint.
- **Two caveats given directly**: the "full" Folia form includes a turnaround variation on the repeat (a quicker V→i resolution the second time through); what's commonly called "La Folia" today is specifically the *later* Folia — an earlier, somewhat different version of the form also existed historically.
