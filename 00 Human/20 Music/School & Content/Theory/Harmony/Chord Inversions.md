# Chord Inversions

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Chords]]. Same notes, reordered so a chord tone other than the root is in the bass. The bass note is the single most important note for a chord's feel.

## The inversions of C major

| Position | Notes | Slash symbol |
|---|---|---|
| Root position | C E G | C |
| 1st inversion | E G C | C/E |
| 2nd inversion | G C E | C/G |

- Triads have 2 inversions; 4-note chords ([[Seventh Chords]]) have 3 (Cmaj7/B etc.)
- Read "C/E" as **"C over E"**
- Alternate letter system: Ca = root, Cb = 1st inv, Cc = 2nd inv, Cmaj7d = Cmaj7/B
- Worked 4-note example with feels per inversion: [[Chord Naming by Context]]

## Slash chords: inversion vs non-chord bass
- **Inversion** — bass note IS a chord tone (C/E)
- **Non-chord bass** — bass note isn't in the chord (C/D). More specialized; works in fewer contexts
- A slash chord often *analyzes* as a root-position chord: F/D sounds as Dm7; C/D ≈ D7sus4(9). One symbol for playing, another for analysis ([[Chord Naming by Context]])
- **Full non-chord-bass catalog**: all twelve major triads over a fixed bass note, worked and named one by one (which ones are just inversions vs. genuinely new chords, and how to decode each) → **[[Slash Chords]]**

## Why invert
1. **Less hand movement** — faster changes, less looking down
2. **Voice leading** — keep common tones between chords; smoother progressions ([[Chord Progressions]])
3. **Bass line control** — walk the bass stepwise under static chords (C → C/B → Am…)
4. **Comfort** — nearby shapes instead of jumps

## On guitar, inversion is mostly *omission*
Everything above assumes you play all the notes and choose which goes lowest — true at a piano. On guitar you have four fingers and six strings, so the live decision is usually **which tones to drop**, and the bass note follows from that rather than driving it.

- **Keep the 3rd and 7th** (the guide tones) — they carry quality and function. **Drop the 5th first**, then the root. A rootless voicing is not an exotic case, it's the default when a bass player is present.
- `E B D G` under a chord diagram is a **Cmaj9 with no C in it** — 3rd, 7th, 9th, 5th. Nothing about that reads as "inversion" in the classical sense, but it is the same underlying choice.
- An **altered** 5th (♭5/♯5) is a colour tone, not a 5th — it stays when a plain 5th would go.
- Because a written pitch exists in several places on the neck, the same "inversion" has multiple grips with different tone and different reachable next chords — see [[Reading Notation on Guitar]].

Full treatment, the six-category system it comes from, and drop 2 / shell / rootless families: **[[Chord Voicings]]**, built from [[Joe Pass Guitar Chords - Enrichment|Joe Pass Guitar Chords]] p.2–13.

## Shape-level vs. functional inversion
A guitar voicing can be *labeled* an inversion (some chord tone other than the root sits under your fingers) while *functioning* as plain root position — because function depends on the lowest note of the **whole ensemble/song**, not on the guitarist's own voicing. If the bass player (or another instrument) is still holding the root underneath, an "inverted" guitar shape isn't actually inverting anything musically — it's just a rootless-sounding voicing of the same root-position chord. Two separate practices follow from this:
- **Shape practice** — cycling through a chord's inversion fingerings purely as fretboard mapping (e.g. jumping around a D7 chord's inversion shapes while still "just thinking of D," with no intention that a bass instrument actually sound each one).
- **Functional use** — a specific moment in a real piece where the bass line *itself* has an inversion happening, driving genuinely different low-end motion. This is the sense that actually produces the "why invert" benefits above (voice leading, stepwise bass control).
Both are worth practicing, but they are not the same skill — a guitarist can be fluent in inversion shapes without ever using a functional inversion in a real arrangement, and vice versa. → Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared, [[Inverted Guitar Chords - Guitar chord inversions explained]]

## Classical context: doubling & the restricted 2nd inversion
In strict 4-voice writing, WHICH note gets doubled depends on the inversion (root position doubles the root; 1st inversion doubles soprano for major, bass-or-soprano for minor, bass for diminished; 2nd inversion always doubles the bass/5th) — full table and the three narrow contexts where 2nd inversion is actually allowed (cadential 6/4, passing 6/4, pedal 6/4) live on [[Part-Writing]]. Diminished triads are always used in first inversion in this style.

- **A 7th chord minus its root reads as a specific, nameable triad — the mechanism behind rootless-drop-3 comping and one concrete altered-dominant reharmonization.** Cmaj7 (C-E-G-B) with the C removed is an **E minor triad**; Dm7 (D-F-A-C) with the D removed is an **F major triad**; G7 (G-B-D-F) with the G removed is a **B diminished triad** — not a coincidence, a fixed relationship (a 7th chord minus its root = the triad built on its 3rd). - **A second, independent worked source for the "advance every note one slot" derivation method (previously documented here only from Jack Ruch's E7 example) — plus a precise clarification of inversion-naming convention.** **Naming:** counted by how many times you've inverted, not by which chord tone sits on the bottom in isolation — root on the bottom is root position (not "un-inverted," its own category); first time inverted (3rd in bass) is **1st inversion**; second time (5th in bass) is **2nd inversion**; for 4-note chords only, third time (7th in bass) is **3rd inversion** — a common mix-up flagged directly (it's *not* "3rd inversion because the 3rd is on the bottom"). **Method, same mechanism as the Jack Ruch entry below:** identify each string's current chord-tone role in a voicing, then advance the note on each string to the next role up in the chord's own formula (triad: 5→1→3→5…; add the 7 for a seventh chord: 5→7→1→3→5…) — repeating the cycle walks root position through every inversion and back to the start an octave up. Recommends knowing the major scale's whole-step/half-step *structure* (not note names) cold, so "next chord tone up this string" can be counted directly. **Worked in full on three different chords** (a change from Jack Ruch's single-chord E7 treatment): **open D** (two different chord-tone subsets — top three strings 5-1-3, and open-D-root-retained 1-5-3 on strings 4/3/1 — each independently walked through all three inversions, showing the method is shape-independent); **C** (5th-string 1-5-3 voicing, same walk); **Cmaj7** (adds the 7th to the C shape for a 1-5-3-7 close voicing with a half step between 7 and root — "creepy gorgeous" — then walks all three inversions of the 4-note chord; **the 3rd inversion, 7 in the bass, is flagged as genuinely awkward both to finger and to hear** — "quite unstable," an honest counter-example to treating every mechanically-valid inversion as equally usable). **Generalizes to any big chord:** rather than inverting a whole 6-string barre chord at once, pull any 1-3-5 (or larger) chord-tone subset out of it and invert just that piece with the same method — different subsets of the same big E-shape chord land on shapes **physically identical to ones already found on D and C earlier in the video**, demonstrating there are only so many usable close-voicing shapes and they recur across "parent" chords. **Practice philosophy:** spend real time deriving a chord's tones yourself before checking a diagram (the derivation is the skill, "even if that took you like an hour, that's amazing study time"), and once mapped, the higher-value practice is re-deriving on a *new* chord repeatedly rather than memorizing a few shapes to mastery — "teaching yourself to fish instead of just getting a fish." - **A universal method for deriving any chord's inversions on any string set: cycle every note in the voicing up to the next role in the chord's own formula.** For a dominant 7th (root-3rd-5th-♭7th), take any 4-note voicing and advance each note one slot — root moves to where the 3rd was, 3rd moves to where the 5th was, 5th moves to where the ♭7th was, and the ♭7th loops back to the root — and repeating the cycle three more times walks through root position and all three inversions in order, generating a genuinely new fingering each pass rather than just relabeling the same notes. Explicitly framed as generalizing to **any chord quality on any string set** — swap in that chord's own formula (major triad root-3rd-5th, minor 7 root-♭3rd-5th-♭7th) and the same "advance every note one slot" move produces its inversions too, no memorization of individual shapes required once the method is internalized. Worked in full on E7 across three adjacent four-string sets (top four, middle four, bottom four) for 12 shapes total — see [[Chord Voicings]] for the complete worked shape set and its application to an 8-bar blues. → [[Chord Voicings]], [[Seventh Chords]], [[Blues Form and Harmony]] — Jack Ruch, [[Must Know Chords for the Blues - Find Inversions for Any Chord]]
- **Fill the interval gap between one voice-led chord's note and the next with a passing chromatic tone, to build advanced-sounding harmony without naming any theory.** After voice-leading a progression by inversion (moving each note the smallest distance possible when the chord changes — "thinking on guitar like a composer writing for an orchestra or string quartet"), any voice whose move is a whole step or a minor third (not just a half step) can get a **passing note filling that gap** rather than jumping straight there — demonstrated live and unplanned, moving single notes a half step at a time until a new chord (e.g. a plain shape becomes G minor by lowering one note; another move creates a sus chord before it resolves). Framed as literally how harmony as a concept originated: "moving melodies because you wanted the melody to move, and the harmony ends up being something different because the melody moved." → [[Chord Voicings]] — Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared, [Soloing, Theory, Fingerstyle, & More - My 10 Favorite Lessons of 2023](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xeFuUkMRBBM)
- **Pick ONE inversion type and stay in its 5-fret hand position for a whole diatonic scale run, rather than switching shapes chord to chord — the guitar-position form of "pick the inversion for the smoothest voice leading."** Applied to all seven diatonic triads of a major key (G major: G Am Bm C D Em F#dim G), using close triad shapes on the top three strings (G-B-E): committing to root position, or to 1st inversion, or to 2nd inversion for the *entire* run keeps every chord change within a single 5-fret hand position instead of jumping around the neck — the position choice itself, held constant across a full scale, is what produces the smooth connected sound, not a chord-by-chord inversion pick. Three separate 5-fret positions cover the same G-major diatonic set this way: a 1st-inversion position low on the neck (frets 1-5), a 2nd-inversion position in the middle (worked frets: B minor 7-7-7 barred, C major 9-8-8, D major 7-7-5), and a root-position set high up (the 12-12-10-family shape, requiring the widest stretch on the F#dim). Same underlying principle as this note's existing "smoothest bass motion" entries below, generalized from one chord change to a whole scale's worth. → [[Chord Voicings]] (the three triad shapes themselves) — Daniel Seriff, [[Copy This Triad Practice Routine, It'll 10X Your Guitar Playing]]
- **Change only the bass note under an unchanged chord (a slash-chord move) to create movement without touching the harmony itself.** Example: an F minor 7 chord with its bass raised a fourth to B♭ — identical chord tones, different bass note, "feels completely different." A cheap way to add motion to a static-feeling loop. **Left-hand-bass variant: reverse which chord's bass note plays first in a two-chord vamp, without changing the chords.** Brian Wilson's "Surf's Up" verse cycles Gm7 and Dm7, but the bass plays the SECOND chord's note under the FIRST chord's harmony (and vice versa) instead of tracking the chords in lockstep — same two chords, reversed bass order, completely different mood. → [[Bassline Writing]] — Matt Johnson (Sol State), [Master Pianist: 15 Ways To Make BORING Chords EXCITING](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E_-wCr48RJM)
- **Pick each chord's inversion for the smoothest available stepwise bass motion into the next chord, even on the simplest progression.** Worked on I–V–vi–IV (C–G–Am–F): rather than jumping the bass root-to-root, the C chord is voiced in 1st inversion (3rd — E — in the bass) so the bass can drop just a whole step into the G chord's 2nd inversion (5th — D — in the bass), instead of leaping a 4th. Guitar fret numbers given: Cmaj7 1st inv. on the A string 7-9-5-8 (3rd-7th-1st-5th bottom to top). General takeaway: choosing an inversion isn't only about which note ends up on the bottom in isolation — it's about which inversion makes the bass line into (and out of) that chord smoothest, chord by chord through the whole progression. → [[Extended Chords]], [[Chord Voicings]] — Max Konyi, [[Beautifying Simple Chords]]
- **Vary root position vs. inversion at each step of a melody-first harmonization for smoother bass motion** — when building chords underneath an already-written top-line melody (see [[Chord Progressions]]'s melody-first method), deliberately alternate root position and inversions rather than defaulting to root position every time; this is what turns an otherwise-jumpy root-driven bassline into a smooth, stepwise one while the melody stays fixed on top. Worked example targeting A minor: a Cmaj9 voiced in 1st inversion (3-9-5-7-1 from the bass up) as a passing chord, and a Cmaj7 voiced over G (an inverted C chord, not a true G chord) leading into a tritone-substitution cadence. → [[Chord Progressions]], [[Extended Chords]] — [[How to Write Neo-Soul and R&B Chords - A Simple Method|Max Konyi]]
- **Hear root position vs. an inversion from the bass note's own scale-degree feeling, without needing to identify the whole chord first** — a root-position bass note carries the "1" feeling (solid, landed, "bang"); an inverted bass note carries whatever ITS degree's feeling is (a 3rd in the bass reads as "3," a 5th as "5") — since none of those match the landed "1" feeling, you can hear that a chord is inverted before you've worked out which inversion or even the full chord. Trained the same drone-and-repetition way as the 12 scale degrees. → [[Scale Degree Ear Training]] — Max Konyi, [[Answering Your Ear Training Questions (Part 3) Chords and Progressions]]
- **Use shared common tones between adjacent chords as the guide for which note to invert** — when a chord progression has an ugly jump (one voice leaping a wide interval to the next chord), check which notes are already common between the two chords first; the note that ISN'T shared and has to jump is usually the one worth octave-shifting into an inversion, since the shared tones give a natural anchor to build the smoother voicing around. → [[Chord Progressions]] — You Suck at Producing, [How To Write Chord Progressions](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FVQHDTueSIA)
- **Invert the destination chord of a V(7)→I cadence for a smoother landing** — instead of resolving onto the I chord in root position, voice it with a non-root tone (e.g. the 5th) on the bottom; the top voice then moves less between the dominant and the tonic, giving a smoother resolution than a root-position landing. → [[Chord Progressions]] — You Suck at Producing, [You Suck at Music Theory No. 1: Dominance](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uaivGz5sryQ)
- **Bridge two chords with a non-chord bass tone** — hold the *first* chord's voicing, raise only the bass note up two scale steps toward the target chord's root, land on the target a beat later (D → D/E → A).
