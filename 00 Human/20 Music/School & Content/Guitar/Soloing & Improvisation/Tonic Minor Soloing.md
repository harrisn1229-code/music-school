# Tonic Minor Soloing

**Imply a momentary V7–i resolution *inside* a single static minor chord, instead of parking on minor pentatonic for the whole bar.** A bebop-flavored alternative for any minor tune or minor-blues vamp — the [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *Minor blues* rule that "the V frequently stays a dominant" gives the harmonic reason this works: a minor tonic and its own V7 are close enough in sound (they share the tonic-minor sixth-chord family below) that you can hint at both without the rhythm section ever playing the V chord.

## The core sound: minor 6 / minor-major 7 as "tonic minor"

A static minor chord (**i**) can be voiced or implied as a **minor 6** or **minor-major 7 (m(maj7))** chord — both of these contain *both* the i chord **and** its own V7 chord's guide tones stacked together. That shared-tone relationship is what "tonic minor" means here: soloing lines that outline a minor 6/mM7 sound over the plain minor chord automatically flirt with the V7-of-i resolution without anyone changing the harmony.

- **B♭m6** = B♭ D♭ F G — contains G, which reads as the **major 3rd of F7** (V7 of B♭ minor).
- **B♭ minor-major 7** = B♭ D♭ F A — contains A, the same F7 major-3rd relationship from a different extension.

## Device 1 — a single outside note implying a quick 5–1

The cheapest version: land on **one note foreign to the plain minor scale** that happens to be the **3rd of the chord's own V7**, then resolve it down a half step into a chord tone. The ear hears a compressed V7→i cadence even though nothing but the i chord is sounding.

- Over **E♭ minor**, play **D natural → E♭**. D is not in E♭ minor (natural or harmonic), but it *is* the major 3rd of **B♭7**, the V7 of E♭ minor — so the ear hears an implied B♭7→E♭m resolution compressed into two notes.
- Over **B♭ minor**, play **A natural → B♭**, by the identical logic: A is the major 3rd of **F7**, the V7 of B♭ minor.
- **General recipe, any minor key:** find the major 3rd of the key's own V7 (a half step below the tonic) and resolve it down into the root (or another chord tone) — instant implied 5–1 over a chord that never actually moves.

## Device 2 — a full 5–1 line played straight over the static minor

Take it further: play a **complete V7-to-i arpeggio/scale line** (the kind you'd play if the rhythm section actually walked V7→i) directly over the **unchanging minor chord**. Worked example: a line that clearly outlines **F7 moving to B♭ minor** — full dominant-to-tonic vocabulary — played in its entirety over a **static B♭m** vamp, then a plain minor-pentatonic/blues phrase tagged onto the end of it to land back in "home" minor language. The two vocabularies (implied-5-1 and plain minor pentatonic/blues) are meant to be mixed freely in the same phrase, not treated as separate toolkits — start a line in tonic-minor/5-1 language, resolve, then drop into pentatonic/blues phrasing to close it out.

## Why it beats straight minor pentatonic

Plain minor pentatonic over a static minor chord is harmonically safe but harmonically flat — it never generates the tension-and-release the actual changes of a tune would. Tonic minor borrows the bebop habit of *implying* harmonic motion that isn't literally being played, the same principle that makes reharmonization-while-soloing work (see [[Reharmonization]], [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]) — except here it's compressed into a single static chord rather than spread across a whole progression.
