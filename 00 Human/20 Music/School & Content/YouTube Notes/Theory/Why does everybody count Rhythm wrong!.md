## Techniques

- **Core critique of standard counting: "and" gets reused at every subdivision level, which is confusing.** Standard practice counts quarter notes as "1 2 3 4", eighth notes as "1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and", and sixteenths as "1 e and a 2 e and a...". The claim: because "and" appears in both the eighth-note and sixteenth-note counting schemes (at different actual rhythmic positions), it doesn't give a beginner a clean, unambiguous verbal handle on which subdivision density they're actually looking at.
- **The fix — one distinct multi-syllable word per subdivision level, so the word's own rhythm matches the notes:** a single beat-syllable word for quarter notes (spoken once per beat), a two-syllable word for eighth-note pairs (spoken twice per beat, syllables landing on each eighth), a three-syllable word for triplets (three syllables per beat), and a four-syllable word for sixteenth notes (four syllables per beat, syllables landing on each sixteenth). Half notes and whole notes each get their own single sustained word (spoken/held across 2 and 4 beats respectively) since traditional counting has no standard verbal convention for held longer notes at all. Two more distinct words are assigned to the two "backwards" syncopated groupings — an eighth tied into two sixteenths, and two sixteenths tied into an eighth (the workflow's earlier "aceding"/"pizza sub" examples — a common heavy-metal/thrash syncopation shape, cited by name-dropping Slayer as a genre where the second pattern is common).
- **Why this works as a device, independent of the specific (profane) words chosen:** this is functionally the same idea as established rhythm-solfège systems used in real music pedagogy — Kodály's "ta" (quarter) / "ti-ti" (two eighths) / "tri-o-la" (triplet) syllables, or the Takadimi system taught in college aural-skills courses — where the number of spoken syllables is deliberately made to equal the number of notes in that subdivision, so the *sound* of counting the rhythm out loud already carries its rhythmic density before any notation is read. The video reinvents this general approach independently rather than citing it, using comedic words instead of the standard pedagogical syllables — the underlying mechanism (map spoken-syllable density to note density, one distinct word per subdivision level rather than reusing "and") is the actual transferable technique, separate from the specific vocabulary used in the video.
- **Progressive drilling method shown**: present a rhythm using the new counting words, first slow then fast, then combine multiple subdivision levels within the same rhythm (e.g. a bar mixing quarter + eighth-pair + triplet + syncopated groupings back to back) as the difficulty ramps — the same "isolate the new element, then chain it into more complex combinations" practice shape used elsewhere for scale/technique drilling.
- **Practical takeaway for Nate, since this is a teaching-device pattern rather than literal vocabulary to adopt**: when a beat's subdivision is hard to feel by counting numbers, try substituting a spoken nonsense-word (real word or made-up) whose syllable count matches that subdivision level — a two-syllable word for eighth-note pairs, a three-syllable word for triplets, a four-syllable word for sixteenths — and keep that word consistent every time that density shows up, rather than defaulting straight to "1 e and a" style counting for every density at once.
