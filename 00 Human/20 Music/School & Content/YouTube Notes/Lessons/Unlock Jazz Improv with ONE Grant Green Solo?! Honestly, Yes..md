# Unlock Jazz Improv with ONE Grant Green Solo?! Honestly, Yes. 🙌

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fr5Y99XAlQw)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fr5Y99XAlQw)

## Synopsis

Nathan Borton reverse-engineers Grant Green's solo on "Cool Blues" (from the album *Born to Be Blue*, a B♭ jazz blues) into **three named "essential sounds"** he argues every jazz improviser must have, framed as a complete master-class-in-miniature rather than a lick catalog: **5-to-1 ideas** (single-note lines that outline a V→I pull, including strongly altered versions using 9/♭9/♯9), **2-equals-5 / 5-equals-2** (soloing over any dominant 7th chord using the melodic language of its related ii minor chord, and vice versa), and **tonic dominant** (treating a dominant 7th as a *stable* home sound rather than a tension needing resolution, via a major-7-arpeggio-off-the-♭7 trick and the dominant bebop scale). A fourth umbrella, **blues language**, adds three further devices: chromatic walk-ups/walk-downs between chord tones, classic movable blues-opening vocabulary usable over any chord in the form, and a named five-note **Kansas City blues scale**. This is the third Grant Green solo-breakdown video now in the vault (alongside [[How Grant Green Played Jazz Blues]] by Jack Ruch and Borton's own [[Why Grant Green's Minor Blues Soloing Is PERFECT (Jazz 101)]]) and it's the most abstract of the three — Borton names and generalizes each device explicitly ("this will work over any chord in the jazz genre") rather than staying tied to this one tune, which is the most valuable thing about it: these read as reusable soloing *tools*, not just annotations on one solo. The video is a Patreon funnel (a written etude/PDF is gated there), so the free content here is the concept-naming and one demonstrated example per device — exact fretboard positions and full multi-bar transcription aren't in the free captions.

## What's taught

- **Sound 1 — the 5:1 sound: single-note lines outlining a V→I pull, deployed at internal chord changes, not just the final cadence.** Two phrases from the solo "share the same sound" — a V→I chord motion voiced melodically. Applied concretely at the **I→IV transition** (B♭7 → E♭, i.e. treating the arrival on E♭ as a fresh "I" preceded by its own implied V): the line creates forward momentum "instead of using the stagnant chord-to-chord playing that many intermediate musicians often do." → [[Guide Tones]], [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *Why every chord is a dominant 7th*
- **Sound 1, altered version — 9, ♭9, ♯9 make the 5:1 pull stronger.** Two further 5:1 phrases lean on altered dominant tensions rather than plain guide tones: one uses "the great alterations of nine and flat nine," another uses "the sharp nine phrase and the flat nine" — stated directly as pulling more strongly to the target I chord than the plain (unaltered) 5:1 line demonstrated first. General lesson: altering the dominant *increases* the felt resolution pull rather than just adding color. → [[Altered Chords]]
- **Sound 2 — "2 equals 5, 5 equals 2": solo over a bare dominant 7th using its related ii minor's language, no ii chord actually sounding.** Framed as a reharmonization-for-soloing trick: any dominant 7th (worked example, F7) can be thought of as the V of an implied 2-5-1, so its related ii is a fourth below (C minor for F7) — over the actual F7 in the recording, Green "is really not playing an F7-like arpeggio... he's really just playing C minor": up the **C Dorian scale**, then a descending **5-3-2-1** pattern, outlining C minor squarely over the sounding F7. Generalized directly: **"any of the dominant 7 chords found in the blues, you can use minor language from its related two chord and it will sound awesome."** Distinct from (but the same underlying ii/V relationship as) [[Chord Substitution]] § 4 *Interpolating a ii* — that section comps an actual ii chord in front of the dominant; this is soloing the ii's *sound* over the dominant while it's still the only chord sounding. → [[Chord Substitution]] § 4, [[The ii-V-I]], [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]
- **Sound 2, mixed with Sound 1 — a 5:1 phrase resolving into its related-ii language, recurring at the same structural spot (bar 8) nearly every chorus.** Green "will also mix the 5:1 concept with the 5-equals-2 concept by playing 5:1 language moving to its related two chord," stated as happening "pretty much every time in the blues form on bar eight" — a repeatable, form-anchored combination move rather than a one-off. Cross-reference to Borton's Pat Martino breakdown for more of the minor-over-dominant vocabulary (video not yet in the vault). → [[Turnarounds]], [[The ii-V-I]]
- **Sound 3 — tonic dominant: treat a static dominant 7th as a resolved, stable sound rather than tension needing release, via extensions.** Explicitly named "a more focused version of the 2-equals-5 concept, but still kind of in the same realm." Over a sitting E♭7 with no resolution happening, Green highlights the chord's **natural 9th and 13th** as melodic content — the extensions *are* what makes an unmoving dominant chord sound stable rather than stuck. Directly elaborates the vault's existing "the dominant 7th is the home sound, not a tension" principle → [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *Why every chord is a dominant 7th* (now confirmed with a concrete on-the-neck technique, not just the harmonic argument).
- **Sound 3, technique 1 — major-7-arpeggio-off-the-♭7 trick for the tonic-dominant sound.** "Playing the major 7 arpeggio starting on the flat-seven degree of our dominant chord" — over E♭7, play a **D♭ major 7 arpeggio** (D♭-F-A♭-C = the ♭7-9-11-13 of E♭7) to land squarely on the chord's own natural extensions without having to think in extension numbers at all — a portable "borrow the maj7 arpeggio a whole step below the dominant root" formula for any dominant 7th. → [[Extended Chords]], [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]
- **Sound 3, technique 2 — the dominant bebop scale for the same tonic-dominant sound.** "Using the dominant bebop scale over whatever dominant chord you're playing" — matches the vault's own **Bebop dominant** row (Mixolydian + a chromatic passing tone between ♭7 and root) on [[Bebop Vocabulary]] § 1. Demonstrated adding "that chromatic passing tone from our bebop scale and then landing on the thirteenth" — the bebop scale's rhythmic on-the-beat mechanic used here specifically to land on a chosen extension (the 13th) rather than a plain chord tone. **Combine techniques 1+2** for the fullest tonic-dominant line, per Borton's own closing demonstration. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]] § 1
- **Sound 4a — blues language, walk-ups/walk-downs: a chromatic note connecting two chord tones of the dominant chord.** "Anchors the root... and walks down chromatically from the flat seven to the fifth" — a ♭7→(chromatic)→5th descending connector. Stated as reversible (**5th → ♭7 going up**) and extendable (**3rd → 5th**, which Green also uses in this solo, "the third-to-fifth walk-up"). A named, generalized version of the chromatic-connector devices already catalogued on [[Blues Lead Phrasing and Touch]] § *Five chromatic connector licks over a 12-bar blues* and cross-referenced from [[Blues Form and Harmony]]. → [[Blues Lead Phrasing and Touch]]
- **Sound 4b — blues language, classic movable opening vocabulary: works over any chord in the form.** The specific phrase Green opens his "Cool Blues" solo with is called out as generic, reusable "classic blues language that outlines whatever dominant or major chord we're playing over" — usable as-is over B♭7 (where Green plays it), and just as validly over E♭7 (IV) or F7 (V) without modification, because it outlines chord *function* (dominant/major) rather than one specific chord's exact notes.
- **Sound 4c — the Kansas City blues scale: a five-note distillation of the blues scale, guitaristic and movable, with an emphasis on the 6th.** Named directly as "just a five-note scale that kind of distills the sound of the blues scale into five notes and really focuses on that six" — a "very guitaristic pattern that we can move around the fretboard." Green uses it to "riff on and play some melodic ideas." Exact interval formula isn't given in the free captions (gated to Borton's linked lesson) — captured here as a distinct, named blues-vocabulary source worth a home even without the full derivation. → [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]] § *Kansas City blues scale*

No plugin, effect, mixing, or sound/tone-selection content — pure instrument/theory pedagogy, same lane as the vault's other Grant Green breakdowns.
