# Nobody Teaches This — But It Unlocks the Whole Fretboard

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8l_URt1u5Sw)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8l_URt1u5Sw)

## Synopsis

Daniel Seriff teaches a specific, rarely-explained way of deriving the CAGED chord shapes from each other — not by walking a shape up the neck (the standard CAGED explanation), but by **sliding a known chord shape sideways, one string at a time, from the thick strings toward the thin strings**, and fixing the one note that lands on the B string each time (standard tuning's only non-perfect-4th interval, a major 3rd between G and B, means any shape pushed onto the B string reads a half step flat and needs a compensating fret bump). Applied to the open/barre E shape, this mechanically produces the A shape, then the D shape. Applied to the open G shape, the same slide-and-compensate move produces the C shape, then the F shape. The payoff is conceptual, not a new fingering: all three shapes in each family share **one interval skeleton**, just losing a string (and gaining the B-string fix) each time — so a player who knows the E shape's interval layout already effectively knows the A and D shapes, and likewise G→C→F. A quick major-scale/interval-numbering review opens the lesson as a prerequisite. ## What's taught

- **Prerequisite reviewed on-camera:** major scale = W-W-H-W-W-W-H, and scale degrees ("numbers") are key-independent — the actual teaching tool used throughout is scale-degree numbers (1, 3, 5, 7), not letter names, so the same shape's numbers transfer to any key.
- **E shape (open/barre) spelled by number:** low to high, **1-5-1-3-5-1** (worked in the key of E: E-B-E-G#-B-E). Framed explicitly as still "just a triad" (3 unique notes: root, 3rd, 5th) despite 6 fretted notes — the doubling/repetition across strings is what makes open and barre chords sound full without amplification (stated as likely the historical reason for these particular voicings, pre-amplification).
- **The string-shift derivation method (the core technique of the video):** instead of moving a chord shape up/down the neck (standard CAGED), **copy the exact fingering onto the next string down (toward the floor/thinner strings)** — the string that falls off the bottom (originally the lowest string played) is dropped. The note now landing on the B string is off (flat) because of the guitar's one non-uniform tuning interval (G→B is a major 3rd, not a perfect 4th like every other string pair) — **compensate by raising just that B-string note one half step (one fret).**
- **E → A shape:** sliding the E shape down one string and compensating the B string produces the **A shape** — 1-5-1-3-5 low to high (A string=root, D=5th, G=root, B=3rd [compensated], high E=5th). Stated explicitly: same interval structure as the E shape, minus the note that fell off the low E string.
- **A → D shape:** sliding again (drop the A-string note) raises a raw major-7th sound before compensation; compensating the B string produces the classic **D shape** — 1-5-1-3 across D-G-B-E strings (only 4 strings played; low E and A excluded). **Practical tip: the fully-fretted "stretchy" version of this pushed-down D-shape voicing (a wide 4-string stretch) is genuinely hard to play — Seriff recommends using it as a visualization/interval-reference tool and playing only the comfortable strings rather than forcing the whole grip.**
- **G shape spelled by number:** 1-3-5-1-3-1 (classic open/3-finger G). Seriff's own aside: the open-B-string 3-finger G "sounds really goofy" for regular rhythm playing — he recommends a beefier, open-power-chord-style G as the everyday default, and frames the 3-finger version here only as the shape needed for this derivation exercise. Also notes the inner-four-strings portion of the G shape works well for Hendrix-style embellishments combined with the box pentatonic.
- **G → C shape:** slide down one string (drop the high-E note), the G-string note now lands on B and needs the one-fret compensation → produces the classic **C shape** (no low E played): 1-3-5-1-3 across A-D-G-B-E.
- **C → F shape:** slide down one more string (drop the A-string note), compensate the B string again → produces what Seriff calls **"the F shape"** — 1-3-5-1 across D-G-B-E only (no low E/A). Notes this is functionally "part of the E-shape barre chord" but treats it as its own reusable, highly movable grip — useful specifically for staying off the muddy low E/A strings, works well as a second-guitarist part playing higher/more embellished voicings, and takes well to Hendrix/SRV-style pinky embellishments.
- **Framing/payoff stated directly:** "the E, the A, and the D shapes are all born from the exact same interval pattern" (and separately, G/C/F share their own single pattern) — the practical benefit is not needing to separately memorize each shape's numbers; understanding the derivation "will quickly improve your ear-to-guitar-neck relationship."
