# Bass Scale Shapes — Major, Minor, Pentatonic & Blues

From BassBuzz's beginner "7 Days to Learning Bass Scales" routine — five movable fretboard shapes (major, major pentatonic, minor, minor pentatonic, blues) taught with a running spice-cabinet metaphor: a scale is a subset of the 12-note chromatic alphabet guaranteed to sound coherent together, and a **shape** is just a memorized finger pattern that locates that subset relative to a root note — movable to any starting fret, so "C major scale" and "F major scale" are the exact same shape, just anchored to a different root. Each shape below is drilled first as a bare scale, then internalized by playing a real riff/melody built from it — melodies use more of the scale's notes than a typical bassline does, which is why riffs (not basslines) are the internalization tool here. The freeform half of internalization — improvising over a drone instead of copying a fixed riff — lives on its own page: [[Improvising Over a Drone Track]].

## Major scale
Root example: **C major**, played from the **8th fret of the E string** rather than open position, specifically to avoid a bigger stretch. Fingering: **middle–pinky–index–middle–pinky–index–ring–pinky**. The "everywhere" scale — happy/triumphant sound, basis for most of the rest of tonal theory (formula and key table: [[Scales]]).
- **Internalization riff:** Howard Shore's Shire theme ("Concerning Hobbits") from *The Lord of the Rings*, played low in the same fretboard position as the scale shape, same fingering pattern (middle-pinky-index-pinky-index-pinky-middle, etc.) — can also be played up an octave using the identical shape.
- **Alternate position, C major from the 3rd fret of the A string** (a different BassBuzz lesson's home position for the same shape, one string set over from the 8th-fret-E-string version above): fingering up **middle–pinky–index–middle–pinky–index–ring–pinky**, back down **pinky–ring–index–pinky–middle–index–pinky–middle**. Named beginner failure mode taught here: forcing the fretting hand to hold a stretch across all four frets of the shape at once, rather than **microshifting** (letting the hand creep finger-to-finger for comfort using the same fingering) — called the single most common thing that turns a shape learnable in a day into a weeks-long struggle. Transposability demo from this position: relocating the identical shape to the 3rd fret of the E string gives **G major** (for "Brown Eyed Girl," Van Morrison), and to the 5th fret of the E string gives **A major** (for "Stir It Up," Bob Marley) — general rule, anchor the shape's root on the **E or A string** so enough strings remain below to finish the shape.
  - **Hidden notes — open strings inside the shape.** Sliding this same shape down to the 2nd fret and then the 1st fret eventually lands its index-finger notes on **open strings** rather than a fretted note — same shape, same note relationships (confirmed by the fretboard dot pattern), just a changed fingering since an open string doesn't need fretting. Unlocks **F major** and **B♭ major** from the 1st-fret positions, which beginners often wrongly assume the shape "can't reach" because the fingering looks different there. Named application: Jaco Pastorius's version of "The Chicken" in B♭, playable with this exact 1st-fret open-string variant.
  - **Copy-paste technique — extending range by duplicating chunks.** The shape is three "chunks," one per string it spans; because the shape is movable, any chunk can be copied into an adjacent octave to reach notes outside the shape's fixed 8-note pool. **Down for low notes:** in D major, the shape's root sits on a middle string, putting the bass's beefiest low notes out of reach — copying the shape's **top two chunks down an octave**, anchored on the low D, recovers those low notes with the same fingering (useful for "kicking a song into heavy gear"). **Up for high notes:** in A major, copying the **top two chunks up an octave** from the low A to the high A unlocks extra high notes for a bass fill. Either direction, the very last chunk usually can't be copied once the bass runs out of strings — the notes lost that way are just unreachable, not different. *(Related but distinct from [[Bass Pentatonic Riff-Writing Recipe]]'s octave-shift move, which relocates one or two individual notes — two strings up, two frets over — to extend a single pentatonic riff's melodic range mid-phrase, rather than duplicating a whole per-string chunk to extend the shape's overall range.)*
  - Two reusable follow-on skills built on top of this shape: [[Steal Like a Bassist — Ear-Training from Known Melodies]] (locating a known melody by ear inside the shape) and [[The Bingo Method — Number-Pattern Riff Generation]] (generating original basslines/solos from numeric patterns through the shape).
  - **Two more internalization riffs, taught via a scale-degree-first analysis method rather than by-ear/tab-copy:** "Deck the Halls" in **E major** — starts on the 5th scale degree, walks down 5-4-3-2-1-2-3-1; and (caption-garbled, medium confidence) a **G major** melody, root→5th→4th→3rd→2nd→octave→back to 5th→repeat→4-3-4, whose phrasing and the video's own next-sentence name-drop strongly point to the *Star Wars* main title. Explicit teaching principle behind both: analyze what **scale-degree number** each melody note is (not its raw tab position), so the melody transposes cleanly to any key later — the source video calls this out as "the biggest hint" for melody practice, and it's the same transposable-vocabulary goal as [[Steal Like a Bassist — Ear-Training from Known Melodies]], just derived analytically instead of found by ear. → BassBuzz, [[How to Learn Bass Scales (Become a Better Bassist, Not a Robot)]]
  - **Three real analyzed bass lines demonstrating "root notes on the chord changes, then add scale flavor around that foundation" — a stated bass-line-construction principle in its own right, distinct from the internalization riffs above (those are melodies played straight; these are basslines built from the shape):**
    - **Bob Babbitt / James Jamerson, F major (Motown-style; specific track unconfirmed — caption-garbled, no song title captured).** Root notes **F, G, B♭, C** on beats 1 and 3 (the chord changes) as the foundation; flavor on top — a connecting **A** (the 3rd) bridging G to B♭, and on the C chord a classic Motown fill: **5th–6th–octave–octave**, called out as a memorizable mini-shape worth stealing into other lines.
    - **Tom Hamilton (Aerosmith), A major.** Root notes A, E, F♯, D on the changes; pattern root→octave→3rd→4th (into the E), dip to the 2nd, back up through the 5th to the 6th (F♯), walk down through the 5th to the 4th (D), close 4th→5th→root.
    - **Aston "Family Man" Barrett (Bob Marley's bassist), A major — very likely "Stir It Up" (see the alternate-position A-major reference above; the source video steals this exact line two beats later in its own outro jam).** Root notes reduced to just **A and D** (bass rests through the guitar's E chord); over A: **1-2-3-1**; over D (the 4th degree): **4-5-6-4**, the identical mini-shape repeated up a string.
    - *(The Hamilton/Barrett attribution boundary is caption-garbled in the source — both patterns are captured faithfully but which phrase belongs to which bassist is medium-confidence, not verbatim.)* → BassBuzz, [[How to Learn Bass Scales (Become a Better Bassist, Not a Robot)]]

## Major pentatonic scale
Derived by **removing the 4th and 7th degrees from the major scale shape** — 7 notes down to 5 ("penta" = five), same shape with two fingers lifted out rather than a pattern learned from scratch. Matches [[Scales]]'s major-pentatonic formula (1 2 3 5 6) exactly. Root example: **C**, played from the **3rd fret of the A string** — a different fretboard position than the major scale's C above, same pitch. Frets are further apart in this position, so expect to **micro-shift** the fretting hand along the string rather than holding one big stretch.
- **Internalization riff:** the intro guitar riff from "My Girl" (The Temptations, 1964; originally played by Robert White of Motown's Funk Brothers) — same fingering as the scale (middle-pinky-index-pinky-index-pinky), micro-shifting as needed.
- Named songs that lean on this scale's flavor: Ramble On (Led Zeppelin), Jump, Jive an' Wail (Louis Prima), Crazy Little Thing Called Love (Queen).
- **This shape is the *static* shape only — there's a second, "shifting" hand shape for the same five notes** (two hand positions instead of one, tighter reach, and the only version that can set up a slide), plus a four-step recipe for actually composing riffs with these notes rather than just drilling the shape: [[Bass Pentatonic Riff-Writing Recipe]].

## Minor scale
Root example: **F♯ minor** (transposable to any root, same shape). Fingering: **index–ring–pinky–index–ring–pinky–index–ring**; explicitly flagged to **micro-shift rather than force a big stretch** on the lower frets, to protect the hand/wrist. The "sad"/melancholy counterpart to major (formula: [[Scales]]'s natural-minor 1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7).
- **Internalization riff:** the guitar riff from "Crazy Train" (Ozzy Osbourne; originally Randy Rhoads) — same fingering as the scale shape (index-ring-pinky-ring-index-pinky-ring-pinky-index-pinky-ring), plus an **open low E** that isn't part of the fretted shape but is just the lower octave of the scale's 7th degree — i.e. it still belongs to the scale, it's just played open instead of fretted.
- **Named songs using this scale (from a metal-focused commentary video):** **Iron Maiden's "Aces High"** — a dense, fast riff that looks intimidating ("OMG, there's so many notes") but is fully explainable by the **E minor scale**: once the scale's already drilled, every note in the riff is one you've already practiced in order, so it isn't learned from scratch. Bassist **Steve Harris** is cited as saying he doesn't know formal theory and just plays by ear — yet basically every Maiden riff (The Trooper, Wrathchild, Rime of the Ancient Mariner named alongside Aces High) runs on this same minor scale, a real-world instance of "theory-by-ear still produces theory-shaped results." → BassBuzz, [[10 Songs That Taught Me Metal (Easy to Effin’ Hard)]]

## Arpeggios built from the same minor-scale shape (bonus)
Not covered by the 7-day routine above, but a direct extension of the minor scale shape's root-position notes: an **arpeggio** is just the 1st, 3rd, and 5th degrees of a scale played as a line rather than the full scale run — so a **D minor arpeggio** (D–F–A) is literally three notes already inside the D minor scale shape documented above, just with the 2nd, 4th, 6th, and 7th degrees skipped. Worked example from a metal-commentary video: Metallica's Cliff Burton, citing J.S. Bach as a direct influence, opens a famous bass intro with exactly this — a **D minor arpeggio leading into a C major arpeggio** (1-3-5 of C: C–E–G) — described as still sounding "super badass" as a shred-adjacent melodic device decades later. **General recipe pulled from the same source:** take a scale shape you already know, pick out its 1-3-5, experiment with both the major and minor arpeggio built the same way from different roots, and add distortion for the tone. Not enough standalone arpeggio content yet from a single video to justify a dedicated arpeggio technique note — flagged here as a gap worth filling if more arpeggio-specific material shows up. → BassBuzz, [[10 Songs That Taught Me Metal (Easy to Effin’ Hard)]]

## Minor pentatonic scale
Derived by **removing the 2nd and 6th degrees from the minor scale shape**. Matches [[Scales]]'s minor-pentatonic formula (1 ♭3 4 5 ♭7). Root example: **E minor pentatonic**. Fingering: **index-pinky-index-ring-index-ring**.
- **Internalization riff:** the intro riff to "Knock on Wood" (Eddie Floyd; bass by Duck Dunn), which walks the scale in order — fingering index-pinky-index-ring(or pinky) on the 9th fret, back to index, back to ring/pinky; listen for the offbeat notes.
- Named songs using this scale: Moby Dick (Led Zeppelin), I Shot the Sheriff (Bob Marley), Paranoid (Black Sabbath), Black Night (Deep Purple) — all flagged as having trickier rhythm or faster note runs than Knock on Wood, which is exactly why Knock on Wood was picked as the teaching riff instead of any of those.
- **Shifting-shape variant:** the same minor pentatonic notes fretted across two hand positions instead of one (pinky on root, index-pinky for the next two notes, shift up two frets for the rest) — used for riffs the static shape above can't reach cleanly, and the only version that can set up a slide mid-riff. Full fingering, when to reach for it over the static shape, and the riff-composition recipe built around both shapes: [[Bass Pentatonic Riff-Writing Recipe]].

## Blues scale
Built from the minor pentatonic **plus one chromatic passing note** (the "blue note," also called the **tritone**) inserted between the 4th and ♭5th degrees — matches the six-note minor-pentatonic-plus-♭5 formula documented on [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]]. Root example: **D blues scale**. Fingering: **index-pinky-index-middle-ring-index-ring**, or the easier-stretch alternative **index-pinky-index-middle-pinky-index-pinky**.
- **Naming caveat, straight from the lesson:** "blues scale" is a name, not a rule — it's usable over blues changes but not exclusive to them, and playing a blues progression doesn't require it either.
- **Internalization riff:** "Sunshine of Your Love" (Cream; Jack Bruce on fretless bass) — two fingerings offered: the full scale-fingering stretch (ring-index start), or a compressed alternative (pinky-index-pinky-pinky-middle-index, then stretch index-pinky-index) that delays the pinky stretch until the very end of the phrase.
- Named songs using this scale: Heartbreaker (Led Zeppelin), I Want to Take You Higher (Sly and the Family Stone), Bombtrack (Rage Against the Machine).

## Focus philosophy: one or two scales at a time

Named directly as "a big mistake" seen repeatedly in students: trying to learn a bunch of scales all at once, which spreads focus so thin that no single scale ever gets used well. The fix — and the stated reasoning *behind* the pacing the 7-day structure below already implements structurally (one shape per day, not five at once): **focus on just one, maybe two scales at a time**, and fully "squeeze the musical juice out of them" via internalization riffs and real playing before moving on to the next. → BassBuzz, [[How to Learn Bass Scales (Become a Better Bassist, Not a Robot)]]

## A leaner alternative: the 3-step, 15-minute daily routine

A compact single-session structure (as opposed to the 7-day rotation below, which spreads all five shapes across a week) for getting one scale into actual playing fast:
1. **Robot Stage (~5 min)** — drill the bare scale shape "like a robot" until it's memorized cold, with good fretting technique.
2. **Melody (~5–10 min)** — learn a melody or bass line built from the scale, analyzing scale-degree numbers rather than rote-memorizing tab positions (method and worked examples above, § Major scale).
3. **Create (~5 min)** — write original bass-line ideas using the scale, applying the foundation-then-flavor principle: root notes on the chord changes first, then scale-derived flavor notes filling in around that foundation (worked examples above, § Major scale). Practical tip for backing tracks to practice this over: search "jam tracks major scale."
→ BassBuzz, [[How to Learn Bass Scales (Become a Better Bassist, Not a Robot)]]

## The 7-day practice structure
All five shapes above get folded into a 7-day rotation rather than "a new scale every day" — deliberately paced, because a scale only actually sticks with repeated use across multiple sessions, not a single pass:
- **Day 1** — major scale shape + Shire theme riff, 5–10 min.
- **Day 2** — major pentatonic shape + My Girl riff, 5–10 min; review day 1 if time allows.
- **Day 3** — first drone-improv session (major / major pentatonic) — method: [[Improvising Over a Drone Track]].
- **Day 4** — minor scale shape + Crazy Train riff, plus review of days 1–3.
- **Day 5** — minor pentatonic shape + Knock on Wood riff; review whichever prior shape is the weakest link first.
- **Day 6** — second drone-improv session (minor / minor pentatonic).
- **Day 7** — blues scale shape + Sunshine of Your Love riff, plus a final review pass across every shape and riff covered.
- Dosage per day is small on purpose — roughly 5–10 minutes of shape/riff work, plus (on improv days) another 5–10 minutes of drone improv; review of prior days is folded into most days rather than treated as separate homework.
