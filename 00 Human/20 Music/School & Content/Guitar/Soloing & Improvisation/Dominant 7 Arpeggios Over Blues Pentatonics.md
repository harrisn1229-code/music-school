# Dominant 7 Arpeggios Over Blues Pentatonics

Part of [[Guitar Positions]]. **Stay in the I-chord pentatonic box for most of the solo, then drop into the IV7 or V7 chord's own dominant-7 arpeggio exactly when that chord arrives** — the arpeggio is a brief, targeted insert triggered by the chord change, not a wholesale scale swap. Worked over a **12-bar blues shuffle in G**, using G major and G minor pentatonic as the home base and full four-note dominant-7 arpeggios (root–3rd–5th–♭7) for the IV (C7) and V (D7) chords only — the I chord (G) is never given its own arpeggio because the pentatonics already outline it well. Sits between [[Diagonal Pentatonic Blues Soloing]] (one shape, one note edited per chord — the minimal-footprint version of "track the changes") and [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]] (a full CAGED arpeggio for *every* chord, built for progressions where chord tones diverge chord-to-chord) — this technique targets only the two chords that actually need it (IV7, V7) and leaves the I chord alone, using **complete four-note arpeggio shapes** rather than a single altered note like [[Single-Note Pentatonic Substitutions Over Changes]].

## The core idea: pentatonic home base, arpeggio only on the changes
Default vocabulary is **G major pentatonic and G minor pentatonic**, freely mixed, the way most blues players already solo. The addition: **the instant the IV or V chord hits, reach for that chord's own dominant-7 arpeggio** (root, 3rd, 5th, ♭7) instead of continuing to run pentatonic licks over it — then return to pentatonic once the chord passes or the phrase resolves. The stated payoff: solos that "really sound like the chord progression, that really outline the changes," because the ear hears the arpeggio notes land squarely on each new chord rather than pentatonic notes that merely don't clash.

## Why it works: the target notes the pentatonics don't have
The specific value of reaching for the arpeggio is the **notes it adds that neither G major nor G minor pentatonic contain** — most notably **the major 3rd of D7 (F♯)**, which is in neither pentatonic scale and is called out explicitly as a strong target: "a note like that is really strong for driving home the sound of that chord." The general principle (matches [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] and [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]] § *What to play over the I7*): a lot of the arpeggio's notes are already shared with the pentatonic scales close by on the neck, so only a few notes actually need to be consciously targeted — the ones *not* already covered are what make the line sound like it's tracking the harmony instead of just riding one scale through the whole form.

## The four-shape vocabulary per position
At any one CAGED-anchored position on the neck, four shapes sit close together and get combined:
1. **G major pentatonic** (the I chord's bright/BB King-box sound)
2. **G minor pentatonic** (the bluesier alternative, freely mixed with #1)
3. **C7 arpeggio** (root–3rd–5th–♭7 of the IV chord) — reached for on the C7 bars
4. **D7 arpeggio** (root–3rd–5th–♭7 of the V chord) — reached for on the D7 bar

**All four shapes sit physically close together at each position** — the pentatonic notes and the arpeggio notes overlap enough that switching from a pentatonic lick into an arpeggio target and back again is a small hand movement, not a jump across the neck. Worked explicitly: starting a G minor pentatonic phrase, ending it on a note (e.g. G) that's *also* in the C7 arpeggio, and using that shared note as the pivot into arpeggio-based lines over the C7 bar, then dropping back to G minor pentatonic when the chord resolves back to G, then targeting the D7 arpeggio's chord tones (3rd, 5th, ♭7, root) over the V bar.

## Only five arpeggio shapes total, reused across two chords
**There are only five dominant-7 arpeggio shapes** (one per CAGED root position) needed to cover this whole system — because the video works over just two dominant-7 chords (C7 and D7), the same five shapes get reused, just anchored to a different root each time. Once the five shapes are visualized and under the fingers, moving between C7 and D7 arpeggios at any position becomes the same physical shape shifted to a different root rather than five new shapes per chord.

## Practice progression: position by position, then connect horizontally
1. **Work one CAGED position at a time.** At each of the five positions up the neck, map all four shapes (G major pentatonic, G minor pentatonic, C7 arpeggio, D7 arpeggio) before trying to solo with them.
2. **Practice creating solos confined to that one position** — limit yourself to just that position's four shapes and build lines: pentatonic phrase over G, pivot into the C7 arpeggio on the IV chord, back to pentatonic, pivot into the D7 arpeggio on the V chord, back to pentatonic. *(mirrors [[One-Position Fretboard Overview Drill]]'s confine-then-troubleshoot method, applied here to the arpeggio-overlay vocabulary specifically)*
3. **Move up to the next position and repeat** — all five CAGED positions get the same four-shape treatment, including the position built around the "famous BB King box" for the major pentonic, where the same overlay logic applies.
4. **Once each position is solid individually, connect them horizontally** — move across the fretboard from position to position mid-solo rather than staying boxed in one spot, using the chord tones as landmarks so "you're never going to get lost wherever you are."
5. **Slow the video down and isolate one position at a time** if the full five-position pass is too much at once — explicitly recommended for players new to arpeggio-over-pentatonic playing, since it "will take you a while to get a grasp on it" the first time through.

## Turnaround application
The **V-to-IV turnaround move** near the end of a 12-bar chorus (bar 10→11, the classic "walk from the five chord down to the four chord") is called out as a spot where players already play licks that are, functionally, arpeggio-shape-based moves without necessarily knowing it — take that same concept (arpeggio shapes tying two chords together in a short turnaround phrase) and apply it anywhere similar chord motion happens in a progression.
