# 5 Things EVERY Bass Player SHOULD Master

![](https://youtu.be/9Vhl5HBS8Nc)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://youtu.be/9Vhl5HBS8Nc)

## Synopsis

Rick Beato (a classically-trained bassist before becoming a guitarist/producer) covers five bass-playing fundamentals spanning arrangement, voice-leading, tone-shaping technique, and studio/mix considerations specific to bass — several drawing directly on his own production/mixing experience, not just performance.

## Techniques

- **Use space deliberately — dropping the bass out and bringing it back in is a real arrangement tool, not just "less playing."** Since bass is the harmonic and tonal foundation of a mix, its absence is immediately noticeable, and its RETURN reads as a significant impact moment. Worked reference: "Eleven Ways" (Owen Beverly, bass by Les Hall) uses exactly this drop-out/return move for dramatic effect. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]], [[Bassline Writing]]
- **Choose 3rds and 5ths deliberately based on what the vocal melody is singing, to avoid weakening the voicing with parallel octaves.** If the vocal melody is singing the chord's 3rd, avoid also playing the 3rd in the bass — doubling it (even an octave apart) weakens that voicing's character. Same logic, milder effect, for the 5th. Reach for the 3rd/5th in the bass specifically when the melody is on the ROOT (or otherwise not on the note you're about to double) — a genuine voice-leading rule connecting bass-line note choice to whatever the lead vocal is doing at that moment, not an independent decision. → [[Bassline Writing]], [[Chord Progressions]]
- **Playing/picking position changes tone via physical string length, independent of the instrument itself — near the bridge = brighter/thinner/more mid-forward (good for cutting through, solos); near the neck = fatter/mellower/more low end.** The mechanism is literal string length: more string is set ringing when plucked/fretted farther from the bridge, producing a fuller low-end-weighted tone; less effective string length near the bridge yields a thinner, more mid-range-forward tone that cuts. Applies whether playing fingerstyle or with a pick. Cited reference: Jaco Pastorius visibly varying plucking position across a performance for exactly this reason. Separately: matching bass/amp to the part matters as much as playing technique — a producer's-eye note on having multiple basses (Rickenbacker, P-bass, Jazz bass, Ernie Ball) and amps (Darkglass, SVT) for different tonal starting points. → [[Bass Design]]
- **Mute every string you're not actively playing, with the fretting hand — described as make-or-break for a usable bass take.** Extraneous ringing strings muddy the whole mix; even when playing chords (multiple simultaneous notes), the strings NOT part of the voicing still need active muting. A recurring real mix-session diagnosis: "why does the bass sound bad here" traced back to un-muted strings ringing together underneath the intended note. → [[Bass Design]], [[Vocal Mic Technique]]
- **A specific bass EQ frequency map, tied to what each range actually does for the part.** **~1.5-2 kHz**: where the bass's "brightness"/top-end presence lives — boost here to help it speak/cut. **~800 Hz**: a secondary "speaks here" midrange point. **~50-60 Hz**: sub extension, the big low-end foundation. **~300 Hz**: the specific frequency where bass starts becoming audible on very small speakers (phone/tiny radio speaker) — sweep an EQ boost through a signal on a small speaker and this is where it "clicks in." → [[Bass Design]], [[EQ]], [[Referencing and Translation]]
- **Distortion/overdrive helps bass cut through a dense mix by adding harmonic content in the mids — doesn't need to be heavy, even slight amp overdrive works.** A straight clean DI signal tends to read "hollowed out" in the mid-range (good bottom, good top, weak middle where the ear actually locates the instrument in a busy mix); any distortion generates harmonics that fill exactly that gap. Can be applied selectively — split the bass signal into multiple tracks and distort only the top/mid content, leaving the low end clean and undistorted. String age is a related tonal factor: new strings carry more top AND bottom end; older strings lose both extremes and settle into a more consistent midrange — some bassists deliberately never change strings for that specific character. → [[Bass Design]], [[Saturation and Distortion]]
- **Bass compression should preserve the transient, then hold a longer, gradual release for consistent low end — attack/release chosen around that specific shape, not a generic setting.** A release long enough to avoid a sharp cutoff on sustained notes, paired with a transient that's allowed through before the gradual release kicks in — the goal is a consistent low end across the performance, since (unlike guitar) a bass mistake or an inconsistent note is immediately obvious to a listener. → [[Bass Design]], [[Compression]]
- **Playing slightly BEHIND the kick drum ("in the pocket," late) is a specific, real technique for making the low end read as huge — a named studio observation, not just general "good pocket" advice.** Beato attributes a specific "sessions" bass sound (implying LA session players) directly to this: the bass consistently sitting a hair behind the kick, rather than locked exactly on it. Framed as one deliberate pocket choice among several (on-top, behind, ahead) — the right one depends on the song, but slightly-behind-the-kick is called out by name for its low-end-size payoff specifically. → [[Rhythm and Groove]], [[Pocket and Microtiming]]
