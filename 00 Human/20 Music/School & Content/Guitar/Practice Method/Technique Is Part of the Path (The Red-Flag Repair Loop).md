# Technique Is Part of the Path (The Red-Flag Repair Loop)

Jared's (Sound Guitar Lessons) direct answer to a trap that shows up constantly once you have a real goal and a real path toward it (see [[The Practice Priority Pyramid]]): technique breaking down mid-session gets mentally filed as a *separate, competing* problem — "I should be working on my goal, but instead I have to go fix my technique" — which produces the feeling of getting derailed off the real path. His correction: **technique isn't a fifth category alongside scales/positions/ear-training, it's the thing that lets any of those actually come out of your hands** — so it belongs on the same path, targeted exactly as specifically as everything else on the checklist, not siphoned off into generic, unrelated drilling. Where [[The Practice Priority Pyramid]] and [[Vision-Driven Practice and the Overwhelm Reflex]] operate at the goal/vision level (*what* belongs on a practice list and *why*), this note operates one level down, in the middle of an actual session: *what do you do the instant something on that list breaks*. Companion practice-diagnosis note to [[Recording Yourself for Self-Diagnosis]] (Jens Larsen's parallel "find the gap, name it specifically" method) and shares its core spaced-return logic with [[Interleaved Practice, Chunking, and Deliberate-Practice Drills]]'s trouble-spot targeting.

## The reframe: don't reach for the wrong ingredient

**Burger analogy.** Missing a bun for a burger and grabbing a baguette or croutons instead "solves" the shape of the gap (something bread-like) without solving the actual gap — the burger still isn't right. On guitar: when a passage breaks down technically, reaching for a generic, unrelated technique exercise is the baguette. The fix is always the **exact** missing ingredient — the exact flub, the exact arpeggio, the exact scale slur — not an adjacent substitute that feels productive.

**Golf-fitness analogy.** A golf-specific fitness trainer builds exercises (e.g. weighted medicine-ball rotations) targeted at the precise muscles/motions a golf swing uses — not generic advice like "get fit, go hiking on weekends," which improves cardio without making anyone a better golfer. Same logic for guitar technique: general technique drilling has real value, but *targeted* work aimed at the exact motion your goal requires is what actually closes the gap.

## The red-flag repair loop (the core reusable method)

The general-purpose loop, demonstrated live across two case studies below:

1. **Play the real thing** — the actual goal material (the piece, the improvisation, the passage), not a generic exercise.
2. **When something breaks down, treat it as a "red flag"** — a data point, not a failure. "That was a problem" is useful information, not a reason to feel behind.
3. **Go directly to the exact spot, not the general area.** Identify precisely what broke — which two fingers, which string transition, which tempo — rather than a vague sense that "the fast part" or "the sweep section" is hard. 🎬 *Research backing (same channel, a separate video):* Anders Ericsson's *Peak* gives this exact recipe — "push yourself well outside your comfort zone and see what breaks down first, then design a practice technique aimed at improving that particular weakness" — and a case study (a memory-research subject, "Steve") showing an apparent hard ceiling was actually just one or two specific problem components, not the whole skill maxing out. Full extraction on [[Repetition with Manipulation (Getting Past a Plateau)]]. — Jared, [[How to Get Better at Guitar - The science of getting past a plateau]]
4. **Build or reach for the most direct, targeted fix for that exact flub** — a "speed burst" (see below), a cross-string transfer (see below), or an isolated-tempo workshop against a backing track — whatever actually targets the specific mechanical gap.
5. **Test it small and fast — don't commit to a long, unverified detour.** Explicit warning against going "off on a monthlong practice of this thing that you think was the solution" before confirming it's actually the right fix.
6. **Return to real playing as soon as possible.** The isolated drill is a brief repair stop, not a new destination — the point is always to get back to the goal material, where the next red flag surfaces naturally.
7. **Repeat continuously** — alternate between real playing and short targeted technique detours rather than doing either exclusively; expect to cycle through this many times in one session, not just once.
8. **Use your own red flags as your practice curriculum, not someone else's generic list.** Direct paraphrase: instead of treating what a teacher or a book says to practice as the practice item, treat your own real, felt limitations from your own actual playing as the practice item — framed as the mechanism for becoming "your own best teacher."

## Two named repair techniques

### The "speed burst" (slow → fast → slow)
For a spot that's breaking down on **synchronization** specifically (e.g. two fingers not landing at exactly the same instant, not a raw-speed problem): repeat the exact transition **slow, then fast, then slow again**, as a repeated set. *(General starting point — no fixed rep count or tempo ratio given; adjust to the spot.)* Worked example: a Super Mario Bros. theme arrangement where fingers 1–2 and 4–3 weren't landing together — the speed burst measurably improved it across two practice days.

### Cross-string transfer
Take any lick, arrangement passage, or fragment (named use case: classical/solo-guitar arrangement material) and **deliberately move it to a different set of strings**, without regard for whether it sounds musically "weird" there — the goal isn't a usable musical result on the new strings, it's that displacing a familiar shape from its comfortable position builds strength/flexibility/dexterity that transfers back to the original spot. Can be combined with the speed burst on the same passage.

## Worked case study — 12-bar blues improv (the loop demonstrated live, start to finish)

Working "alternate hybrid picking" (alternate picking combined with the middle and ring/annular fingers simultaneously) applied to playing in octaves, over a 12-bar blues loop (four bars B♭7, two bars E♭7, back to B♭7, then a V–IV–I–V turnaround):

1. Improvise for real over the loop while bringing in the octave/hybrid-picking technique — comes out sloppy (red flag).
2. **Don't default to a generic technique exercise.** Workshop the exact motion against the backing track instead: strip away note/improvisation content entirely, focus purely on staying clean and relaxed at the current tempo.
3. Once clean at that tempo, push the tempo up on the isolated technique, still ignoring note content.
4. **Recombine incrementally with real musical content** — once the technique holds up, layer it back onto actual improvisation (e.g. outlining the chord tones of each change using the technique, mapping the next chord the same way), testing it inside real harmonic movement rather than in isolation.
5. **When it breaks again, that's expected** — go back to the isolated drill, not backward through the whole practice plan. Loop steps 2–5 repeatedly within the session.

## Why technique gains feel slower than conceptual gains — set expectations accordingly

Technique (strength, flexibility, dexterity) is physically slow to build: **"watering a plant" analogy** — growth is invisible while you're doing the work and only shows up after repeated sessions with recovery time between them (adaptation needs rest, not just repetition, so one long session doesn't substitute for several shorter ones spread out). This is contrasted with **conceptual/vocabulary learning** (a new scale, song, or chord shape), which can feel like visible same-session progress precisely because the underlying technique is usually already in place — you're just layering new information on top of existing physical capability. Practical implication: don't judge a technique fix by same-session results, and don't be discouraged when a fix takes days or weeks rather than one sitting — that's the expected shape of technique gains, not a sign the approach is wrong. Ear training is named as sharing this same slow-gain shape.

**General conditioning still matters separately.** This targeted-repair loop supplements, not replaces, a standing general technique routine — Jared references his own daily warm-up ritual (a free PDF, not detailed in this video — see [[Vision-Driven Practice and the Overwhelm Reflex]]'s note on the same undetailed-but-consistent habit).
