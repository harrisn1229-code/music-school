# Songs that use Microtonality

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q1XOnIk2ai8)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q1XOnIk2ai8)

## Synopsis

A survey of microtonality (pitches between the standard 12 semitones) from experimental classical composers through to blues-derived quarter-tone bends, vocal glissando, and a full microtonal rock album — closing with an important myth-check: a song not recorded at standard concert pitch is NOT microtonal, a common point of confusion.

## Techniques

- **24-TET (quarter-tone tuning) as the standard microtonal extension of 12-TET**: doubles the 12 standard equal-tempered pitches per octave to 24. Early-20th-century classical composers (Wyschnegradsky, Ives) achieved this on two standard pianos, one tuned a quarter-tone flat relative to the other — no microtonal notation needed on either individual piano part, since the microtonality emerges purely from the *interaction* between the two instruments (Ives' *Three Quarter-Tone Pieces* deliberately pendulums between hearing one piano alone — plain 12-TET — and both together — the microtonal blend).
- **Glissando/pitch-slide instruments as the most common vector for microtonality in ordinary pop/rock, because they pass through the in-between pitches whether or not that's the compositional intent**: Gershwin's clarinet glissando (*Rhapsody in Blue*) and an EDM "riser" (the pre-chorus buildup in "Uptown Funk") both work specifically because the instrument/sound can glide continuously through non-12-TET pitches rather than jumping in discrete semitone steps — restricted to a keyboard's fixed 12 pitches, neither effect would be possible.
- **Quarter-tone string bends as a blues-derived microtonal blue-note technique**: bending a note up a quarter-tone (rather than a full semitone) lands it between two standard pitches — Jet's "Are You Gonna Be My Girl" bass riff bends D up to D-half-sharp (not all the way to D♯), functioning as a blue note specifically because a pitch fully outside the 12-tone scale reads as maximally "blue"/tense.
- **Vocal glissando as ornamental (passing-through) microtonality vs. a song built structurally on microtonal intervals — a real distinction, not just a matter of degree**: Paramore's "Misery Business" (Hayley Williams glides between two scale notes at the chorus's end) is the passing-through case — the microtonal pitch is never rested on long enough to register as a "note" in its own right. Contrast: King Gizzard & the Lizard Wizard's *Flying Microtonal Banana* (2017) is genuinely composed in 24-TET throughout, using purpose-built guitars with extra frets (inspired by the Turkish bağlama's fret spacing) — a half-flat 2nd and half-sharp 6th over an F♯ minor scale, described as landing in an "exotic limbo" between Aeolian, Phrygian, and Dorian. **A structural consequence of composing in real microtonal intervals**: the album is harmonically STATIC (droning on one key center, melody-driven rather than chord-progression-driven) rather than using functional harmony — attributed to how difficult/confusing it is to stack quarter-tone intervals into workable chords using Western harmonic conventions.
- **Microtonal modulation — genuinely re-tuning the listener's ear a quarter-tone over the course of several chords, not just landing on an isolated microtonal note**: Jacob Collier's a cappella arrangement of "In the Bleak Midwinter" modulates from standard E major to the microtonal key of G-half-sharp-major over four chords, each subtly widening/narrowing intervals — demonstrated by playing a fixed sine-wave reference pitch against the passage: the same pitch sounds increasingly "in tune" as the modulation progresses, proving the listener's harmonic reference point has genuinely shifted by a quarter-tone, not just that one odd note was played.
- **The microtonality myth-check — worth remembering as a common misconception**: a recording not made at standard concert pitch (A=440Hz) — e.g. "Friday I'm in Love" (The Cure) or "Don't Look Back in Anger" (Oasis), both pitched slightly off standard — is NOT microtonal. Microtonality is about the *intervals between notes* being smaller than a standard semitone; a globally pitch-shifted recording keeps all its original semitone-sized intervals intact, just transposed as a whole — the relationship between notes is unchanged even though the absolute frequencies are.
