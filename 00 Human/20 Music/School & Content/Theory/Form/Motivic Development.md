# Motivic Development

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Musical Form]]. A **motive** is a short musical fragment used as the raw material to generate a much larger amount of music. 8 named ways to develop one:

| Technique | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Repetition** | Repeat the phrase immediately — the simplest form, doubles the material instantly |
| **Sequence** | Repeat the motive starting at a different pitch level; intervals within it don't have to match exactly |
| **Inversion** | Flip the motive upside-down — a downward leap in the original becomes an upward leap |
| **Interval contraction** | Shrink the intervals within the motive |
| **Interval expansion** | Widen the intervals within the motive |
| **Diminution** | Play the motive faster |
| **Augmentation** | Play the motive slower |
| **Rhythmic metamorphosis** | Change the motive's rhythm in any way other than tempo |
| **Imitation** | An "echo" of the motive between different voices (different instruments in an ensemble, or different registers on one instrument) |

## From videos

- **A worked real-world example of true melodic inversion generating an entire new piece: Rachmaninoff literally turning a Paganini theme upside-down.** *Rhapsody on a Theme of Paganini*, Variation 18: Rachmaninoff takes Paganini's 24th Caprice's 5-note theme and inverts every interval — where the original ascends a minor 3rd then descends a semitone, Rachmaninoff's version descends a minor 3rd then ascends a semitone (every subsequent interval flipped the same way) — and that single inverted phrase becomes the seed for the entire variation. Described as the technique's most extreme case in the video: **the more thoroughly a motive is transformed, the less audibly related the result is to its source**, to the point that without the piece's own title, the connection to Paganini would be undetectable by ear — contrast with plainer "borrow-the-whole-melody" cases (e.g. The Korgis' "If I Had You" lifting the SAME Rachmaninoff piece's melody and harmony near-verbatim) where the lineage stays obvious. → [[Sample Clearance]] for the wider borrowing-chain context this example was cited in — David Bennett Music Theory, [[19 Songs Based On Classical Pieces]]

- **"Underlick" — a fan-coined term (not a formal theory word) for a singable INSTRUMENTAL motif that the vocalist deliberately never sings, anywhere in the song.** On Billie Eilish's "Birds of a Feather," a simple D–A–D♭ instrumental figure plays under the vocal from early in the arrangement — catchy enough to get stuck in the listener's head despite having, in Finneas's words, "nothing to do with the actual songwriting." Distinct from a normal hook/riff: the whole device depends on the melody staying purely instrumental — sung by no one — rather than eventually being handed to the vocalist as a top-line, which is what a normal "singable" motif usually becomes. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]], [[Chord Progressions]] — Finneas, [[Behind the Track - 'BIRDS OF A FEATHER' by Billie Eilish]]
- **Pop/rock bass writing has its own informally-named parallel to formal motivic development: seven "steal, don't copy" methods for turning an existing bass line into a new one, ordered by how much of the source survives the transfer.** Steal half (keep a verbatim opening, write an original continuation) ≈ straight repetition/quotation; steal the rhythm but change the notes, or steal the notes but change the rhythm, ≈ **rhythmic metamorphosis** and pitch substitution respectively (see table above); steal notes+rhythm but reorder/re-octave them ≈ inversion/permutation of the shape; change the feel (tempo, drum beat, tone, key, same anchor notes) ≈ **augmentation**/**diminution** plus a timbral shift; steal from a different instrument's part ≈ **imitation** across a different "voice"; steal from a different genre ≈ the same shape re-orchestrated in a wholly different idiom. The video demonstrates all seven stacking on one source riff (Megadeth's "Peace Sells") to generate several genuinely distinct-feeling lines from a single seed — same underlying principle as this page's table, ear-trained/pop-songwriting framing rather than classical-theory framing. → [[Bassline Writing]] — BassBuzz, [[7 Stupidly Simple Methods for Killer Bass Lines]]
- **Two concrete, independently-usable ways to keep a repeated motif from going "robotic and predictable and boring": change its rhythm, and change how the phrase ends.** Demonstrated live over a jazz standard ("I Want to Be Happy"): a 2–3 note motif is stated, then restated with the same or similar notes but a genuinely different rhythmic placement, and separately restated resolving to a different ending gesture than before — sometimes both changes stacked in the same restatement. A motif can also drift structurally over several restatements into "an artistic representation" of the original — same underlying contour, no longer literally derivable from the first statement's shape — which is offered as a sign of real development rather than mechanical transposition. Paired with a fretboard-practical trick for the *moving-a-motif-through-changes* half of the job: think of the motif as a **movable shape** on the neck and reuse (transpose/adjust) that shape as the harmony moves, rather than recalculating note names chord by chord. The whole method is framed as the fix for unstructured "noodling" soloing practice — give it an explicit, nameable goal (motivic development) instead of leaving melodicism vague — and pairs with a **listen → "what should come next?" → play** loop as the actual moment-to-moment practice drill, plus a "don't overstay a motif" rule (deliberately pivot to a new idea once it's run its course, worked at the bridge of the same solo). Full bar-by-bar breakdown of both a "before" (harmony-connecting but non-motivic) solo and the motivic "after" solo over the same tune → [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]] § *A self-analysis variant*. → [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]], [[Chord-Tone Soloing]], [[The Five Pillars of Jazz Guitar Practice]] — Jens Larsen, [[Is Your Improvisation Practice a Waste of Time? (And How to Fix That)]]
- **Pull material straight from a tune's own theme and rhythmically displace it inside the solo, rather than only building new lines over the changes — the device Jens Larsen calls out as essential for staying interesting at very high tempo.** Watching Jonathan Kreisberg's solo on the standard "The Song Is You" (~300 bpm, mostly eighth-note lines): what keeps the solo from reading as pure scale-noodling isn't note choice, it's connecting ideas "over a larger context" by reusing the tune's own theme material and shifting where it lands rhythmically. A second, contrasting instance in the same video: Gilad Hekselman displaces the theme of "The Way You Look Tonight" almost immediately at the *melody statement itself* (shifted roughly two beats by the second A section, in half time) rather than waiting for the solo — the same device applied earlier and more aggressively. → [[Syncopation and Metric Displacement]] (the rhythmic-placement mechanism both examples use), [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]] — Jens Larsen, [[My Favourite YouTube Jazz Guitar Videos! - John Scofield, Jonathan Kreisberg & Gilad Hekselman]]
- **Restrict a motif to a genuinely small pool of notes (e.g. one arpeggio) and develop it across chord changes without letting it read as an obvious "motif."** Kurt Rosenwinkel builds a passage over B♭m7 → Amaj7♯11 → E♭7(Lydian dominant) starting from a motif stated as an F minor7 arpeggio restricted to *only* those notes, then develops the same shape across the next two chords (descending arpeggio → fifth interval into a descending Amaj7♭5 arpeggio → further development re-emphasizing the ♯11/Lydian color on E♭7). Flagged as the harder half of motivic development: sticking to a small pool of notes "and not immediately start mixing up all the different sounds and use the entire scale," while still avoiding the common failure mode where motif-based playing reads as too obvious/repetitive. → [[🎵 KURT ROSENWINKEL - Nefertiti (Ballad Session)]], [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]] — Kurt Rosenwinkel via Jens Larsen, [[Kurt Rosenwinkel - This Is What Makes His Style Unique]]
- **Self-referencing repetition as a deliberate joke: restate a phrase, then repeat it again a half-step down, fully out of key, once** — a funk melody (Nate Smith, "Bounce") states a hook, restates and develops it in a back-and-forth "oh yeah, oh yeah" pattern, then on one pass drops the entire phrase a half-step — completely outside the established key — before returning to normal. Reads as musical humor specifically because everything else has stayed diatonic; the device is sequence-like motion (repeating the motive at a new pitch level) pushed deliberately non-diatonic for a single surprise repeat, not a modulation. → [[Chromatic Mediants and Pop Modulation]] — Max Konyi, [[As I Hear It - The Secrets of Great Music Ep. 01|As I Hear It - The Secrets of Great Music \| Ep. 01]]
