## What's taught

- **(1:06) Interval definition.** An interval is the pitch difference between two notes — **melodic** (horizontal, played successively) or **harmonic** (vertical, played simultaneously, as in a chord).
- **(2:42) Cents as the underlying unit.** Every semitone in 12-tone equal temperament = 100 cents; most guitar tuners display tuning offset in cents. The semitone/half-step (100 cents) is the smallest interval in the diatonic system, but not the smallest possible — some ethnic scales use microtonal intervals smaller than a half step, playable on an adjustable-fret guitar with custom-placed frets (mentioned as an aside, link in the video's own description).
- **(2:42–4:20) Deriving interval names from the major scale.** Comparing every scale degree of C major back to the tonic C gives the seven basic interval names: C→D = 2nd, C→E = 3rd, C→F = 4th, C→G = 5th (recognizable as the power-chord interval), C→A = 6th, C→B = 7th, C→C(octave) = octave. A same-note pairing (C→C, no distance) is a **unison**, also called a perfect prime.
- **(4:20) Why each interval type further splits into major/minor/perfect/augmented/diminished, with the half-step counts:**
  - 2nd: minor 2nd = C→D♭ (1 half step), major 2nd = C→D (2 half steps).
  - 3rd: minor 3rd = C→E♭ (3 half steps, minor-sounding), major 3rd = C→E (4 half steps, major-sounding).
  - 4th: perfect 4th = C→F (5 half steps, consonant); augmented 4th = C→F♯ (6 half steps) — this is the **tritone**, one of the most dissonant intervals, commonly used to create tension.
  - 5th: perfect 5th = C→G (7 half steps, consonant); diminished 5th = C→G♭ (6 half steps); augmented 5th/sharp 5 = C→G♯ (8 half steps).
  - 6th: major 6th = C→A (9 half steps); minor 6th = C→A♭ (8 half steps, minor-sounding).
  - 7th: major 7th = C→B (11 half steps); minor 7th = C→B♭ (10 half steps).
  - Octave has no quality variants — always 12 half steps, C to C.
- **(7:46) Interval shapes on the fretboard.** *(Numbers are internally consistent with standard guitar theory but the transcript is auto-captioned over a technical, number-dense passage — treat exact fret/string counts as generally reliable, not a substitute for confirming by ear.)* Root on the low-E (6th), A (5th), D (4th), or B (2nd) string unless noted; the G (3rd) string shape differs because of the guitar's G→B major-3rd tuning exception (same point [[Interval Ladder]] makes independently as "the B string breaks the pattern"):
  - **Octave** — 2 frets up + 2 strings up from a 6th/5th-string root; 3 frets up + 2 strings up from a 4th-string root (the G-string offset again).
  - **Perfect 5th** — 2 frets up + 1 string up from a 6th/5th/4th/2nd-string root; 3 frets up + 1 string up from the 3rd (G) string. Raise the top note one more fret for an augmented 5th, lower it one fret for a diminished 5th.
  - **Perfect 4th** — same fret, one string up (directly stacked) from a 6th/5th/4th/2nd-string root; the 3rd (G) string shifts by a fret, same B-string exception.
  - **Major 3rd** — 1 fret down + 1 string up from a 6th/5th/4th/2nd-string root; same fret + 1 string up from the 3rd (G) string.
  - **Minor 3rd** — 2 frets down + 1 string up from a 6th/5th/4th/2nd-string root; 1 fret down + 1 string up from the 3rd (G) string.
  - **Major 6th** — string-skipping shape: 1 fret down + 2 strings up from a 6th/5th-string root; same fret + 2 strings up from a 4th/3rd-string root. Minor 6th = one fret lower than the major-6th shape.
  - **Major 7th** — string-skipping shape: 1 fret up + 2 strings up from a 6th/5th/4th-string root; 2 frets up + 2 strings up from the 3rd (G) string. Minor 7th = one fret lower than the major-7th shape.
  - Extended-interval shapes (♭9, ♯9, ♯11, ♭13, 13) are shown on-screen as fretboard diagrams but not narrated with fret/string counts — no concrete numbers to extract here; the video itself defers deep extended-interval work to a planned follow-up.
- **(10:53) Inversion, worked on a real chord.** Take the C5 power chord (root C, low; fifth G, high). Flip it — play C on top of G instead — and the interval is now G→C, a **perfect 4th**, because that distance is 7 half steps read the other way. General rules demonstrated across every interval pair: **the numbers sum to 9** (2nd↔7th, 3rd↔6th, 4th↔5th; octave stays an octave), **quality flips** (major↔minor, augmented↔diminished; perfect stays perfect — the tritone/augmented-4th is its own inversion, unique among intervals, because it splits the octave exactly in half), and **consonance/dissonance is preserved through inversion** (a consonant interval inverts to another consonant interval, a dissonant one to another dissonant one). Practical takeaway: an inverted pair (e.g. minor 6th E→C and major 3rd C→E) can serve the same musical purpose with a different width — useful for voicing the "same" harmonic idea narrower or wider.
- **(14:57) Parallel intervals — a short history and a modern use case.** Parallel 5ths/4ths (the same interval shape sliding consecutively across different roots) trace to medieval music, were formally prohibited in 14th-century counterpoint pedagogy (the video's host notes learning this rule directly in counterpoint classes — same rule this vault's [[Species Counterpoint]] documents as "parallel perfect intervals forbidden"), fell out of favor for centuries, and were rehabilitated with full compositional consent starting in the early 20th century. Now common throughout Western music in every style. **Modern guitar-specific use:** doubling a riff in parallel 4ths or 5ths produces a thicker, darker sound — a direct, practical application of the historically-taboo device.
- **(16:23) Extended intervals (beyond the octave).** An interval between C and the D that occurs *past* the octave is a **9th** (not a plain 2nd) — same letter distance concept, just compounded past 12 half steps. Alterations follow the same major/minor logic: ♭9 (C→D♭ in the next octave), ♯9 (C→D♯). The commonly-used extended intervals in chord contexts are the **9th, 11th, and 13th**, each independently raisable/lowerable (♭9/♯9, ♯11, ♭13).
- **(17:56–21:48) Why bother learning intervals — five stated payoffs, each with a concrete example:**
  1. **Deriving the guitar's own tuning.** Compare the fretboard's perfect-4th shape across string pairs — it sits at the same fret except for the G→B pair, which reveals the tuning is E-A-D-G in perfect 4ths with **G→B as the one major-3rd interval** (the same exception the shape section and [[Interval Ladder]] both flag).
  2. **Understanding chord construction.** Told a major7 chord is built from a major 3rd + perfect 5th + major 7th (all measured from the root), a player who knows the shapes can locate and visualize the chord directly on the neck rather than memorizing a fixed grip.
  3. **Ear training / relative pitch.** Interval practice builds the ability to choose notes by ear rather than by convenient fingering — the antidote to "brainless soloing."
  4. **Breaking out of memorized patterns** to write melodies that originate in the head (cited: Steve Vai's "For the Love of God" theme, said to have started as a whistled tune) rather than under the fingers.
  5. **Setting pitch-shifter and harmonizer effects** — both parametrize by interval, so interval fluency turns effect-parameter selection from trial-and-error into a deliberate choice. *(No concrete plugin, unit, or setting is named — a general point about the effect category, not an actionable production technique, so it isn't routed to a specific effect page.)*
