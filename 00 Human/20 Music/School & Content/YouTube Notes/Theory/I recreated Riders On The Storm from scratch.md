# I recreated "Riders On The Storm" by The Doors from scratch

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0PSLvxH21B8)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0PSLvxH21B8)

## Synopsis

A production-recreation breakdown of "Riders on the Storm" (The Doors), instrument by instrument, with the band's own live-performance workaround for having no bassist, a "less is more" guitar performance philosophy, and the sound-design story behind the track's rain/thunder atmosphere.

## Techniques

- **Live band workaround for no bass player: split keyboard duty across both hands on two separate keyboards** — live, Ray Manzarek played a dedicated bass Rhodes keyboard with his LEFT hand for the bassline while his RIGHT hand played the main Rhodes part on a second keyboard, simultaneously covering both roles a bass guitarist would normally play. (In the studio, a session bassist was used instead — the live setup was a genuine performance-constraint solution, not the studio arrangement.)
- **"Not overplaying" as a describable guitar performance style, not just a vague compliment**: Robbie Krieger's part is almost entirely simple lead lines (rarely full chords), drenched in tremolo effect, including the guitar solo itself — described as a "master class" specifically BECAUSE of the restraint, not despite it. A reusable arrangement principle: an instrument's part can carry a track's character through tone/effect (the tremolo) and restraint rather than through note density or harmonic complexity.
- **A jazz-derived drum approach inside a rock song, named by what it AVOIDS**: the drums (John Densmore) don't place the snare on the typical rock backbeat — the part is built around the ride cymbal keeping time rather than the snare "powering through" the song, giving the track a jazz-inflected, less driving pulse than a standard rock beat would.
- **Tastefully deployed environmental SFX, sourced from a first-hand account of the original session**: a continuous loop of rain plays as a constant bed throughout, while thunder claps are manually dropped in and out at chosen moments (per drummer John Densmore's account of the original 1971 session, quoted directly: engineer Bruce Botnick had thunderclap tape machines cued up to drop in "wherever we wanted just like God") — the rain is ambient/constant, the thunder is a deliberately placed accent, not both treated the same way.
- **A doubled whispered vocal layer, run alongside the main lead vocal, for an "uncanny/unsettling" effect** — Jim Morrison's original recording adds a second vocal track whispering the same lyric underneath the primary sung take, rather than a harmony or ad-lib; the effect comes specifically from the semantic doubling (same words, different vocal treatment) rather than from pitch or rhythm variation. → [[Sound Selection Guide]]
