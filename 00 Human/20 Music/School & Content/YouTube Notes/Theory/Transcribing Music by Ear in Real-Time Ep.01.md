# Transcribing Music by Ear in Real-Time | Ep.01

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zmHYznNl48M)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zmHYznNl48M)

## Synopsis
The first episode of Max Konyi's real-time transcription series — he picks random songs cold from Spotify playlists (never heard before) and narrates, out loud, the entire internal process of working out chords and melody as the song plays for the first time. The explicit goal is to de-mystify what looks like "magic" by making the internal calculation, guesswork, and pattern-matching fully explicit, take by take. This episode covers a country track, two pop tracks, and a deep-house track — genre chosen specifically to range from harmonically simple to production-obscured. This is the origin video of the "abstracted key-center map" framing and the "no-perfect-pitch" tonic trick that recur across the whole [[Transcribing and Playing by Ear]] series (Ep.02, Ep.03).

## Techniques
- **The mental map: an abstracted, key-agnostic chromatic scale held around an unknown "1."** Konyi describes holding a mental scale — 1, ♭2, 2, ♭3, 3, 4, ♯4, 5, ♭6, 6, ♭7, 7 — not tied to any specific key (not "C major," just "the tonic and its relations"), filled in with real letter names only once the actual key is deduced. Every piece of harmonic/melodic vocabulary built through years of practice slots into this abstract map, which is why it transfers across every key without needing perfect pitch.
- **The no-perfect-pitch trick for finding "1," demonstrated live and repeated across all four songs:** sing the note that feels like the current chord's root/tonic (no need to know its pitch), then play any note on the instrument and judge what scale degree *that* note feels like relative to the sung note. Back-calculate: "I played a G and it sounds like a fifth to me, so the note I sang must be C."
- **Track bass motion continuously as the primary landmark.** On the country track: heard a half-step bass drop into an inverted chord, then a classic descending-diatonic-bass harmonization (a very common device — the whole progression falls out once the bass line's shape is recognized: I, then a chain of diatonic chords walking the bass down the scale).
- **Chord quality recognition and bass-degree recognition combine, not calculate.** Explicitly *not* doing active music-theory calculation in the moment — recognizing "that's the sound of four," "that's the sound of five" the same way you'd recognize a bird call or a face, purely from repetition. Theory only gets invoked as backup when a chord *isn't* instantly recognized (worked example: hearing a major chord that's "lifted up only a little" from a known IV chord — reasoned it must be V, the next major chord up the diatonic series, rather than recognizing V outright).
- **Melody reading uses the same degree-feeling recognition as chords, chained onto the already-established key.** Once the key is locked (C major, in the country example), a melody note's degree is read directly off its felt quality ("that has a third quality to me") — Konyi is explicit he doesn't know the note is literally E, only that it feels like scale-degree 3 in the already-known key of C, and the letter name falls out afterward.
- **Half-step bass motion between chord qualities is a strong, fast diatonic landmark.** On the first pop track: heard two bass notes a half-step apart, the first chord reading as major/major-7, the second as minor — narrows immediately to one of only two possible diatonic spots (major key: IV→iii; minor key: ♭VI→v), since a major-to-minor half-step drop is otherwise rare in a diatonic system.
- **Perceptual "collapse" toward an answer as more information accumulates.** On the second pop track (which flirted between major and minor throughout), Konyi describes each new chord as narrowing the field of possibility — "it's collapsing towards some answer" — rather than solving the whole progression from one landmark alone.
- **The ear can genuinely flip-flop between hearing relative major and relative minor as the same tune plays**, independent of what the "correct" analysis is — Konyi explicitly reports experiencing this live on the second pop track (mind said "C minor," ear kept hearing scale degrees that implied "E major" instead) and states plainly that this instability is a real perceptual phenomenon he can't force himself out of, not something to paper over with a single premature answer.
- **Deep-house example: extended chords tracked by root + rough quality, not exact voicing.** On a fast deep-house track, bass notes and rough chord qualities (B minor, D major, G major, E minor — reading as i–♭III–♭VI–iv in B minor) were caught confidently, but exact extensions ("11th? Something like that") were left approximate rather than forced to a precise answer — an explicit acknowledgment that first-pass transcription doesn't need every extension nailed.
- **Fast melodic runs are read as a gesture, not note-by-note.** On a fast descending melodic line, Konyi describes catching only the first and last notes precisely and reading the middle as "just descending a scale" by gestural feel (informed by years of scale/solo practice) rather than consciously identifying each individual passing tone — likened to seeing colors flash by too fast to name individually.
