# Jazz Comping - A Difficult Conversation With Aimee Nolte

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=58YGhoUBo7w)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=58YGhoUBo7w)

## Synopsis

A 20-minute conversation between Jens Larsen (jazz guitarist/YouTuber) and Aimee Nolte (jazz pianist/YouTuber), filmed at Aimee's studio in LA, tackling a topic Jens says he gets asked about constantly: how do a guitarist and a pianist — two harmony/comping instruments — share the comping role in the same group without getting in each other's way? Rather than a settings-and-shapes lesson, this is an etiquette and listening-skills conversation, illustrated with two live demos (a chorus of "Take the A Train" and a pass at "Corcovado"). The most valuable material is the **explicit division-of-labor logic**: call roles at the top of the tune, default to piano as the primary comper in an unrehearsed jam session, comp in the rhythmic holes a soloist's motif leaves open, track register against the other comping voice, and know the handful of exceptions (Freddie Green rhythm guitar, pre-arranged hits, bossa-nova groove ownership) where two harmony instruments genuinely can sound at once. It closes on the value of simply not playing — laying out is treated as a skill and a legitimate full-time choice, not a fallback.

## What's taught

- **Call comping roles out loud at the top of a tune** — the concrete script given: "you comp the head, I'll comp the first solo, you take the second solo, I'll comp the head out, and you can add fills." Removes the real-time guesswork that causes collisions.
- **Default hierarchy for an unrehearsed jam session: piano is the primary comping instrument**, and a guitarist defaults to functioning like a horn player (solo when cued, otherwise sit out) unless explicitly cued to comp. Framed as a practical jam-session default, not a general instrument-hierarchy claim. With musicians you already know, this call can shrink to a nonverbal cue — one player just starts comping and the other catches on and switches within a bar or two.
- **Comp in the holes, not on top of the soloist's motif.** Once a soloist establishes a repeating, predictable rhythmic idea, place comping hits in the rhythmic gaps that idea leaves open rather than doubling it — by the second or third repetition the holes become predictable. Stated as a more general comping instinct too: playing something a little repetitive/predictable gives the soloist a stable, comfortable surface to build on and interact with, which paradoxically makes the interaction *easier* than constantly varying what you play.
- **Rhythmic comping/fill ideas modeled on big-band horn kicks** — both players describe hearing these comping rhythms as an imitation of what a horn section would punch in behind a soloist.
- **The comping conversation runs through the whole rhythm section, not just soloist↔comper** — a drummer can pick up and double a comping hit (or originate one the comper catches), and a second comping instrument or vibes can join the same call-and-response. An *unplanned* second comping instrument breaks this into noise rather than conversation — demonstrated live: the two intentionally try comping at the same time for a couple of beats and it's audibly a mess.
- **Register-tracking ("canceling each other out"):** with a soloist, one comping instrument, and rhythm section, watch the soloist's hands/range and move opposite — soloist goes up, you go down, and vice versa. Named as harder when the comper is also covering the bass line alone (e.g. unaccompanied solo guitar); easier with a bass player present since the comper's only job is register-tracking + harmony. Going *higher* than the soloist is called out as the more delicate direction — it risks visibly pulling attention off the soloist — so it's used more sparingly than dropping lower.
- **Extension/alteration awareness as a harmonic version of the same "don't collide" skill** — track which alteration the other harmony instrument has committed to (e.g. a natural 13) and don't simultaneously play a clashing alteration of the same extension (e.g. a ♭13).
- **Laying out is a legitimate, valued choice — including for an entire tune** once roles are assigned. Explicit conversation analogy: staying quiet for a while in a group conversation reads as confidence, not insecurity; comping works the same way. Not playing also buys thinking time — it lets the soloist define what's actually needed before you commit to anything. Reference points named: late-period John Coltrane playing piano-less (with McCoy Tyner out of the band), and Jim Hall's extensive laying-out in duo playing, especially the Jim Hall/Bill Evans duo album, held up as the model for two harmony players making room for each other.
- **Laying out is a practiced skill, and habitual full-space comping makes it feel unnatural at first.** A player who mostly performs *without* another harmony instrument (e.g. piano-less trio) gets used to filling all the harmonic space themselves; deliberately laying out with a comper present can feel strange initially — the fix is staying locked to the form (still tracking where you are in it) while consciously not filling it.
- **Fills as the paired role when the other player is comping** — again modeled on horn-section kicks. **Octaves are named as a reliable default texture for guitar fills** when not comping full chords.
- **The Freddie Green exception: straight-quarter-note rhythm guitar can run under piano comping without collision**, because it functions like a second drummer laying down the pulse rather than a harmonically assertive comping voice — the shapes are simple enough that "everything fits," and the rhythmic role is completely predictable. Noted as sometimes looked down on ("just sitting there strumming") and often nearly inaudible in a big band mix, but described as "the best thing in the world" for how it locks the rhythm section together. Demonstrated live over the same tune.
- **Pre-arranged unison hits as a second way two harmony instruments can sound together** — write the hit ahead of time as an arrangement decision rather than negotiating it in real time. Diana Krall's band is cited as using arranged hits regularly; also framed as a general way to make an arrangement feel tighter, independent of the two-comper problem.
- **Groove ownership flips for straight-eighth tunes — bossa nova given as the worked example** ("Corcovado"/"Quiet Nights"). The guitar carries the recognizable rhythmic groove pattern; the piano stays out of that pulse-carrying role and instead lays down sparser harmony/fills that *answer* the guitar rather than doubling its rhythm — cited as the original João Gilberto-era convention (the pianist would "never play the groove," laying down harmony and fills, with the guitar supplying the groove). Only one instrument should carry the ostinato at a time. Explicitly contrasted with the swing/Freddie Green case above, where the predictable-pulse role more often falls to guitar while piano is freer on top — same underlying principle (one instrument owns the predictable pulse, the other is free to comp), the specific assignment flips by style.
- **A good comper doesn't just avoid rhythmic/registral collisions — they track and don't step on the other player's chosen chord extensions**, called out directly as a closing compliment (not playing a ♭13 under a stated natural 13).
- Both live demos ("Take the A Train," "Corcovado") are worked with Jens on guitar and Aimee on piano, walking through several of the above roles (bass-player stand-in comping, comping-in-the-holes, Freddie Green rhythm guitar, and the bossa groove-ownership convention) in real time rather than only described.
