## Techniques

- **A short bass solo doubled in length by reversing the recording and appending it to itself** — "You Can Call Me Al" (Paul Simon): the original one-bar bass solo was made to sound twice as long by playing it forward, then immediately backward, rather than performing or editing a longer take. A cheap, purely editorial way to extend a short performance without more playing.
- **Moving an entire song up an octave (and tightening the rhythm guitar part) as the core of a genre-reinvention arrangement**: Nile Rodgers' account of turning David Bowie's "Let's Dance" from a 12-string acoustic folk demo into the funk/disco hit — after learning Bowie's original chord voicings, Rodgers deliberately shifted the whole guitar part up an octave (making it "shrieker and tighter") as the key move that reframed the song's genre, rather than changing the chords or melody themselves.
- **Rest of the catalog, kept as trivia rather than technique** (no transferable device, but useful factual context for these songs' own pages if they get enriched): a hidden Beatles "Ticket to Ride" orchestral snippet audible in the background of an interview clip at the end of Pink Floyd's "Eclipse"; the string intro to "Wish You Were Here" sampling Tchaikovsky's Symphony No. 4; the opening piano chord + laugh on The Police's "Roxanne" being Sting accidentally sitting on a piano; Jeff Beck improvising the drum beat that became "Superstition" (Stevie Wonder) while covering for an absent drummer; the "young boy" in "Killing Me Softly" being songwriter Don McLean, referencing his own song "Empty Chairs"; "Wonderwall" (Oasis) being named after a 1968 film scored by George Harrison; "Lola" (The Kinks) requiring a re-recorded lyric (Coca-Cola → Cherry Cola) to satisfy BBC product-placement rules; Flea (Red Hot Chili Peppers) playing bass on "You Oughta Know" (Alanis Morissette), including an audible slap timed to the lyric "a slap in the face"; "Smells Like Teen Spirit" being inspired by a graffiti message referencing a deodorant Kurt Cobain didn't recognize at the time; Kate Bush writing "Wuthering Heights" at 18 and becoming the first female UK artist with a self-written #1.
