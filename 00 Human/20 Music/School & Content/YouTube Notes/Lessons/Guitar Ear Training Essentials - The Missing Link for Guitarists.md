# Guitar Ear Training Essentials - The Missing Link for Guitarists

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B5Dz7cCdHQw)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B5Dz7cCdHQw)

## Synopsis

Lesson 2.9 of Jared's own guitar course — explicitly the **first lesson in his "Ear Training" category**, i.e. the foundational, singing-based entry point that sits underneath the more advanced "Learning to Listen" interval/chord app-drilling system he demonstrates later in [[How I Organize My Guitar Practice (So I Never Want to Stop)]] (category 4 of that video's spreadsheet tour). Where that later system trains splitting apart stacked chord voicings with a metronome and streak targets, this lesson is the prerequisite underneath it: **can you sing a scale degree or solfège syllable back accurately at all.** Jared's framing is personal and direct — he played guitar 10 years before anyone told him he should be listening and singing, calls it "the most life-changing practice" of everything he's worked on (ahead of reading, improvising, or theory), and is candid that it's still one of his weaker spots even after deliberate work. The core diagnosis: guitar's frets let you find the right note by looking at your hand instead of your ear, so guitarists specifically (more than other instrumentalists) can go years without ever being forced to identify a sound. The fix he teaches is a **singing curriculum**, not a listening app: sing the major scale up and down with scale-degree numbers, then again with solfège, then apply both systems to real, known melodies (a generic ascending/descending pattern, "Row Row Row Your Boat," "Three Blind Mice"). No good vocal tone required — the only bar is "am I thinking of the right note, and can I hear when I'm on it."

## What's taught

- **Why guitarists specifically are behind on ear training.** Frets remove the need to listen — you can tune once, then just find fret positions by eye/hand and never be forced to identify what a pitch or interval actually sounds like. Other instruments (voice, fretless strings, wind) demand pitch-finding by ear constantly; guitar doesn't, so the skill atrophies unless trained on purpose.
- **Singing is the diagnostic test for real ear-training progress**, not a side activity. Jared's claim, echoed independently on [[Scale Degree Ear Training]] ("singing the tonic trains it more effectively than only listening for it" — Max Konyi): if you can sing a sound, you're proven aware of the actual distance/pitch; if you can only recognize it passively, you may not be. No vocal-tone requirement — the only thing being tested is pitch accuracy ("am I thinking of the right note, and can I hear when I'm on it").
- **Drill 1 — Sing the major scale with scale-degree numbers, up and down, in escalating chunks:**
  - `1-2-1`
  - `1-2-3-2-1`
  - `1-2-3-4-3-2-1`
  - full scale: `1-2-3-4-5-6-7-1` and back down `1-7-6-5-4-3-2-1`
  - **Notable substitution: sing "sen" for scale degree 7**, not the usual solfège "ti" or a two-syllable "seven" — Jared's own stated reason is purely practical: he wants a **single syllable** for degree 7 so the number-singing system stays parallel (one syllable per degree) all the way through, the same way "one" through "six" already are.
  - Milestone is qualitative, not tempo-based: work up from small chunks to the full scale at your own pace; no clean-streak or BPM target given (contrast [[Scale Degree Ear Training]]'s Konyi method, which does use drone + confusion-pair drilling but is also not tempo-gated).
- **Drill 2 — Guitar-aided starting point for a shaky singer.** If singing the full scale cold is "impossibly hard," don't try to force it — bootstrap with the guitar as a pitch reference instead of a cappella:
  - Treat the **open G string (3rd string) as scale-degree 1**.
  - Treat the **open B string (2nd string) as scale-degree 3**.
  - Play those two as anchors, then try to **find and sing scale-degree 2 by ear**, unaided, landing it between the two known reference pitches.
  - Once 2 is solid, extend upward: find and sing **4, positioned right above 3**.
  - Continue extending one degree at a time rather than attempting the whole scale at once — this is the "start small" on-ramp into Drill 1's full-scale version.
  - **Practicable without an instrument** — once the scale is internalized this way, Jared explicitly says you can keep drilling it a cappella while walking down the street or humming, no guitar required.
- **Drill 3 — Sing the same major scale with solfège** (do-re-mi-fa-sol-la-ti-do), up and down, same escalating-chunk approach as Drill 1. Same accuracy bar — not vocal polish, just "I know where I am and what the distance is supposed to be."
- **Drill 4 — Apply numbers/solfège to real melodies, not just the raw scale.** Once the scale itself is singable, Jared moves to actual melodic material, tracked with **up/down direction lines under each number** so the singer knows which way to leap without needing the melody already memorized:
  - A generic demo melody (previously taught, harmonizable with a single open-G-major chord): `1 2 3 1 | 3 4 5 3 | 4 5 5 6 5 4 3 1 | 5 6 5 4 3 1 | 1 5 | 1 1 5 1`.
  - **"Row Row Row Your Boat"** by scale-degree number (transcript is rough on the repeated-word sections, captured qualitatively): opens `1 1 1 2 3 3 2 3 4 5`, a long repeated-5 "merrily merrily" passage, then resolves down through `5 3 1 1 5 4 3 2 1`.
  - **"Three Blind Mice"** by scale-degree number, again transcript-rough on repeats: opens `3 2 1 3 2 1 5 4 4 3 5 4 3 5`, moves through a `1 1 6-1 5 5` figure repeated with variation, closing `5 5 4 3 2 1`; also sung with solfège syllables in the source PDF (not fully legible in the transcript — flagged below).
  - **Homework as stated: get to the point of singing all three (demo melody, Row Row Row Your Boat, Three Blind Mice) with BOTH numbers and solfège**, using the accompanying PDF handout (referenced but not attached to this transcript) for the exact solfège syllables where the transcript garbled them.
  - **Familiar-melody rationale, stated explicitly**: deliberately using tunes you already know (nursery rhymes) so the ear/voice practice is anchored to a sound you can already verify against memory, rather than sight-reading an unfamiliar shape.
- **"Start small, be kind to yourself" framing.** Jared's own admission: he couldn't match a single pitch with his voice at all when he started this, after already having played guitar for 10 years — explicitly normalizes starting from zero and doing partial passes (a couple of notes at a time) rather than the full exercise.
- **The payoff claim that closes the lesson**: this exact skill — hearing "that sounded like six to five in the scale" and knowing where six-to-five sits on the fretboard — is how by-ear players can pick up melodies without notation, because the sound and the fretboard location get linked through the same scale-degree structure trained here.
