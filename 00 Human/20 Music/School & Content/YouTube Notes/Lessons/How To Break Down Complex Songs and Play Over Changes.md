# How To Break Down Complex Songs and Play Over Changes

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XHNgYFoZMW4)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XHNgYFoZMW4)

## Synopsis
Jack Ruch's guitar lesson on breaking down a long, non-diatonic chord progression into small, familiar pieces using two harmonic devices — secondary dominants and major/minor ii-V-i cadences — and then outlining the changes in a solo using CAGED-based arpeggio shapes practiced "in position." He builds an original 9-chord progression in the key of C specifically to demonstrate the method, first playing it straight as rhythm guitar, then breaking it into three-chord chunks he analyzes and arpeggiates individually, and finally connecting the chunks with a "voice-leading exercise" — playing continuously in time, shifting arpeggio shape at each chord change — before playing a full recorded solo over three passes of the form. The most valuable idea isn't any one lick — it's the meta-skill: recognizing secondary-dominant and ii-V(-i) shapes inside a scary-looking chart turns "an original chord progression" into "three or four things I already know," which is exactly how to approach any hard tune (he name-checks Ray Charles-style standards as the kind of material this applies to). Everything is built around CAGED bar-chord shapes, so it slots directly into the vault's [[Guitar Positions]] system and pairs with [[Secondary Dominants]] and [[The ii-V-I]].

## What's taught
- **The demo progression (key of C)**, built specifically to contain the two devices below: **C7 – E7 – Am7 | Gm7 – C7 – Fmaj7 | Bm7♭5 – E7 – Am7** — played first as straight rhythm guitar before any breakdown, with chord names shown on screen (not reproduced here; tabs/charts/backing track are Patreon-only).
- **Chunk 1 — secondary dominant: C7 → E7 → Am7.** E7 isn't diatonic to C major; it's borrowed as the **dominant of vi** (**V7/vi**), a functioning dominant chord whose job is to create tension/release that resolves specifically into Am — not just "a chord that sounds a little off," a chord with a defined target. Framed as a reusable device: any dominant-7 chord can substitute for its diatonic neighbor to add tension/color that pulls toward a specific chord, and the E7→Am7 case is exactly the pattern documented on [[Secondary Dominants]] (V7/vi is called out there as the single most common secondary dominant). References a separate, more in-depth secondary-dominants lesson of his (not captured in this video).
- **Chunk 2 — major ii–V–I targeting the IV chord: Gm7 → C7 → Fmaj7.** A textbook major-key ii–V–I ([[The ii-V-I]]), except its "I" (F major 7) is actually functioning as the **IV** of the home key (C) — the whole three-chord unit is a **secondary ii-V**, a self-contained cadence dropped in front of a diatonic target chord to give it more pull/color than landing on F directly would. Offers **C13 in place of plain C7** ("to make it a little prettier") as a free color upgrade on the V chord — a general-purpose move, not specific to this progression.
- **Chunk 3 — minor ii–V–i targeting Am, reframed as a general "landing spot" tool: Bm7♭5 → E7 → Am7.** Explicitly the minor-key version of the same device — ii°7 (half-diminished) → V7 → i — matching [[The ii-V-I]]'s documented minor version (Dm7♭5→G7♭9→Cm7 in C minor there; here the same shape targets A minor). **General reharmonization principle stated explicitly, worked from a plain example:** given any simple progression (his worked example: C → Am), treat the target chord as a landing spot and ask "what 251 gets me there?" — for a **major** target, drop in a major ii-V a whole step above it (e.g. targeting F: Gm7-C7); for a **minor** target, drop in a minor ii-V a whole step above it (e.g. targeting Am: Bm7♭5-E7). Offered as a composition/arranging tool in its own right, not just an analysis trick — "how do I spice this up... seeing these chords as a potential landing spot for a secondary dominant or a 251."
- **Arpeggio-shapes-in-position method (the practical "outlining changes" tool)** — practice one CAGED-derived bar-chord shape's arpeggio per chord, choosing each new shape for physical proximity to the previous chord's shape rather than starting fresh every time:
  - **C7** — root-position arpeggio off a **C-shaped bar chord**: ascending pattern root–3rd–5th–♭7 repeating (root, 3rd, 5th, ♭7, root, 3rd, 5th, ♭7, root).
  - **E7** — the **closest E-shaped bar chord** to the C shape just used (physically adjacent on the neck, not a fresh position): arpeggio starts on the *lowest available note in that position*, which is the **5th** (B), not the root — 5th–♭7–root–3rd–5th–♭7–root–3rd–5th.
  - **Am7** — played as a **D-minor CAGED shape moved up the neck** (the relative-minor trick: reuse the CAGED minor shape rather than inventing a fresh fingering) — starts on the **minor 3rd** (C): ♭3–5th–♭7–root–♭3–5th–♭7–root–♭3.
  - Same approach continues through **Gm7 → C7/C13 → Fmaj7** and **Bm7♭5 → E7 → Am7**, each chord's arpeggio picked from whichever CAGED-family shape sits physically closest to the one before it, so the whole progression can be played through without jumping around the neck. Full method write-up, generalized: [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]].
- **Two-stage practice method for the arpeggio shapes:**
  1. **Out of time first** — run each arpeggio shape up and down repeatedly on its own, no backing track, purely to train fingers/ears/eyes on the shape before trying to use it musically.
  2. **Then a "voice-leading exercise," in time, over the backing track** — play continuously through the changes, switching arpeggio shape exactly at each chord change, staying in whatever direction you're already moving (ascending keeps ascending into the next chord's shape; descending keeps descending) rather than resetting to the root every time. Explicitly framed as harder than the out-of-time drill but the real payoff — it reveals the actual chord-tone connections between adjacent chords, and starting on a different beat/chord-tone each pass produces a different fingering and a different line every time ("endless possibilities").
- **Chunk-then-connect practice strategy (the "breaking it down" method, generalized):** work each 2–3-chord chunk (C7-E7-Am7, then Gm7-C7-Fmaj7, then Bm7♭5-E7-Am7) as its own small, manageable unit until it's comfortable, THEN start tying adjacent chunks together into longer phrases, THEN connect the whole thing — explicitly presented as the master strategy for any intimidating long progression: isolate the small harmonic devices you recognize (a secondary dominant, a 251), get fluent on each in isolation, then stitch. "This is the real key to practicing these long progressions."
- **Lick-splicing across arpeggio shapes** — once each chunk's arpeggio is comfortable, take fragments from different shapes and recombine them freely (worked example: top part of a Gm7 shape + middle part of a C7 shape + bottom part of an Fmaj7 shape) rather than always playing a shape start-to-finish — framed as how real vocabulary/lines get built once the raw shapes are under the fingers.
- **Recognizing 251s/secondary dominants on a chart, by ear or by eye, as the actual unlock for reading hard tunes** — "if you can look at a chart and visually see like oh that's a 251, that cadence there, then you can start to build vocabulary that works over those chord progressions" — the harmonic analysis isn't academic here, it's what turns an unreadable chart into "things I already have vocabulary for."
- **Full worked solo** — after teaching the pieces, plays a complete solo through three passes of the whole 9-chord form, built from this same arpeggio vocabulary (licks/fragments from each chunk stitched together). Tabs/backing track/notation for the whole lesson (rhythm track, voice-leading exercise, and the full solo) are Patreon-only, not shown in enough visual/verbal detail in the video itself to transcribe exact fret numbers beyond what's captured above.
- **Recommended context for the whole approach:** framed as essential once you're playing "standards or classics" — cites Ray Charles specifically — i.e. real-world tunes routinely aren't fully diatonic, and this is the general method for handling that, not a one-off trick built only for his demo progression.
