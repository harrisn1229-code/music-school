# Positional CAGED Soloing with Matched Modes and Arpeggios

Brandon D'Eon's method for covering the whole neck over a static diatonic progression by walking through the five CAGED positions, and at each one working out the matching mode, pentatonic shape, and chord-tone arpeggios for every chord in the progression. Complements [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]] (that page's method is for fast-moving, non-diatonic progressions — closest-CAGED-shape-per-chord-change; this page is for a single diatonic progression held in one key, walked position by position across the whole neck) and [[CAGE System Scale Shapes]] (the underlying position/shape system this method is built on).

**The core reframe, and the actual point of the video:** most players who feel "stuck in the box" haven't mastered chord-tone targeting even in the one box position they already know — escaping to new positions doesn't fix that gap, it just gives you more positions to be equally unfocused in. Master chord-tone targeting in one position first.

## The five-position map (worked example: Em–G–D–C, key of E minor)

| Position | Mode | Pentatonic (CAGED shape) |
|---|---|---|
| 3rd (frets ~2–7) | G Ionian (major) | G major pentatonic |
| 5th (frets ~5–8) | A Dorian | D-shape pentatonic |
| 7th (frets ~7–10) | B Phrygian | C-shape pentatonic |
| 10th (frets ~10–13) | D Mixolydian | A-shape pentatonic |
| 12th (the "box") | E Aeolian (minor) | G-shape pentatonic (standard minor pentatonic box) |

The five positions fall out of walking the parent major scale's own pentatonic notes (here, G major pentatonic's degrees land at frets 3, 5, 7, 10, 12) — the 15th position is skipped as redundant, since it repeats the 3rd position's shapes one octave up.

## At every position: locate the chord tones, not just the scale shape

For each chord in the progression, physically find and target its chord tones **within that position**, rather than defaulting to running the scale or pentatonic shape regardless of which chord is currently sounding. This is named as the real differentiator between amateur and advanced improvisers — amateurs play the same scale shape through every chord change; advanced players shift emphasis to the current chord's tones using the same fixed hand position.

**Chord tones aren't limited to a compact barre-chord grip's fretted notes.** A chord's full tone set (e.g. Em = E, G, B) usually appears in more places within a given position than the handful of notes in its barre-chord voicing — those extra instances are still legitimate soloing targets even though they're not part of the compact chord shape.

## The graduation test

In the box (12th) position, play four passes over the progression: (1) scale only, (2) pentatonic only, (3) chord tones only, (4) all three blended, tracking the chord changes. Fluency at pass 4 is the explicit marker of having actually mastered the position, as opposed to just being comfortable running the scale shape in it.

## From videos

- **CAGED pros/cons, as a system for connecting arpeggios/scales across the neck.** Pros: easy to accent chord tones once all 5 shapes are memorized; shapes connect naturally across the neck (the same two neighbor shapes always flank a given shape, in every key); builds a stronger intuitive grasp of the chord/scale/arpeggio relationship than book study; forces note-level (not shape-level) thinking, which reads as more musical when improvising. Cons: the 2-note-per-string CAGED fingering gets awkward once extended to full 7-note (heptatonic) scales; resulting arpeggio shapes can be clumsy for sweep picking; only 5 patterns exist (C-A-G-E-D) vs. the 7 needed to fully map modes, so CAGED alone under-serves mode study — see [[CAGE System Scale Shapes]]'s 3-note-per-string alternative for that. — BERNTH, [6 Life-Changing Guitar Music Theory HACKS!](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LvRRN2fhcGY)
- **Build a chord → arpeggio → 7th-arpeggio → 3-note-per-string scale progression from a single known chord shape, all sharing one visual anchor.** Worked over one basic E minor voicing (root E on the 7th fret A string): the arpeggio is built by finding the same three chord tones (E-G-B, root/♭3/5th) in nearby positions around that shape, capped at two notes per string; the minor 7th arpeggio adds exactly one new note (D, the ♭7) to that same shape with nothing else changed; the 3-note-per-string E minor scale then extends the same visual anchor into full-scale shred material. The stated payoff: because every layer is visualized as an extension of the *same* chord shape rather than a separately memorized pattern, a player can move fluidly between arpeggio and scale phrasing mid-improvisation while still "seeing" the underlying chord.
