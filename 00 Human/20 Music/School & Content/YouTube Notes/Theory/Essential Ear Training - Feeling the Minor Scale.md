# Essential Ear Training - Feeling the Minor Scale

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=F-wUQh1jEA4)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=F-wUQh1jEA4)

## Synopsis
The natural-minor-scale (Aeolian mode) companion to Max Konyi's major-scale feeling-state video — explicitly framed as the third entry in an informal series after "Feeling the Major Scale" and [[The Secret of Ear Training]]. Debuting a prototype "circle visualizer" tool (tuned to a key, showing which scale degree is currently sounding as Konyi plays around it), the video drone-drills all 7 degrees of natural minor with particular focus on the 3 degrees that differ from major (♭3, ♭6, ♭7), and lays out the circle-of-fifths arrangement as a predictive tool for which degrees get confused with which. This is the direct source for [[Scale Degree Ear Training]]'s circle-adjacency principle and [[Diatonic Modes]]'s Aeolian-specific ear-training guidance.

## Techniques
- **Series context — this builds on two prior videos.** Explicitly a follow-up to a "Feeling the Major Scale" video and, before that, [[The Secret of Ear Training]] — the feeling-based approach itself isn't re-derived here, just extended to the three degrees minor adds/alters relative to major.
- **The circle visualizer tool: degrees arranged in a circle by fifths, not by pitch order, live-tracking whichever degree is currently sounding against a drone.** Notes a fifth apart sit adjacent on the circle; notes a half-step apart melodically can sit almost opposite each other on it — the visual is a direct picture of [[Scale Degree Ear Training]]'s "melodic closeness ≠ feeling closeness" principle, with the specific example given: 1 and 5 are melodically distant (a fifth) but harmonically/feeling-wise close (adjacent on the circle), while 1 and ♭2 are melodically adjacent (a half-step) but feeling-wise close to opposite.
- **Only 3 degrees actually differ between major and natural minor: ♭3, ♭6, ♭7.** Everything else in the minor scale carries the identical feeling state already learned from major-scale training — meaning minor-scale ear training doesn't require relearning all 7 degrees from scratch, only isolating and drilling the 3 that changed.
- **Degree ♭3 — "minorness" itself.** The defining degree of what people mean when they call a passage "minor": dark, serious, gloomy, with a real touch of sorrow. Most confused with ♭7 (lighter, less serious) and ♭6 (more intense, more hopeless).
- **Degree ♭6 — more intense than ♭3, one step short of ♭2's extremity.** Carries a genuinely negative emotional valence — desperate, hopeless, "no joy, no hope" — distinct from ♭3's merely somber quality; sits between ♭3 and ♭2 on the intensity scale of "flat" degrees.
- **Degree ♭7 — the lightest "minory" feeling in the whole system, explicitly NOT dark.** Powerful and almost fun despite technically being a flat/minor-leaning degree — the video is explicit that ♭7 shouldn't be lumped in with ♭3/♭6 just because all three are "flat" degrees; each has its own distinct emotional character.
- **Drilling method: play each degree against a sustained tonic drone, attend to the feeling with the same "getting to know a friend" openness Konyi models across the series** — no urgency to name it correctly immediately, just repeated exposure while consciously aware "that's degree X."
- **Comparative drilling sharpens contrast fastest.** Rather than drilling each of the 3 new degrees in isolation, deliberately compare adjacent pairs back to back (5 vs. ♭6, 6 vs. ♭6, ♭3 vs. ♭6) — hearing two similar-but-distinct feeling states immediately next to each other trains the boundary between them faster than drilling either one alone.
- **The feeling-state of a given degree is octave-invariant and (for most listeners without perfect pitch) transfers unchanged across keys** — reiterated as a foundational property: degree ♭6 in the key of D feels the same as degree ♭6 in the key of A♭, and the same whether played an octave up or down, because the feeling comes from the relationship to the tonic, not the absolute pitch.
- **Theoretical labels are a mnemonic anchor, not the actual skill.** Words like "glowing," "spiky," or a scale-degree number are described as a "mental touchstone" — useful scaffolding to recall or translate a feeling onto an instrument/DAW/MIDI while learning, but explicitly not what's actually being trained; the felt recognition itself is the target, independent of whether you can verbally name it.
