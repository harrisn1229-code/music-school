## Synopsis
Second lesson in the "You Suck at Drums" course — applies episode 1's beats/bars fundamentals to actually programming a first drum loop in a Drum Rack. Covers basic kick/snare placement, the half-time groove trick, velocity-as-lifelike-detail tied to the strong/weak beat hierarchy, and — the video's real payload — the three-changes method for turning one low-energy Intro loop into a second, higher-energy "A" clip without falling into the "one four-bar loop forever" trap. A foundational video for anyone building drum-loop arrangements clip-by-clip rather than as one static pattern.

## Techniques
- **Baseline kick/snare placement:** kick usually lands on beats 1 and 3, snare on 2 and 4, in a standard 4/4 pattern.
- **Half-time groove trick for fast tempos** — genres running at very high BPM (trap, dubstep, future bass, reggae — 150–160+) can be made to feel like half that speed without touching the tempo: put the snare/clap on beat 3 only (instead of 2-and-4) and drop the kick underneath it. → [[Time Signatures & Note Values]] (already routed)
- **The second kick can go anywhere — including the "and" of a beat** — halfway between two beats is called the "and" (counted "one and two and"); switching the grid to eighth notes lets you place notes at that resolution. Going one step further, the sixteenth-note grid lets you count "one e and a, two e and a" for even finer placement.
- **Strong vs. weak metric position determines velocity** — the strongest spots are right on the beat (1, 2, 3, 4); weaker spots are the "and"; the weakest are the "e" and "a" of a beat. Lowering the velocity of a hit that lands in a weak spot (especially one at the very end of a loop) makes it read as more lifelike — paying attention to strong-vs-weak placement and adjusting velocity accordingly is described as "a great way to make your drums sound more lifelike." → [[Time Signatures & Note Values]] (already routed)
- **The "three changes" method for building a second, higher-energy clip (I → A):** duplicate the low-energy Intro clip, rename it, and make exactly three changes that each raise the energy. A change is either (1) an existing element made more active throughout the loop, or (2) a brand-new element added. Each change needs to be immediately obvious and present throughout the loop — not one hit added in a corner, and not so dense it just sounds like noise. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]] (already routed)
- **Duplicating a one-bar loop to two bars needs its own micro-variation** — Duplicate Loop doubles the length, but if the second half is identical to the first it doesn't actually feel any different; nudge at least one element (e.g. move a kick slightly) so the ear doesn't clock an obvious repeat.
- **Hi-hat as a steady quarter/eighth/sixteenth-note timekeeper**, with velocity dropped on the weaker (off-beat) hits — the same strong/weak logic applied to a second instrument, achieved fast by highlighting the first couple of hits and hitting Cmd+D repeatedly to copy the pattern across the bar.
- **Snare placement communicates hierarchy: keep the important snares obviously stronger than filler ones** — when adding extra snare hits beyond the core 2-and-4, put them only in the weakest possible spots (the "e" or "a" of a beat) and avoid stacking them directly on top of kicks; then lower the velocity on the new snares relative to the strong 2-and-4 hits so it stays clear which snares are the "real" backbeat and which are ornamental.
