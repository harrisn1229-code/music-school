# Modal Pop

**Modes as they're actually used in popular music** — which is almost never as scales to solo with, and almost always as a source of *chords* that major and minor don't provide.

[[Diatonic Modes]] covers what the modes are. This is what they do in a pop, rock or trap context.

## The three that matter

Pop uses three modes with any regularity. The rest are colour.

| Mode | Characteristic | The chord it gives you |
|---|---|---|
| **Mixolydian** | ♭7 | **♭VII** (B♭ in C) |
| **Dorian** | ♮6 in a minor context | **IV major** (G in D Dorian) |
| **Aeolian** (natural minor) | ♭6, ♭7 | **♭VI and ♭VII** (F and G in A minor) |

Each is defined by **one note**, and that note produces one chord you couldn't otherwise have. That's the whole mechanism.

## Mixolydian and ♭VII

**The single most common non-diatonic chord in rock and pop.**

`I – ♭VII – IV` (C–B♭–F in C) is everywhere. The B♭ isn't in C major — it comes from **C Mixolydian**, which is major with a ♭7. You can equally describe it as borrowed from the parallel minor; both readings are valid and produce the same chord.

**Why it sounds the way it does:** ♭VII has **no leading tone**. There's no B natural pulling up to C, so the progression lifts and drifts rather than pulling home. That's the "modal" quality — motion without the dominant's gravity.

This is also a Beatles signature. Along with descending chromatic bass lines under a held chord ("Michelle," "Something" — see [[Chromatic Bass Lines]]), the borrowed ♭VII is the device that shows up across their catalogue and is a large part of why the harmony sounds like it does.

## Dorian and the major IV

**Dorian is natural minor with a raised 6th** — and that raised 6th changes the chord built on the 4th degree from minor to major.

The clearest demonstration, and the placement question worth internalizing (POP-17):

| Vamp | Mode | The deciding note |
|---|---|---|
| **Dm – G** | D **Dorian** | **B natural** (G major needs it) |
| **Dm – Gm** | D **natural minor** | **B♭** |

**One note decides it, and it's the entire colour difference.** Dorian lifts — the major IV against a minor tonic sounds hopeful, unresolved, forward-leaning. Aeolian stays dark.

Dorian vamps are all over neo-soul, house, lo-fi and modern R&B precisely because they're minor without being bleak.

## The Aeolian cadence

`♭VI – ♭VII – i` — **F – G – Am** in A minor.

All three chords are diatonic to natural minor. It's everywhere in trap, metal and film scoring, and it works because it climbs stepwise into the tonic with no dominant at all — the ♭VII moves up a whole step to i, which is a completely different feel to V–i.

Related and equally common: `i – ♭VI – ♭III – ♭VII` (Am–F–C–G), already listed in [[Chord Progressions]] under minor keys. That's the minor-key equivalent of the axis loop, and it's the default harmonic bed for a large amount of trap and drill.

## Making a mode actually sound modal

The trap: play a mode over a progression that keeps resolving to the parent major and it just sounds like the parent major.

Three things make it land:

1. **Emphasize the characteristic note** — the ♭7 in Mixolydian, the ♮6 in Dorian. If it's absent, the mode isn't audible.
2. **Use the chord that note produces** — ♭VII, or the major IV. This is what actually communicates the mode.
3. **Avoid the parent's dominant.** Playing a V7 pulls the ear straight back to the parent key. Modal writing generally avoids functional dominants entirely — which is why so much modal pop is two chords looping.

Think **parallel, not derivative**: "D minor with a raised 6th," not "the white notes from D." See [[SCL-C04 — Parallel vs derivative mode thinking]].
