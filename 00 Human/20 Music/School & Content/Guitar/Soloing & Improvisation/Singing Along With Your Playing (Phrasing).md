# Singing Along With Your Playing (Phrasing)

Part of [[Music Theory]] — a **practice method for developing phrasing**, distinct from [[Blues Lead Phrasing and Touch]] (note-choice/touch vocabulary within a phrase) and [[Solo Dynamics and Arc]] (the macro shape of a whole solo). This note is about *how to train the instinct* that produces good phrasing in the first place, by literally singing/vocalizing along with your own improvising — a specific 4-step method, not a vague "sing more" suggestion.

## The four elements that make phrasing sound good

Named directly as the target: any phrase that hits these four reads as strong phrasing, regardless of note choice.

1. **Strong, concrete rhythm** — not ambiguous timing. The rhythmic shape has to be decisive, not smeared.
2. **Repetition** — either purely rhythmic repetition, or pitch/note repetition (repeating the actual notes), or both.
3. **Ideas reacting to / building on what was just played** — a phrase should sound like a response to the previous one, not a disconnected new idea.
4. **Conclusions** — ideas need to actually resolve/finish, not just trail off or run out.

The claim (and the reason this method works): singing along with your own playing pushes all four of these *automatically*, because you physically can't sing the way you can shred — you have to breathe (forces pauses/space), you naturally gravitate to simpler/catchier material (forces repetition), and there's a felt desire to resolve a vocal phrase (forces conclusions). Cited as a documented habit of Keith Jarrett, Kurt Rosenwinkel, and Glenn Gould — all known for audibly singing/humming along while they play/improvise.

## The 4-step method (the actual "secret weapon")

Practiced in order, each step building on the last:

1. **Step 1 — play constant notes, no breaks, no phrasing at all (the deliberate opposite of the goal).** Over a simple vamp (worked example: a Cmaj7–Fmaj7 I–IV loop), just keep playing continuously — any key or no key, any tempo, varying rhythmic durations freely (triplets, 8ths, quarter notes) — with **zero rests, on purpose**. The point isn't musicality here; it's proving to yourself that your fretting hand can go *anywhere, anytime* without conscious note-by-note thought (no "what fret do I play" in your head). This "wall of sound" fluency is the technical floor that step 2–4 are built on — you can't focus on phrasing while your hand is still hunting for notes. *(This is the unconventional, counter-intuitive step — it's the literal opposite of phrasing, done deliberately as a prerequisite.)*
2. **Step 2 — vocalize out of time, no backing track, no pressure to match pitch.** Very slow, on your own — sing/vocalize along with your playing, but the singing does **not** have to be technically good and does **not** have to match the guitar's pitches. Stay loose and relaxed; forcing it to be "too perfect" reads as tense, which defeats the point. It's normal for the vocal to only loosely track the contour at first, occasionally landing on pitches by accident rather than by design — that's fine. If your voice runs out of range, just let it jump octaves or drift rather than forcing a match.
3. **Step 3 — do it for real, in time (with a backing track or on your own), consciously targeting the four elements above.** Now actually try to phrase: non-ambiguous rhythm, repeat pitches/rhythms, react to what you just played, and land conclusions — while singing along. Reported effect: playing tends to get "simpler" and more relaxed than playing without singing — the singing acts as a natural brake on over-playing and pulls the guitar toward something "lyrical" and "groovy."
4. **Step 4 — embody the same feeling *without* singing out loud.** The actual goal — don't want to depend on literally singing forever. Try to "sing in your mind" while playing: stay as connected to the four phrasing elements as you were in step 3, but silently. Expect it to be hard at first (you'll catch yourself losing the internal thread and drift back toward step-1-style note-hunting) — cycle back to a little out-loud singing, then try silent again; repeat in the woodshed over time.

## The three broad ways to work on phrasing (context from the same channel)

Framed as three parallel approaches — this video develops only the third in depth; the other two were referenced as separate videos not yet in the vault at the time this note was created:

1. **Phrasing models** — deliberately choose a rhythm, repeat it, alter it slightly, and build compositional structure with it across a solo (an A-phrase / B-phrase / A-phrase / C-phrase form, where C contrasts or concludes). **Now covered in full** — see [[Phrasing Blueprints]] for the four fixed letter-form structures (A A' B, A A A B, A A B A, A A B C) and their drills. A close cousin from the same "Nail the Changes" course, not the same exercise: [[Using Space and Pauses in Phrasing]] — also a fixed-rhythm/free-pitch drill, but built around inserting deliberate *rests* rather than letter-form repetition.
2. **Borrow rhythms from speech or existing melodies** — take the rhythm of a spoken sentence/phrase, or the rhythm of a melody/song you know, and play *that same rhythm* over a totally different scale/tonality/note choice — it won't sound like the source at all, but it's a legitimate way to practice phrasing independent of note choice. Still not covered by a dedicated video in the vault.
3. **Sing along with your own playing** — this note's method, above.

## Bonus: combine with melodic patterns for the "ultra combo"

Stated directly as the single most game-changing pairing: **melodic patterns/sequences** (breaking up note selection into a logical, repeating pattern run through a scale/key) combined with the phrasing work above.
