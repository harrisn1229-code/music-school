# Diagonal Pentatonic Blues Soloing

**One diagonal fretboard shape, one altered note, one transposition — a low-overhead alternative to tracking a separate arpeggio per chord over a I7-IV7-V7 blues.** Contrasts directly with [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]'s CAGED-arpeggio-per-chord method: instead of re-fingering a fresh arpeggio at every chord change, this technique keeps one continuous pentatonic shape running and moves only what has to move. Worked in the key of A over exactly the I7-IV7-V7 progression (A7-D7-E7) documented on [[Blues Form and Harmony]], and grounded in the same note-choice logic as [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]] — a direct guitar-fretboard application of that page's "one scale over all three chords" vs. "following the changes" practical notes, landing on a hybrid: mostly one shape, with a single deliberate note swap where the changes actually demand it.

## The diagonal shape

**A "diagonal 3-2 major pentatonic"** — a two-notes-per-string fingering that climbs diagonally across the neck (fret gaps alternating roughly 3 then 2 frets per string, with an irregularity on the B string) rather than sitting in one vertical box position. Starts on the root at the 5th fret (A, low E string). *(The source names the pattern directly as "diagonal 3-2 major pentatonic" and calls out the B-string fingering as the one spot that breaks the regular 3-2 alternation, but doesn't give the complete fret-by-fret map on all six strings in the transcript — treat the exact fingering as best confirmed against the source's free diagram download, referenced in the video description, rather than fully reconstructed here.)* The pitch content is plain **A major pentatonic: A B C♯ E F♯** — the shape's value is that it's playable diagonally across the whole neck rather than boxed into one position, which is what lets the same physical logic keep working as the chord changes (below).

## Step 1 — I7 (A7): straight major pentatonic

**A major pentatonic (A B C♯ E F♯) over A7**, the I chord — inspired directly by BB King's use of major pentatonic (not just minor/blues scale) over the tonic dominant. The C♯ is the chord's major 3rd, so it "lines up with the chord perfectly." Same idea as [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]] § *What to play over the I7* — Mixolydian-leaning material (major 3rd intact) reads as the "sweet" end of that page's blend, here delivered as a fixed fretboard shape rather than a scale-degree list.

## Step 2 — IV7 (D7): change exactly one note

**The core hack: drop the C♯ down a half step to C natural, and leave every other note untouched.** Over A7 the C♯ was the major 3rd and sounded great; over D7 that same C♯ is the chord's **major 7th**, which clashes against a dominant-7 chord (major 7ths don't belong on a dominant sound). Lowering it a half step turns it into **C natural — the ♭7 of D7** — and the resulting five-note set becomes consonant against the new chord without requiring a scale change or a position shift: **A B C E F♯ over D7**, checked chord-tone-by-chord-tone against D7 (root D, major 3rd F♯, 5th A, ♭7 C):
- **A** = the 5th of D
- **B** = the 6th (13th) of D
- **C** = the ♭7 of D
- **E** = the 2nd (9th) of D
- **F♯** = the major 3rd of D

Every note in the altered shape is a real, consonant extension of D7 — nothing borrowed or "outside," which is why the single-note edit works as cleanly as it does. Physically, the altered shape sits as a two-string-set pattern that repeats up the neck the same way the unaltered diagonal shape did in Step 1 — same diagonal logic, one note different.

## Step 3 — V7 (E7): transpose the same altered shape up a whole step

**No new shape needed — take the exact D7-altered pattern from Step 2 and slide it up a whole step (two frets).** Because E7 sits a whole step above D7, the same relative shape that outlined D7's chord tones lands squarely on E7's chord tones when moved up two frets. So the full system across a 12-bar blues in A is: **A major pentatonic diagonal shape (I7) → lower the 3rd a half step (IV7) → slide that altered shape up a whole step (V7)** — one shape, one edited note, one slide, covering all three chords of the form.

## Why this is being framed against arpeggio-chasing

The video's explicit pitch (title: "Stop Chasing Arpeggios") is that tracking a fresh arpeggio for every chord change is overkill on a diatonic-to-itself 12-bar blues where I, IV and V are all dominant 7ths a 4th/5th apart — see [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *Why every chord is a dominant 7th*. Because the three chords share so much common tone content (worked out chord-tone-by-chord-tone above), **one continuous scale shape with a single note edit tracks the changes about as well as swapping arpeggios would, for far less fretboard movement.** This is a genuine alternative to — not a replacement for — [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]'s CAGED-per-chord method: that method is built for progressions that *aren't* this forgiving (secondary dominants, fast-moving ii-V units, minor substitutions) where chord tones really do diverge chord to chord; this technique is built for exactly the case where they don't (a static blues I-IV-V), and trades some of that method's harmonic precision for a much simpler physical task.

## Practical

- **Portable to any key/any 12-bar blues** — the whole system transposes: find the I chord's major pentatonic, identify its major 3rd, and apply the same "half step down for IV, whole step up for V" logic relative to that key's I-IV-V.
- **Only one note actually needs conscious tracking** (the 3rd/♭7 swap between I and IV) — the rest of the shape, and the whole IV→V move, is mechanical (slide up a whole step), which is what makes this genuinely simple to execute in real time compared to naming/fingering three separate arpeggios.
