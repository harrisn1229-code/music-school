# Jazz Comping — Rhythmic Motif and Walking Bassline

Part of [[Jazz Harmony]] — a **rhythm-first comping method**, deliberately separate from [[Chord Voicings]] (which shapes to grip) and [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] (soloing over the changes). This note is about *when* to strike the chord and what the bass note underneath it is doing, applicable to any tune regardless of its harmony.

## The core tip: one repeatable rhythmic motif, not constant variety
Pick a simple, single rhythmic idea — a specific pair or handful of beats where the chord lands — and **repeat it consistently** before reaching for anything fancier. A demonstrated starting motif: hit on the **"and" of beat 1, and on beat 3.** Repetition is what makes comping read as cohesive and intentional; constantly reinventing the rhythm bar-to-bar reads as scattered, even if each individual choice is "correct." *(General starting point — pick any two-hit-per-bar pattern and hold it for a full pass through the form before varying.)*

**Sequencing matters:** lock the motif in first, *then* introduce variation or departures from it. A deliberate detour away from the motif, followed by a return to it, reads as "grounded" — the return only lands because the motif was established as the baseline first. This mirrors the "overuse a device to internalize it, then use it tastefully" practice logic already documented on [[Chord-Tone Soloing]].

## Riff comping — a named, larger-scale sibling of the core motif idea
Instead of a repeatable one- or two-beat rhythmic hit, stick with a whole **melodic/rhythmic riff** for a stretch of the form — demonstrated at roughly **8 bars per idea**, tracked to a tune's own internal repetition (an AABA-shaped tune's first static-feeling 8 bars get one riff, its more harmonically-moving 8 bars get a second, the repeated A section brings the first riff back, and a different tag/ending idea closes it out). Two reasons to reach for it:
- **Repetition itself builds energy and sits well in a groove** — especially valuable over a static feel (Latin/bossa/samba are the clearest case, where the guitar part usually has to lock to one distinct repeating pattern regardless of what's being soloed over).
- **Riff comping is the practice on-ramp to genuinely open/free comping**, not a separate, lesser approach. Even "open" playing with almost no audible groove still requires the whole band to have silently agreed on what groove they're *not* playing — riff-based practice through a tune's changes builds the vocabulary and rhythmic habits that make later free playing sound intentional instead of aimless, rather than skipping straight to open playing without that foundation.
- **Doubles as ear-training for absorbing an unfamiliar rhythm into your default vocabulary** — take a rhythm you don't already reach for, force it through a whole tune's changes a few times, and by the source's account it tends to "stick" as usable material afterward. *(General starting point: pick one riff, run it through a full chorus of a tune before switching, then repeat with a second, different rhythm.)*

## Bonus: walking bassline with the chords
Layer a moving bass note under the same comping rhythm:
- **Beats 1 and 3: play the current chord's root** as the bass note.
- **Beats 2 and 4: pass toward the *next* chord's root** with a chromatic or scale-step approach note — a half step below, a half step above, or a whole step above the target. Which approach direction sounds best depends on the specific interval between the two roots; try more than one.
- **Works best on progressions where chords change every two beats** — there's a clean "root, approach-note, new root, approach-note" cycle available. On a chord that lasts a full bar, apply the same beats-1-and-3 root logic "passing to itself" (root, [passing tone], root, approach-to-next).
- **Once the bassline is solid, the chord voicing itself can be freed from a fixed grid** — e.g. moved to beat 1 + the "and" of beat 3 instead of a rigid every-beat pattern — for a less mechanical feel. Same "vary only after the base pattern is locked" sequencing as the primary motif tip above.

- **Three named "chord punches" (Jared Borkowski's own term) giving this note's own rhythmic-motif idea concrete, drillable named variants, plus a fuller taxonomy of connecting-note directions than this note's existing "half step above or below" bullet.** Built over dominant-7th shell voicings walked through the circle of fourths with tritone-sub-derived approach notes (→ [[Chord Substitution]]): **sustain** (the chord lands on the downbeat where harmony changes and rings as long as possible — this note's own baseline motif); **short up punch** (a very short, staccato chord struck on the "and" of beat 1, displaced off the downbeat entirely — named as the ingredient that "starts to really bring this to life"); **double punch** (the downbeat chord *and* the staccato up-punch combined in the same bar). Same "drill each in isolation, then mix by ear" sequencing already central to this note. **Connecting-note options, expanding this note's existing half-step-above/below pair:** half step below works "pretty much always, on any chord"; **combining approach directions across a progression** (some from above, some from below) is called out as where the line "really starts to be expressive"; a **whole step above** the target is more physically awkward and has less pull than a half step but is worth the variety; a denser version connects with **eighth notes — whole step above, then down a half step, then to the chord** (a three-note descending run instead of one approach note); and a **whole step below / chromatic-from-below** approach is flagged as working especially well specifically when the *target* chord is minor. **Practical deployment: drop the whole technique into a turnaround** (worked over E♭7-B♭7-G7-Cm-F7) at the exact point a tune's harmonic rhythm speeds up to two-beats-per-chord, then drop back out into normal comping — framed as a deliberate momentary device, not a continuous approach. → [[Chord Substitution]], [[Chromatic Bass Lines]], [[Turnarounds]], [[Chord Voicings]] — Jared Borkowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared), [[How To Start Walking Bass Lines For Guitar]]
- **Two more rhythmic-displacement/variety tools from Andrew Green's *Jazz Comping* (reviewed by Nathan Borton), both aimed at the same failure mode this page already targets: comping that's too predictable.** **Anticipation and delay:** Green's stated problem — always striking the chord exactly on the downbeat reads as predictable and boring — is fixed by displacing the strike by an **eighth note**: play the chord an **eighth note before** the downbeat (anticipation) or an **eighth note after** it (delay), and mix both directions across a tune rather than committing to one. A finer-grained sibling of the "anticipation on beat 4/and-of-4" entry below (Green's version displaces by a fixed eighth-note interval rather than shifting to a specific earlier beat) and of this page's own "and of 1, beat 3" base motif and the Charleston-displacement entry further down — same principle, displace off the expected strong beat, applied at eighth-note granularity. **Long and short notes:** vary chord **duration**, not just placement — cut chords short or let them ring long. **The selection rule Green gives, stated as the more important half of the tip:** don't default to one duration; **listen to what the soloist is doing and respond to it** — duration is a reactive, ensemble-aware choice, directly the same "read the room" discipline as [[Ensemble Listening and Comping Restraint]]. *(General starting point: take this page's own base motif and run it three ways through a full chorus — on the beat, anticipated by an 8th, delayed by an 8th — before mixing all three within one pass; separately, practice cutting the same motif short vs. letting it ring, switching only in response to what a soloist/recording is doing at that moment.)* → [[Ensemble Listening and Comping Restraint]], [[Chord Voicings]], [[Guide Tones]] — Nathan Borton, [[Andrew Green's Comping Book... What I Learned!]]
- **Anticipations — strike the upcoming chord early, ahead of its own downbeat, typically on beat 4 or the "and" of beat 4 of the preceding bar.** A rhythmic-placement device distinct from this note's own repeatable-motif and walking-bassline content: rather than a fixed groove repeated bar to bar, an anticipation deliberately displaces one specific chord hit *forward* into the previous measure so the harmony arrives before the barline instead of on it — matches the general "chord tone from the next chord, sounded early" definition already on [[Non-Harmonic Tones]], applied here as a guitar comping placement choice rather than a voice-leading/analysis term. Stated to combine directly with chromatic voicing-approach (slide the anticipated chord in from a fret away) for a denser, more forward-leaning comp — the same "stack devices only after each is solid individually" sequencing already central to this note. **A second, source-named practice method for building this note's own rhythmic vocabulary:** transcribe/imitate rhythms off a real recording (Count Basie named specifically as the go-to source), then **write out your own etude** applying a pulled rhythm rather than only imitating it passively — deliberately mix in whole notes and half notes alongside eighth notes so the etude doesn't default to one rhythmic density, then apply drop 2/drop 3 voicings to the written rhythm, varying which root string (E, A, or D) is used bar to bar. Full five-step build order (voicings → rhythm/etudes → 9th voicings → chromaticism → anticipations → top-note melodies) on the video's own page. → [[Chord Voicings]], [[Non-Harmonic Tones]] — Nathan Borton, [[Jazz Comping 101 - THIS Is What You Need To Know]]

- **Momentum across chorus boundaries: don't over-emphasize beat 1 of a new chorus, but never drop it entirely — let the return happen on its own momentum rather than being manually announced.** Peter Bernstein's comping-continuity tip for multi-chorus tunes: you don't need to "hit that first [beat]" of a new chorus hard or make "a big thing" out of landing squarely on it — "you're basically coming back around for another lap," and the sense of returning to the top of the form should arrive naturally, driven by the momentum of the chorus that just finished, rather than a deliberate accent. But the downbeat still has to be there, just not oversold — framed as "starting strong the next chorus" through carried-over feel, not through emphasis. A form-level counterpart to this page's own bar-level "lock the motif in first, then vary" sequencing — the continuity discipline scaled up from one bar to a whole chorus. → [[Ensemble Listening and Comping Restraint]] — Peter Bernstein, [[Peter Bernstein Comping Masterclass - Lesson]]
- **The Charleston rhythm, displaced off beat 1 — a direct extension of this page's own rhythmic-motif logic, applied to the specific rhythm already documented as the "default go-to" on [[Chord Voicings]] and [[Blues Form and Harmony]].** Those existing entries document the Charleston in its textbook form (a stab on beat 1 and the "and" of beat 2). This source shows it's routinely displaced by working jazz players: struck starting on **beat 2, the "and" of beat 1, or beat 3** instead, freely combined with plain quarter notes and half notes — the displacement itself is what supplies "surprise and propulsion" rather than any change to the rhythm's own internal shape. Demonstrated across three worked 2-5-1/blues comping lines, including one starting the Charleston explicitly on beat 3 before resolving into a quartal voicing (→ [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]). Reads as the rhythmic-placement half of a device whose harmonic half — reusing one chord voicing off different chord tones — is documented on the new note [[Multifunctional Voicings]]. → [[Multifunctional Voicings]], [[Chord Voicings]], [[Blues Form and Harmony]], [[Quartal Chord Voicings]] — Nathan Borton, [[The No. 1 Book for Jazz Guitar Comping (And Its Biggest Secrets)]]
- **Two fully worked, note-by-note basslines over a Rhythm Changes A section, giving this note's own "approach note" bullet actual reasoning instead of just a rule — plus a specific rule for one chord-quality change and a "shared chord tone" melodic device.** Core organizing idea: default to **the 5th (or ♭5) of the current chord** as the connector into the next root — stated as the systematic, low-friction choice because it's harmonically logical *and* sits comfortably under most jazz grips, demonstrated across an entire 8-bar A section (`B♭ G7 | Cm7 F7 | B♭ G7 | Cm7 F7 | B♭ B♭7 | E♭ E♭m6 | B♭/F G7 | Cm7 F7`). **Specific rule for a dominant→same-root-minor move (F7 → Fm):** use the 5th, never the 3rd, as the connecting note — the 3rd "masks the fact that we're changing to a minor chord" by pre-announcing the wrong quality; the 5th stays neutral and lets the target chord's own minor 3rd do the work. **A second, more melodic bassline over the identical form leans on chromatic leading notes and a "shared chord tone" device instead of straight 5ths:** when the 3rd (or another chord tone) of the *current* chord is also a chord tone of the *target* chord, using it as the connector produces a big, satisfying melodic leap that still sounds fully inside both chords — described as sounding "almost like a dominant resolving" even with no such chord actually played. **Repeat-note handling matches this note's existing Joe Pass rule exactly:** across the `B♭ → B♭7 → E♭ → E♭m6` stretch (harmony already moving chromatically on its own), the bassline just repeats the same note rather than adding its own motion. **A recurring slash-chord/inversion device:** resolving to B♭maj7 with F (the 5th) in the bass — pointed out as literally the same pitches as a D minor triad over F, just read differently depending on what's implied above. **Practice sequencing, applied here to note choice rather than rhythm:** play the systematic all-5ths version until it's solid, then deliberately mix in the more melodic/chromatic approach — the "lock in the plain pattern before varying" logic already central to this note, restated as "if you play the all-5ths version for five minutes you'll get tired of it." **Also: write basslines out deliberately before improvising them** — compose fixed lines, internalize, then start altering pieces once comfortable ("fitting patterns together") — and **target medium-tempo standards built on this turnaround-plus-move-to-IV shape** specifically, because simultaneous bass + chords at fast tempo is genuinely difficult on solo guitar and not that often needed in practice. - **A second, independently-sourced "chords and walking bass" example — Joe Pass over the A section of "Love for Sale," with the moving-bass construction rules spelled out step by step.** Framed by the source as a technique "not everybody approves of," even though it's one of the most Pass-associated comping devices there is: play the **full chord**, then **move that same chord shape up the neck in steps** as the bass line advances, adding color tones (e.g. the ♭3rd of the next chord, played **as an inversion**) as connecting notes en route — the bass line reads as "a simple stepwise melody moving from one chord to the next," not independent arpeggio choices. **Two practical exceptions, both framed as "practicality beats intricacy":** on a chord that doesn't reward a full stepwise walk, **skip straight to a leading note** near the target instead (e.g. A♭maj7 → skip down to a leading tone → A♭7♭13); on a chord that's basically stationary, **just drop to the 5th** rather than composing a bass melody — "the most simple bass change you can make, but it still works." **Repeated-note handling:** when a chord repeats, or the harmony is only moving by a half step, **just repeat the bass note** rather than inventing new movement — the chord change (or the half-step motion itself) already supplies the "something happening." **Pass's own stated rationale for the whole approach**, quoted directly: the four comping elements (bass note, chord, melody, fill) are never literally simultaneous, "it's an illusion... you're playing a bass note sometimes, a chord sometimes, the melody most of the time, and you're filling," all "done by doing something with motion" — and two rules for choosing what to leave out at any moment: if the chords are already doing a lot (dense turnarounds, passing chords), keep the bassline and fills minimal; if the bass is doing a lot (walking bass, as here), fills and complicated chords get in the way. → [[Chord Voicings]], [[Comping Fills]], [[Turnarounds]] — Jens Larsen, [[The Secret Jazz Chord System of Joe Pass]]
- **A quarter-note-pulse comping foundation, the plainer sibling of this note's own rhythmic-motif idea, plus a first rhythmic embellishment on top of it.** Over the real, difficult "slow-medium jazz tempo" of "I've Got the World on a String," a **solid quarter-note pulse with chords landing mostly on the beat**, played on **shell voicings alone** (a drop-3 shape, a shell "bass-one" shape, a single drop-2 shape covering most of the tune) is called "a great take on the song" in its own right — a deliberately simpler starting point than this note's own two-hit-per-bar motif, useful as the very first rung before adding rhythmic variety. **First embellishment on top of the plain pulse: split the bass note and the chord apart** — strike the bass note, then the chord shape separately, rather than together — demonstrated on an E♭7 and a Cm in the same tune, purely for rhythmic interest with no harmonic change. Suggested practice order: shell voicings on a steady quarter-note pulse through a whole tune first, then add the bass/chord split, then fills (→ [[Comping Fills]]) — matches this note's own "lock in the plain version before varying" sequencing logic. → [[Chord Voicings]] — Jens Larsen, [[The Secret Jazz Chord System of Joe Pass]]
