# Roundabout — Yes

> *Fragile* (1971). This note is a bass-focused breakdown of Chris Squire's part — ghost notes, a commonly-missed 16th-note syncopation, an anticipation/push device, a genuinely rule-breaking drum-and-bass conversational section, whole-song use of space, and a buried melody-doubling bass line in the bridge — as taught by [[Why This Bass Line Effin’ Kills It → Roundabout by Yes (+Tab)|BassBuzz]]. Framed by the video as the direct influence behind Geddy Lee, John Entwistle, Steve Harris, and Les Claypool's own playing.

## Chords
This is an 8-plus-minute prog epic with real tempo and meter shifts across sections, so this covers only the two sections underneath the bass part broken down below — the **main verse-ish riff section** and the **"I'll be the roundabout" chorus** — not the harmonics-only intro, the "in and around the lake" bridge, or the extended closing guitar-solo section. Chord transcriptions for this song genuinely disagree more between sources than most tunes on this page (different capos, different enharmonic spellings, different section boundaries), consistent with how harmonically restless the writing is — treat the below as "as commonly transcribed," not a definitive chart.

**Verse-ish riff section**, roughly in **G major**: G – Em – G – Em – G – A – C – A – Bm – A – G → **I – vi – I – vi – I – II – IV – II – iii – II – I**. That "A" (a major II chord, not the diatonic Am) is a non-diatonic borrowed color — Yes leaning on chromatic-mediant-adjacent harmony well outside a plain major scale → [[Chromatic Mediants and Pop Modulation]]

**Chorus ("I'll be the roundabout"):** G – C – G – F – Bb – G – C – G – F – Bb – G – Em – G → **I – IV – I – ♭VII – ♭III – I – IV – I – ♭VII – ♭III – I – vi – I**. The **F (♭VII)** and **B♭ (♭III)** are both borrowed from G's parallel minor / Mixolydian world — the kind of chromatic-mediant color that makes Yes's harmony feel bigger than a plain diatonic pop chorus while still resolving home to G each time → [[Chromatic Mediants and Pop Modulation]] · [[Borrowed Chords]]

No controlled-vocabulary progression tag fits this cleanly (not ii-V-I, not axis, not a turnaround) — the borrowed ♭VII/♭III chorus movement is genuinely its own thing, so `progressions` is left empty above.

## The main riff: ghost notes for contrast

The riff is full of ghost notes — cover the string(s) with the fretting hand, then pick/pluck for a thumpy, unpitched, percussive sound (marked `X` in tab). Playing every note clean instead loses contrast and fills up the sonic space, leaving less room for the rest of the band to cut through — ghost notes deliberately break the riff into a mix of pitched notes and percussive thumps. Full technique, cross-genre examples (Chuck Rainey, Kim Deal, Mark King), and a practice drill → [[Ghost Notes (Slap Bass)]].

## The main riff: a syncopation most players miss

Notes mostly land on the beat, but there's an off-beat emphasis later in the riff that gets missed often enough that BassBuzz calls it out directly (and admits playing it wrong himself before listening closely). 16th-note count: "1 e and a, 2 e and a, 3 e and a, 4 e and a." The riff plays "1 e and, 2 e and a, 3 e and—" then starts its next note group early, on the **"and" of 3** — that's the syncopation. **The common mistake:** starting that note squarely on beat 4 instead — a 16th note late. The end-of-riff shape is otherwise a common one (scale degrees 4–5–7 of the minor pentatonic) — the syncopation, not the notes, is what sets this apart from an unsyncopated cousin like "Paranoid" (Black Sabbath, same notes, straight quarters) or the Tenacious D "Tribute" guitar riff. Full device (with the strong/weak-beat framework and the "and"/"e"/"a" weight hierarchy) → [[Syncopation and Metric Displacement]].

## The main riff: anticipation (the push)

The riff normally starts squarely on beat 1 — except the first time it's heard (and a few times after), where the first note lands early, on the **"and" of the previous beat 4**. Named directly as **anticipation**, also called a **push** — it adds excitement and pulls the listener toward the next section. Important detail: pushes are usually played by multiple instruments together (bass + drums here) — from just one instrument, the same early note reads as a timing mistake, not a device. A second worked instance later in the track pairs the push with a drum fill leading into it. Full device (and why it's distinct from harmonic "anticipation" in [[Non-Harmonic Tones]]) → [[Syncopation and Metric Displacement#Anticipation ("the Push")]].

## The chorus-ish section: drum-and-bass conversation, rule broken on purpose

BassBuzz's own baseline advice elsewhere: as a safe default, play the bass in the kick drum's rhythm. This section of Roundabout breaks that rule deliberately — bass and drums share the same 8th-note grid but bounce/ping-pong off each other rather than locking, and drummer Bill Bruford swaps the **snare** in for spots the **kick** would normally hold. Snare, kick, and bass sometimes line up, sometimes don't — that disagreement is what pulls listener attention, and there's room for it because the hi-hat plays plain quarter notes and the vocal has big gaps here. A direct A/B demo shows the section sounds noticeably flatter if the bass lines up 100% with the drums instead. **Guardrail from the video:** this isn't a rule to apply by default — test conversational bass/drum rhythm with a real drummer before committing, and don't reach for a busy counter-kick-drum part on a simple pop song where it isn't earned. Extends the "lock to the pocket, not the kick map" method step and gets its own worked example → [[Bassline Writing]].

## Whole song: use of space as arrangement contrast

Zoomed out, the bass track has long blank stretches where it isn't playing at all — presence/absence itself is a contrast device. Density then swings hard between the sections that ARE playing: the main riff is dense even with ghost notes thinning it → the chorus riff is much sparser → the closing guitar solos invert that completely, with the bass hitting every 16th note. General lesson: view a song from a bird's-eye verse/chorus/bridge level and ask whether there's contrast — when the vibe shifts and the bass part doesn't, the two simplest levers are fewer notes ↔ more notes, or short notes ↔ long sustained notes. Full note, more worked examples → [[Bassline Writing]].

## The bridge: doubling the buried organ melody

The bridge's obvious bass part is the one you'd play live. On the record, Chris is simultaneously **doubling the keyboard/organ melody on bass, in the same register** as that main bass part — two real bass parts' worth of material at once, described as "freaking mayhem," demonstrated by playing both together against the drums. Read as a direct line back to earlier melodic bassists — Paul McCartney, Jack Bruce, and John Entwistle are named explicitly — and pointed at more of the same across Yes's catalog and Squire's 1975 solo album *Fish Out of Water*. A second 70s-melodic-bass worked example (Marvin Gaye's "Your Precious Love," bass doubled note-for-note by guitar) lives on → [[Bassline Writing]].

## Tone and gear: the recipe, plus a no-Rickenbacker fallback

Squire's tone: clicky, growly, notes audibly buzzing as they sustain. Real gear: a **1964 Rickenbacker**, both pickups engaged, into **two separate amplifiers** — described as unreplicable exactly (a P-bass with flatwounds won't get there, and even the official signature reissue reportedly didn't sound like the original). Four achievable steps toward it on any bass:
1. **Strings** — bright stainless-steel round wounds, specifically **Roto Sound Swing 66** (what Chris used).
2. **Pick** — **gray Herco heavy** picks, plasticky and bright.
3. **Position** — pluck/pick near the **bridge** for more edge.
4. **Attack** — play hard and nasty.

**No Rickenbacker or no pick? Use Geddy Lee's version instead.** Geddy plays this exact riff fingerstyle on a Jazz bass: engage **both pickups**, turn the bass's tone up, crank treble/high-mids (bass or amp). Two plucking-hand options named: **clacky downward plucks** (Geddy's default a lot of the time, also covered for Steve Harris), or, specifically for this riff, a **back-and-forth strum with the index finger** rather than string-by-string alternating plucks. Full tone-chain framing and cross-references → [[Bass Tone Chain Priority]]; pick-vs-fingers decision framework → [[Pick vs. Fingers on Bass — Tone and Technique Tradeoffs]]; bridge-position plucking → [[Playing Fast on Bass — Efficient Technique, Setup, and Bridge Plucking]]; the strum technique → [[String Crossing and Plucking Patterns]].

## Steal this

- **Ghost-note a dense riff on purpose** to open up sonic space for the rest of the band, not just for percussive flavor. → [[Ghost Notes (Slap Bass)]]
- **A note that arrives early on the "and" of the previous beat 4, played together with the drums, is a push** — exciting when locked with another instrument, sloppy-sounding alone.
- **Deliberately let the bass NOT lock to the kick** in a section that has room for it (sparse hi-hat, gapped vocal) — test with a real drummer before trusting it on a real song.
- **Chart a song's felt density verse-to-chorus-to-bridge and make sure the bass part actually tracks it** — fewer/more notes or shorter/longer notes are the two cheapest levers.
- **A second, buried bass part doubling the keyboard melody** is a real (if mayhem-inducing) arranging move, not just a live simplification of "the" bass part.
- **String + pick + plucking-position + attack is a portable tone recipe** even without the exact original instrument — and there's always a fingerstyle fallback path.
