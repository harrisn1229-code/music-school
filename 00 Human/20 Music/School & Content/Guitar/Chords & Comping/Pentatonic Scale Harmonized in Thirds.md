# Pentatonic Scale Harmonized in Thirds

**Stack the pentatonic scale's own notes in 3rds (skip one scale tone, not one chromatic note) instead of pulling chords from the full parent major scale, and cycle the resulting shape through every degree of the scale to get a full set of usable, pentatonic-flavored chord voicings and arpeggio patterns from a single physical grip.** This is the tertian (3rds) counterpart to [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]'s stacked-4ths pentatonic harmonization — that page names "harmonizing pentatonic scales instead of the full major/melodic-minor scale" as an extension flagged for later; this note is that extension, worked in 3rds rather than 4ths. Because the pentatonic scale skips the 4th and 7th degrees, stacking "thirds" within it doesn't produce plain triads the way harmonizing a full major scale does — some of the resulting intervals land wider than a diatonic 3rd, which is exactly what gives the shapes their open, non-tertian-sounding character even though the construction method is nominally tertian.

## The construction method

1. **Pick a starting note in the pentatonic scale** (worked from C in C major pentatonic: C D E G A).
2. **Stack up three scale-degrees from that note** (not three chromatic half steps) — from C, count three notes up the pentatonic scale (C→D→E→G) to land on G, giving a first interval; from the next scale tone (E), repeat the same three-scale-degree stack to add another note on top. The result is a chord built entirely from pentatonic-scale tones, using "thirds" measured in scale steps rather than fixed diatonic 3rds.
3. **Slide the same shape to the next scale degree and repeat** — move the whole stacked shape up to start on D, then E, then G, then A, cycling it through all five scale positions the way a harmonized-scale drill cycles a triad shape through every diatonic degree (same principle as [[Chords by Key]]'s tonic-to-tonic mapping and [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]'s "3-note quartal chords through D Dorian" drill, just with this stacked-in-3rds pentatonic shape instead).
4. **A second, different voicing shape** built the same way (also derived purely from the pentatonic scale's notes) is available alongside the first — moving it through the scale produces its own family of chord colors, distinct from the first shape's family. Two independent shapes, each cycled through five scale degrees, gives ten usable chord voicings from one five-note scale.
5. **Arpeggiate each shape** rather than only strumming/plucking it as a block chord — turns the same fingering into a melodic line-generating device, not just a comping voicing. Combine the arpeggiated chord tones with the straight scale run and the skip-note practice patterns (see [[Pentatonic Superimposition and Side-Slipping]] §Foundations) to build actual solo lines — "this is just a combination of the pentatonic scale, the patterns through the scale, and the chord shapes."

## Why it works

The pentatonic scale is already a maximally-consonant subset of the parent major/minor scale (no half-step clashes against the implied tonic — see [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]] for the analogous "hard to play wrong" property of the minor pentatonic/blues scale). Restricting the chord-building material to *only* those five notes guarantees every resulting voicing stays inside that same consonant, non-clashing note pool, no matter which scale degree the shape starts from or how far it's stacked — which is what makes "a lot of mileage just out of that one chord shape" possible: the ear never hears a wrong note because the source material was pre-filtered down to the pentatonic scale before any stacking happened.

## Practical

- Treat this as a **voicing-generation drill, not a fixed chord vocabulary to memorize** — the value is the stack-and-slide method itself, reusable in any key/scale the pentatonic is built from (major or its relative minor).
- Pairs naturally with [[Diagonal Pentatonic Blues Soloing]] and [[Dorian from the Minor Pentatonic Box]] — once a pentatonic box is under the fingers for soloing, the same five notes are the raw material for this chord-shape drill, so the fretboard geography is already familiar.
- Contrast with [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]'s pentatonic-in-4ths method: that harmonization introduces a natural 6 (Dorian color) against a minor chord; this 3rds-based method stays inside the plain pentatonic's own note pool with no added color tones, so the two techniques produce genuinely different-sounding chord families from the same starting scale — worth drilling both and comparing.
