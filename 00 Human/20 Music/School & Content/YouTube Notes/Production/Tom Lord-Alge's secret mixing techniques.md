# Tom Lord-Alge's secret mixing techniques

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8cIKEfm9x2U)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8cIKEfm9x2U)

## Synopsis

Tom Lord-Alge revisits his own 18-year-old analog mix (multiple singers on the verse vocals, spread across seven or eight tape channels) and translates each analog-era move into a modern-DAW equivalent: driving a console channel's line input for saturation, committing (printing) processed comps to tape rather than leaving them open-ended, a snare reverb that's actually an emulation of a specific vintage hardware unit, and a filtered slapback delay used as a hook effect. The back half is a single extended demonstration and the clear centerpiece of the video: recreating classic analog tape flanging — normally done by physically varispeeding a half-inch tape machine back and forth by hand to sweep the flange — using a modern delay-compensation plugin (the Eventide Precision Time Align) automated to sweep a couple of milliseconds around zero delay. He walks through both the original 2000s tape method and the live in-the-box recreation, explicitly framing it as "tape flange in a DAW, solved."

## Techniques

- **Console saturation by deliberately overdriving an SSL channel strip's line input — used as a texture stage on a printed/committed vocal comp, achievable in-the-box with an SSL-channel-emulation plugin insert.** "I was doing console saturation by overdriving the line input of the console, which you can do with the SSL plug-in. Again, I got the sound that I wanted, and I committed it." → [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[Committing and Printing]]
- **Commit processed vocal comps to tape (print them) once the sound is right, rather than leaving the option open to revisit — described as standard practice on an analog mix, and endorsed as a philosophy even now: "when something is perfect, you just commit it and leave it."** The same verse-vocal comp, once dialed in (distortion/saturation + panning), stayed committed for 18 years without revision. → [[Committing and Printing]]
- **A snare reverb identified on-screen as Slate VerbSuite Classic's "CLA Snare" algorithm — itself an emulation of a specific piece of vintage studio hardware, the Sony/MCI DRE-2000 (referred to as the "FG-2000").** Added as "extra spank" on the verse vocals in a remix pass of his own 18-year-old mix. Unowned plugin — see [[Plugin Wishlist]]; the general lesson (a named plate/snare-reverb algorithm behind a mix's signature vocal or snare reverb sound) belongs on the reverb effect page. → [[Reverb]], [[Plugin Wishlist]], [[Vintage Gear Worklist]]
- **A filtered, swept slapback delay used as a hook effect specifically in the chorus** — printed to a channel on the analog console rather than automated, because "in mixing in 2000, it was a nightmare" to automate that kind of move without Pro Tools. The filter sweep on the delay repeats is called out directly as audible, deliberate ear candy tied to the song's hook. → [[Delay]]
- **The standout technique — recreating classic analog tape flanging in a modern DAW using a delay-compensation plugin automated to sweep near zero.** The original hardware method: print the exact audio you want flanged to half-inch analog tape (with generous pre-roll, since the technique needs time to "catch" the sweep), fly that tape back into the multitrack, and manually varispeed the tape machine — slowing and speeding it — while listening for when the flange sweep lands where you want it in the track; it can take dozens of bars of trial to catch the sweep on cue. The modern in-DAW method, demoed live: duplicate the exact same audio onto two tracks; insert a delay-compensation plugin (he names the **Eventide Precision Time Align** specifically, chosen because it lets you push delay in either direction — ahead of *or* behind zero, not just add positive delay) on one of the two identical tracks; set it to zero delay as the starting point; then **automate that delay value sweeping back and forth by a couple of milliseconds around zero** — the classic flange "jet engine" sweep appears purely from that automated near-zero delay modulation between two otherwise-identical signals. "You just automate the movement — tape flange in a DAW, solved." → [[Modulation]], [[Align Delay]], [[Automation]], [[Plugin Wishlist]], [[Tape Flanging Emulation]]
