# Width and Stereo

**What it is:** the left-right dimension — real width (two different performances), psychoacoustic width (micro-pitch/delay tricks), and M/S width (processing the sides separately from the center).

## The width hierarchy (best to cheapest)
1. **Double-track it.** Two real performances panned L/R is width no plugin matches ([[1 Electric Guitar Amp Track|Electric Guitar Amp Track]], [[1 Double Vocal Track|Double Vocal Track]]). Stacks in fours, hard-panned as you track, first take checked in mono before the pan spreads it (Kuk Harrell, [[Creating vocal stacks with Kuk Harrell]]).
2. **Micro-pitch** ([[MicroShift]]): detuned L/R copies under a mono center — width *and* focus ([[Width Send]]). This is the modern form of **[[Artificial Double Tracking (ADT)]]** — the Beatles/Lennon "fake a double from one take" trick (invented at Abbey Road, 1966).
3. **Chorus/ensemble** ([[Chorus-Ensemble]], [[kHs Chorus]]): modulated-delay width — vintage, audible motion.
4. **Haas/delay offset** ([[kHs Haas]], EchoBoy L/R offset): free but the most mono-fragile.
5. **M/S widening** ([[S1 Imager]], [[Ozone 9 Imager]], [[Wider]]): boosts the side channel — use in single dBs; it can't create sides that don't exist.

## The rules that keep width safe
- **Mono-check everything** ([[Utility]] mono flip / [[Stereo Tool]]): a widened element should get quieter in mono, never vanish or comb. If it disappears, reduce the effect or raise its crossover.
- **Low end mono, always** (<100–120 Hz — Utility Bass Mono, Imager band 1 to zero): stereo lows phase-cancel on clubs/vinyl/phones.
- **Width is relative.** The sides only feel wide against a solid center (lead vocal, kick, snare, bass stay centered) and a narrow verse makes a wide chorus bloom — automate width per section.
- **Where width is cheap and safe:** hats/percussion group, pads, backup stacks, piano/keys sides. **Where it's dangerous:** anything low, anything that must survive mono, the lead vocal's dry center.
- Widening also *cleans the center* — pushing keys/pads outward is a masking fix as much as an aesthetic one ([[Masking and Carving]], the M/S carve).

## From videos

- **Centering the loudest/most-important elements can beat spreading them, when the source was never designed for stereo — plus a caution that repositioning a buried element makes it read as newly added.** Giles Martin's remix philosophy for the Beatles catalogue: because these records were engineered and creatively decided for mono (stereo was barely a format in 1965 UK, and the familiar hard-left/hard-right Beatles stereo mixes were Capitol Records' American commercial afterthought, not the band's intent), his own remixing rule is to put **drums and lead vocal in the center** — "respecting the mono" — and spread only the surrounding instruments to the sides, rather than the old hard-panned split. Two stated reasons: (1) power — "if you want more power from something like a drum kit, it coming out of two speakers as opposed to one is a better option"; (2) headphones — most listening today is on headphones, where a hard-panned mono-era mix (a whole instrument jammed in one ear) reads as distracting/dated rather than characterful. Separately, a direct confirmation of this note's own panning-reads-as-loudness principle from the opposite direction: demixing recovered isolated finger-snaps buried in a backing-vocal track on "Here, There and Everywhere" — once given their own channel and repositioned, listeners (Giles included) perceived them as "new," despite being present in the original mix all along; **panning something far left or right makes it read as louder even at unchanged gain**, worth remembering whenever a newly-separated/isolated element gets a wider pan than it had buried in a stem. → [[Depth and Space]], [[Masking and Carving]] — Tape Notes (Giles Martin), [[TN 190 The Beatles (Giles Martin) - Full Video Episode]]
- **Keep an arrangement dead-center/mono through a verse, then hard-pan a specific layer wide for a short, deliberately jarring contrast moment before returning to mono.** "Rock Music" runs almost everything centered through the verse, then two guitar layers suddenly go hard left/right for a brief "super wide" section before collapsing back to mono — an arrangement-level width EVENT rather than a constant stereo image. → [[Depth and Space]] — Charli XCX / A.G. Cook / Finn Keane (Tape Notes), [[TN 185 Charli XCX, A. G. Cook, Finn Keane - Full Video Episode]]
- **Build width by running two DIFFERENT mono effects, one per channel, rather than one stereo effect.** On a split-note patch (independent processing per hand/channel), route a phase shifter on the left channel's effect chain and a flanger on the right, each fully mono — then bring some of the dry/straight signal up the middle. The deliberate mismatch between the two channels' effects is what generates the width and complexity, more than either effect alone (or the same stereo effect on both sides) would. A general technique worth trying whenever a patch/bus feels flat in stereo despite already having reverb/delay on it: two different mono treatments, panned apart, instead of one wide stereo treatment. → Anthony Marinelli Music (feat. Stephan Schmidt & Tim Sund), [[Hands-On FM Synthesis Companion - Tim Sund's C15 Patches]]
- **The foundational mid-side mechanism, spelled out explicitly for the first time here, plus three techniques this note hasn't covered yet: mid-side compression widening, mid-side expansion/saturation isolation, and a new EQ target.** Mechanism: duplicate a signal to both channels, then phase-invert one copy — an identical signal reads centered (0°), phase-inverting one copy spreads it to the full 180° field, the same underlying phase-cancellation principle behind this note's existing delay-based widening entries above. **Mid-side compression:** compress mid/side as separate channels and the mid — which usually carries more signal energy — gets hit with more gain reduction automatically, so the master ends up with a relatively quieter mid and louder side (more width) as a side effect of ordinary compression. **Mid-side expansion/saturation:** same isolate-one-image logic applied to expansion and saturation, e.g. saturating only the side image adds width/character without coloring the mono-compatible mid core. **New EQ target: ~700 Hz on the side image carries a lot of guitar body** — boost it there to make guitars read bigger/wider, filling a guitar-specific gap alongside this note's existing 7 kHz side-shelf and 80 Hz/2–2.5 kHz mid/side vocal-and-low-end recipes. → [[Phase Alignment]], [[Compression]], [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[EQ]] — Sage Audio, [What is Mid Side Processing?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z2zGgchC3bM)
- **An unintended-Haas-effect caution specific to per-channel EQ.** Zero-latency EQ applied to only one side of an otherwise-identical hard-panned stereo pair (e.g. a doubled guitar, hard left/right) shifts that channel's phase relative to the other — effectively a slight delay — which can trigger the precedence effect and pull the perceived image toward the unprocessed side. Full mechanism (FFT/Nyquist, why minimum-phase EQ shifts phase at all) on [[Phase Alignment]]. → [[Phase Alignment]], [[EQ]] — Sage Audio, [How EQ Changes Phase](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2_v4VrPkzec)
- **The stereo field has three zones, and traditional panning only reaches ONE of them — plus a spectral (HRTF-style) panning technique that's a natural-sounding companion to delay-based panning.** Center 90° (where a standard pan pot operates) vs. the left/right 45° zones beyond it that together make the full 180° field — a pan pot alone caps a mix at the center 90°; reaching the outer zones needs delay-based phase cancellation instead. Width = position within the field; depth = amplitude + temporal processing (reverb/delay), a genuinely separate axis. Delay-based panning mimics real inter-aural time difference: delay just the right channel and the source reads as coming from the left (left ear hears it first), and vice versa. **Spectral panning** does the equivalent with frequency instead of time: attenuate just one channel's HIGH frequencies (mimicking how a real head shadows highs on the way to the far ear) for another naturally-directional cue — combine both with traditional panning for placement anywhere across the full 180°. Mastering-stage width control should stay mostly mid/side gain balance, NOT delay-based widening — delaying whole instrument groups risks severe phase issues, over-widening costs a master its impact, and heavy 180°-field content translates poorly to mono. **More widening is not better at the mastering stage.** → [[Depth and Space]], [[Referencing and Translation]] — Sage Audio, [What is Stereo Imaging?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=h1aNrMEi5XA)
- **Four delay-based widening methods, from subtle to maximal, all working via phase cancellation between near-identical signals.** (1) Sample delay on just one channel (L or R) — alters L/R phase relationship directly. (2) Mid/side EQ boosting the side channel — similar widening via a different mechanism. (3) Full hard-panned duplicate-and-invert: duplicate a mono signal, phase-invert one copy (confirm true inversion — summed, they should produce silence), pan hard L/R — takes a mono signal from dead-center to the full 180° field. (4) Directional placement via SHORT delay (up to ~35 ms) on one channel only — delaying the right channel makes the left ear hear it first, so the brain perceives the source as coming from the left (Haas-effect-style), distinct from the symmetric-widening methods above since it shifts apparent SOURCE DIRECTION rather than just spreading width. → [[Phase Alignment]], [[Delay]] — Sage Audio, [How to Create Depth and Width with Reverb and Delay](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XGSytDiGJl0)
- **Side-image compression + makeup gain — the INVERSE of compressing the mid image to widen by contrast, and a dense, detailed side image most mixes never try.** With a mid/side compressor UNLINKED for independent processing, lower the threshold on the **side** image only until roughly **1–2 dB of attenuation** (more on an instrument bus than the mix bus) — this narrows/centers the image on its own. The payoff is adding MAKEUP GAIN back to the compressed side image: since compressing then amplifying reduces dynamic range and boosts every quieter part of the signal, the result is a dense, detailed side image — an aspect of a mix that rarely gets compressed, and often what's actually missing. Demoed on a drum bus and the full mix bus. Distinct from this page's existing mid-image-compression-for-width entries (which attenuate the CENTER so the sides read louder by comparison) — here the side itself is directly compressed and pushed. → [[Compression]] — Sage Audio, [Mix Polishing: Advanced Techniques to Finalize Your Mix](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7AfSb6uMJhs)
- **Decorrelated stereo highs generate worse lossy-codec (MP3/AAC) artifacts than correlated ones — a real cost of aggressive high-frequency stereo widening.** Lossy encoding relies heavily on attenuating high frequencies to shrink file size. When high-frequency artifacts land identically on left and right (correlated), other high-energy centered content masks them; when they land only on the SIDE image (decorrelated — e.g. after a stereo expander widens the highs), there's much less to mask them, and the side image's own spatial/directional cues make the artifacts stand out more. Stereo expansion is fine within reason, but the more high frequencies get decorrelated, the more aggressive lossy-encoding artifacts become on release — worth checking a widened master against an MP3/streaming bounce, not just the WAV. → [[Mix Handoff]] — Sage Audio, [5 Advanced Compression Techniques (Simple to Complex)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V1lZaf8NWd0)
- **Check mono fold-down on stereo vocal EFFECTS specifically (reverb, delay, micro-pitch, chorus), not just the mix as a whole.** Most main vocals are already mono, so summing to mono rarely costs the dry voice — but stereo vocal effects live partly or wholly in the side image and get thinned or lost on the mono fold-down most phone/portable speakers perform. Test: send the stereo effect via an aux, insert the free plugin MSED on the return, mute the side image, and listen to what survives — applies to any stereo instrument, not just vocals. Keep at least part of a vocal's effects centered so their loudness doesn't collapse on mono playback. → [[Referencing and Translation]], [[Plugin Wishlist]] — Sage Audio, [Perfecting Vocals on Modern Playback Systems](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pzEG3HxI3WE)
- **Four cues drive sound localization; stacking all four (not just amplitude panning) makes panning read as genuinely spatial rather than a knob-turn.** (1) Amplitude — standard pan-pot; panning turns DOWN the opposite channel, it doesn't boost the target side. (2) Timing — a sound from the left reaches the right ear ~3 ms later; DAWs don't do this automatically, needs a stereo delay. (3) Frequency — a sound bending around the head to the far ear loses some high end (weaker frequencies absorbed/diffused more), so the far ear gets a slightly darker signal — even alone, this reads as panned. (4) Environment/reverb — most important cue; since real sound interacts with its environment before reaching the ears, reverb placed BEFORE amplitude/delay/frequency panning gives the most realistic sense of placement (see [[Depth and Space]]). **Practical use: apply distinct localization cues (not just pan position) to deliberately separate two similar-sounding layers** — e.g. two guitars playing the same melody an octave apart, which would otherwise read as one grouped source (see [[Artificial Double Tracking (ADT)]] for why). → [[Depth and Space]], [[Artificial Double Tracking (ADT)]] — Sage Audio, [Psychoacoustics: The Least Talked About, Most Important Part of Music Production Pt.1](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SZ_2yIPp4g4)

- **Too-centered has its own failure mode, not just too-wide: a master that's too centered reads closed-in and lacking a natural sense of space.** This note's existing "rules that keep width safe" section above is mostly written from the too-wide side (mono-check, keep the low end mono, width is relative) — worth stating the opposite failure explicitly too. Mechanism recap from the source: identical L/R signal reads centered, differing L/R signal creates the perception of width; mastering typically shapes this with mid-side EQ/compression/saturation and stereo imagers (which also layer in psychoacoustic width tricks on top of straight M/S routing). Also a genre/context caution worth pairing with the club-mono convention already logged in [[Referencing and Translation]]: wanting a wide stereo image on an EDM track headed for clubs (often multispeaker mono playback) is worth reconsidering before committing to it, since the venue caps how much of that width actually translates. → [[Critical Listening for Mastering]], [[Depth and Space]], [[Referencing and Translation]] — Sage Audio, [Before You Master Music, Watch This](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tEqcGkgHgqc)
- **Dynamic (rather than static) stereo widening: an expanding shelf on the side image that only triggers with high-frequency content, plus a mid-vs-side dynamic-power distinction.** Shelf on the **side image**, ~**7 kHz and up**, range ~**2 dB**, threshold lowered/automatic until the band audibly triggers and expands — because it's dynamic, the widening isn't constant, it tracks how much high-frequency instrumentation is actually present, reading as a musical effect rather than a flat width bump. Distinct companion technique on the same source: a **dynamic band on the mid frequencies** for power/impact reads completely differently depending on which image it's placed on — mid image → drums/vocal read more dynamic/impressive; side image → guitar body and temporal processing (reverb) read more dynamic — worth balancing both rather than defaulting to one. → [[Pro-Q 4]], [[EQ]], [[Masking and Carving]] — Sage Audio, [How to Use Dynamic Eq During Mastering](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tvuBplNrYMs)
- **A matched-boost/matched-attenuate mid/side EQ width recipe — distinct from this note's existing mid/side EQ entry below, which pairs a side HPF + mid boost with no matching side attenuation.** Boost the **mid** image at **~80 Hz** (kick) and **~2 kHz** (vocal), then **attenuate the side image at those same two frequencies** to reduce phase cancellation between the mid and side content — a matched-pair move rather than boosting the sides alone. Separately: a **high shelf on the side image** widens perceived top end "more times than not." Framed as a common, safe mastering practice for width without introducing unwanted phase changes; the source channel points to its own earlier dedicated stereo-width video for more depth (not yet in this vault). → [[EQ]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [How to EQ Your Master](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GF-XMVa3lW8)
- **A mid-side limiter as a stereo-expansion tool: attenuate the mid image more than the side, at the master's very last stage.** A limiter-specific variant of the mid-image-*compression* width trick below (attenuating the center makes the sides read louder by comparison) — here it's the mastering limiter itself doing the attenuating, not a dedicated mid/side bus compressor upstream of it. Named alongside channel-unlinking (see the dual-mono/Channel Link entries on [[Pro-L 2]], [[Limiting]], [[Master Chain]]) as the other of two ways to get stereo expansion from a limiter; unlinking won't widen every master, but can make L/R read as more unique/distinct. → [[Pro-L 2]], [[Limiting]] — Sage Audio, [How to Limit Your Master](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SrFwQqDMJQM)
- **Combine a per-band dynamic duck with per-band mid/side placement, rather than treating imaging and dynamic ducking as separate passes.** In a dynamic-EQ vocal-vs-instrumental duck (build bands on the instrumental, key each Dynamic band's threshold off an external vocal sidechain — see [[Masking and Carving]]), assign each band's **Stereo Placement** independently: one dynamic band ducking only the **mid** image, another ducking only the **side** image. The result is a single move that clears the vocal's specific frequencies AND shapes where in the stereo field that clearing happens — distinct from every other mid/side entry on this note, which pairs a *static* processing choice (compression, saturation, EQ) with mid/side routing rather than a *dynamic, externally-triggered* one. → [[Pro-Q 4]], [[2 Inst-Synth Group]], [[Sidechaining]] — Sage Audio, [EQ Masterclass](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iPBnL6mx9c0)
- **The manual mid/side routing trick, stated as an explicit workflow: duplicate the channel, encode/decode each copy separately, process each in isolation.** When a plugin doesn't offer native mid/side routing, duplicate the channel/track and put an M/S encode/decode utility on **both** copies — one instance isolating the mid image, the other isolating the side — then insert whatever processor is needed on each copy independently. - **A three-move mid/side EQ recipe, simpler than the mastering-chain version already logged below.** High-pass the **side** image to lock the lows mono (the single most common M/S EQ move); boost **~2.5 kHz on the mid** image specifically to help a vocal cut through in a master, without brightening or widening the side; a **high shelf on the side** image expands perceived highs/brightness without touching the center. → [[Ghz Midside 3]], [[EQ]] — Sage Audio, [Mid Side Masterclass](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SHxih1xc5yM)
- **Mid/side saturation as its own technique, not just a routing curiosity: saturate the mid image's lows for a more centered low end, saturate the side image's highs for expanded stereo highs.** Owned: [[Saturn 2]] natively supports M/S routing for this; [[Roar]]'s mid/side routing mode is Ableton's own native equivalent; [[Stereo Tool]]'s independent Mid Sat/Side Sat controls map onto the same two-part recipe without needing the duplicate-channel trick. - **Mid-image compression's applicability is broader than a mastering-only trick — works on drums, vocals, and full mixes/masters (subtly on the latter two).** Whenever the mid image is compressed more than the side, the mix reads wider, because attenuating the (usually louder, more transient-heavy) center makes the side read louder by comparison — and this is often the natural outcome anyway, since the mid image typically triggers a compressor more than the side does. - **De-linked mid-side bus compression as a stereo-width move, plus a "dual phase rotation" feature for a fuller low end — two distinct mechanisms on the same master-bus compressor.** Unlike the mid-image-*only* compression trick below, this move keeps both images compressing but **de-links the triggering** so the mid image (which usually drives the detector more) compresses more than the side — same net "sides read louder relative to the center" width result, reached a different way. - **Mid-image-only compression as a stereo-width move — a mechanism distinct from every EQ-based imaging trick above.** With a mid/side-capable compressor, compress **only the mid (center) image** by **0.5–1.5 dB** and skip makeup gain entirely (makeup gain cancels the relative-loudness shift the trick depends on) — attenuating the center makes the sides sound louder *relative* to it, reading as genuine stereo expansion without ever boosting the sides directly. Frequency-specific version: a multiband/mid-side tool so only certain bands widen (owned: [[Pro-MB]], Stereo Placement set to Mid on the target band). Free option: Melda M Compressor with its routing switched from Stereo to Mid. Described as one of the source video's favorite mastering-width moves for how natural the result sounds. → [[Pro-MB]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [Mastering Compression Settings](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YUn31aE9X_8)
- **Side-image-only high-pass placement rule + a side/mid air-frequency choice, both from a mastering EQ pass.** The HPF: attenuate the **side image only** (not the full signal) with a high-pass filter placed **between the source's fundamental range and its 2nd-order harmonic range** — this makes everything below that filter identical L/R (mono) without touching the fundamental itself, run at each of a mastering chain's two EQ passes (corrective before dynamics, tonal after — dynamics processing reintroduces side-image lows, so the HPF gets reapplied). The air-frequency choice, on the second (tonal) EQ pass: boost the air band on the **side** image alone to expand the stereo image, on the **mid** alone to focus it centrally, or identically on both — presented as a genuine creative choice with no fixed answer, though "a great starting point more often than not." Also: a **side-image bell in the mid-range** widens the stereo image without unbalancing overall tone. → [[Pro-Q 4]], [[EQ]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [The Only Music Mastering Video You'll Ever Need](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=M88T8jFL2uU)
- **Measured stereo-width research: L/R channel RMS deviates by at most 0.8 dB in commercially successful masters, and mono-compatible mixing has been the dominant convention for roughly 40 years** (some early stereo records tried unconventional instrument panning; it died out quickly). Not a width ban — a mix can stay RMS-matched while still being wide, since width mostly lives in the *side* image rather than a level imbalance between the two channels. Practices that follow from the finding, specifically at the mastering stage: keep the four foundational/power elements centered (lead vocal, bass, kick, snare — pan only for a deliberate creative effect); never run L/R-differential EQ (same frequency, different amount, per channel); keep a mastering limiter's channels linked or mostly linked so gain reduction doesn't land unevenly on one side; expansion above 200 Hz is fine but should be small and done via M/S-balance/EQ rather than a delay-based expander. A demonstrated A/B backs the last point: widening the side image with EQ vs. the same section widened with a delay-based stereo expander — the delay-based version audibly loses focus on the centered elements, because amplifying the side image only accentuates stereo information already present in the mix, while a delay-based expander manufactures new inter-channel differences that can smear the centered elements instead. → [[EQ]], [[Limiting]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [Audio Mastering Science: Using Research to Make Informed Decisions](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=21dpmumO4So)

- **Panning reads as a volume increase — expect (and budget time for) a second level pass after panning, not just before it** — with every element dead center, everything is competing for the exact same position; the moment something moves even partially off-center it suddenly reads as louder/clearer purely because it's no longer sharing that position with everything else. - **A thick, full-spectrum harmonic element (chords/pads) panned hard to one side can make the OPPOSITE side of the mix feel hollow — keep it closer to center than a thin part would need to be** — a large, wide-sounding chordal/pad part occupies enough perceptual space on its own that panning it fully to one side leaves the other side of the stereo field feeling empty by comparison, distinct from thinner single-note parts which don't create the same lopsided effect when hard-panned. - **Panning is a hard L/R crossfade, and foundational hits stay centered while detail layers spread** — a first-principles mechanic worth knowing before any width trick: panning fully to one side sends literally zero signal to the opposite channel (no bleed/crossover) — every fancier stereo trick (Haas, M/S, etc.) is built on top of that simple crossfade. - **Duplicate + hard-pan alone doesn't widen — the two copies need to actually differ** — two identical copies panned hard L/R sound virtually the same as the mono original, because both still reach both ears at the same instant; real width needs the copies offset in time or pitch. - **Sample-delay widening: fake three vocals from two.** Double-track, keep one copy center, duplicate it and offset *one channel* by a few samples (e.g. Logic Sample Delay L 344 / R 1387 samples) so you effectively get one center + one hard-L + one hard-R — huge width from just two takes. Works on guitar too when you only tracked one. Slightly phasey; mono-check. → [[Stereo Field]] — Disclosure, [Who Knew](https://youtu.be/e9d7KYEG3VM) & [Talk (with Khalid)](https://youtu.be/PBbnvElf9ac)
