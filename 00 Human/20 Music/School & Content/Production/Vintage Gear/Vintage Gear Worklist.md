- [ ] ARP 2600 — ARP — Anthony Marinelli's stated favorite/desert-island synth (self-contained semi-modular with built-in speakers, "one of every module," easiest to program by his account — see [[Lightning Round w Super Synth Tech Rob Rosen - Clip]]). **Live-performance mod source for whenever this gets its own note**: a set of custom hardware mods installed ~1979 for pre-memory live/session flexibility — per-oscillator octave switches (Minimoog-style, with fine-tune preset-voltage knobs giving effectively 5 selectable octave positions, also usable to dial in fixed intervals between oscillators for instant unison/interval switching), a switch extending ADSR/AR times to ~10× the stock max (long enough to "hit a note and walk away"), a separate gate/clock input for the AR envelope generator independent of the keyboard gate (lets one envelope free-run off a clock/LFO while the other stays keyboard-triggered), clean on/off switches on filter/VCA inputs to kill noisy-pot hum when a module isn't in use, and extra mult (passive multiple) jacks patched in for routing flexibility and as informal mini-to-1/4" cable extenders. — Anthony Marinelli Music, [ARP 2600 Custom Mods](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KoiG6ToV5Gs). **A detailed A/B against the modern Korg ARP 2600 M reissue, worth mining for whenever this note is built**: raw sawtooth reads fuller/lower-content on the Korg (less inherent high-end sizzle — compensate by driving the preamp for saturation); sine wave is purer/more accurate on the Korg vs. a more character-distorted vintage sine; triangle wave is brighter/more harmonically buzzy on the vintage unit vs. a purer, lower-harmonic-content Korg triangle (a real programming consideration — filter more aggressively on the vintage unit to compensate); filter sweep is smoother/more linear low-to-high on the vintage unit vs. the Korg, whose sweep concentrates more action near the top of its range; vintage unit has more resonance headroom in the high range. **Envelope generator character is the biggest functional difference**: on the VCA's linear input, the Korg gives very short, clean, percussive decay times while the vintage unit reads scratchier/anomalous and "squishes"; on the exponential input the Korg gives a crisp near-click transient vs. a "pebbly" vintage attack — the Korg is the clear pick for clean percussive synth-drum-style attacks. On the AR envelope generator, the Korg has a quicker attack but a much longer maximum release than the vintage unit (which has the reverse balance) — genuinely different creative range, not just "better/worse." **New capabilities the Korg reissue has that no vintage 2600 ever did**: a built-in reverb (smooth, long decay, praised as a standout feature), true 2-voice/unison capability via a second Keyboard Control Voltage output on its 3620 keyboard, and a ring modulator that's genuinely usable as a second VCA when DC-coupled (nulls out cleanly, no audio-mode leakage). Preamp is more linear/controlled on the Korg (good for subtle overdrive without losing control, though it can still be pushed "out of control" at extremes like the vintage unit's famously less-controlled preamp). Everything else (sample & hold, voltage processors, white noise) is identical between the two. **Verdict**: the reissue can replicate nearly everything the vintage original does with minor nuance adjustments, plus adds real capabilities the original lacks — judged a worthwhile "second/third 2600" at its price point rather than a mere approximation. — Anthony Marinelli Music, [[ARP 2600 Tonus vs Korg 2600 M - Synth Showdown]]. **Earliest-production-run history and design evolution ("Blue Marvin," unit #19 of ~20-25 built, provenance traced from a stranger's basement via a Reddit post to restoration in partnership with the Alan R. Pearlman Foundation)**: this earliest ARP 2600 run predates "multiples" (mult jacks) entirely, has no fine-tune control on the filter's initial frequency (coarse-only, unlike the oscillators which did have fine-tune from the start), and has a genuinely unlabeled/ambiguous voltage-processor section compared to later units' clearly labeled +10V/-10V/keyboard-CV sources. Modules are a different physical shape (rectangular vs. the later gray-face units' square modules) and are fully epoxy-potted — unrepairable if they fail, a real reliability risk on any surviving unit this early. **Production timeline**: ~20-25 "Blue Marvin" units → the "Gray Meanie" (same futuristic case, ~35 units, added multiples + filter fine-tune) → the switch to the later road-case-style chassis for durability, since the earliest case design was fragile and prone to cracking. **A hand-corrected silkscreening error**: this early unit's waveform icons were originally silkscreened swapped/incorrect, and were individually hand-repainted by an ARP employee's artist wife. **Genuinely wide open input headroom on this earliest unit**: mixing multiple oscillators in past roughly the halfway mark clips immediately into rich, warm, Moog-Modular-like harmonic saturation — later "cleaned up" 2600 revisions require driving levels much further to get the same character, a real gain-staging difference worth knowing when comparing eras. **A microtonal keyboard feature lost in later revisions**: the earliest ARP keybeds had a variable-tone-interval control letting a physical half-step key movement output any interval (not just a chromatic half-step) — reconfigurable to whole-tone, quarter-tone, or even a full 13-tone-per-octave scale, effectively turning a 4-octave keyboard into up to 8 "octaves" of tone control; a percussionist who'd played with George Harrison taught a 13-tone scale learned in India, usable directly on this keybed. Later 3620 keybeds dropped this in exchange for a built-in LFO and a pitch/mod wheel. **Keybed mechanism changed generation to generation**: earliest units used a Pratt-Read-style spring keybed (feel closer to a Minimoog's), later 3620 units moved to a cheaper contact-wire ("guitar-wire"-style) mechanism — many players prefer the older keybed's feel. — Anthony Marinelli Music (feat. Ryan Hawkins & Nick Tomasi), [[Rare ARP 2600 Blue Marvin Discovery w Ryan Hawkins & Rob Rosen]]

- [ ] Mellotron — Streetly Electronics — arguably the first "sampler": each key triggers a pre-recorded tape loop of an individual instrument note (originally 3-track tape strips), giving a keyboard player access to real recorded flute/string/choir tone rather than a synthesized approximation. On "Strawberry Fields Forever," played individual flute-tape notes for the intro; on "The Continuing Story of Bungalow Bill" the same instrument instead played back one long pre-recorded tape loop of a Spanish guitar intro as a single sustained sample rather than note-by-note — the (still-unidentified) player of that original guitar recording is uncredited/unknown — David Bennett Music Theory, [[13 songs you didn't know sample other songs]]
- [ ] Fairlight CMI — Fairlight
- [ ] Synclavier — New England Digital — **also functioned as an early all-in-one DAW**: a sequencer with physical transport buttons (record/punch/start/stop/rewind, modeled on a 24-track tape machine, no command-line-style interface) doing sound design + sequencing + multitrack-style recording in one machine; scored the 1983 film *War Games* using all-FM-synthesis patches (bass, "ska guitar," doubled tracks, an icy transition texture, a live-playable delayed-vibrato effect sound), with its internal click driving a LinnDrum via a Dr. Click clock converter (48 ppqn) — same clock-sync lineage later demonstrated on a modern analog rig, see [[How I Create Dubstep on Analog Synths (without a computer)]]. Limited polyphony (32 voices for a long stretch) forced an arrange-while-building workflow — bounce to multitrack in stages, printing EQ/effects/reverb at bounce time rather than deferring to a mix pass — detailed further in [[Analog vs Digital Workflow Philosophy]] — Anthony Marinelli Music, [[War Games - The Sounds I Made For The Film]]. **Also used for the additive/wavetable pipe-organ layer** underneath the Prophet-5 + Jupiter-8 on Michael Jackson's Thriller album-opening chord: 3 voices, each a sine-wave octave stack (fundamental + up to 4 octaves, drawbar-organ logic but all-octaves rather than octaves+thirds+fifths) with light FM added for buzz and one voice detuned ~8 cents (438 Hz) for width — Anthony Marinelli Music, [[How I Programmed The Synth Opening On Michael Jackson's Thriller The Synclavier Pt.3]]. **Also carried one of four layers on the "Billie Jean" bass**: a 4-internal-component FM patch (a base component, a brighter/punchier component, an attack transient, and a fixed, mostly pitch-independent "digital hash" texture that behaves almost like a percussion element) — Anthony Marinelli (feat. Ernest Tibbs), [[MJ's Billie Jean Bass - It's 4 Instruments!]]. **"Beat It"'s intro is a recreated Synclavier factory preset**, originally an uncredited Danny Yeager composition/performance from a mail-order promotional demo record ("The Incredible Sounds of the Synclavier II") — the team recreated the exact preset and replayed the same 7-note phrase rather than sampling the demo directly; the preset's reverb tail is load-bearing to its identity (a short un-reverbed attack reads noticeably different/worse) — Anthony Marinelli Music (feat. Kevin), [[MJ's Beat It Intro - Real Sound & Story]]. **The "Smooth Criminal" bass**: a pitched-up stereo piano-hit sample layered with a monophonic sample of George Duke's own Minimoog (2+1 voices = 3 total, combined via the partial/timbre system). Demonstrates real-time self-sampling for iterative sound design (layer new partials onto an existing sample, e.g. add a kick to a snare sample, then re-sample the combined result as one new sound), a DIY chorus/flange trick (duplicate the sample + detune the copy), and real-time pitch/tempo transposition of a whole sampled sequence, printable back into the sequencer. Sequencer detail: originally 8-16 real-time tracks, expandable via 32 physical buttons assignable to up to 200 tracks, each named button/file recalling an entire designed timbre — Anthony Marinelli, [[1st Look At MJ's Smooth Criminal Bass]]. **Full "Beat It" preset anatomy** (reverse-engineered parameter-by-parameter, confirmed against a modern app emulation): 4-partial FM patch, near-3.5 (not integer) FM ratios per partial for the bell/gong character, partials paired an octave apart, monophonic (1-voice) polyphony, 8-bit resolution, portamento on one partial only, and "chorus" = a full extra copy of all partials pitched slightly higher — Anthony Marinelli (feat. Kevin Maloney), [[MJ's Beat It Intro Breakdown on Synclavier]]. **"We Are the World" bell intro**: FM bell patch built live in session per Quincy Jones' color-language direction ("silver bells, need golden") solved by tweaking FM ratio alone; recording deliberately stopped while the bell was still ringing to preserve its natural tail; this pre-polyphonic-sampling era (1984-85) meant the finished intro had to be physically copied/spliced onto the front of the already-mixed multitrack tape — Anthony Marinelli, [[MJ's We Are the World Synclavier Intro]]. **Hardware/design detail**: 32-voice FM/additive engine (24-harmonic sine-stack carrier + sine modulator per voice); New England Digital deliberately declined to add analog filters despite requests, offering only simple digital high-cut/high-mid switches — the resulting 8-bit aliasing/"hashy" buzz is now a prized, hard-to-reproduce character on modern clean digital synths; 128 KB floppies stored FM patches AND sequences (hard drives were sample-only); optional Winchester drives 5-20 MB before SCSI; MIDI (1-in/4-out 5-pin DIN, up to 8 outputs) added after the fact, on top of an already-DAW-like built-in sequencer; SMPTE generate/sync support added later — Anthony Marinelli (feat. Kevin Maloney), [[Synclavier Under the Hood Tour]]. **Resynthesis, keyboard hardware, and the modern product line**: frame-based resynthesis of samples (see [[Sampling and Sound Selection]] for the technique); the polyphonic-aftertouch keybed used modified pressure sensors originally built for a Sequential Prophet T8 (integrated by outside engineer Jeff Tripp — the same "adopt third-party hardware" pattern as the original's Able 60-sourced monitor/computer); random per-partial stereo panning; RAM cost ~$450-500/MB at the time (fully-loaded systems ran into the hundreds of thousands of dollars). Modern line: Arturia Synclavier V (Cameron Jones' own code, not a third-party emulation) → Synclavier Go (iPad) → Synclavier Regen (hardware desktop unit) — Regen is the first Synclavier product to add real analog-style filters, something deliberately never included on the original — Anthony Marinelli (feat. Kevin Maloney), [[Synclavier Product Specialist Kevin Maloney - Full Interview]]. **Scored the film *Stand By Me*** entirely in resynthesized FM (upright bass, flute, wine-glass instrument) — real-time, director-in-the-room workflow with instant transpose/solo/start-stop, no click track — Anthony Marinelli, [[Stand By Me - The Sounds I made For The Film]]
