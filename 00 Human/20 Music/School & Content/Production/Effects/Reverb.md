# Reverb

General best practice for reverb — space, depth, and creative sends. Specific tools: the [[ValhallaVintageVerb]] family, [[Reverb]] (Ableton), [[Ozone 9]] tools. See also [[Depth and Space]], [[Delay]].

## What it is
Putting sounds in a room — or in an impossible space — to set depth, glue, and mood.

## Owned (first-call)
[[ValhallaVintageVerb]] · [[ValhallaRoom]] · [[ValhallaPlate]] · [[ValhallaShimmer]] · [[Reverb]] (Ableton) · [[Hybrid Reverb]] (Ableton, convolution + algorithmic) · [[Spectral Blur]] (Ableton Creative Extensions, clear ambient freeze) · [[Ozone 9 Low End Focus]] (for tightening verb lows via EQ)

- **Driving a spring reverb hot/direct rather than through a level-controlled send is itself a stylistic choice — for a deliberately wetter, less "controlled" reverb blend than a typical mix-bus send.** On a synth-lead patch (ARP 2600, "Everybody Loves the Sunshine"), the dry filter/VCA output is sent straight into the reverb's input rather than at a trimmed send level, producing a much wetter tail than usual, then blended back against the straight signal at the output mixer. - **The design goal stated as directly as possible: a good vocal reverb/delay should be inaudible AS an effect — perceived only as natural space/width, not as an identifiable processed quality.** Jimmy Douglass, layering outboard hardware reverb/delay (an EMT/AMS "480"-class unit) with internal reverb/delay on a lead and background vocal: "if you don't really listen closely, you don't hear anything at all, it just sounds like space... a natural wideness on the vocal, and that's what we like." The practical test isn't "can I identify the reverb," it's "does the vocal read as wider/more three-dimensional without sounding processed." → Jimmy Douglass, [[Mixing Justin Timberlake Vocals - Jimmy Douglass]]
- **An 80s/Phil Collins-style reverb as a genre-signature vocal character, used deliberately even when it means the lead sits lower/more washed-out in the mix.** Kevin Parker (Tame Impala) cites this reverb style directly as an influence on "The Less I Know The Better"'s vocal treatment — identifies, in retrospect, that the vocal reverb reads more washed-out/mixed-low than he'd choose today, but that exact quality is part of what makes the track read as classic Tame Impala rather than a mixing miss to correct. A useful reminder that "too washed out" is genre-relative, not an absolute fault. → Kevin Parker, [[Behind the Track - 'The Less I Know The Better' by Tame Impala]]
- **Reverb parameter vocabulary worth knowing on any unit: diffusion, complexity, and frequency-dependent decay.** Diffusion controls whether the input signal or the early reflections feed the reverb TAIL (0% = tail generated from dry input, 100% = from early reflections) — a structural choice about what's actually reverberating. "Complexity"/delay-tap count trades realism for CPU — more taps = more nuanced, less artifact-prone, at real per-instance cost. Frequency-dependent decay (decay LENGTH that varies by band, distinct from simple high/low damping which only changes tonal balance) gives much finer control over how a tail resolves. Also: algorithmic vs. convolution isn't just cheap-vs-expensive — a convolution reverb can be built FROM an algorithmic hardware unit's own impulse responses (Liquid Sonics' Seventh Heaven, unowned, captures the algorithmic Bricasti M7 this way), and a reverb with its own multi-band tail-specific EQ can make a separate follow-up EQ unnecessary. → [[Plugin Wishlist]] — Sage Audio, [Free vs Cheap vs Expensive Reverb Plugins](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wkBSKgSDkhs)
- **Layer three DIFFERENT reverb types on one vocal, each with a distinct job, rather than one "vocal reverb" send doing everything.** (1) A very short room/ambient reverb purely to thicken the vocal, no obvious sense of space. (2) A medium-length reverb with dense reflections and a mid-range emphasis, specifically to blend the vocal into the rest of the mix. (3) A long, stylistic reverb to give the vocal its own character/identity, deliberately separated from the surrounding instrumentation. Three sends, three purposes (thickness / blend / identity). → [[1 Main Vocal Track]] — Sage Audio, [Vocal Masterclass](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dq-vQAGsd8w)
- **Pre-delay follows the standard BPM formula directly; decay time needs by-ear correction because reflection density distorts the relationship between the numeric ms value and how "in time" it actually sounds.** Pre-delay: same `60,000 / BPM` note-value math as [[Delay]]. Decay: the formula gets you close but a "2-second whole note" computed from it can read shorter than 2 seconds in a full mix if the algorithm front-loads reflections and thins toward the tail (dense early reflections mask each other, so the tail reads faster in context than in a solo listen). Correction moves once close: rebalance early-vs-late reflections while listening in the mix, or nudge decay time **±10–20 ms** until it locks with the track; a small EQ bump around **~3 kHz** on the reflections helps fight masking from competing mix content, and moving reflections to the less-busy side of the stereo image helps further. → [[Delay]], [[Masking and Carving]] — Sage Audio, [How to Time Every Audio Effect to the BPM](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MXz2hPL36PQ)

- **A convolution engine used as a literal shared virtual room for a whole ensemble — every source gets its own insert into the SAME impulse response, with pre-convolution EQ and independent reverb time per source, rather than one send everyone shares equally.** Dry, separately-tracked film-score instruments (same mic type, different rooms) read as merely "continuity," not a real shared space, until routed through Vienna Symphonic Library's MIR Pro: each section (violins, violas, harp, cello, bass, french horns) is its own "shell"/insert feeding the identical convolution IR, each with its own EQ **feeding into** the convolution (not after it) and its own independently adjustable reverb time — so every section gets a tuned corner of one shared room instead of one-size-fits-all decay. Minimal extra processing on top (a little low end on cello/bass, a "tiny bit" of compression on french horns) — nearly all the perceived quality comes from the shared space itself. - **A front-to-back "panner" built from early reflections alone — the reverb tail stays off entirely.** A reverb's three signal paths are dry (direct), early reflections, and the tail proper (demoed on Waves True Verb, unowned — see [[Plugin Wishlist]]); turn the **tail off** and rely only on the **early-reflections** balance to place a source psychoacoustically closer to or farther from the listener — "tricking your ears" into hearing the direct source as nearer or more distant purely from how present/timed the early reflections read, with zero added wash. Distinct from every wet/dry-mix depth trick already logged on this page — depth from early-reflection *balance*, not decay length or overall wetness. Owned-lane equivalent: [[Pro-R 2]] exposes early-reflection controls separately from its main decay; Ableton's [[Reverb]] separates the Early Reflections section from the Diffusion Network the same way. → [[Depth and Space]] — Jaycen Joshua (Mix with the Masters), [Mixing Summits with Jaycen Joshua](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L6xx7DWKzng)

- **Algorithmic reverb is delay taps + EQ filters whatever the room label says, and convolution reverb is just an impulse response you can record yourself.** Spring, plate, chamber, and room reverb labels are almost always algorithmic underneath — many delay taps at various levels, shaped with EQ filters, synthesizing reflections rather than sampling a real space. Convolution/IR reverb instead uses a transient recorded in a physical space, which encodes that room's size, frequency response (nodes/anti-nodes), and attack/decay times. **To build one:** record a clap or other sharp transient in the room you want to emulate (a phone works), edit the recording down to just the transient + tail, export as WAV, and load it into a convolution reverb — the room becomes a reusable reverb setting. **To time it:** `60,000 ÷ BPM` = one quarter note in ms; multiply/divide for other note values (×4 for a whole note) to set decay/RT60 in time with the track rather than by ear — the same formula [[Delay]] uses for delay time. → [[Hybrid Reverb]], [[Delay]] — Sage Audio, [Reverb Masterclass](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9G1ME30K2ro)
- **An optional, very subtle "collective" master reverb — 100% wet on its own aux, dialed in with the aux's channel fader, not the reverb's own dry/wet.** Create an aux track, place a good-sounding studio-emulation reverb on it with wet/dry left at 100%, then control how much comes through purely via the aux track's own fader. Explicitly mix-dependent, not a default: cohesive on some masters, but a room reverb applied heavily across everything can ruin a mix — the discipline is keeping it blended in *very* subtly. A companion mid-frequency aux (upward processing on isolated mids, see [[Parallel Processing]]) can also route its output into this same reverb aux, so recovered mid detail shares the same space as the rest of the mix instead of sitting apart from it. → [[Parallel Processing]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [How to Master a Song Start to Finish](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aUygMNRx2yE)
- **Algorithmic reverb IS built from delay — more taps read as a smoother "room," fewer taps read as an obviously delay-like, discrete echo** — algorithmic reverb plugins synthesize room reflections from many delay taps under the hood; "light"/simpler reverb engines use fewer taps, so each repeat reads more like an individual echo rather than a smooth wash. Reverb and delay can sound very similar with matched settings — the delay taps are the shared mechanism underneath both. → [[Delay]], [[Reverb]] (Ableton) — Sage Audio, [Delay Masterclass](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wjzowK7iurc)
- **Creative temporal mid/side: a harmonizer on a vocal's mid image paired with reverb on the side image, rather than one send at one level for the whole signal.** The general move — different delay/reverb type on the mid vs. the side, via the duplicate-channel mid/side routing trick — also demoed on drum overheads for distinct sides-only stereo imaging. → [[Delay]], [[Width and Stereo]] — Sage Audio, [Mid Side Masterclass](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SHxih1xc5yM)
- **Reverb's decay taps concentrate amplitude in whichever frequency range decays longest — a "just add space" move quietly EQs the signal.** A reverb with a longer decay rate in one frequency range (worked example: high mids, roughly **2–5 kHz**) needs more decay taps in that range to represent the longer tail; more taps means more amplification concentrated there, and if the reverb keeps re-triggering off the dry signal, those extra taps overlap and add to one another, amplifying the range further still. Net effect: a signal only intended to get "space" comes back with more energy in whatever band its reverb decays longest in — worth knowing before blaming a brightness problem on the dry source. → [[LEAD Slot]] (2.5–6 kHz), [[EQ]] — Sage Audio, [All Processing is Connected - Crucial Knowledge for Pro Mixes & Masters](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XV-0rdUrgHs)
- **Turn temporal effects (reverb, delay, modulation) down before sending a mix for mastering — mastering-stage limiting/compression lifts quiet reverb/delay tails more than the mix engineer heard.** Reverb reflections sit lower in amplitude than the dry signal almost always (unless gated), so any compression/limiting applied downstream lifts those quiet tails disproportionately along with everything else — the practical reason Sage Audio tells clients to pull temporal-effect levels down before mastering rather than leave them at mix-reference level. → [[Mix Handoff]], [[Compression]] — Sage Audio, [All Processing is Connected - Crucial Knowledge for Pro Mixes & Masters](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XV-0rdUrgHs)
- **The numeric target behind the bullet above, from a blind listening study (30 listeners): −9 loudness units below the dry signal is the most popular reverb/temporal-effect level.** Most listeners prefer some dry signal over a heavily reverberated one, and −9 LU relative to dry was this panel's sweet spot — a concrete anchor for "how much reverb is too much" beyond ear-only judgment. Worked mechanism for why mastering amplifies it: a range with good reverb but a peak:RMS ratio hotter than a crest-factor target (see [[Multiband Compression]]) needs peak compression to fix, then makeup gain to restore the original tonal balance — and that makeup gain lifts the same quiet reflections right along with everything else. No reliable way to reverse this from a finished stereo file, so the fix stays upstream at the mix. Demoed on a drum track: reverb added, then mastering-style compression + makeup gain — the reverb reads noticeably more audible with no change to the send itself. → [[Master Chain]], [[Clipping and Loudness]], [[Compression]] — Sage Audio, [Audio Mastering Science: Using Research to Make Informed Decisions](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=21dpmumO4So)
- **Reverb on a MASTER (not just a mix send) is a cohesion tool, used to nudge a mix back toward the middle of a "disjointed ↔ blurred" spectrum — never when the mix is already too blurred.** If every element in a mix has a distinct decay rate/reflection set (recorded in different rooms/DI'd differently), the record sounds disjointed; if everything shares nearly identical decay/reflections, it blurs together and instruments get hard to distinguish. Master-bus reverb is only for pulling a too-disjointed mix toward the cohesive middle — reaching for it on a mix that's already too blurred only makes the masking worse. Same underlying job as bus compression (uniform dynamics) but on the space/ADSR axis instead. Needs the most realistic/transparent reverb available (a convolution IR reverb, not a colored/stylistic one) since it's touching 100% of the signal. **Chain position: after any upward compression, before the peak limiter** — dial the reverb in with the limiter engaged (its gain makeup exposes reverb tails that seemed subtle unlimited) and check both loud and sparse sections, automating the reverb send over time if no single static level works for both. → [[Master Chain]], [[Limiting]], [[Automation]] — Sage Audio, [Reverb on a Master: Everything You Need to Know](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vzJSKU7kGrU)
- **On a mastering-stage reverb, the EQ section targets COHESION per frequency band, not tone — and controllable early/mid/late reflections fill specific depth gaps.** Reframe: a reverb's built-in EQ isn't there to shape the reverb's color here, it's there to raise or lower *perceived cohesion* independently per range — e.g. lows already sound cohesive (leave alone) but mids ~1.5 kHz and highs ~6 kHz sound disjointed (as if recorded in different spaces) → boost the reverb's reflections specifically in those bands. Separately, split early/mid-distance/late reflections matter as much as the EQ: a mix with only dry + a late tail (masked until it emerges) reads as fake/flat depth — add early *and* mid reflections to bridge the gap; a mix with early+late but no mid-distance layer has the same gap in miniature — add mid-distance reflections, EQ'd to roughly match the mix's implied reverb character. Keep it subtle either way — "a little goes a long way," same discipline as 1–2 dB of glue compression. → [[EQ]], [[Depth and Space]], [[SUB Slot]], [[BASS Slot]], [[MIDS Slot]], [[AIR Slot]], [[ValhallaRoom]], [[Hybrid Reverb]] — Sage Audio, [Reverb on a Master: Everything You Need to Know](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vzJSKU7kGrU)
- **Reverb as a rhythmic tool, not just a space tool — two moves:** (1) put a **tremolo AFTER a reverb return** (not on the dry signal) set to 16th notes, so the reverb tail itself gets chopped into a rhythmic pulse instead of just decaying smoothly; (2) push a clap's reverb **pre-delay** far enough that the reverb's onset itself lands on a chosen rhythmic subdivision, turning pre-delay into an audible groove element rather than a subtle spacing parameter.
