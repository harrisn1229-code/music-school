# Guitar Theory! - Demystifying Compound Intervals vs Chord Extensions

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pCMKDxzxuew)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pCMKDxzxuew)

## Synopsis

A short, no-exercises lecture pulled from Jared's paid course ("Chords on Command") that nails down one specific, easy-to-fumble piece of chord-spelling theory: **when does a chord tone above 7 get called an extension (9/11/13), and when does it just keep its plain scale-degree name (2/4/6)?** The video directly answers a question that's easy to get wrong by pattern-matching ("9 = 2 an octave up") — that shortcut is only sometimes true. Jared's real point is that extension labels (9/11/13) are a *chord-tone-role* label, not an *interval-distance* label: a chord tone is only called an extension if it's sitting on top of a **complete lower structure** (a full triad 1-3-5, or a full seventh chord 1-3-5-7). If the corresponding lower-numbered tone is *missing*, the "extra" note reverts to its plain 2/4/6 name regardless of how far it's voiced from the root. Two worked examples anchor it: a Cmaj7♯11 (the ♯11 is a half-step above the 4, but is never called ♯4, because it's sitting on a complete 7th chord) and a Cadd9 vs. Csus2 (same 1-2-5-ish shape — call it add9 with the 3 present, sus2 without it). No exercises, no fretboard diagrams beyond the two chord examples — it's a pure naming-logic lesson meant to unblock the chord-spelling material that follows it in his course.

## What's taught

**Core distinction: chord-tone extension labels vs. compound-interval labels are two different naming systems for the same physical note.**
- A **compound interval** name (10th, 12th, 13th) describes literal distance from the root — always more than an octave, always the "add 7" rule (3rd → 10th, 5th → 12th, 6th → 13th interval).
- A **chord-tone extension** name (9/11/13, or ♯11 etc.) describes the note's *functional role* in the chord and does **not** require it to be more than an octave from the root — it can be voiced right next to the root and still be called a 9, 11, or 13 (or below the root — same label either way).
- Core chord tones (1, 3, 5, 7) *never* get renamed even when voiced far from the root — a 3rd voiced an octave-plus-up (a literal "10th" interval) is still just called "the 3," never "the 10." Likewise a 5th voiced a 12th up is still "the 5," not "the 12" — there is no such thing as a chord tone called 12.

**The governing rule (this is the actual "rules" the video promises):**
- If there is a **complete triad (1-3-5)** or **complete seventh chord (1-3-5-7)** present, any additional scale tone (2, 4, or 6) added on top is labeled as an **extension**: 2→**9**, 4→**11**, 6→**13**.
- If the corresponding lower tone is **missing**, the added note keeps its **plain scale-degree name** instead:
  - **3 missing** → an added 2 stays **2** (not 9); an added 4 stays **4** (not 11).
  - **3 present** → an added 2 becomes **9**; an added 4 becomes **11**.
  - **7 missing** → an added 6 stays **6** (not 13) — it "takes the role" of the 7.
  - **7 present** → an added 6 becomes **13**.
- Worked example 1 — **Cmaj7♯11** (1-3-5-7-♯11): the ♯11 sits a half-step above the plain 4th (which by ear-counting off the major scale would read as "♯4"), but because it's stacked on a *complete* seventh chord, it's named ♯11, never ♯4.
- Worked example 2 — **Cadd9** (1-3-5-9) vs. **Csus2** (1-2-5): identical near-identical shape/pitch-class content, different label purely because the 3rd is present (→ 9, "add9") or absent (→ 2, "sus2"). This is the clean illustration of "same note, different name, depending on what else is in the chord" — not a coincidence, it's the rule in action.
- Terminology aside: a "tenth" as a plain interval term literally means "a compound 3rd" (3rd + octave) — nothing to do with whether that note functions as a chord-tone extension. The C-to-E interval in Cadd9 is a tenth by distance, but the E is still just "the 3" as a chord tone.

**Why this matters practically (Jared's framing):** these are the rules underlying correct chord *spelling* — get them wrong and a voicing gets mislabeled (e.g., you'd call something a "9 chord" when it's actually a sus2, or write ♯4 when the correct jazz/chord-chart convention is ♯11). No exercises in this video — it's pure naming-logic prerequisite for chord-spelling practice that follows later in his course.
