# Paul Epworth opens up the session for Adele's "Rolling In The Deep"

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4WMVIlxNd18)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4WMVIlxNd18)

## Synopsis

A short Mix with the Masters session walkthrough: Paul Epworth (writer/producer of Adele's "Rolling in the Deep," and separately its co-writer) opens his Pro Tools session for the final East Coast Studios recording and narrates, section by section, what got re-tracked versus kept from the original Logic demo. The throughline is a **deliberate hybrid of demo and re-record** — re-tracking drums, piano, and acoustic guitar for a better performance/sound while consciously preserving specific demo elements (the stomp/clap layer, the MIDI piano-bass part) that already worked, rather than treating "re-record" as an all-or-nothing decision. Three concrete techniques stand out as reusable beyond this one song: a **physical second kick drum** (a 28" marching band bass drum) set up in front of the main kit kick and damped with blankets for character, blended with a subtle sample-replacement layer via a Logic drum-replacement plug-in; a **binaural head** used twice for very different jobs — capturing the whole kit's stereo image, and later switching the acoustic guitar to a noticeably wider recording specifically at the pre-chorus as a structural width-automation move; and a **clap reverb send with its pre-delay used as a groove tool**, deliberately pushed to add "swing and timing" to the rhythm track rather than just space. A companion piano mic'ing habit (a pair of small-diaphragm condensers, "K86s," strapped directly to the piano's soundboard — attributed to Abbey Road) and an offhand, hedged mention of a hardware stereo bus compressor ("may well have been a 1178") round out the tracking-stage detail. This is a companion piece to [[Tom Elmhirst breaks down his mix of 'Rolling in the Deep' by Adele]] — Elmhirst's video covers the mix stage of the same record; this one covers the tracking stage that fed it. The captions cut off mid-sentence at the very end (Epworth mid-thought about the acoustic guitar performance) — everything captions provided is captured below.

## Techniques

**Drums**
- **Re-tracked with the same kit as the demo (Ludwig Super Classic) for continuity, played by a live drummer (Leo Taylor), completely unquantized.** Epworth's explicit reasoning: keep timing variation and get the best live performance rather than "get into editing" — stated as a deliberate choice for "rawness and authenticity," not a compromise. → [[Pocket and Microtiming]]
- **A second, physically distinct kick drum layered in front of the main kit kick for character: a 28" marching band bass drum, set up in front of the Ludwig kit kick and damped with blankets over the top.** Described as adding "something to the vibe" — a live-tracking-stage equivalent of the digital layered-kick pattern this vault already documents (see [[1 Drums Kick Track|Drums Kick Track]] §2 "layer two"), done with two physical drums and mic'ing/damping instead of two samples. → [[1 Drums Kick Track|Drums Kick Track]], [[Kick Design]], [[Layering and Stacking]], [[Drum Mic Technique]]
- **Kick mic'd with an NS10 studio-monitor driver on the front head, paired with a D12 (a classic kick mic).** The driver-as-mic trick (a passive speaker driver used in reverse as a contact/proximity mic) is credited with the kick's "quite bright," "loose slappiness" character on top of the D12's low/subby foundation — two mics on one drumhead for two different tonal contributions rather than one mic doing everything. → [[Drum Mic Technique]], [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **Subtle sample reinforcement via a Logic drum-replacement plug-in — a "nice tight kick" layered under the live kick specifically because the raw kick was "low," "subby."** Epworth names the tool as (per the captions) "app trigger," almost certainly **Steven Slate Trigger** (a Logic-era drum-replacement/sample-trigger plugin, drag-and-drop workflow) — the plugin name itself is caption-garbled and not fully certain, but the described workflow (drag-and-drop sample under a mic'd live pass, either to sound-replace a bad recording or to add drum-machine grooves under a real kit) is unambiguous and matches Trigger's documented function. Framed as something that "helped move production ideas forward very quickly" at the time (Epworth dates the habit to "five, six years ago" relative to this video). *(Plugin name uncertain — caption garbling; the technique itself is transcript-clear.)* → [[Plugin Wishlist]], [[Sound Selection Guide]], [[Layering and Stacking]]
- **A binaural head used to capture the kit's overall stereo image** — rather than (or in addition to) a standard overhead/room pair, a binaural head mic captured "the overall sound of the kit," described as giving "this sort of stereo image of the kit" when you listen to the kit as a whole. → [[Drum Mic Technique]], [[Width and Stereo]]
- **The demo's stomp and clap layer was kept in the final record rather than re-recorded** — visible in the session as tracks carried straight over from the Logic demo; additional claps and a re-recorded tambourine were added at East Coast on top of the kept demo layer, rather than replacing it wholesale. *(Song-specific arrangement history — kept on this page.)*
- **Clap reverb send's pre-delay used deliberately as a groove/timing tool, not just for space.** Epworth opens the clap reverb to show how the pre-delay is "used to try and give a little bit of swing and timing to the feel of the rhythm track" — the pre-delay amount is treated as a rhythmic parameter (pushing the clap's perceived placement) as much as a spatial one. No exact pre-delay value given (qualitative). → [[Reverb Send Room]], [[1 Drums Snare-Clap Track|Drums Snare-Clap Track]], [[Pocket and Microtiming]]

**Bass**
- **The demo bass was kept as-is in the final record** — bounced audio out of Logic, not re-recorded. Specifically the low end is a MIDI piano-bass part played from Logic's stock EXS24 sampler, kept over a later live piano-bass attempt tracked at East Coast **because it felt tighter and more consistent** than the live take. A clear real-world case of "the programmed part wins over the re-recorded one" when consistency matters more than performance nuance for a foundational part. *(Song-specific decision — kept on this page; the general judgment behind it is common ground already implicit in this vault's chain notes.)*

**Piano**
- **A newly-tracked piano part praised specifically for its left-hand counter-rhythm, which the producer credits with making the track feel faster than its actual tempo.** Epworth: the pianist's left-hand counter-part "helps to make the track feel a bit faster than it is" — "these little discrete rhythms" are framed as a direct contributor to the track's felt energy/groove, independent of the actual BPM. A reusable perceived-tempo device: a busy, syncopated counter-rhythm under a slower part reads as more urgent than the tempo alone would suggest. → [[Pocket and Microtiming]], [[Rhythm and Groove]]
- **Piano tracked with a pair of small-diaphragm condensers ("K86s") strapped directly to the piano's soundboard, left in place permanently.** Attributed to a technique "we discovered that Abbey Road like to do." Praised specifically for its flexibility: because the mics are on the soundboard rather than floating over open strings, the lid can be opened or closed at will — open for isolation/character, closed if another instrument needs tracking in the room at the same time — without having to re-rig the mics either way. → [[1 Acoustic Piano Track|Acoustic Piano Track]]
- **A hedged, unconfirmed mention of a hardware stereo bus compressor across the piano signal path: "probably the best stereo compressor available in the studio at the time... may well have been a 1178 or something."** Not a confirmed setting or even a confirmed unit — Epworth is speculating from memory, not reading off the session. Best-match owned equivalent for a fast-FET stereo bus compressor in this role: [[UAD UA 1176LN]] (the 1178 was UREI's dual-1176-in-one-chassis stereo unit). *(Speculative/qualitative — no settings, and the unit itself isn't confirmed.)* → [[UAD UA 1176LN]], [[1 Acoustic Piano Track|Acoustic Piano Track]], [[Vintage Gear Worklist]]

**Acoustic guitar**
- **A structural width-automation move: the original mono/narrower demo acoustic guitar runs from the top of the song through the first pre-chorus, then the recording switches to a newly re-tracked, deliberately WIDER acoustic guitar performance from the pre-chorus onward.** Framed explicitly as an intentional "change of width of image" tied to song structure — thinner/narrower through the verse, widening exactly at the pre-chorus arrival — rather than one fixed acoustic-guitar width for the whole song. → [[Width and Stereo]], [[1 Acoustic Guitar Track|Acoustic Guitar Track]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **The wider re-recorded guitar performance uses a binaural head again, specifically for the wider image** — a second, distinct use of a binaural head in this same session (the first being the drum kit, above). Epworth demonstrates the width source by muting the close mic: with the close mic muted, "it scoops a lot of room-y-ness out" — confirming the binaural signal is carrying most of the perceived width/room character, with the close mic filling in detail. → [[Width and Stereo]], [[1 Acoustic Guitar Track|Acoustic Guitar Track]]
- **Personal, song-specific aside:** Epworth calls the acoustic guitar part difficult to play for a non-guitarist producer ("I'm not a great guitarist... the rhythm of it has to be so specific"), took a while to get, and isn't fully convinced the takes are mistake-free — framed as possibly part of the record's charm.
