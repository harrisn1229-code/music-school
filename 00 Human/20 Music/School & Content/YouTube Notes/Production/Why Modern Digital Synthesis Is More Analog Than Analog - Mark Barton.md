# "Why Modern Digital Synthesis Is More Analog Than Analog" - Mark Barton

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kk-3vXOAtVo)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kk-3vXOAtVo)

## Synopsis

A genuine conversation, not a demo — Anthony Marinelli talks with Mark Barton (co-designer of the Synthesis Technology Zeroscillator, a former Apple speech-synthesis researcher, now a Cherry Audio third-party developer) about his paper of the same title. Barton's technical signal-to-noise argument for digital's superiority is real, but the conversation's real value is Marinelli's pushback on what that argument does and doesn't settle for actual music-making — captured in full as a new technique note.

## Techniques

- **The technical claim**: 64-bit floating-point digital audio (53 bits of accuracy) exceeds analog electronics' ~100 dB signal-to-noise ceiling by hundreds of dB; a DAC's reconstruction filter mathematically reconnects digital's discrete steps into a genuinely continuous waveform, so the "digital is stepped, analog is continuous" objection doesn't hold once the full chain is considered.
- **The blind-test development standard**: Cherry Audio ships an emulation only once it passes a genuine blind A/B against the original — proven necessary by a real case where listeners who believed they preferred a vintage Synclavier over its modern "Regen" reissue turned out to be picking at chance (50/50) once they didn't know which was playing.
- **What the technical argument doesn't settle**: listener indifference to real-vs-virtual knobs doesn't mean performer experience doesn't matter — no synthesizer interface (analog or digital) yet matches how a fine acoustic instrument lets a skilled player avoid "fighting" it for expression.
- **A synthesizer being defined by its limitations, reframed as a virtue, not a deficiency** — more available options isn't automatically better music; "give me one good filter and one good knob, I don't need five."
- **Deliberately tapping an analog system's natural unpredictability (drift, overdrive quirks, subtle inconsistency between "identical" settings) as an ongoing creative resource** — genuinely distinct from digital, which has to have unpredictability deliberately programmed in rather than emerging from imperfect components.
- **Excess choice as a real creative cost independent of the analog/digital question** — with near-unlimited plugin/preset options now available, actively narrowing your own choices is still a necessary discipline to finish a piece.
- **The circuit-swap thought experiment**: if a beloved instrument's hardware were secretly replaced with a digital emulation behind an identical panel, would you feel different once you found out? An honest "not really" is a real signal about what was actually doing the emotional work — sound, or something else (familiarity, ritual, ownership).
- **Barton's own decision process for selling all his analog gear** (including a Moog Modular System 3C with real provenance — formerly owned by Mort Garson, later passed to Marinelli): a literal pros/cons list where analog's column (mainly familiarity/fun-factor/panel feel) stayed short while digital's column (repeatability, preset recall, unlimited module instances, expanded sonic palette, sound quality) "went off the end of the page."
