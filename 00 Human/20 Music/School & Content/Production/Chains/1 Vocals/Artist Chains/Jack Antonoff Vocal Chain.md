# Jack Antonoff Vocal Chain

**Reference/vibe:** not one fixed signal chain but a philosophy of matching mic + processing to the emotional read a specific song needs, then deliberately breaking his own rules when it serves the song. Three distinct approaches across three songs on the same album: a bone-dry room-mic'd band take, a deliberately mistuned/broken vocoder hook, and a fully tuned lead vocal (the only one on the record) used specifically because the artifact reads as "a soul stuck in a machine."

**Signal source:** Shure 50-series dynamic ("singing on a 50") on *I'm Not Joking* for maximum transient/proximity feel; Shure SM58 on *We Should Talk*; unspecified on *Dancing* (recorded twice as two separate full takes).

## Chain overview — three songs, three deliberately different vocal treatments

### 1. "I'm Not Joking" — dry, room-mic'd, zero-plugin band take

- **Mic:** a Shure 50-series dynamic, chosen specifically over a condenser (47) or an SM58 for how close/live it feels — "I want to feel like it is live... you lose that transient with [a booth mic]." Deliberately not isolated in a booth; recorded live with the band.
- **Processing:** none. Antonoff turns off every plugin in the session mid-demo to prove the vocal (and the whole band take) sounds no different — the point of the song is that almost nothing is processed.
- **Backup layer:** a hard-panned "choir of a man talking to himself in the mirror" — multiple takes of himself doing different character voices/registers, layered as a gang-vocal texture rather than a normal harmony stack. → [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]]
- **Contrast technique (described, NOT used on this song — a "what not to do" demo):** his normal horn-doubling move — double the horn track, offset it (ideally on tape), pan hard, add a chorus/doubler on one side, ride automation into the bus. Real technique, just deliberately withheld here to keep the take pure. → [[Layering and Stacking]]

### 2. "We Should Talk" — tight machine grid + loose human "shitstorm," mistuned vocoder hook

- **Foundation:** the **Korg M1**'s (unowned — see [[Vintage Gear Worklist]]) built-in drum sounds with their stock early-90s reverb kept as-is (unashamedly, for the "Shania Twain"-era character) as the rhythmic grid, plus a static/unmoving ARP synth note held for tension underneath.
- **Mic:** Shure SM58, tracked live in the room over the grid.
- **Hook processing — TC Helicon, deliberately mistuned:** the "We should talk" hook vocal is doubled through a TC Helicon **left on the wrong pitch/tuning setting on purpose** — "wasn't even set to the right tuning but it's cool." A broken-preset-as-texture move, not a mistake he fixed.
- **Loose instrumental layer ("personality bits"):** a trace (3-string Cuban instrument, "12-string mandolin world"), doubled with a baritone 6-string played high plus a second baritone doing a rhythmic pattern, a direct-DI'd Ovation acoustic-electric (praised for a Lindsey Buckingham-style tone), banjo, and Moog Model D noise — all played slightly loose/"sloppy," explicitly to remind the listener "we're not living on this grid" against the tight M1/ARP foundation. → [[Layering and Stacking]], [[Rhythm and Groove]]
- **Space Echo played as a pitched instrument:** turn the unit's echo return up loud enough to self-oscillate/feed back, then manually ride the delay-time knob live to tune the resulting drone to a note that's "not really in key but hovering around it" — a real, repeatable technique (self-oscillating tape-echo feedback, pitch-tracked by ear) distinct from using the unit as a normal echo. → **Roland Space Echo RE-201** (unowned — see [[Vintage Gear Worklist]])
- **The one tuned vocal on the whole album:** normally records a tuned scratch vocal then re-tracks it untuned for the final — on this song he kept the Auto-Tune specifically because the correction artifact read as "a soul stuck in a machine," matching the lyric's theme of being trapped in forward motion. → [[Vocal Tuning]]
- **General vocal-processing philosophy stated here:** TC Helicon / Korg VC-10 vocoder / Auto-Tune are treated as three different flavors of the same idea — a voice audibly processed/"digital and cold," used as an emotional device (someone "stuck" or "trapped") rather than for correction or polish.

### 3. "Dancing" — two full takes layered on top of each other

- **The core trick:** recorded the ENTIRE song twice as two independent, complete performances, then played both simultaneously as the final mix — not doubling one take, but layering two different full recordings. Credits Elliott Smith's habit of re-performing (not just doubling) guitar and vocal as the direct inspiration, taken one step further.
  - **V1** — minimal: one detuned acoustic guitar + one doubled vocal take, recorded at Church Studios, London.
  - **V2** — ornate: harpsichord, clavichord, an E-mu sampler and an unidentified Korg synth both holding sustained "stagnant" pad notes, and heavily tremolo'd electric guitars — a dense wash described as "a kaleidoscope of memory."
- **Manual fader-style automation:** rides V1 and V2 in and out of each other by hand in Pro Tools, explicitly likening it to riding a physical console fader rather than drawing automation curves — "manual automation isn't super common on Pro Tools."
- **Session color convention (his own, non-standard):** bass = red, main lead vocal = blue (other vocal/double takes = other shades of blue), guitars = green, ear-candy/texture tracks = yellow, older/alternate vocal takes = orange-brown — a personal navigation system for high-track-count sessions.
- **String part as a single unprocessed take:** wrote a "stagnant" string-style part (cello + plucked-violin idea) and played/recorded it himself in one take rather than sending it out for real strings, then drowned it in reverb — the pitchiness and imperfection of the single take is treated as a feature ("blips of memory"), not corrected. → [[Reverb]]

## Recurring, album-wide device (not song-specific)

**Harpsichord + clavichord as a deliberate "forgotten instrument" character**, used three ways across the record: straight (as a harpsichord), as a big stacked reverb-drenched dreamscape texture, and as a direct substitute wherever he'd normally reach for celeste/glockenspiel/tubular bells — chosen specifically because the sound has fallen out of use and reads as fresh again. The clavichord's pitch bends under harder playing, giving it a percussive, slightly "wild"/out-of-tune quality he leans into rather than avoids. Named (alongside the baritone guitar) as one of the two defining textural "heroes" of the whole album. → [[Sound Selection Guide]]

## Mixing/production philosophy (applies across the whole record, not one song)

- **Judge a mix outside the room.** Always leaves the studio and listens walking/driving before calling a mix done — the test is whether it reads as "just a song, in its best outfit" rather than "a bunch of interviewed parts."
- **Vocals can be deliberately UN-glued from the track.** Rather than always matching reverb/frequency content to blend the vocal into the instrumental (his usual approach), sometimes leaves the vocal sonically separate on purpose for a "hear the overdubness" aesthetic, citing later Lou Reed/Beatles records as reference. → [[Vocal Mic Technique]]
- **When a big vocal stack hits a wall, mute — don't add.** When five-to-ten-track harmony/double stacks stop cutting through, muting roughly half the tracks (rather than EQ'ing or compressing harder) makes the remainder read as bigger, because each surviving part gets more space. → [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]]
- **Seek out unfamiliar gear on purpose.** States his best takes come from playing synths/instruments he doesn't know well — deliberately avoids "tasteful" playing on gear he's mastered, because familiarity produces safer, more generic results ("golden age" synth recordings existed because nobody knew the "right" way to play them yet).
- **Happy accidents are real but not the whole story.** Distrusts studio lore that reduces every good idea to a single lucky accident, but agrees the actual mechanism is putting yourself in a position (live band, unfamiliar gear, deliberate imperfection) where accidents can happen — not a passive "it just happened."
