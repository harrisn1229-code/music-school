> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **The form — it tells you how much of the chart is actually new material**
> An AABA tune with an 8-bar A and an 8-bar bridge is often only 16 bars of distinct harmony once the repeats are accounted for, not 32.
>
> **Q2.** **AABA and ABAC — a repeat sign after 8 bars with two endings signals AABA**
> You can read the shape mechanically off the page even when the book does not spell it out. A tag appended to the last A is common and worth spotting early.
>
> **Q3.** **The last two bars of each A are a turnaround roughly 90% of the time**
> The condition is that the tune is AABA and its first chord is the tonic. It solves a chunk of the chart by pattern-matching before any of it is analysed.
>
> **Q4.** **Chunking a passage into one nameable idea instead of several chord symbols**
> *Also accepted:* a secondary ii-V into IV reduces to "the IV chord" · a four-bar reset reduces to "a turnaround"
> The real payoff is recognising the same device in the next tune, so a new standard arrives half-analysed.
>
> **Q5.** **Substitute something that fits — and judge it against the melody, not the page**
> Overriding a stock harmonisation is normal practice among players who know the tune, especially in printed tags, not a liberty needing permission.
>
> **Q6.** **A scale degree of the chord sounding right now — not of the parent key**
> Reading it against the key hides the relationship that tells you which shape fits; reading it against the current chord reveals it.
>
> **Q7.** **The 5 of C, then the 4 of D**
> One pitch — G — renumbered by the chord under it: the 5th of C, then the 4th of D. Same pitch, different functional meaning, different go-to shape, one beat apart.
>
> **Q8.** **By which chord tone the melody note is and which string it lands on**
> That is the actual lookup mid-arrangement — "the melody note is the 4th and it is on string 2" — rather than an index by chord type. Keep a 3-note and a 4-note version per slot plus a 2-note fallback.
>
> **Q9.** **Do not harmonise every note — it is physically easier and it sounds better**
> Fewer full-hand jumps because you often land the melody note alone with one finger, and the space between chord grabs reads as phrasing instead of a wall of block chords.
>
> **Q10.** **The 3rd and the ♭7 — the 3rd fixes major or minor, the ♭7 fixes dominant or major 7**
> Between them they determine the chord's quality, which is why the root and 5th can go to the bass player.
>
> **Q11.** **The identical shape slides down a half step for the IV and up a half step for the V**
> One movable grip covers the whole form: A7, down a half step to D7, back up, up a half step to E7.
>
> **Q12.** **Two notes are the smallest thing that still states a chord, and everything larger is built on them**
> *Rubric (5):* says the 3rd and ♭7 are the minimum that fixes a chord's quality, so everything else is optional (2) · connects it to the drop-priority rule from Lesson 1 (1.5) · connects it to a second earlier idea, such as register and staying out of a vocal's way, or one movable shape across a form (1.5)
> The diad is where the course's central rule bottoms out. Lesson 1 gives the priority order — guide tones, then the extension, then the root, then the 5th — and the diad is simply that order followed all the way down until nothing optional is left. Everything richer is this pair with colour added: a shell voicing is the diad plus the root, a rootless 9th or 13th is the diad plus an extension. It also answers the arrangement problem from the same lesson, because two notes leave room under a vocal where a barre chord does not, and it demonstrates the Lesson 2 economy of one movable shape relocated across a form rather than a grip memorised per chord. And it is the same reduction the chord-melody method depends on: knowing which notes are load-bearing is what makes it safe to leave the rest out.
