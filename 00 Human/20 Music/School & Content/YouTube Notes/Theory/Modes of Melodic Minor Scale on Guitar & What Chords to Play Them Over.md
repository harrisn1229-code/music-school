## What's taught

**Core framing (mode definition):** a mode = take any scale, pick any non-root note, and treat *that* note as the new home base/root — same notes, different tonal center, different flavor. Melodic minor has 7 notes, so it has 7 modes, mode *n* = start on the *n*th degree of the parent scale. Learn the **physical shape of the parent scale** first (melodic minor here) — modes come "for free" once you know where each degree sits in that one shape; you don't need seven separate fingerings.

**Mode 1 — Melodic minor itself (i chord).** Play the shape straight from the root. Works over two chord types: **minor-major 7 (mΔ7)** — "very dissonant, mysterious" — and **minor 6 (m6)**, a minor triad with a major 6th added. → matches [[Scale Modes Reference]] Melodic Minor table row **i — mΔ, Aeolian nat.6/nat.7**.

**Mode 2 — Dorian ♭2 (ii chord).** Start on the melodic minor scale's 2nd degree. Described as "Dorian is the 2nd mode of major, add a ♭2 to it." Played over **minor 7♭9** — flagged as an unusual/rare chord he doesn't reach for often, but still a real target. → matches page's row **ii — m7, Dorian ♭2** (this video adds the specific ♭9 extension).

**Mode 3 — Lydian augmented / Lydian ♯5 (♭III chord).** Start on the 3rd degree. "Lydian is the 4th mode of major, augmented = ♯5." Played over **major 7♯5 (Δ♯5)** — noted as rare, showing up in "more modern jazz" writing. → matches page's row **♭III — Δ♯5, Ionian ♯5** — same chord/scale, video names it from its own-root Lydian-family perspective ("Lydian augmented") rather than the page's parent-tonic-anchor name ("Ionian ♯5"); both describe the identical seven notes.

**Mode 4 — Lydian dominant (IV chord).** Start on the 4th degree. "Lydian = major scale with ♯4; dominant/Mixolydian = major scale with ♭7 — combine them." Played over **dominant 7♯11** (example given: F7♯11, reasoned as "F dominant 7 with a natural B-natural" i.e. the ♯11 color tone). Practice tip given directly: **outline the plain dominant-7 chord tones first as a skeleton, then add the mode's color notes around it** — "always a great way to map things out." Explicitly flagged as **the most useful of the seven** — "I use this more than the others because dominant 7♯11 is a type of chord that comes up more often than those other examples." → matches page's row **IV — 7, Lydian ♭7** (same scale, page's "Lydian ♭7" = this video's "Lydian dominant," the standard jazz name); this bullet is a second, independent confirmation of the page's existing "Lydian dominant is the most-used melodic-minor mode in practice" note (already logged from an earlier same-channel video, "Soloing, Theory, Fingerstyle, & More - My 10 Favorite Lessons of 2023").

**Mode 5 — Mixolydian ♭6 (V chord).** Start on the 5th degree. "Mixolydian (major scale with ♭7) plus a ♭6." Played over **dominant 7 with a natural 9 and a ♭13** (example: G7 / G9♭13). → matches page's row **V — 7, Mixolydian ♭6**.

**Mode 6 — Locrian natural 2 (vi chord).** Start on the 6th degree ("6th chord of melodic minor is half-diminished"). Played over **half-diminished with a natural 9 added** — flagged as unusual since a plain half-diminished chord doesn't normally get a natural 9. Anecdote: Jared heard **Pat Metheny**, in a workshop, stop a take mid-recording to tell a bandmate to specifically add the natural 9 on top of a half-diminished chord — cited as "obviously an artistic vision... and also heavy theory stuff." Noted as a mode Jared himself doesn't reach for often but likes the sound of. → matches page's row **vi — ø, Locrian nat.2**.

**Mode 7 — Altered scale / super Locrian (VII chord).** Start on the 7th degree. Called "probably the most widely used and referenced mode of melodic minor." Played over **altered dominant chords** — any dominant marked "alt," or with ♯5, ♭5, ♯9, or ♭9 — or more loosely over *any* dominant chord for extra tension. Demo chord: **B7♯9♭5** (altered B7). → matches page's row **VII — 7, Locrian ♭4/♭♭7** (page's parent-anchor name for the same scale jazz players call "altered"/"super Locrian").

**Practice philosophy (stated at the end, general advice not specific to melodic minor):** improvise with **chord tones ~90% of the time**, layering scales/modes/chromatic notes around that skeleton rather than defaulting to running scales — matches the "outline chord tones first, then add mode color" practice note given inline at Mode 4, and echoes the same chord-tone-first discipline already logged elsewhere on [[Scale Modes Reference]] (Jack Ruch's altered-scale-resolution and half-whole-diminished bullets).
