# Ghost Notes (Slap Bass)

BassBuzz's level-3 addition to the 5-level slap exercise (see [[Slap Bass 5-Level Daily Drill]]): muted, unfretted slaps and pops used deliberately, both for percussive texture and — the more important argument in the video — as a rhythmic anchor that helps the *rest* of the line stay locked in time.

## What a ghost note is, mechanically

A ghost note is a **muted slap or pop** — the fretting hand covers the string(s) being struck **without fretting an actual note**, so the strike produces a percussive "thwacky" sound instead of a pitch. Marked as `X` in tab/notation. The strike itself is the exact same physical motion as an un-muted slap that misses its mute — the only difference from a mistake is that it's now deliberate and placed on purpose.

## Why it matters beyond texture: a rhythmic anchor

The video's real case for ghost notes isn't just "it sounds cool" — it's timekeeping. The analogy given: **it's easier to stay locked to a metronome that clicks frequently than one with big gaps between clicks.** Ghost notes work the same way for a slap line — keeping the thumb moving in a semi-regular pulse (even on strikes that don't sound a pitch) gives the ear and the hand a denser rhythmic grid to lock against than sparse "real" notes alone would provide.

- **Not a rule to always fill every gap** — sometimes a real musical phrase needs the space, and the video is explicit that you shouldn't ghost mechanically over every rest.
- **But even pros lean on it as a default habit.** Reference example: **Marcus Miller** ("one of the ancient Greek gods of slap") — watch his thumb on a groove and it's bouncing on almost every eighth note in the bar, whether or not each strike sounds a pitch. He doesn't need the crutch to play with good rhythm, but organizes lines so the thumb keeps moving anyway. BassBuzz's own framing, from 20 years of playing: keeping the thumb ghosting away like that "just feels good."

## Practicing it

- BassBuzz's exercise pattern for level 3 layers ghost notes onto the level-1/level-2 slap-and-pop pattern, marked as `X` in the tab — play the slap/pop motion exactly as normal but with the fretting hand covering the string instead of fretting a note, so no pitch sounds.
- Same underlying mechanical skill as covering unplayed strings for muting (see [[Slap Thumb Technique — Angle, Anchoring, and Muting]]) — a ghost note is that same "cover the string with the fretting hand" motion, just applied *to the string being struck* instead of to the neighboring strings.

## From videos

- **A third generalization, past slap and past the Roundabout contrast case: a picked ghost note that solves a stroke-direction problem, not just texture or contrast.** BassBuzz's breakdown of Nirvana's "Smells Like Teen Spirit" (Krist Novoselic, played with a pick per [[Pick vs. Fingers on Bass — Tone and Technique Tradeoffs]]'s eighth worked example) places a ghost note (muted pick pluck, `X`) on **beat 3**. The stated reason is mechanical, not sonic: down-strokes feel more natural landing on the beat and up-strokes off the beat, so the ghost note on beat 3 supplies a "free" down-stroke that isn't tied to a real pitch — which then lets the following, real off-beat note fall naturally on an up-stroke, and resolves back to a down-stroke on beat 4. Full picking pattern given, down/up marked: `down up down down up down up down down down down up down down`. **This is a distinct payoff from both other worked examples on this page** — the slap original is about rhythmic density as a timekeeping anchor, the Roundabout case is about arrangement-level contrast/space — this one is about a ghost note actively solving which stroke lands where, so the surrounding "real" notes fall under the hand without fighting the pick's natural down-on-beat tendency. → [[Pick vs. Fingers on Bass — Tone and Technique Tradeoffs]], [[Pick Grip, Attack Mechanics, and Picking Patterns]] — BassBuzz, [[The 5 Levels of Smells Like Teen Spirit (For Bass)]]
- **Ghost notes beyond slap — the same technique as a general contrast device, not a slap-specific one.** A separate BassBuzz breakdown of Chris Squire's playing on "Roundabout" (Yes) uses ghost notes the same mechanical way (fretting hand covers the string(s), then pick or pluck for a thumpy, unpitched sound, marked `X`) but applied to a **picked/fingerstyle riff, not a slap line** — confirming the mechanic generalizes past slap to any right-hand approach. The stated payoff here is explicitly about **contrast**, not just percussive texture: playing the riff with every note clean/fretted instead loses contrast and the notes fill up more sonic space, leaving less room for the rest of the band to cut through — ghost notes break a dense riff into a mix of pitched notes and percussive thumps on purpose, which is the arrangement-level reason to reach for them, not just a right-hand flourish. Real cross-genre worked examples given directly, none of them slap-specific: **Chuck Rainey**'s bass line on "Rocksteady" (Aretha Franklin), **Kim Deal**'s bass line on "Hey" (Pixies), and — the one slap example — **Mark King** on Level 42's "Love Games."
