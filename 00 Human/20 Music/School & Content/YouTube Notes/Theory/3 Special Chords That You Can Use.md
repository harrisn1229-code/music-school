# 3 Special Chords That You Can Use

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wJqK8LPJKsQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wJqK8LPJKsQ)

## Synopsis

Three specific slash-chord substitutions for common diatonic chords, each demonstrated with a real song example, aimed at swapping a predictable triad for a "lusher"/more floating equivalent without changing the underlying harmonic function.

## What's taught

- **IV over V** (in C: F/G) — substitutes for a plain V chord resolving to I; effectively a **G9sus4** or **G11**. Examples: *The Long and Winding Road* by The Beatles (in E♭: A♭/B♭ resolving to E♭); *Don't Know Why* by Norah Jones (in B♭: E♭/F resolving to B♭).
- **V over IV** (in C: G/F) — the reverse; commonly appears inside the "Royal Road" progression (a well-known Japanese pop chord progression, IV-V-iii-vi): the right hand moves to V while the left hand stays put on IV, producing a floating, suspended feel. Example: *Peaches* by Jack Black (*The Super Mario Bros. Movie*), in D♭ — A♭/G♭, functionally an A♭7 in third inversion; the song's use of the same Royal Road progression is plausibly not a coincidence given its film context.
- **"The Tiny Dancer chord"** (in C: G/A, i.e. a V-quality triad over the 6th-degree bass note) — substitutes for a plain vi chord (Am); effectively an Am11 missing the 3rd and 5th, or read differently, an Am9sus4. Named for Elton John's *Tiny Dancer*, where the chorus climbs through successive right-hand inversions of G over a static left-hand A bass, creating a "climbing" effect over the hovering slash-chord sound.
