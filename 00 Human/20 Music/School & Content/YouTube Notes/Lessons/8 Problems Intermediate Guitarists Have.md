# 8 Problems Intermediate Guitarists Have

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5PZWUjAzlyU)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5PZWUjAzlyU)

## Synopsis

Eight common intermediate-player problems with proposed fixes — mindset/methodology content in the same vein as [[25 Hard Truths About Guitar]] and [[Why is there SO MUCH conflicting guitar advice]] from earlier in this batch, with two genuinely concrete, actionable techniques (a solo-freshening method, and a chord-progression-memorization test for getting lost while improvising).

## Techniques

- **Problem 1 — Not knowing what to practice, caused by "option paralysis."** Too much freely-available guitar content leads to consuming widely but shallowly rather than deeply. Fix: pick one source/video and go deep on everything it teaches before moving to the next, rather than sampling many sources at once.
- **Problem 2 — Asking permission-seeking questions ("should I learn CAGED/triads/modes?") instead of just trying the thing.** Reframe: the only real cost of trying something is time, and a lifetime of guitar playing makes that cost small — actually learning the thing lets you judge its value directly, instead of debating it unresolved.
- **Problem 3 — Solos feel boring even with real technical ability.** What makes a solo interesting is more about emotional delivery than note choice — even a plain pentatonic scale played with real feel wins over a technically fancier but flat delivery. Concrete refresh method: **add exactly one new element you're actively practicing into your existing soloing vocabulary at a time** (e.g. blend in one unfamiliar-but-adjacent pentatonic box shape alongside the one you already know well) — keeps solos both confident (built on known material) and fresh (one deliberate new addition per pass).
- **Problem 4 — Jumping between topics too quickly instead of going deep.** Named difference between amateur and professional practice: professionals stay on one topic for months; amateurs sample many topics briefly. Worked example: both intermediate and professional players "know" the fretboard notes, but a pro can locate any note in under a second while an intermediate player needs real time to work it out — the gap is depth of repetition on the same material, not different underlying knowledge.
- **Problem 5 — Struggling to memorize scales comfortably.** Baseline fix: raw repetition (the video's own blunt benchmark — play the scale 1,000 times). But reps alone can plateau; layer in **deliberate variation** — start from scale degrees other than the root, start on strings other than the low E, add string-skipping, work different interval patterns through the scale — to force genuine internalization rather than one fixed muscle-memory pattern. **Target state, explicitly named: you should be able to clearly visualize the scale shape before you physically play it** (the same fluency most players already have with the basic minor pentatonic box) — that visualization fluency is the actual finish line for "knowing" a scale, not just being able to play it slowly.
- **Problem 6 — Fear of learning music theory.** Framed as a time-cost concern more than a difficulty concern. Key argument for prioritizing theory over pure technical practice: **theory, once learned, decays far more slowly than physical technique/speed** — physical chops fade without continuous maintenance, while theory knowledge is comparatively durable and cheap to re-activate even after a long gap.
- **Problem 7 — Inconsistent practice.** No new mechanism — restates the general consistency-beats-intensity theme already covered in [[Practice Mindset — Hard Truths and Realistic Expectations]].
- **Problem 8 — Getting lost mid-improvisation over a chord progression.** Diagnosis: getting lost means you don't actually know the underlying progression well enough — compare to how well you know a favorite song (able to identify exactly where you are in it after being away from it for a moment). **Concrete self-test: play the backing track, leave the room for a minute or two, come back, and try to immediately identify where you are in the chord chart by ear.** Most backing-track progressions loop in under a minute, so the actual memorization task is small — get that one minute of harmony as familiar as a favorite song and getting lost stops being possible.
