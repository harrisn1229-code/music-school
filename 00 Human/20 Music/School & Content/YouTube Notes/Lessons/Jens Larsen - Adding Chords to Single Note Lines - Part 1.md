# Adding Chords to Single Note Lines - Part 1

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W_HtIFFZ2F4)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W_HtIFFZ2F4)

## Synopsis

A jazz-guitar lesson from Jens Larsen tackling a topic he says he never found taught anywhere else: how to punctuate an improvised single-note solo line with chord voicings, turning a bare melodic line into something that reads as harmonically self-supporting. Larsen frames it explicitly as material he worked out from analyzing his own playing rather than from a method book, and deliberately keeps the whole lesson to one static chord — A minor 7, treated as the ii of G major (A Dorian) — so the focus stays on the *mechanism* of adding chords rather than on navigating a moving progression (he flags a planned follow-up video that extends this to real progressions). The lesson has three parts: **why** to add chords (clarifying vague/extension-heavy lines, filling space between phrases, emphasizing specific notes), **when/where** to add them (mainly at the end of a melodic idea, which requires pre-planning via target notes), and **two drilled exercises** (chord landing directly on the target note vs. landing right after it, the latter being Larsen's own preferred habit because it protects the melody note's audibility). He closes with three worked examples showing increasingly dense applications — from a single closing chord to a full "second voice" texture running under the whole line — plus practical voicing-building notes (keep voicings small, expect to need multiple fingerings for the same target depending on what precedes it). Short, dense, all reasoned through with played illustrations rather than notated examples on screen (a downloadable PDF of the examples is offered via his website, not captured here).

## What's taught

- **Three reasons to add chords under a single-note line:** (1) clarifies a line that's mostly upper extensions/vague-sounding by making the underlying chord audible under it — "I'm really I really do mean to play this," not implying a different chord; (2) fills space/silence between melodic statements, adding a sense of groove; (3) emphasizes a specific note by simply making it louder (more notes sounding at once) without changing dynamics or technique.
- **Best placement: the end of a melodic idea, on the sustaining note.** This is described as the easiest and most natural spot — it clarifies the phrase harmonically right as it resolves.
- **Placing the chord requires thinking ahead, not reacting in the moment** — you need to already know your line's target note before you arrive at it, so the chord voicing is ready in time; Larsen explicitly ties this to **target-note practice** as a prerequisite skill (references an earlier lesson of his on target notes, with more planned).
- **Two-exercise drill, both worked as harmonizing every note of A Dorian in the ~2nd–8th-fret area** (deliberately not boxed into one fixed hand position):
  1. **Exercise 1 — chord struck simultaneously with the target note.** Small voicing added directly under each ascending scale tone; use fewer notes low (an interval or three notes max — low strings don't sit well under a full voicing, sound muddy, and want to function as the root) and more notes as you climb into the higher register.
  2. **Exercise 2 — chord struck right after the target note, not on it.** Same drill, but delaying the chord fixes the main problem Exercise 1 exposes: hitting melody note + chord together functions like a strum and can bury the melody note. Larsen says this delayed placement is what he actually does most often himself.
  3. Practical check for both: **record yourself** to verify the melody note is staying clearly audible on top — if it isn't, swap to a different voicing of the same target note rather than forcing the first choice.
- **Voicing-building rules:** keep voicings small on purpose (faster to grab mid-line, more flexible) — this is a personal, situational choice more than a fixed rule; expect to need **several different fingerings for the same target chord**, chosen by whatever note/position comes right before it in the line, since the voicings aren't tied to one fixed shape.
- **Practice sequencing after the two exercises are solid:** move to improvising simple lines and adding chords only at phrase endings; start **rubato** (out of time) before locking placement into strict tempo; only after that, start varying where the chord lands — opening a phrase, or woven through it as a recurring second layer, not just closing it.
- **Three worked examples, increasing in density (all over A minor 7 / A Dorian):**
  1. A minor **shell voicing** (root–3rd–7th) opening, an E minor pentatonic melodic fragment, ending on an **A minor 13** voicing.
  2. Opens on an **A minor 11** voicing, plays the scale, and harmonizes the melody's B (the 9th) with **a C major triad shape over the A minor sound** — Larsen calls this his most-used voicing here, describing it as "a nice dark sound" (this is functionally an upper-structure triad: C major = 3–5–7–9 of Am9).
  3. The densest example — polyphonic, chords used as a genuine **second voice** filling around the melody (especially in bar 2): opens with an E minor pentatonic fragment, harmonizes the first note with an A minor triad using the chord-right-after-the-note approach, then shifts into a rhythmic pattern adding a chord after *every* melody note, with an accent chord landing on the final note of the phrase.
- **Explicitly framed as foundation-only:** the whole lesson stays on one chord/one mode on purpose; Larsen states a follow-up ("Part 2" implied by the title) will apply the same method across real moving progressions rather than a single held chord.
- Larsen offers a **free downloadable PDF of the examples** via his website (linked in the video description, not captured in this transcript) and mentions his newsletter for new lesson/scale-chart/etude-chart releases.
