# I Feel Fine — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

Four chords, one key, and a form that comes out bulky because every section is short — including **three verses in a row** in the middle, one of them the guitar solo. The famous item is the opening feedback, and Pollack's correction to the folklore is worth keeping: it isn't random noise but the deliberate isolation of a **natural harmonic sounding an octave above the plucked string**, and the note plucked was chosen, not stumbled onto.

**Form.** An 8-bar intro after the out-of-tempo feedback, quoted almost verbatim later in the back half of the solo section. The verse is **10 bars**, splitting 6 + 4 by the rhetoric of the lyric and tune rather than by the harmony — asymmetric, but the dramatic shape stays arch-like and closed. The bridge is 8 bars, the same phrase twice, left open so it hands off cleanly.

**Harmony.** Firmly in one key throughout, four chords total. The verses get by on I, IV and V; **iii is the only addition and appears only in the bridges**, used as the step from I to IV where it would normally head for vi — the cheapest unusual-sounding chord available in a four-chord song. The verse's entire first phrase sits static on the tonic.

**Melody.** Trademark noodling on two or three notes inside a narrow range. Blue-note placement is scheduled rather than scattered: **♭7s early in the verse, and the ♭3 withheld until near its end** ([[BLU-C06 — Blue notes and the blues scale]]), so the section escalates without any new material.

**Arrangement.** The lead guitar is a secondary hook in its own right — licks in the intro and between the verse phrases keep it lurking constantly, so the solo doesn't arrive out of nowhere. Absence is scheduled throughout ([[ARR-C04 — Arrangement interest is scheduled absence]]): the band is held out until late in the intro, and the backing vocals join across the **back** half of the verse but reinforce the **front** of each bridge phrase before retreating — same parts, and the two sections stop sounding alike. John's lead sits **below** the other two voices, which is where the tang comes from.
