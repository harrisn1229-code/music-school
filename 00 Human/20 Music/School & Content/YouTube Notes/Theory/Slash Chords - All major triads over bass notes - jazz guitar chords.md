# Slash Chords - All major triads over bass notes - jazz guitar chords

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SkqeHCvTO14)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SkqeHCvTO14)

## Synopsis
Jens Larsen tackles slash-chord notation (`Bb/C`, `A/C`, `D/C`…) head-on: what the symbol actually means, why it reads as mysterious even though it's mechanically simple, and a complete systematic catalog rather than a handful of cherry-picked examples. His core claim is that a slash chord is almost always "a triad or normal seventh chord over some bass note," occasionally a genuine two-key polychord but rarely — and the fix for the "mysterious" feeling is a fixed three-step method: treat the bass note as the root, relate every note of the upper triad to that root as a scale degree, and read off the resulting chord name (dominant, major-7-type, or an ambiguous/diminished-flavored color) to know how it wants to resolve. The video's real content is a **complete chromatic sweep**: all twelve possible major triads stacked over one static C bass note, each one named and interpreted in turn — some collapse to plain inversions (Ab/C, F/C), several land on chords you'd already know under a different name (Eb/C = Cm7 outright; G/C = a rootless Cmaj9 voicing Jens says he uses constantly), and a few are genuinely new, ambiguous, or dissonant-and-resolving colors (B/C, A/C, Gb/C, D/C, Db/C). He closes with three short worked ii–V–I progressions putting specific entries from the catalog to use, a note that the same take-the-bass-as-root method applies unchanged to minor/diminished/augmented triads and to four-note chords (not worked out here), and a comping-context aside — when you're not actually playing the bass note yourself, the "extra" note can be voiced as a color tone on top of the triad instead of underneath it, a sound he flags as an unexplored follow-up topic. The single most useful reframe in the video: don't file slash chords under a separate scary category — treat each one as *another voicing of a chord type you already know* and choose it (or not) by ear, the same way you'd choose between two grips of the same chord.

## What's taught
- **Definition and the "how to read it" convention:** upper letter = a chord (usually a triad, sometimes a full seventh chord), lower letter = the bass note. `Bb/C` reads "B-flat over C." A true polychord reading (both letters are independent full chords stacked, not one chord over a bare bass note) exists but is the exception, not the default assumption.
- **The interpretation method, stated as a repeatable procedure:** (1) treat the bass note as the root even if it isn't a tone of the upper triad; (2) relate every note in the upper triad back to that root as a scale degree/extension; (3) name the resulting chord from those degrees to know its function (dominant vs. major-7-type vs. ambiguous) and how it wants to resolve. Full breakdown and the complete twelve-triad catalog worked out from this method → **[[Slash Chords]]** (new theory reference).
- **Worked opening example, `Bb/C`:** notes B♭-D-F relative to a C root are ♭7-9-4/11, giving **C7sus4(9)** — demonstrated as the "how do you even start" example before the full chromatic sweep begins.
- **All twelve major triads over a C bass, chromatically:** C/C (plain C, no slash needed) → B/C (C diminished with a major 7 — a sus4-diminished suspension device) → Bb/C (C7sus4(9), same as the opener) → A/C (C7♭9,13 — "diminished harmony" flavor, usually heard as dominant) → Ab/C (just 1st-inversion Ab, not a new chord) → G/C (rootless Cmaj9-type voicing — his own frequent go-to for a Cmaj7 sound) → Gb/C (C7♭9♭5 — dissonant, wants to resolve) → F/C (just 2nd-inversion F, not a new chord) → E/C (Cmaj7♯5) → Eb/C (**recognize this on sight — it's just Cm7**) → D/C (genuinely ambiguous: no 3rd, no 7th present, reads as either Cmaj7♯11/lydian or C7♯11/lydian-dominant depending purely on context) → Db/C (C7sus with ♭9/♭13, a "phrygian"-sounding tension chord). Full table with every degree spelled out → **[[Slash Chords]]**.
- **Three worked ii–V–I applications**, all in F, all written on the chart by their functional name rather than as slash symbols (his stated preference — "remember the slash chords are voicings," not the notation you write down): (1) Gm7(11) → A/C [=C7♭9,13] → Fmaj6/9; (2) Dm7 → G7(♭13/♯5) → D/C used as the tonic voicing [=Cmaj7♯11, a lydian-colored resolution]; (3) Gm11 → Gb/C [=C7♭9♭5] → straightforward drop-2 Fmaj7.
- **The method generalizes beyond major triads and beyond triads entirely** — explicitly stated to work identically on minor, diminished, and augmented upper triads (not worked out in this video), and on four-note upper chords over a bass note too, where the bass note's role often reads as a bass-motion device standing in for a known chord (his example: `Dm7/C` functioning as a half-diminished-adjacent sound) rather than a fresh color to analyze from scratch.
- **Comping without the bass note — a flagged-but-unexplored idea:** when you're not covering the bass yourself, the note that would've been in the bass can instead sit as a color tone ON TOP of the upper triad — worked example floated but not demonstrated: the Gb/C voicing with C on top of the Gb triad instead of underneath it. Jens explicitly calls this out as a candidate for a dedicated follow-up video (viewer-request-gated).
- **The philosophy, stated directly at the close:** the biggest problem with slash chords isn't playing them, it's that they don't sort into familiar categories (m7/dominant7/maj7) at a glance — so decode by ear-and-degrees rather than trying to memorize the symbol shape, and once decoded, treat each one as an alternate voicing of a chord type you already know rather than a new category to learn.
