# Songs that use the Melodic Minor scale

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-8CLp1uSFoA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-8CLp1uSFoA)

## Synopsis

The melodic minor scale explained from first principles — why it's the only Western scale that traditionally uses different notes ascending vs. descending, what specific problem it was invented to fix (a follow-up to harmonic minor, which fixes its own problem but creates a new one), and where it actually shows up in real music (mostly jazz/classical, with a few notable pop exceptions).

## Techniques

- **Three main minor scales, each fixing a problem the previous one creates**: natural minor (Aeolian) is the "default" minor scale. Harmonic minor raises the 7th degree specifically to create a strong leading-tone pull into the octave (natural minor's 7th is a whole step from the octave — a weak resolution; harmonic minor's raised 7th is a half step — a strong one), fixing harmony/resolution but creating an awkward gap: the raised 7th widens the 6th-to-7th interval to a minor 3rd/augmented 2nd, which sounds "disjointed" in a melodic line (every other step in the scale is a plain half/whole step). Melodic minor fixes THAT gap by also raising the 6th when ascending (closing the awkward jump, evenly spaced climb to the octave) — but since the leading tone only wants to resolve upward (not downward), composers historically reverted to plain natural minor when descending, since there's no resolution-pull reason to keep the raised notes going down.
- **Ascending melodic minor = harmonic minor with a raised 6th** (equivalently: major scale with a lowered 3rd). **Descending melodic minor = identical to natural minor.** This ascending/descending split is the traditional convention; modern usage (especially jazz, where it's called "the jazz minor scale") usually treats just the ascending form as a standalone scale in both directions.
- **Character**: because the ascending form is "major scale with a ♭3," it has a distinctly "whimsical, curious" color — still minor/melancholic, but less "sorrowful/mournful" than natural or harmonic minor.
- **Bach's own inconsistency, cited directly as evidence these are conventions, not rules**: his Bourrée in E minor follows the ascending/descending melodic-minor convention cleanly; his Fugue in A minor (WTC Book 2) follows it in one passage (bar 25) then abandons it immediately after in a directly adjacent passage, freely mixing raised/natural 6th-7th in both directions.
- **"Yesterday" (Beatles)** — the deep-dive example: modulates from F major to its relative minor (D minor) by bar 3; the ascending melody line climbs D melodic minor (raised 6th/7th audible on "seemed so far away"), the descending line reverts to natural minor (lowered 6th/7th) — McCartney actually followed the full traditional ascending/descending convention, both melodically AND harmonically (the accompanying chords shift between a raised-6th-flavored Em and lowered-6th/7th chords like B♭ and C). A​/B'd directly against a harmonic-minor-only rewrite of the same melody, which the presenter judges "more serious and dramatic" but less smooth/melodic than McCartney's actual choice — likely a subconscious jazz/classical influence given McCartney's own account of the melody arriving in a dream, worrying he'd unknowingly plagiarized an old jazz tune.
- **Green sleeves** (traditional) — uses melodic minor, though which notes are raised/natural varies by which historical version of the melody is being played (some versions raise only the 6th throughout, freely mixing the 7th; others freely mix both).
- **"Carol of the Bells"** — a clean example of the full ascending/descending convention, contributing to its "whimsical yet haunting" character.
- **"Autumn Leaves" (jazz standard, Joseph Kosma)** — end of the A section shifts to the relative minor and the melody ascends the melodic minor scale, giving it a "mellow" quality.
- **"Lullaby of Birdland" (George Shearing)** — opening 2-bar melody uses melodic minor before switching to natural minor for the next 2 bars.
- **"Possibly Maybe" (Björk, 1996)** — outro is in C melodic minor (verse is separately in B Lydian): the raised 7th appears in the vocal melody, the raised 6th in a repeating accompanying arpeggio.
- **Why full-song melodic-minor pieces are rare**: chords built purely from melodic-minor's own note collection tend to sound "limited and a little odd" — the scale is valuable for melody-writing in the minor key, but composers writing in "the minor key" typically blend elements from natural/harmonic/melodic minor freely rather than picking one and staying in it exclusively.
