# Goal-Tiered Practice and the Self-Taught Bass Commandments

BassBuzz's framework for structuring self-taught bass practice without a teacher — six "commandments," delivered as a comedy bit (a self-serious "bass ghost" narrator) wrapped around a genuinely concrete practice-design system. The throughline: a self-taught player's biggest failure mode isn't lack of material, it's **picking the wrong material** with no way to check whether it's actually useful — the video's opening cautionary examples are a player stuck at "barely progressed beyond 6 months" after *two years*, and one stuck unable to play a single full song after *14 years*. The fix is a three-tier goal ladder (long/medium/short) that turns "what should I practice today" from a guess into a derived answer, plus four practice categories (songs, technique, fretboard, theory) to check the ladder's short-term goals against so nothing gets skipped. This is the same underlying problem — and largely the same answer, "clarify the goal, then let the goal choose the material" — that [[Vision-Driven Practice and the Overwhelm Reflex]] documents from a guitar channel; the two notes are companions from different instruments converging on the same fix, worth reading together.

## Commandment 1 — Set thy goals: the long/medium/short-term ladder

**Core mechanism: without goals you can't judge whether any given exercise or theory topic is useful *for you*** — a routine that's great for one player (e.g. jazz walking-bass drills) is a waste of time for another (e.g. a beginner who just wants a Chili Peppers bassline). The fix is three linked goal tiers, each solving a different failure mode of the tier above it:

- **Long-term goal** — the destination, months out (e.g. "play in my friend's bar band," or "learn Hysteria by Muse"). Motivating, but if it's the *only* goal you set, you'll feel like a failure for the entire stretch before you're actually close to it — a beginner aiming straight at a hard song like Hysteria won't get meaningfully near it for months, and will "feel like a failure the whole time" with only the long goal to check progress against.
- **Medium-term goal** — a **process** commitment, not an outcome: what you *do*, not where you *get*. *(examples given)* "Practice 20 minutes a day, 4x/week for the next 3 months" for a moderate long-term goal; "an hour a day, 5x/week for 6 months" for an ambitious one. Named as the fix for demotivation specifically because it's something you fully control — you either showed up today or you didn't, independent of how far off the long-term goal still feels. Every session is a checked box, which is concrete progress even when the long-term goal itself hasn't moved yet.
- **Short-term goal** — the actual day-to-day practice material, derived by **breaking the long-term goal down into its component skills**. *(worked examples)* For a "play in a bar band" long goal: daily focus on solid rhythm-with-a-drummer and building simple basslines over a chord chart. For a "learn an advanced song right out of the gate" long goal: daily plucking-speed practice and fretting-hand coordination work.

**The catch the video names directly:** handing you those two worked examples above is a shortcut — as a self-taught player without a teacher's expertise, *you* have to do the long→short breakdown yourself for your own goal, which is exactly where self-taught players "flounder, practice the wrong stuff, and rage-post on Reddit years later." **Commandments 2–4 exist specifically to give the four categories to break any long-term goal down against**, so the breakdown step isn't a total guess.

## Commandment 2 — Learn thy songs early (motivation as fuel, not a reward)

**Songs, not drills, are what make people want to keep playing bass** — cited stat: per Fender's own data, **90% of new guitarists quit within a year** (BassBuzz applies this to bass as the same cautionary number). The claim: skipping songs in favor of "earning" them after enough exercise-grinding starves the exact motivation that keeps a self-taught player showing up, so songs belong in the rotation from early on, not gated behind a technique threshold. Secondary benefits beyond motivation: playing real songs naturally trains **rhythm, technique, and ear** at the same time, rather than needing separate isolated drills for each. **No-theory alternative also offered:** if learning existing songs isn't the draw, freeform noodling/making up your own parts by ear is a legitimate substitute for staying inspired — but the video is explicit this only works *in combination with* Commandment 3 (technique), since without coordination you can't execute either a learned song or your own noodled ideas.

## Commandment 3 — Suffer not poor technique: the exercise-quality checklist

**Technique = the physical skills (fretting, plucking, muting)** — named as the usual answer to "why can't I play my goal song yet." Since there's effectively an infinite pool of possible exercises and no teacher to pick for you, the video gives a **checklist that makes almost any exercise effective**, rather than trying to name "the right" exercises:

1. **Focus on *how* you play, not *what* you play** — the exercise choice matters less than the quality of attention on your own execution while doing it.
2. **Practice with a metronome or drum track** — timing discipline built into every rep, not bolted on later.
3. **Start slower than you think you should.**
4. **Don't speed up until you can play it decently at least 5 times in a row** *(explicit clean-reps gate before advancing tempo)* — the same clean-reps-before-progression logic as the guitar-side [[Backward Chaining Practice]] and [[The Check-Off Method for Repertoire Practice]] gating mechanics, applied here as a bass-agnostic exercise rule rather than a specific combination order.
5. **Track progress in a simple journal.**

Applies to BassBuzz's own catalog of fretting/plucking/muting drills already in the vault — see [[Fretting-Hand Strength, Stretch, Stamina, and Finger Control]], [[String Crossing and Plucking Patterns]], [[Bass Note Muting Technique]], and [[Fretting-Hand Finger Independence Drill]] — this checklist is the meta-layer for running *any* of them well, not a replacement for them.

## Commandment 4 — Make thy life easier: fretboard knowledge + theory as force multipliers

Framed as **optional-but-much-harder-without**: you *can* become a solid bassist without either, but both remove a specific tax on every song you try to learn:

- **Fretboard knowledge** (note names + where they live on the neck, plus recognizing reusable shapes/patterns) — the payoff is turning a tab into **chunked, familiar information** instead of "random meaningless numbers" you have to memorize one at a time. See [[Bass Fretboard Note Memorization System]] for the vault's existing note-naming drill this feeds directly into.
- **Theory** — not framed as mandatory for everyone, but named as accelerating three things specifically: understanding what you're playing, memorizing songs faster, and coming up with your own original parts. Points at BassBuzz's own dedicated theory video (see [[Music Theory for Bass]]) rather than teaching theory content in this video.

**The four-category checklist, assembled:** songs (Commandment 2) + technique (Commandment 3) + fretboard + theory (Commandment 4) = the full beginner coverage map. **Worked example applying it** (goal: learn "Hysteria" by Muse): songs — covered by definition; technique — will need heavy practice, this is the hard part of that particular song; fretboard/theory — not strictly critical if you're willing to just memorize the tab number-by-number, but knowing the note names and which scale they come from makes memorization faster and keeps the *other* categories from going neglected while all the practice time pours into technique. **Sample short-term daily routine given for an ambitious (1 hr/day) medium goal:** warm-up 5 min, scales 5 min, fretting exercises 10 min, plucking exercises 10 min, song practice in small chunks 15 min, free/fun playing for decompression 5 min — offered as *(general starting point)*, scaled to however ambitious the medium-term goal actually is.

## Commandment 5 — Avoid common self-taught pitfalls (quickfire list)

A named grab-bag of mistakes the video calls out as chronic and progress-killing, each with a one-line fix:

| Problem | Solution |
|---|---|
| Buying the wrong bass | Get a 4-string fretted bass you actually enjoy playing, and get it professionally set up. |
| Pressing way too hard with the fretting fingers/thumb | Do minimum-viable-pressure (MVP) exercises — **now detailed in a later BassBuzz video:** rest a finger on the string with zero pressure (confirm via a silent pluck, no note sounds), add pressure gradually until the string just starts to rattle against the fret, then add only a tiny bit more until the note goes clean, and hold exactly that pressure — no harder. Named as something to actively re-check even by experienced players, since pressure creeps back up the longer a session runs. Compare [[Fretting-Hand Strength, Stretch, Stamina, and Finger Control]]'s Exercise 1, which covers the *opposite* failure — not pressing hard/close enough — so the two notes bracket the correct pressure range from both sides. → BassBuzz, [[Practice THIS Daily (10 Min Technique Fix)]] |
| Putting stickers on the frets to mark note names | Never do this — named as a hard no, no nuance given. |
| Learning bass only through Musician/Guitar Hero/Rocksmith-style games | Treat those as **dessert, not dinner** — supplement real lessons/practice with them, don't substitute. |
| Practicing unplugged | Use an amp or headphones — you need to hear the instrument the way it actually sounds, and it's more fun/motivating. |
| Leaving the bass in its case or closet between sessions | Keep it out on a stand (or on the couch) so it's always ready to pick up on impulse. |
| Learning entirely alone and feeling isolated | Build musical community — jam with friends, or engage on bass-focused online forums. |

## Commandment 6 — Thou shalt be patient (the actual #1 failure cause)

Named directly as **the single biggest cause of self-taught bassists failing** — explicitly contrasted against marketing claims of "magic 30-day transformation." Self-teaching a skill is a long game: not a perfect routine, not unbroken daily consistency, but **showing up repeatedly past the point the initial excitement has worn off**, with progress that's gradual rather than dramatic. The video's closing move ties patience back to Commandment 1's short-term goal problem: since picking the right day-to-day material only gets harder to sustain as months pass, it points to a companion "what order to learn everything on bass" curriculum video as a way to keep short-term goals fresh over a 6–12 month horizon — **that companion video is now in the vault: [[What to Learn on Bass (in Order)]], distilled into [[The BassBuzz 55-Step Bass Learning Roadmap]].** Its own Level 2 (Step 21) independently names "notice your progress" as the single most-missed self-taught habit — the same diagnosis as this commandment, from the opposite direction (patience vs. progress-blindness are two faces of the same failure to see gradual movement).
