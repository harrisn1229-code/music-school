# Trace and Tracy — Grant Green

> A minor blues in B♭ minor. Nathan Borton pulls four foundational concepts out of Green's solo over this tune — tonic minor soloing, color-tone emphasis, a named rhythmic embellishment ("the dilla rhythm"), and classic blues-language vocabulary — see [[Why Grant Green's Minor Blues Soloing Is PERFECT (Jazz 101)]] for the source video and the broader routing. For the minor-blues form itself, see [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *Minor blues*, which documents the same "the V frequently stays a dominant" rule this solo's tonic-minor devices are built on.

## Chords

**Key:** B♭ minor · **Form:** minor blues (i–iv–V family, per [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *Minor blues*: `i7 – iv7 – … – V7 – i7`, V typically staying a dominant rather than resolving to minor). The source breakdown names only the two chords it solos over directly — **B♭m7 (i)** and **E♭m7 (iv)** — matching that skeleton; a full bar-by-bar chart isn't given in the lesson, and independent listings for this tune (recorded as "Tracin' Tracy," from the *Sunday Morning* session) only confirm the same i–iv minor-blues relationship, not the complete 12-bar sequence. → [[Tonic Minor Soloing]]

## Solo breakdown (Nathan Borton's transcription)

- **Tonic minor — implied 5–1 inside a static chord.** Two phrases from the same line: **D natural → E♭** over the E♭ minor (iv) chord (D is the major 3rd of B♭7, V7 of E♭ minor — an implied 5-1 compressed into two notes), and **A natural → B♭** over the B♭ minor (i) chord (A is the major 3rd of F7, V7 of B♭ minor), the identical device transposed to the other chord. → [[Tonic Minor Soloing]]
- **A full 5–1 line played straight over a static chord.** A complete phrase outlining **F7 moving to B♭ minor**, played in its entirety over an unmoving B♭m vamp (the V chord isn't actually sounding), with a plain minor phrase tagged onto the end to land back in blues/pentatonic language. → [[Tonic Minor Soloing]]
- **Color tones over B♭ minor.** Deliberately lands on and emphasizes the **9th (C natural)**, the **natural 13th (G)**, and (named as a further option) the **11th (E♭)** of B♭ minor — chord extensions used as a conscious melodic feature of the tonic-minor and blues lines rather than incidental passing tones. → [[Extended Chords]]
- **The "dilla rhythm."** A named rhythmic embellishment cell that interrupts a straight eighth-note line to move through scale or arpeggio tones — demonstrated walking down the tonic minor scale, and separately embellishing a **B♭ minor 6 arpeggio**. Part of Green's broader habit in this solo of constantly shifting between eighth notes, quarter notes, and triplets. → [[Dilla Rhythm Ornamentation]]
- **Blues language, end of chorus 1.** A phrase built from a **small, one-octave blues box** — moved around the neck rather than parked in a single position, the same "many small boxes over the whole neck" idea documented on [[String-Group Pentatonic Bursts]] and [[Diagonal Pentatonic Blues Soloing]]. → [[Blues Lead Phrasing and Touch]]
- **Blues language, start of chorus 2.** The **blues scale played vertically, up and down the neck** rather than horizontally within one box — milking the high register — and again landing on the **minor 11th** extension (part of the blues scale itself), tying the color-tone habit above directly into blues-scale vocabulary. → [[Blues Lead Phrasing and Touch]], [[Extended Chords]]

## Steal this

- **Compress a V7→i cadence into a single outside note** (the V7's major 3rd, resolved down a half step into a chord tone) any time you're stuck on a static minor chord — cheap, immediate harmonic motion with no change to the actual changes.
- **Play the whole 5-1 line, not just the outside-note shortcut**, when there's room — a complete dominant-to-tonic phrase over a chord that never moves reads as far more sophisticated than pentatonic alone, and it still resolves cleanly because the line itself supplies the resolution.
- **Consciously target the 9/11/13 of a static minor chord** as a feature, not a byproduct — the same extensions that make comping voicings interesting (see [[Extended Chords]]) work as melodic landing points in a solo too.
- **Break up a straight eighth-note run with one syncopated embellishment cell** rather than a whole new subdivision — variety from placement, not from switching feels.
