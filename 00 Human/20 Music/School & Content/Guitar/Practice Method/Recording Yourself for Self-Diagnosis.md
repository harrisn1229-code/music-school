# Recording Yourself for Self-Diagnosis

A self-teaching *process* — how to use recordings of your own playing to do the job a teacher would otherwise do: listen to your playing, identify what to work on, and evaluate whether it's actually improving. Complementary to [[The Five Pillars of Jazz Guitar Practice]] rather than a slice of it — the Five Pillars framework says *what categories* a complete practice system covers; this note answers *how you find out what needs work* inside any of those pillars without a teacher in the room. Also a natural partner to [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]] (which analyzes *other people's* recorded playing) and [[Woodshedding Changes with a Looper]] (which uses recording/looping as a rehearsal tool, not a diagnostic one) — this note is specifically about turning a recording of *yourself* into an accurate, actionable diagnosis.

## Why recording beats live self-assessment

While you're playing, your attention is on performing — you can't simultaneously give full analytical focus to how it actually sounds. Recording splits the two tasks apart in time: play now, evaluate later, each with full attention. Recording is specifically good at catching things that are hard to hear in the moment: swing feel (triplet-feel eighths vs. straight/even), general phrasing habits, staying stuck in one octave/register of the instrument, and other phrasing tendencies that only become obvious from the outside. — Jens Larsen, [[Guitar Practice - How To Be Your Own Teacher]]

## Setup

- **Video, not just audio** — a smartphone is sufficient; get clear enough framing to actually see your hands if you're checking technique.
- **Protect the audio signal.** If the recording distorts, you lose the ability to judge timing/phrasing detail and some notes drop out entirely — a bad recording defeats the exercise before you even listen back. Basic mic distance/level care matters more than gear quality; "a phone and a coffee mug on the table" is explicitly enough.
- **Dedicated technique-review use:** point the camera specifically at your picking hand (or fretting hand) for a session focused purely on mechanics, independent of how the notes sound.
- **Metronome before backing track when starting out.** A metronome is the harder, more honest test — it forces you to generate and hold your own sense of time/groove rather than riding a backing track's built-in feel. Once your own time is solid, move to a backing track or a groove app (Drum Genius named) to test whether you actually lock in with a real rhythm-section feel. — Jens Larsen, [[Guitar Practice - How To Be Your Own Teacher]]

## The obstacle: nobody likes hearing themselves back

Near-universal, not a personal flaw — even Allan Holdsworth and Michael Brecker were famously uncomfortable listening to their own recordings. Everything you play already sounds like a cliché to you because you've already practiced it, even when it sounds fresh to a listener. It gets easier with repetition — push through the discomfort rather than avoiding the recordings. Two concrete counter-moves:
- **Keep a running list of three things you're working on and three things you already do well**, side by side — stops the process from turning into pure self-criticism.
- **Don't listen back immediately — let time pass first.** Right after playing you still remember what you *meant* to play and where you flinched, and that memory biases how you hear the take. Waiting (same logic labels use when deciding which take makes an album — distance first, then judge) removes the bias so you're hearing what's actually on the recording. — Jens Larsen, [[Guitar Practice - How To Be Your Own Teacher]]

## The evaluation checklist — three rules, applied every time you review a recording

1. **Don't get distracted from the one thing you're evaluating.** If the recording is for checking eighth-note swing feel, play long eighth-note lines and listen *only* for that — don't get pulled into judging melodic content, phrase interest, or any other flaw you happen to notice in the same take. Note other issues for their own dedicated pass; evaluating everything at once evaluates nothing well.
2. **Name the gap specifically — never just "good" or "bad."** "That was bad, make it better" is the worst feedback a teacher can give because it's not actionable. Turn a vague reaction into a specific, exercise-able target: "I need to play more evenly on a swing groove," "I need to play more laid-back." This requires already knowing what the target sound is and being able to hear the difference between that and your take — if you can't yet hear the target, that's the actual gap to close first (see [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]] for building that reference).
3. **Measure progress over multiple sessions, and keep each recording short.** One day's take-and-review won't show change — improvement shows up across several sessions, so record regularly (daily is a good habit) but keep each clip to a few choruses or a single chorus of a standard — long recordings turn the review itself into wasted practice time. Archive clips and periodically compare old against new; hearing real improvement is what sustains the motivation to keep doing this. — Jens Larsen, [[Guitar Practice - How To Be Your Own Teacher]]

## A ready-to-use self-diagnosis session structure

Combine the above into a repeatable loop, usable as a drop-in stage inside any [[The Five Pillars of Jazz Guitar Practice]] session block:
1. Pick **one** specific thing to check this session (a swing-feel question, a technique/hand-motion question, a "does this line lock with the band" question) — don't record a diagnostic session with no target.
2. Set a metronome (or backing track, once time is solid) and record a short take — a chorus or two, not a long session.
3. **Let it sit** — don't review immediately; come back after a break (hours, or the next day).
4. Review against the single target only, and write down a specific, exercise-able gap (rule 2) — plus one thing that's already working, to balance the self-critical instinct.
5. File the clip (with date + target) so it can be compared against a later take on the same target — this is what turns individual reviews into a visible progress trend.

## Why bad technique has to be faced eventually (the case for feedback at all)

**Bad technique has real consequences whether or not anyone corrects it — the correction isn't optional, only its source is a choice.** Illustrated with a vivid personal anecdote: while studying upright bass, the source's teacher (Karen Zimmerman, Santa Rosa Symphony) placed a sharp pencil just below where his elbow should sit, because dropping the elbow ruins upright strength and intonation — not to stab him, but so that *he* would stab himself the instant he reverted to the bad habit. **"Bad technique has consequences. Sometimes that's your teacher [with a pencil], but most of the time it's that you have to struggle way harder to play anything."** The broader point: at some stage you have to actually face and fix a bad-technique habit rather than route around it indefinitely — a teacher's correction (however memorable) is just one *source* of the feedback that forces that reckoning; self-recording (below) is presented as the accessible substitute when a teacher isn't available. — BassBuzz, [[10 Years of Bass Advice in 10 Minutes]]

**A concrete worked example of what real expert technique feedback looks like**, given as a short on-camera cameo from guitar teacher Alex Leach: (1) when gripping a chord, already be thinking about the *next* chord shape rather than reacting to it after landing the current one; (2) tighten up excess picking-hand movement; (3) find a fixed anchor point on the guitar for the picking hand rather than leaving it floating. Useful less for the specific guitar content than as a template for what "concrete, actionable feedback" sounds like — three short, specific, checkable notes, not a vague "sounds a bit off." The source then used exactly these three criteria as the review checklist for his own self-recorded practice sessions (see below) — a direct worked example of turning one-time expert feedback into a repeatable self-assessment target. — BassBuzz, [[10 Years of Bass Advice in 10 Minutes]]

## Self-assessment as a real substitute for a teacher, not just a stopgap

**The case against treating a private teacher as unconditionally "the gold standard":** most people don't have a teacher's access, time, or budget — but more importantly, **self-taught musicians with no teacher at all still reach elite technique**, which means the self-assessment skill is learnable on its own, not just a workaround. Named examples: **Billy Sheehan** ("king of all shredders," self-taught playing in bars and clubs), **Paul McCartney** (self-taught on bass), and **Tina Weymouth** (Talking Heads, self-taught in "just a few months"). **The logic stated directly: those players must have had *some* self-feedback method, or they'd still be stuck with bad technique and rhythm** — meaning "assess your own playing critically and improve it" is one underlying skill everyone eventually needs, whether or not a teacher is in the room. **Reframing of a teacher's actual value, worth keeping alongside this note's own framing above:** a good teacher's real contribution is helping a student *build* their self-assessment skill and giving them criteria to assess with — not fixing the playing directly. This is the same conclusion this note already reaches independently (see "The evaluation checklist" above) from a different channel and instrument, now with a second, independent argument for it. — BassBuzz, [[10 Years of Bass Advice in 10 Minutes]]

**Named companion principle for what to actually record for — don't just repeat blindly:** mindless, unfocused repetition of something already being played badly doesn't fix it — **"if you practice playing crappily... you just get better at playing crappily."** This is the reason a self-recording session needs a specific target (rule 1 in this note's evaluation checklist) rather than just capturing more reps of the same unexamined habit — repetition alone isn't deliberate practice, and recording without a specific question to check against just documents the bad habit instead of fixing it. — BassBuzz, [[10 Years of Bass Advice in 10 Minutes]]
