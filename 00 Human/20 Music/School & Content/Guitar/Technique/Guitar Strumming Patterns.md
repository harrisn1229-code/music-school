# Guitar Strumming Patterns

Five named, reusable pick-hand strumming patterns, ordered from foundational to advanced/specialized. The core mechanical idea underlying all of them: **the strumming hand moves in one continuous, steady alternating down-up motion at all times** — patterns are built not by changing the hand's motion, but by choosing which passes actually strike the strings ("ghost strokes" on the silent passes) and which strings get struck when a stroke does land.

## 1. Basic down-up strum
**D U D U D U D U** — continuous alternating eighth notes. The foundation every other pattern below is built from. Played perfectly evenly it sounds flat and robotic; two fixes:
- **Accent every other downstroke** — creates a felt backbeat/drum-like pulse.
- **Emphasize different strings at different times** rather than the full chord evenly on every stroke.
- Once solid, embellish with an occasional extra 16th-note strum dropped into the pattern.

## 2. Double down-up strum ("the most common strumming pattern of all time")
**D D U U D U** — described as the single most ubiquitous strum in popular music, at any tempo. The mechanics: the hand never stops its continuous down-up motion (same physical motion as pattern 1); the "double" strokes are really a struck stroke followed by a **ghost stroke in the opposite direction that doesn't hit the strings**, then the next same-direction stroke lands. So D-D is really down(hit)/up(ghost)/down(hit), and U-U is up(hit)/down(ghost)/up(hit) — one continuous motor pattern with strategic silent passes, not the hand changing direction twice in a row. This ghost-stroke mechanic is the key that unlocks every compound pattern below.

## 3. Gallop strumming pattern (metal / hard rock)
**D D U** repeated — rhythmically a quarter note followed by two sixteenth notes. Named for its horse-galloping feel.
- **Palm mute at the bridge** — lightly touching the strings (not fully choking them) for a muted chug; pairs well with heavy distortion for a hard-rock sound.
- **"Orchestrate" the chord**: full strum on the initial downstroke, then just the bottom strings on the following hits, rather than the same full voicing every time — applies to any pattern, not just this one. Choosing which strings (low vs. high) get hit on a given stroke, rather than always hitting everything, is a general strumming principle.
- Works with any chord, not just power chords. Break the straight gallop rhythmically mid-phrase for a "song feel," then return to the base pattern.

## 4. Carter-family-style strum (flatpicked folk/country)
Same **D D U** gallop rhythm as #3, reinterpreted: the first downstroke becomes a **single plucked bass/root note** instead of a full strum, then the down-up becomes a strum on just the **upper strings**. Pattern: **root pluck → down-up on upper strings**, repeated.
- Alternate the plucked bass note between the chord's root and another chord tone for variety.
- Flatpicked here (a fingerpicked version is possible but not demonstrated in the source video).
- Strong accuracy drill for the picking hand — hitting one specific bass string cleanly before strumming.
- Pairs naturally with walking bass notes between chord changes, and with muting technique.

## 5. Funky 16th-note strum (built-in 3-against-4 polyrhythm)
Continuous 16th notes (count: "1 e & a 2 e & a 3 e & a 4 e & a"), where **only every third 16th-note articulation is actually struck** — the rest are ghost passes. Striking every 3rd note of a 4-per-beat grid produces a felt **3-against-4 polyrhythm**, which is what makes it sound funky/syncopated.
- **Voicing:** bar just the **top four strings** for a minor 7 chord shape — a go-to voicing for this feel.
- Structure: four groups of "chord-click-click" (chord = a struck down/up, click = a ghost pass), then a **resolving group on beat 4** — a denser run (down-down-up-down-up-down-down-up-down-up-down) that lands the phrase back in a clean 4/4 feel before repeating.
- Optional: add a pickup 16th-note flourish at the start or end of the phrase for extra funk color.
- Pairs especially well with sliding octave shapes, spanning heavy-rock to straight-funk depending on tone/distortion.

## General principles across all five

- **Strum with the fingers as well as a pick.** Down with the backs of the fingernails (works without real nails too), up with the back of the thumbnail. Any single finger or combination works — no single "correct" finger. All five patterns transfer directly to finger-strumming. Full writeup of the finger-strumming mechanic (plus a related fretting-hand tension-release technique) → [[Grip Tension Release and Strumming-Hand Basics]].
- **Dynamics and orchestration make or break a pattern.** Pluck specific notes on purpose (as in #4), play low-then-high to let high strings ring while continuing to strum the bottom, and vary which strings get struck and how hard, deliberately rather than by accident. Don't strum every string evenly all the time unless that's the sound you actually want.
- **Palm/bridge muting** (pattern #3) is a pick-hand execution technique distinct from classical/fingerstyle muting → [[Classical Guitar Expression & Muting]] (fretting-hand and thumb-hop muting for fingerstyle, not palm-muting for strummed rhythm parts).

## From videos

- **A third muting variant, distinct from both above: fretting-hand first-finger touch-mute of an unwanted open low string, for pick-strummed rhythm parts.** Lightly touch (don't fret) the low string with the fretting hand's first finger at the moment of strumming — produces an inaudible click buried under the full strum, letting the player strum harder/more freely without the open string ringing out of key. Distinct from palm/bridge muting above (picking-hand, for a chugging/muted-chug tone) and from [[Classical Guitar Expression & Muting]]'s thumb-hop/thumb-side muting (fingerstyle-specific) — this one is for standard open-chord strumming. Demonstrated across several beginner riffs (Seven Nation Army, Wild Thing/Louie Louie) where an unplayed adjacent open string would otherwise ring. — Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared, [[10 Iconic & Easy Guitar Songs for Beginners]]
- **Stop thinking of strum patterns as just "down up down up" — those arrows only encode direction, not duration.** The same down-up-down-up pattern sounds completely different depending on whether each strum is a half note, quarter note, or eighth note; writing the pattern out with actual rhythmic note values (or reading standard rhythm notation) is what actually captures a strum pattern, not the direction arrows alone. Recommended learning path: **listen closely to a song's actual strum pattern and try to recreate it by ear** (framed as real ear training, not just interval drills) — reading rhythm notation is a valid alternative but more effort than most casual players need. — Brandon D'Eon, [Beginner habit you really need to stop doing](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hiHl8GSt-3A)
