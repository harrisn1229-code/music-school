# Analysing Minecraft's Music (ft. Mumbo Jumbo)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZiM3RUTrvn4)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZiM3RUTrvn4)

## Synopsis

Traces how Minecraft's ambient/minimalist soundtrack (composer Daniel Rosenfeld, "C418") arose specifically as a technical solution to open-world game-scoring constraints, not an aesthetic accident — grounded in Brian Eno's own definition of ambient music — then breaks down the specific chords, mode, and deliberately lo-fi production choices that give the soundtrack its character.

## Techniques

- **Why event-triggered scoring (the video-game default) fails for an open, unstructured world — a real constraint-driven design decision, not a stylistic preference.** Rosenfeld originally wanted biome-themed music (ocean music while sailing, cave music underground), but because Minecraft lets a player cross biomes arbitrarily fast, cued music can't catch up before the next biome triggers a jarring switch. Battle music was ruled out the same way — Minecraft encounters are often "a two-second thing," too short for a dramatic cue to ever land. **The solution**: write one continuous, mood-neutral ambient/"general" soundtrack that can underlie ANY on-screen event without ever contradicting it — since it never commits to a specific emotional cue, it can't clash with whatever's actually happening.
- **Brian Eno's own definition of ambient music, worth keeping as a design principle beyond just game scoring**: "as ignorable as it is interesting" — music that can sit in the background unnoticed, but rewards attention if given it, rather than *either* pure wallpaper (Muzak) *or* something that demands foreground attention. Origin story (Eno's own account, quoted in the video): bedridden, a quiet record of 18th-century harp music mixed with rain sounds outside — too quiet to adjust — became "a great musical experience" specifically because it didn't impose itself, creating "a landscape you could belong to" rather than a piece you had to actively listen to.
- **A composer's own account of the payoff of writing genuinely neutral, event-agnostic music**: Rosenfeld deliberately randomized playback timing (music triggers at a random interval, roughly every 15-30 minutes, for no in-game reason) rather than tying it to anything — the unplanned result is that music sometimes happens to coincide with a meaningful player moment (finishing a build, a sunset, entering a dangerous cave) purely by chance, which players then remember as if the game had scored that moment intentionally. Worth remembering as a general lesson: full context-agnostic randomness can sometimes produce a stronger felt connection than a deliberately-cued moment would, because the listener supplies their own meaning.
- **The major 7 chord's specific "calm but uneasy" quality, mechanically explained**: the major 7th interval (e.g. C to B) is dissonant on its own, but a full major 7 chord also contains consonant intervals (perfect 5ths, major 3rds) between its other notes — those consonances dilute the dissonant interval's bite without erasing it, producing a chord that reads as consonant overall but carries "a potent dash of dissonance." Demonstrated directly: removing the major 7th from "Subwoofer Lullaby" (Minecraft) strips out its uneasy/eerie quality entirely, leaving plain consonance. Same mechanism recurs across the soundtrack — "Sweden" and "Wet Hands" both lean on major 9th chords (which contain a major 7 inside them) for the same Erik-Satie-adjacent "lush but slightly off" mood; "Wet Hands" also uses Mixolydian (lowered 7th) for its "mellow, whimsical" character.
- **Deliberately degraded/lo-fi instrument timbre chosen to match a game's low-fidelity visual identity, not as a budget limitation**: Rosenfeld intentionally used muffled, artificially degraded piano/harp/string tones rather than clean, realistic samples — explicitly reasoning that Minecraft's blocky, low-fidelity graphics are "beautiful" despite (or because of) their low fidelity, and wanted the music to carry the same "natural yet lo-fi" quality rather than a polished, high-fidelity mismatch against the visuals.
