# Good Night — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

Schmaltz by design — the chords, tune and phrasing would already be sentimental before the arrangement pushes them over, and Pollack reads the whole thing as camp. The technically interesting parts are the **verse, where the bass line chooses the chords instead of following them**, and the bridge, which is nothing but an elaborate pedal point dressed as changes.

**Form.** Standard components made to feel longer and more complex than they are by three devices: **the intro previews both bridge and verse material**, the verses are doubled up, and the verse-into-bridge pairing happens three times. The one non-standard element is an instrumental special bridge inserted before the final verse. The outro plays the same half-verse twice — full scoring, then an octave up with thinner forces.

**Harmony.** Pan-diatonic, with **sevenths and ninths used decoratively rather than functionally** and chords moving in stepwise streams ([[POP-C06 — Sus and add9 under a vocal]]). The verse's bass is the engine ([[POP-C04 — Four chords with a moving bass]]): on its first pass the bass moves in bar 4, implying an inverted chord; every later pass holds instead, so **the same melody sits over different harmony**. Later the bass walks scalewise down and approaches the dominant through its third, so V arrives in first inversion before settling to root — a two-stage landing rather than a thud. The bridge is plainly a pedal point ([[POP-C03 — Pedal point]]): the tonic held underneath while three upper voices alternate their neighbours, so the "chords" are an artifact of oscillation. The special bridge makes a **fake pass at the subdominant key** — enough of a diversion to relieve an untroubled home key and motivate the last verse, but never a real modulation ([[HAR-C10 — Tonicization vs modulation]]).

**Melody.** The verse tune is full of wide leaps, but strip them away and the skeleton is a plain descending scale.

**Arrangement.** George Martin scores a string section small even by Mozart-era standards plus thin winds and a small choir, and gets a cast-of-hundreds sound out of it. The **choir takes turns doubling the lead and trailing it**, which removes any need for double-tracking.
