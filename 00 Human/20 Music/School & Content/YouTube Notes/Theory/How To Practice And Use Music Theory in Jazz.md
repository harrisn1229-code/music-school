# How To Practice And Use Music Theory in Jazz

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j37vDF6FHjg)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j37vDF6FHjg)

## Synopsis

Jens Larsen (Learn Jazz Make Music) lays out a **practice methodology** for jazz theory, not a topic lecture — five drills you can run "stuck on a bus," escalating from static recall to fully applied reharmonization. His framing thesis: theory terminology is only useful insofar as it describes music you already play — a "sharp-four double-diminished with the seventh in the bass" is worthless jargon unless you can connect it to a real song moment (he uses "Out of Nowhere" / "Angel Eyes," where that chord is just the ♭6 dominant resolving to I). Each drill is presented as thinking-only (bus/train practice) but he stresses doing it on the instrument too so it lands in the ear, not just the head. The five drills, in escalating order: (1) build the diatonic chords of a scale — including harder scales like harmonic minor, not just major; (2) run it backward — start from a chord and find every scale it could belong to; (3) find the secondary (ii–V) cadence that resolves to every degree of a key, then find those cadences inside a real tune ("I Should Care"); (4) transpose a whole song by chord *function* (scale degree + secondary cadence), never by blind interval; (5) transpose the *melody* too, which requires knowing each melody note's relationship to its chord — the payoff being that jazz standards keep hitting the same melody-note/chord-tone relationship over and over (e.g. the 3rd of the tonic, the 3rd on IV), so once you see the pattern you can harmonize and reharmonize much faster. He closes with a worked reharmonization of "All the Things You Are" using the melody-relationship insight from drill 5, and name-drops solo-phrase analysis as a natural next video (not covered here). No production content — pure practice-method/theory lesson.

## What's taught

- **Exercise 1 — diatonic chords of a scale (including non-major scales).** Picture the scale notes, then stack a 7th chord on every degree. Worked in **E♭ major**: E♭maj7 – Fm7 – Gm7 – A♭maj7 – B♭7 – Cm7 – Dø7 – E♭maj7 (the familiar major-key ladder, see [[Chords by Key]]). Then worked in the harder case, **A♭ harmonic minor** (A♭ B♭ C♭ D♭ E♭ F♭ G A♭): A♭m(maj7) – B♭ø7 – C♭maj7♯5 – D♭m7 – E♭7 – F♭maj7 – Gdim7 – A♭m(maj7). Stated payoff: this gives you the **arpeggios that work over harder-to-place chords** — e.g. the C♭maj7♯5 (enharmonically B major7♯5) from that ladder is exactly what you can play over an E♭7♭9 resolving to A♭major, the kind of move that shows up in "All the Things You Are" and "There'll Never Be Another You."
- **Exercise 2 — reverse: start from a chord, find every scale it fits.** Worked on a plain **D7 (D F♯ A C)**: it's V of G major, V of G harmonic minor, V of G melodic minor, IV of A melodic minor (the Lydian-dominant-producing degree), and it's *hidden* inside B♭ harmonic major if you respell the G♭ as F♯ (stacking the chord tones inside that scale otherwise reads as a plain D minor triad). Point of the drill: it inventories every scale you actually know and surfaces alternate color choices for soloing over one static chord, rather than defaulting to the first scale that comes to mind.
- **Exercise 3 — secondary cadence to every degree of a key.** For each diatonic chord in a key, find its ii–V. Worked fully in **B♭ major** (diatonic chords: B♭maj7 – Cm7 – Dm7 – E♭maj7 – F7 – Gm7 – Aø7 – B♭maj7): I is the plain ii–V–I already in the key (Cm7–F7–B♭maj7); ii (Cm7) ← minor cadence Dø7–G7♭9–Cm; iii (Dm7) ← Eø7–A7 (both chords land outside the key); IV (E♭maj7) ← Fm7–B♭7–E♭maj7; V (F7) ← Gm7–C7–F7; vi (Gm7) ← Aø7–D7♭9–Gm7; vii (Aø7, a minor-quality chord so it gets a *minor* cadence) ← B♭ø7–E7–Aø7. Practiced as a call-and-response quiz with a practice partner: name a key + scale degree, the answer is both the diatonic chord AND its secondary cadence.
- **Exercise 3 applied — reading a real tune, "I Should Care."** Walking the changes and flagging every secondary cadence that's actually present: Eø7–Dm7 (secondary cadence to the ii); Fm7–B♭7 does NOT resolve to E♭maj7 where the key would expect, so it's flagged as *not* functioning as a real secondary cadence despite looking like one; Bø7–E7 likewise doesn't resolve to Am, also not functioning; Gm7–C7–Fmaj7 (secondary cadence to the IV); Bø7–E7–Am (secondary cadence to the vi, later in the form). Stated payoff: once you've identified a cadence type in one tune you recognize the identical shape instantly in a different tune, in a different key — this is explicitly how you get faster at learning new standards.
- **Exercise 4 — transpose a whole song by function, never by blind interval.** Don't move a chord "up a major third" — instead identify each chord's scale-degree/function first (memorize the tune well enough that you know degree + function, not just chord names), then rebuild the identical function sequence in the new key. Worked example, C major → F major: original progression [C] → (secondary cadence to IV) Gm7–C7–F maj7 → Fm6 → back to tonic C; transposed to F major: F → (secondary cadence to IV) Cm7–F7–B♭maj7 → B♭m6 → back to tonic F. Why it matters: you rarely have "a B♭maj7 sound" memorized by ear, but you reliably know what "the IV chord of a major key" sounds like — thinking in function links the transposition directly to your ear instead of to arithmetic.
- **Exercise 5 — transpose the melody too, using melody-note-vs-chord-tone relationships.** Analyze each melody note as an interval against its underlying chord (not just against the key) before transposing. Worked on **"All the Things You Are"** (key A♭): melody starts on the root of the key but the **3rd of the tonic chord**; next note is the 4th of the key but again the **3rd of the (IV) chord**; on the dominant, the melody sits on the **3rd of the dominant chord** (flagged as unusually rare — most standards avoid putting the 3rd of a dominant in the melody); tonic chord gets the **major 7th** in the melody; on the return to the IV-family chord the melody again lands on the **3rd** (i.e. the 6th of the key — called out as a very common landing spot over a subdominant); then a modulation section: 7th, then 3rd on the G7, landing on the clear 3rd over the C major target. Same tune retransposed to F: Dm7–Gm7–C7–Fmaj7–B♭maj7–E7(targeting A major as the 3rd-degree modulation), rebuilding the melody note-by-note using the same chord-tone relationships rather than a blind interval shift. Stated payoff: standards reuse the *same* melody-note/chord-tone relationship constantly (3rd of the tonic, 3rd on the IV), so recognizing the pattern once makes harmonizing and reharmonizing dramatically faster.
- **Reharmonizing "All the Things You Are" from the melody-note insight above.** Since the melody sits on the **3rd of the dominant chord** at that spot (an unusual, exposed note), that spot is very often reharmonized *away* from the plain dominant. Concrete substitution given: the plain E♭7 gets replaced with a **♯4 diminished chord** — in A♭, that's **D half-diminished** — which can itself be expanded into a full secondary ii–V (**Dø7 → G7 → A♭**) instead of the plain dominant. Framed as a generalizable move: whenever the melody carries the 3rd of a dominant chord, try substituting the sharp-4 diminished/half-diminished (or its ii–V expansion) for the plain dominant — you don't need to reason about whether "it's really an E♭7"; the melody note alone tells you the substitute chord works.
- **Closing note (not developed in this video):** he flags **solo-phrase analysis** — running the same kind of theory-recognition drill against transcribed improvised lines rather than just chord changes — as a natural sixth exercise and a topic for a future video.
