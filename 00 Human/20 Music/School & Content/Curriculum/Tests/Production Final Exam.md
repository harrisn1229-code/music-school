>
> **Q53.** **A limiter is a compressor crushing so hard there's nothing left for the engineer**
> *Rubric (5):* says a limiter is the same device at an extreme setting rather than a special mastering object (2) · says what it removes is the dynamic range at the top, which is what mastering works with (2) · concludes that the 2-bus rule is a consequence rather than an independent rule to memorise (1)
> Treat a limiter as its own category and the 2-bus rule is arbitrary — one more thing on a checklist, and one that competes with the obvious fact that masters are loud. Treat it as a compressor at 20:1 and the rule stops needing to be remembered. What a heavy compressor does is reduce the distance between the loudest moments and everything else, and that distance is exactly the material a mastering engineer works with. Printing it means the decisions about the top of the mix have already been made, in the audio, irreversibly, by someone working without the reference material and monitoring the engineer has. The rest of the guidance falls out of the same fact: deliver peaks at −6 to −3 dBFS with a gain move rather than a processor, because a gain move is a scale change that anyone can undo, and send both versions if you want your loudness intent understood.
>
> **Q54.** **Commit the tuning, the timing edits and the distortion · leave the de-esser and the EQ**
> The character distortion commits; the corrective EQ stays open.
> *Rubric (5):* commits tuning, timing edits and character distortion, since each defines what the part is (2) · leaves the de-esser and corrective EQ open as relational or corrective decisions (2) · says the vocal case is harder because its corrective processing sits closer to the performance than an instrument's does (1)
> Run the test on each. Tuning changes what the vocal is — the pitches are the performance, and a mixing engineer receiving an untuned take is being handed a creative decision. Same for timing edits and for a character distortion, which is a sound rather than a treatment. The de-esser and the corrective EQ go the other way: both are answering the question of how the vocal sits, and both depend on what the compressor does afterward and on how the rest of the mix is balanced, which the engineer decides. What makes vocals harder than instruments is that this boundary runs closer to the performance. A saturated bass patch is obviously a sound; a de-esser is obviously a fix. But comping is both — you're choosing a performance, which is committing — and tuning sits right on the line, because the amount you tune is a stylistic decision. The resolution is the test rather than the category: tuning changes what the part is, so it commits, even though it feels like repair.
>
> **Q55.** **Both are the same signal twice, so there's no variation to hear**
> *Rubric (5):* says a copied double is one performance duplicated, and unison without detune is one waveform duplicated (2) · says summing identical signals only changes level (1) · concludes that thickness comes from small continuous differences between near-identical signals — timing, pitch, tone or phase (2)
> The two failures are the same failure at different scales. A copied vocal double is one performance played twice, so every consonant lands at the identical millisecond and every pitch wobble is shared; summing it changes the level and nothing else. Unison with detune at zero is one waveform played several times at the identical frequency, with exactly the same result. What produces thickness in both cases is disagreement: two performances differ in timing, pitch and tone because a singer can't repeat themselves precisely, and detuned voices differ in frequency so their phase relationship slides continuously. Either way the ear is receiving small, ongoing, unpredictable differences between things that are otherwise the same, and that's what it reads as more than one source. The general principle also explains the failed shortcuts. Delaying a copy or detuning a copy manufactures a *fixed* difference, and a fixed difference reads as an artefact — comb filtering, slapback — rather than as another voice.
>
> **Q56.** **Swap the sample · walk the sound ladder · change the waveform, since a filter can't add**
> *Rubric (5):* names the drum version — a weak kick wants a different sample, not more processing (2) · names the bass version, the sound ladder, or swapping a kick that can't survive a high-pass (2) · names the synthesis version, where a filter can only remove what the oscillator generated so a dull patch needs a different waveform (1)
> All three say the chain can refine and cannot transform. In Course 10 it's the opening claim: a kick that isn't hitting is nearly always the wrong sample, so bypass the channel and audition replacements before touching a device. In Course 11 it's the sound ladder — synth patch, different synth, real or sampled bass, DI plus amp sim — plus the sub decision, where a kick that falls apart under a high-pass is telling you it had nothing above the sub to begin with. In Course 13 it's the strongest version, because it's provable rather than merely advisable: a filter can only remove harmonics the oscillator generated, so a sine cannot be brightened by anything downstream at any setting. The shared principle is that the source sets the ceiling. The reason it needs stating three times is that violating it always feels productive — you are working, the sound is changing slightly, and the hour disappears.
>
> **Q57.** **Attention in one case, energy in the other — and both run out**
> The hierarchy allocates the listener's attention: only one part can be the thing being followed, so everything further from the lyric gets pushed back and processed harder to keep the lead in front. The energy curve allocates headroom of a different kind — a track that reaches maximum has nowhere left to go, which is why you start low and come back down after the apex.
>
> **Q58.** **Leave it open — it's relational, and it's exactly the kind of decision the engineer is for**
> Which source owns the sub is a statement about how the kick and the bass sit against each other, not about what either of them is. Removing the high-pass wouldn't change what the bass is, only how it fits, which is the test answering itself. Commit the 808's tuning and its character distortion; leave the ownership decision, and note it in the handoff message instead.
>
> **Q59.** **Saturation adds harmonics · a limiter deletes peaks · normalization just changes playback gain**
> *Rubric (5):* says saturation generates harmonic content and rounds peaks, so the average rises without the meter moving (2) · says a limiter removes dynamic range from the file permanently (1) · says normalization is a playback gain change that alters nothing in the file (1) · identifies the limiter as the trap, since it's the only one that deletes information you can't get back (1)
> All three change how loud something seems and only one of them is dangerous. Saturation works by adding: new harmonics at multiples of the fundamental, plus rounded transients, so the average level rises while the peak reading barely moves. Nothing is lost, and the added content is often the reason the part is audible on a small speaker at all. Normalization works by scaling: the platform measures integrated loudness and turns playback up or down to hit its target, which leaves the file untouched and can be undone by moving a fader. The limiter is the trap, because it works by deleting. It buys loudness by removing the peaks, that removal is printed, and normalization then turns the whole thing back down — so you arrive at the same playback level as a quieter master, minus the transients you spent to get there. Loudness that comes from adding or from scaling is cheap. Loudness that comes from subtracting is paid for once and never refunded.
>
> **Q60.** **Clearance first, the limiter second, the comp last**
> *Rubric (5):* puts clearance first because it decides whether the track can be released at all (2) · puts the limiter next as a delivery blocker that must be fixed before the file goes anywhere (1) · puts the comp last as the only one that's a matter of quality rather than of whether the track ships (1) · notes that clearance also has the longest lead time, so starting it early is what makes the other two worth doing (1)
> The order is set by what each problem can cost and how long it takes to resolve. Clearance comes first because it's the only one that can make the whole track unreleasable, because it can't be fixed after the fact — clearing post-release means negotiating with no leverage — and because it has by far the longest lead time. Identify the source, decide whether interpolation would drop the master side, and if it needs full clearance, decide now whether the song is worth a possible majority publishing split. Everything else is wasted effort until that's answered. The limiter comes second: it's a delivery problem rather than an existential one, it takes a minute to fix, and it has to be fixed before the file leaves, since the version you send is the version that gets mastered. The comp is last because it's a quality question with a cheap test — put it against your best single full take and keep whichever wins. Worth doing, and worth doing after you know the track has a future.
