# 6 Songs That Copy Beatles Songs

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bnA4Ws0B7Ls)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bnA4Ws0B7Ls)

## Synopsis

Six cases of post-Beatles songs borrowing from specific Beatles songs, spanning the full spectrum from unlitigated melodic similarity through granted-permission-plus-co-credit to explicit, celebrated quotation — a useful range calibration alongside the series' other rip-off catalogs.

## Techniques

- **Similar melodic contour, different scale degrees, never litigated**: The Offspring's "Why Don't You Get a Job" (1999) shares an upbeat rhythm and near-identical melodic contour with The Beatles' "Ob-La-Di, Ob-La-Da" — but the two climb different notes doing it (The Offspring climbs scale degrees 1-2-3, "Ob-La-Di" climbs the major triad 1-3-5), a useful worked distinction between "same shape" and "same notes." No legal action in the ~25 years since.
- **Two separate Beatles references in one song**: Oasis's "She's Electric" chorus is melodically close to "While My Guitar Gently Weeps" (near note-for-note in places), while the song's outro directly references the outro of "With a Little Help from My Friends" — two distinct borrows stacked in one track.
- **A melody quoted directly and made unmistakable on a repeat pass**: Frank Ocean's "White Ferrari" lifts a Beatles melodic moment subtly on first appearance, then makes the reference explicit by literally quoting the same Beatles line on the second verse — a "reveal the reference on repeat" structural device.
- **A claimed-unconscious borrow resolved via permission plus co-writing credit, no lawsuit**: Dead Can Dance's "Indus" (1996) closely resembles "Within You Without You," reportedly without the band being especially familiar with the Beatles song — possibly drawn to it via the shared use of Indian instrumentation rather than deliberate copying. After the similarity was noticed, Dead Can Dance contacted George Harrison directly; he granted permission to use the melody, with his publisher requiring a co-songwriter credit (implying an ongoing royalty) as the condition.
- **A shared rare chord progression plus a similar piano-arpeggio detail, judged as influence rather than infringement**: Radiohead's "Karma Police" and The Beatles' "Sexy Sadie" share an unusually rare chord progression, plus a similar (not identical) piano arpeggio pattern played over the G and F♯ chords in both songs. The video's own judgment: distinctive enough to notice, not distinctive enough to call plagiarism.
- **Deliberate, celebrated quotation as a distinct category from either infringement or unconscious influence**: Bad Religion's "You" directly quotes The Beatles' "We Can Work It Out" and nods to McCartney's solo track "Maybe I'm Amazed" via lyric — framed explicitly as quoting rather than copying, the same "homage, not theft" category as the Bittersweet Symphony-adjacent cases elsewhere in this series when the reference is clear and intentional rather than hidden.
