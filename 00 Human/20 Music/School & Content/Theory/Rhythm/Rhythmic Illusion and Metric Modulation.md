# Rhythmic Illusion and Metric Modulation

Part of [[Music Theory]]. **Rhythm as a perceptual phenomenon rather than a notational one** — how a
listener's downbeat can be placed somewhere the players never put it, and what a writer can do with
that. From Nick Clark's ~81,000-character "Rhythmic Explorations," which analyses **77 songs** across
13 techniques.

**What's already covered here, so this note doesn't repeat it.**
[[Time Signatures & Note Values]] already handles odd meters (5/4, 6/4, 7/4, 12/8), tuplets,
polyrhythm and polymeter definitions, and the strong/weak-beat hierarchy.
[[Syncopation and Metric Displacement]] handles syncopation, the push, hemiola, playing over the bar
line and displacement. [[Groove and Microtiming]] is the research-backed account of swing ratios and
humanisation.

**What this adds and they don't:** metric modulation as a first-class device, swing as a *percentage
scale* rather than two options, the two perceptual-trick chapters (misleading intros, syncopated
stabs), and an unusually large worked example bank.

## Metric modulation

His framing, and it is the best one-line definition of it in the library:

> "For the most part, what a key change is to harmony, metric modulation is to rhythm."

**Two clean types**, and separating them is the whole trick:

1. **The tempo changes; subdivision spacing stays constant.** The notes keep arriving at the same
   rate; the *grid* laid over them moves.
2. **The tempo stays constant; the subdivision changes.** The pulse is unmoved; what fills it isn't.

Worked, and this is the fullest example in the document — **Clipping, "Story 2"**: eighth notes hold
at 220 bpm while the meter climbs 3/8 → 4/8 → 5/8 → 6/8 → 7/8 → 8/8, so the *perceived* tempo drops
at every step. Then the vocals switch from 16ths to triplets at constant tempo, resetting the whole
climb at a new pulse (♪=330). Type 1 and type 2 in one song.

Others: **Tom Misch, "Tick Tock"** (triplets → straight 16ths at one tempo, type 2) · **Tigran
Hamasyan, "Drip"** (73 bpm constant, subdivisions cycling through 7s, 5s, triplets and 16ths — the
*spacing* changes because seven notes have to squeeze into the same gap three did) · **Sungazer,
"Macchina"** (existing 16ths regrouped in fives, effectively slowing to 4/5 tempo) · **Sungazer,
"Cytherean"** (quarter stays at 105 across a 7/16 → 15/16 switch, but 7/16 *feels* like 4/4 at 60
with septuplets and 15/16 like 3/4 at 84 with quintuplets — 84 is 7/5 of 60).

And one that crosses a track boundary: **King Gizzard, "Theia" → "The Silver Cord"** — 5/8 at 159
segues into 6/8 that sounds like the same tempo but is 166. Album-level rhythmic analysis.

**Do the arithmetic longhand.** This is the habit worth copying, because it converts directly into
DAW tempo values: 7/8 at ♩=210 → ♪=420 → 420/7 = **4/4 at 60 bpm with septuplets**. 11/16 at ♩=120 →
480 sixteenths → 480/11 ≈ **4/4 at 44 bpm with 11-tuplets**.

## Swing as a percentage, not a switch

[[Groove and Microtiming]] gives the research on where hip hop swing actually sits. This gives the
**scale**, which is the producer's frame — it is Ableton's Groove dial, not a conservatory's:

**Swing % = where the off-beat eighth lands between two quarters, as a fraction of the gap.**

| % | What it is |
|---|---|
| 50 | straight |
| 57 | 4/7 septuplet |
| 60 | 3/5 quintuplet |
| **66** | **triplet — "if someone says a song is swung, it is most likely a triplet swing"** |
| 71 | 5/7 septuplet |
| 75 | 16th-note swing |
| 80 | 4/5 quintuplet |
| 86 | 6/7 septuplet — very heavy |

Anything off 66% he files as a "Dilla" feel. Perceptual ceiling: quintuplets are hearable, septuplets
maybe, past that no. Notation convention: write straight eighths and mark the swing type at the top
of the chart.

Examples: triplet — Toto, "Rosanna." Quintuplet — Jacob Collier, "Time Alone With You" (*he counts it
off at the top*); Tennyson, "You." Between straight and quintuplet — The 1975, "Sincerity is Scary,"
hi-hat drifting ~50–60% and never reaching a triplet. Septuplet — Jesus Molina, "Soul Journey";
approximately, Kendrick Lamar, "How Much a Dollar Cost." **Knower, "Real Nice Moment"** — a live take
where the swing wanders from near-straight to near-triplet apparently at random.

## Dilla time, widened

Named for J Dilla's sequenced feel — kick slightly ahead, snare slightly behind, hats shuffling
"sloppy." The point he insists on:

> "Even though his style is said to have a 'sloppy' or 'drunk' feel to it, with the drum sequencer he
> was able to keep the timing consistent and deliberate, and not just randomly chaotic… Try not to
> confuse sloppy drumming with dilla timing."

⚠️ **He then widens the term well past standard usage** — to any off-grid beat, to any non-triplet
swing percentage, and to traditional South American, African and Indian grooves with intentional lag
or rush. He flags the drift himself. **Keep the school's narrower reading**: Dilla time is a specific
*deliberate, consistent* off-grid placement, which is what [[Groove and Microtiming]] and
[[RHY-C08 — Quantize, humanize, groove templates]] already teach.

Examples: J Dilla, *Donuts* · D'Angelo, *Voodoo* · Mac Miller, "New Faces v2" (tempo seems to
accelerate and decelerate each phrase but always lands back on 110) · Stromae, "Sante" (a charango
intro off-grid in the South American tradition, then a lagging lead against a rushing snare) ·
Sungazer, "Drunk" (4/4 with each beat in **9-tuplets** at ~53 bpm — the band thought of it as 3/4 at
160 with triplets; listeners just hear a drunk 4/4). **Rubato** is folded in here: Bon Iver, "715
creeks"; Debussy, "Arabesque"; Chopin, "Fantaisie Impromptu."

## The two perception chapters — his own categories

Neither of these has a standard textbook name, and both are directly usable in production.

### Misleading intros

*"When a song introduces a rhythm without any other context causing the listener to interpret it in
a certain way, and then when other instruments come in it makes the listener shift where they hear
the downbeat."* Nearest standard concept: metric ambiguity / an implied false downbeat. His term is
better because it names the *event*, not the state.

- **Zhu & Tame Impala, "My Life"** — the riff sounds like it lands on 1 and 3. The drums reveal it is
  on the **e of 2 and the e of 4**.
- **Louis Cole, "Weird Part of the Night"** — the riff actually starts one sixteenth *before* the
  downbeat.
- **Radiohead, "Pyramid Song"** — 4/4 at ~106 with a subtle triplet swing, chords spaced **3+3+4+3+3**
  across sixteen eighths, so a two-bar phrase reads (3+2+3) rather than (4+4).
- **Richard Spaven, "The Self"** — the accent pattern starts on the *second* sixteenth of beat 1, so
  the brain locks a downbeat one sixteenth late. **The fix: follow the snare, which is always on
  beat 3.** That is a transferable transcription method, not just an observation.
- Also: Knower, "Do Hot Girls Like Chords" · Tennyson, "Hard to Pretend" · Avishai Cohen,
  "Pinzin Kinzin."

### Syncopated stabs

Ensemble hits that make the listener lose the downbeat **while the band never does**. Mechanism: the
players subdivide internally — usually 16ths, triplets or a mix — and accent off-downbeat
subdivisions. He calls Louis Cole "the king of the technique."

- **Snarky Puppy, "Lingus"** — his closing example, chosen because it contains a bit of everything.
  Two bars of 4/4 at ~142, but the stab pattern pulls his ear to ~176 (5:4 of 142), reframing it as
  two and a half bars. He guesses **the drummer is counting 142 and Cory Henry is counting 176** —
  which is why Henry's run at 8:11 is plain eighths at 176 and would be three-notes-per-five-16ths at
  142, and why no other soloist plays it. And because there is no click, the band can nudge off-grid
  "to form a better illusion." **Imprecision serving the trick rather than undermining it** is the
  idea to take away.
- Also: Knower, "Do Hot Girls Like Chords" (2:10) and "Nightmare" (2:51, 3:38) · Clown Core,
  "Computers" and "Hell" · Tigran Hamasyan, "Drip" (1:58).

## Beat and subdivision groupings

Standard theory calls this **additive meter**; he never uses the term. The principle: *sixteen 16ths
in a 4/4 bar do not have to be four groups of four.*

- **Avishai Cohen, "Pinzin Kinzin"** — two bars = 32 sixteenths as **(4+5+4+5+4+5+5)**.
- **Nubiyan Twist, "Echoes"** — the most instructive. A bass riff repeats every seven eighths, but
  every third pass is cut to six: **(7+7+6) = 20 eighths = two bars of 5/4**. The snare stays on
  every third beat, holding the 5/4 grid underneath. Then every other pass drops its first note.
- **Snarky Puppy, "What About Me"** — four bars of 16ths as ((5×4)+(5×4)+4+(5×4)), riff starting on
  beat 2 and tying over the barline.
- Also: Billy Cobham, "Red Baron" · Varra, "Buggo II" · Jameszoo, "(Crumble)."

## Displacement vs. adding/subtracting beats

The distinction is clean and worth keeping: **displacement returns** to the original downbeat;
**adding or subtracting beats stays** in the new position. Compare
[[Syncopation and Metric Displacement]].

- Displacement — **RHCP, "Readymade"**: snare on 3 for four bars, then on 4 for four bars, then back;
  countable as (4+4+4+5)+(4+4+4+3) while everything else holds 4/4. Also Sungazer, "Macchina"
  (hi-hat holds 4/4 at 95 while kick and snare displace).
- Adding/subtracting — **Nate Smith, "Skip Step"**: one extra eighth every two bars, "hence the name
  of the song." **Herbie Hancock, "I Thought It Was You"**: a 2/4 bar inserted before the chorus.
  **All-American Rejects, "My Paper Heart"**: the verse's second bar drops two beats.

## Odd meters, with the useful bits

Full catalogue is in [[Time Signatures & Note Values]]. What this source adds is his framing —
**"high numbers are usually felt as a collection of lower numbers"** (11/4 = 6/4 + 5/4) — and that
the same signature is a different piece of music depending on accent grouping. Plus the far end of
the range, which is genuinely new material here:

| Meter | Song | The trick |
|---|---|---|
| 33/8 | **King Gizzard, "Catching Smoke"** | his favourite. Piano groups (8+8+8+9); drums play an 11-beat phrase ×3; melody groups in 11 spaced 3 beats apart. Three simultaneous readings of the same 33 — a genuine polymeter |
| 19/16 | Sungazer, "Threshold" | 4/4 with **19-tuplets at 33 bpm**, "just about as low as possible for our brain to keep track of." 19 is prime, so no other tempo describes it |
| 17/8 | Nate Smith, "Skip Step" | 4/4 + 4/4 + one eighth |
| 15/16 | Gene Koshinski, "Caleidoscopio" | he admits "I don't think a line can always be drawn between when to use 15/8 or 15/16" |
| 9/16 | Richard Spaven, "Fine Line" | 4/4 at 63 with each beat in 9-tuplets grouped (2+2+2+3) |
| flexible | **Willow, "Ancient Girl"** | a mapped sequence — 4×14/4, 15/4, 12/4, 15/4, 13/4, repeating once exactly. Standard term: **mixed meter**; he calls it "flexible time signature" |

## Polyrhythm vs. polymeter — the rule to keep

> "With polymeters, there is usually some type of polyrhythm occurring somewhere, but a polyrhythm
> doesn't always indicate a polymeter."

Best example: **Knower, "Like a Storm"** — two synths at **8:3**, 66 bpm against 176. The 20-beat
phrase at 66 sits against 53⅓ beats of the fast layer, so they only realign every eight phrases; the
vocal rides 66 and the drums ride 176. He ends the section admitting he can't fully count it and
asking readers to write in. Cf. [[RHY-C04 — Polyrhythm and hemiola]].

⚠️ He analyses hemiola repeatedly and **never once uses the word**. Same for *additive meter*,
*cross-rhythm* and *anacrusis*. The document is organised by what he hears, not by received
taxonomy — so translate into the school's vocabulary when quoting.

## The method underneath, which is the real takeaway

Three habits worth stealing outright:

1. **Report your own perception as data, including when it changes.** On "The Self": *"I don't think
   I could go back and hear it any other way, which is interesting to think about how our brains can
   completely block us from hearing something from a certain perspective."*
2. **Give two or three valid readings with the tempo maths for each**, then say which one you feel
   and why — instead of asserting one correct notation.
3. **Slow it down.** He could only feel one of the "Fine Line" groupings at ~70% speed. Some
   groupings only exist perceptually below a certain tempo.

And the claim that makes the whole document worth the time:

> "I can't say I felt a groove until I was confident in what was happening rhythmically."
