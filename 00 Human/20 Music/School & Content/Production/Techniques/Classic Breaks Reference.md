# Classic Breaks Reference

**Four more real, widely-sampled breaks worth knowing by name and by pattern** — not vocabulary to practise, just vocabulary to recognize, sample, or interpolate. These sit alongside the bigger-name breaks already documented on [[Genre Drum Patterns]] (Amen, Funky Drummer, When the Levee Breaks, Cold Sweat, Synthetic Substitution, Cissy Strut, Boom Bap, Planet Rock, Son Clave) — this note covers the four that were transcribed in [[Pocket Operations - Enrichment|Pocket Operations]]'s curated table but not yet written up on their own. Step patterns below are verified against the book's actual page images (not just its text layer), one correction found and applied (see The Big Beat, below).

Every pattern is one bar, 16-step grid, given in raw step numbers (1–16) and in 16th-note beat position (`1 e & a` etc.), per the vault's usual counting convention (see [[Rhythm and Groove]]).

## Impeach the President — The Honey Drippers (1973)

**Pattern** (Pocket Operations p.20 / PDF p.24): BD 1, 8, 9, 15 · SN 5, 13 · CH 1, 3, 5, 7, 8, 9, 13, 15 · OH 11.

In beat position: kick on **1**, **2-a**, **3**, **4-and**; snare on the standard **2** and **4**; closed hat plays a steady 8th-note pattern (downbeat + "and" of every beat) but drops the "and" of 3 and picks up an extra push on the "a" of 2 instead. The open hat fills exactly the hole the closed hat leaves — it lands alone on the **"and" of 3**, right before the snare answers on 4. That hand-off from closed to open on the last upbeat before the backbeat is the pattern's signature move — a small hi-hat swing that gives the whole bar its bounce.

**Origin.** Released in 1973, the single is credited to "The Honey Drippers" but was really Roy C. Hammond (Roy C) working with a group of students from Jamaica High School in Queens, NY. The song's lyric target was Nixon during Watergate — a protest single, not a dance record, and it went nowhere commercially on release.

**Why it recurs.** The break itself — tight, hi-hat-forward, built around that late-bar open-hat lift — got a second life once early hip-hop DJs and compilers (it's a staple of the *Ultimate Breaks & Beats* series) started digging it out. Marley Marl is credited with cracking it open for hip-hop via MC Shan's "The Bridge" (1985), after which it became one of the most reused breaks in the genre — reporting varies, but it's commonly cited at 800+ tracks, with some estimates putting it as high as one in five hip-hop records containing it somewhere. LL Cool J, Ice Cube, 2Pac, Prince, Wu-Tang Clan and Janet Jackson are among the artists who've used it.

## Hook and Sling (Part I) — Eddie Bo (1969)

**Pattern**, the "A" variant of four lettered versions in the book (Pocket Operations p.23 / PDF p.27): BD 1, 3, 10, 14, 15 · SN 5, 7, 8, 11, 13 · CY 1, 3, 4, 6, 9, 10, 12, 15.

In beat position: kick on **1**, **1-and**, **3-e**, **4-e**, **4-and**; snare crowds beats 2 and 4 with extra pushes — **2**, **2-and**, **2-a**, **3-and**, **4**; cymbal runs a syncopated, almost-continuous figure across the whole bar. It's a busier, more syncopated break than Impeach the President — the snare in particular is doing more work than a plain backbeat, which is the "drum solo" quality period write-ups point to. (Pocket Operations transcribes four lettered variants, A–D, of this break; only A is reproduced here — B, C and D are on the same book page if a fuller phrase-length version is ever wanted.)

**Origin.** A 1969 New Orleans funk 45 on the blue Scram/Seven B label, credited to Eddie Bo, built around his own "I got it!" ad-libs over an unusually elaborate drum pattern for the era.

**Why it recurs.** New Orleans funk drummers of that era played looser, more syncopated patterns than their Northern soul/funk counterparts, and this break is a prime example — it's been pulled into hip-hop, pop and electronic production for five-plus decades. DJ Shadow used it on "Lesson 4"; it turns up under Justin Timberlake's "SexyBack," Kanye West's "Good Friday," and LL Cool J's "Mama Said Knock You Out." Jay-Z's "Run This Town" (2009, feat. Rihanna, produced by Kanye West) was the subject of a copyright claim from Eddie Bo's estate/label over an alleged use of this same break — a reminder that "classic break" and "cleared for release" are two different questions (see [[Sample Clearance]]).

## The Big Beat — Billy Squier (1980)

**Pattern** (Pocket Operations p.21 / PDF p.25, corrected — see note below): BD 1, 4, 7, 9 · SN 5, 13 · CH 5, 13.

In beat position: kick on **1**, **1-a**, **2-and**, **3**; snare on the standard **2** and **4**; closed hat doubles the snare exactly, landing only on **2** and **4** alongside it — a thickened backbeat rather than a separate hat pattern. It's a plain kick figure with one syncopated push (the "a" of 1 into the "and" of 2) leading back to a square landing on 3 — the appeal isn't the pattern's complexity, it's the *sound* (see below).

> [!note] Correction to prior extraction
> [[Pocket Operations - Enrichment]]'s text-layer extraction originally labeled the third row **CY** (Cymbal). Verified against the book's actual page image for spec-161: the row is printed **CH** (Closed Hat), landing on the same two steps (5, 13) as the snare, not a separate cymbal part. Step numbers were correct in the prior extraction; only the instrument label was wrong.

**Origin.** From Billy Squier's 1980 debut album *Tale of the Tape*, a rock single that didn't chart. The break itself — drummer Bobby Chouinard's pattern, overdubbed with Squier himself beating a snare case with his hands for extra thump — has a huge, booming quality unusual for a rock record of that period.

**Why it recurs.** Early hip-hop producers and DJs found the huge drum sound and looped it, turning an obscure rock B-side moment into one of the most sampled breaks in hip-hop history — commonly cited around 300 recordings as of 2020. Run-DMC, Jay-Z and Dizzee Rascal are among the artists who've used it; King Just's "Warrior's Drum" is a well-known example. It's a good illustration that "classic break" isn't a genre category — a straight-up rock record can carry a break as reusable as any funk 45.

## More Bounce to the Ounce — Zapp (1980)

**Pattern** (Pocket Operations p.39 / PDF p.43): CH 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15 · OH 1 · HC 5, 13 · SN 5, 13 · BD 1, 9, 10.

In beat position: kick on **1**, **3**, **3-e**; snare on the standard **2** and **4**, doubled by the undocumented `HC` row (see [[Pocket Operations - Enrichment|Pocket Operations]]'s notation-legend note — likely a second hand-clap voice) on the same two hits; closed hat plays a continuous straight-8th run from the "and" of 1 through the "and" of 4, covering every remaining 8th-note pulse in the bar — except the very first one. That opening downbeat is handed to the open hat instead, which plays alone on **beat 1** before the closed hat takes over for the rest of the bar. This is the electro-funk engine-room pattern — steady 8th-note hat motion under a simple kick/snare/clap skeleton, built to sit under Roger Troutman's talk-box lead rather than to be the foreground element.

**Origin.** The lead single from Zapp's self-titled 1980 debut album, produced by Roger Troutman and Bootsy Collins. It peaked at #2 on the Billboard Soul Singles chart and #19 on the Dance Club chart, and the album went gold.

**Why it recurs.** It's one of the most sampled tracks in hip-hop history — WhoSampled lists it on 347+ songs — and its influence runs through both coasts: EPMD's "You Gots to Chill," MC Breed's "Ain't No Future in Yo Frontin'," and The Notorious B.I.G.'s "Goin' Back to Cali" all draw on it. More specifically, Zapp's sound (this track especially) is one of the direct sonic ancestors of West Coast G-funk in the early-to-mid '90s — the talk-box lead and this drum pattern's laid-back bounce are part of what G-funk producers were reaching for.
