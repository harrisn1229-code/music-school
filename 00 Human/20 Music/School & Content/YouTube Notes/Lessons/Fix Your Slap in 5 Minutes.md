## What's taught

- **Diagnosis: "sloppy" slap can be pure hand-mechanics, not a groove/timing problem.** Josh explicitly separates the two — Matt's quarter-note feel and consistency were already solid; the sloppiness came entirely from thumb angle, anchoring, and muting, which is why the fix is mechanical rather than rhythmic.
- **Fix 1 — thumb angle: bring it closer to parallel to the strings.** A steeply angled thumb (pointing sharply down, or up) has a wide "hit box" that catches the adjacent string along with the target string (demonstrated live: aiming for the A string but also clipping the D string at a steep angle). It doesn't matter whether the thumb ends up angled slightly below or slightly above the string — what matters is narrowing the angle toward parallel so the strike zone shrinks to roughly one string's width. *(No specific degree given — "closer to parallel" is the actionable instruction, calibrate by ear/feel for reduced adjacent-string contact.)*
- **Fix 2 — lock the thumb's position relative to the hand; move the whole arm to cross strings.** The failure mode: the thumb "hunts" for the string while the hand stays static, which breaks muscle memory because the thumb has no fixed reference position. The fix: keep the hand's shape/relaxed posture constant, and move the **whole hand/arm assembly** to reach each string — the string-crossing motion is described as coming from the **shoulder and elbow** (down and up), not independent thumb movement.
- **Fix 3 — a small forward wrist bend prevents self-muting.** A flat wrist means the thumb slaps the string and then immediately rests back against it, killing the note right after triggering it. A little forward bend opens a small gap/hollow behind the string so the thumb clears it after the strike. Explicitly don't overdo this — too much causes "duck wrist" (named as its own problem); the target is *"just a tiny bit"* of bend. *(General starting point, not a measured angle.)*
- **Drill: E–A–D–G slap-mute, up and back down, strip out musical content.** Slap and mute each string in turn working up the string set and back down, listening for exactly one clean string ringing at a time — isolates the angle/anchoring fixes from any musical decision-making, same "pull the mechanical piece out and drill it alone" logic as the plucking-hand string-crossing drills in the companion video.
- **Applying it back to real playing: replay the riff as a pure accuracy exercise.** Bring the fixes into the same riff Matt played "before," consciously holding the narrower thumb angle and the locked hand-relative positioning through the whole thing, rather than drilling in isolation only.
- **Habit correction takes deliberate repetition, not one pass.** Josh's framing: the hand will keep drifting back to the old habitual angle, so it takes consciously, repeatedly correcting it ("hit yourself over and over") until the brain defaults to the new mechanics — not a one-time fix.
- **Audible payoff: less adjacent-string ringing almost immediately.** Reported live in the session as soon as the angle/positioning fixes were applied to the riff — a concrete, audible sign the mechanical fix is working, not just a felt one.
- **No reason to avoid slapping on the higher strings (D, G).** Matt admits he doesn't slap much with his thumb up there; Josh's response is there's no technical reason to avoid it — moving the slap motion around the full string set is normal, useful vocabulary, not something to correct away.
- **Format note (not a technique, but worth flagging):** live before/after coaching, same format as the companion plucking video — same host (Josh) and same "we'll pretend it's 5 minutes" framing, this time coaching a guest (Matt, from Source Audio) rather than a regular BassBuzz student.
