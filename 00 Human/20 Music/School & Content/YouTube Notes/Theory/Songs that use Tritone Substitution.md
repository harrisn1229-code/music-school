# Songs that use Tritone Substitution

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dSvO5Tb18tI)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dSvO5Tb18tI)

## Synopsis

A full-depth treatment of tritone substitution — the jazz reharmonization technique of swapping a dominant chord for the dominant built a tritone away — covering the shared-tritone mechanism that makes the swap work, its home turf in bossa nova standards, and several pop/rock songs that arrive at the same sound by ear without the jazz vocabulary behind it.

## Techniques

- **Definition and mechanism**: substitute a dominant chord (e.g., G7, the V of C) for the dominant chord built a tritone (6 semitones) away (D♭7) — C–G7–C becomes C–D♭7–C. **Why it still resolves convincingly**: any dominant 7th chord's characteristic tension comes from its internal tritone (in G7, that's F and B); a dominant chord built a tritone away from the original root will, by definition, contain that *exact same* tritone (D♭7 also contains F and B, just spelled/functioning differently) — so the pull-to-resolution is fully preserved even though the harmonic context and bass movement are completely different (chromatic descent into the tonic instead of a fifth-drop).
- **Origin case study — "Garota de Ipanema" ("The Girl from Ipanema," Antônio Carlos Jobim)**: the original recording (Pery Ribeiro) uses a plain, undoctored I–ii–V–I (Fmaj–Gm–G7–Fmaj-family); nearly every subsequent cover/arrangement tritone-subs the V chord, to the point that most published lead sheets now show the substituted version rather than the original. This use is cited as the likely direct influence behind the tritone sub in the *Wii Shop Channel* theme (itself an explicit bossa nova homage to Jobim's writing, melody and all).
- **"One Note Samba" (also Jobim, earliest recorded by João Gilberto)**: **two separate tritone subs in the same tune** — the V chord (F7, in the key of B♭) is subbed to B7; the vi chord's own dominant (a would-be D7 in a standard 3-6-2-5 turnaround, Dm7–G7–Cm7–F7) is instead subbed to D♭7. Stacking both subs turns the whole turnaround into a smooth, entirely chromatic-descending bassline — each root moving down by a single semitone, chord to chord.
- **Pop/rock examples, explicitly framed as "arrived at by ear, not theory"**: "It Keeps You Runnin'" (The Doobie Brothers) — same tritone-sub-of-a-3-6-2-5 device as "One Note Samba." "Toxic" (Britney Spears, written by Cathy Dennis and team) — in the minor key, a iii→ii→♭II chromatic descent readable as tritone subs of a 6-2-5. Bennett is explicit that he doubts songwriters like Michael McDonald or Britney Spears's writing team consciously thought "tritone substitution" — more likely they found the chromatically-descending dominant chain by ear because it sounds cool, and the theory term is a useful *lens* for understanding the mechanism after the fact, not necessarily the songwriter's own mental model. "Things We Said Today" (The Beatles) — the middle-eight's second rotation swaps what was E (the plain V) for B♭ — Bennett again notes the Beatles almost certainly never encountered the term "tritone substitution" despite a textbook-clean use of it.
