> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **Sing back a heard note · find a heard pitch on the instrument · sing a random pitch then find it**
> The third is the hardest and the most useful — it runs the loop in the direction transcription needs.
>
> **Q2.** **Because every later skill falls back on "sing it or find it" the moment recognition fails**
> *Rubric (5):* says it's the fallback when recognition fails (2) · names at least one skill that falls back on it — degrees, chords, transcription (1) · says that without it you're stuck rather than merely slower (2)
> Degrees, chord qualities and transcription all assume that when you can't *name* what you heard, you can still reproduce it and work from there. Without that, a failure of recognition ends the attempt instead of downgrading it — the difference between working something out slowly and not being able to start.
>
> **Q3.** **Home — rest, completion, stability — independent of the absolute pitch**
> *Also accepted:* the note that feels like home · resolution · where it wants to land
>
> **Q4.** **As the tonic of C major, and as scale degree 5 of F major**
> Same pitch, entirely different feeling. The feeling comes from the note's relationship to the key, not from the frequency, which is why relative hearing is enough.
>
> **Q5.** **Scale-based · progression-based · real-music-based**
> 1) Play a scale, stop one note short, sing the resolution. 2) Play a progression in one key, stop on a non-tonic chord, sing the resolution. 3) Flag the tonic through a song you know, then stop an unheard song at random and sing the resolution.
>
> **Q6.** **Producing the feeling trains it harder than recognising it**
> *Also accepted:* singing is active recall · it proves you have it rather than that you'd know it if you heard it
>
> **Q7.** **The song is unheard, and it's stopped at a random moment**
> Levels 1 and 2 use material you set up yourself, in a key you already established. Level 3 removes both supports.
>
> **Q8.** **Sing it, or find it on the instrument**
> That's the pitch-matching fallback from Q1, which is why the lesson puts it first.
>
> **Q9.** **Conscious access to something the ear already does unconsciously**
> *Rubric (5):* says the ear already infers the tonic automatically (1) · says the training makes that awareness conscious and reportable (2) · connects it to being able to *act* on it — sing it, name a degree against it, find the key (2)
> Hearing a tonic is why music sounds structured rather than like noise, and that happens whether or not you're trained. What's missing untrained is access: you can feel that a phrase resolved without being able to say which note did it, sing it on demand, or measure another note against it. The training converts a background process into something you can query mid-listen, which is what every later skill in the course calls into.
