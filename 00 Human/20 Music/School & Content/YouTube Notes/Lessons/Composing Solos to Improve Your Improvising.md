# Composing Solos to Improve Your Improvising

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3vyPIoIIoMY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3vyPIoIIoMY)

## Synopsis
Jack Ruch's full soup-to-nuts practice method for learning a new jazz standard and turning it into a written-out ("composed") solo etude, worked over the A section of "The Nearness of You" in the key of C major. The throughline is **one physical guitar position, all the way through**: comp the changes in one CAGED position, learn the melody in that same position, map every chord's 4-note arpeggio in that same position, and only then compose a solo — deliberately slow and out of time — by breaking the progression into small harmonic chunks (a 251, a 3-6-2-5 with a tritone sub, a secondary dominant) and reasoning from the harmony what altered chord tones to reach for at each one. It's the same chunk-then-connect discipline already documented on [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]], demonstrated end-to-end on a real standard, plus two harmony-analysis moves — a tritone-substituted 3-6-2-5 and resolving a ii-V to the iii chord via its upper-structure-triad relationship to I — that aren't yet on this vault's ii-V-I/Turnarounds pages in quite this combination. Most valuable idea in the video: **writing (composing) a solo, not improvising it live, as a deliberate intermediate step** — slow enough to reason about the harmony chord-by-chord, which is exactly the discipline that later shows up automatically when improvising for real.

## What's taught

- **The one-position-through-the-whole-tune method: comp the changes, learn the melody, and map the arpeggios all in the SAME single guitar position before ever composing a solo.** Explicit reasoning given for staying in one position at each stage: "this is going to help me see the connection between all these chords" and it "forces me to really learn the guitar one position at a time" — positions get tied together later, but each pass through a tune is deliberately confined to one physical spot on the neck. This applies the [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]] CAGED-adjacency idea to an entire song-learning workflow (comping AND melody AND arpeggios, not just the arpeggio stage). → [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]], [[Guitar Positions]]
- **Phrasing tip when learning a melody: reuse the melody's own rhythm, but substitute different note choices.** Stated as a direct fix for "struggling with phrasing" — playing new pitches in the exact rhythmic pattern of the tune's melody produces phrases that "fit perfectly within the feel of the song," a cheap way to borrow a standard's phrasing without borrowing its actual notes. General-purpose, not specific to this tune.
- **The 4-note arpeggio-mapping drill: play every chord's arpeggio (root-3rd-5th-7th, in whatever order the position gives) confined to one position, not for speed or time-feel but purely to internalize the chord tones** — "you don't have to do this particularly fast or really even in time, it's just a matter of internalizing these chord tones... the better it is to call upon these notes when you're soloing." Framed as a list of ingredients for a recipe: the arpeggio map itself isn't the music, it's the raw material the composed solo below is built from. Same drill, same framing, as [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]'s two-stage out-of-time/in-time method — this video shows only the out-of-time stage, then goes straight to composing rather than a timed voice-leading drill.
- **Composing (writing out slowly) a solo as a distinct intermediate step between arpeggio drilling and live improvising** — not the same activity as either. Ruch works out each phrase deliberately, chord tone by chord tone, reasoning about the harmony as he goes, rather than playing freely; the stated payoff is that "every time you do it you'll come up with different ways to connect the dots, and those connections can become pathways you can use when improvising." This composing-not-improvising framing is the video's real headline idea and complements [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]]'s method (which reduces *someone else's* recorded solo to reusable concepts) — here the "solo" being analyzed and built is one's own, composed from scratch against the harmony rather than transcribed from a record.
- **Chunk 1 — a major 2-5-1 landing on the IV, not the I: Cmaj7 (I) → Gm7–C7/C13 (ii–V) → Fmaj7 (IV).** Named explicitly as "a two-five-one cadence moving to the IV chord," i.e. the borrowed-vocabulary device documented on [[The ii-V-I]] and [[Secondary Dominants]] where any diatonic chord (here the IV) is treated as a temporary landing spot for a full ii-V unit. Composed line: walk the Gm7 arpeggio up in position, land on the 3rd of C7/C13 coming down, then add altered color (♯9, ♭9, ♭13) on the C7 specifically *because* it's the V of a 251 resolving to Fmaj7 — "a perfect situation to throw in some altered chord tones." → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]], [[Altered Chords]]
- **Chunk 2 — a passing diminished chord bridging IV back down to the vi-family/iii, worked with the diminished arpeggio's minor-third symmetry: Fmaj7 → F°7 (diminished passing chord) → Em7.** Ruch plays the F°7 chord tones through its diminished arpeggio, explicitly using the shape's minor-third rotation to move it around the fretboard ("I can move that up a minor third... move those diminished seventh arpeggios around the fretboard") before resolving the line onto the 3rd of the following Em7 — the same symmetric-dim7-arpeggio mechanic already documented in depth on [[Diminished Chords]], here used as connective tissue between two diatonic chords (IV to iii) rather than as a substitute for a dominant chord. → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]
- **Chunk 3 — a "three-six-two-five" turnaround unit with a tritone substitution on the VI7: Em7 (iii) → E♭7/D♯7 (tritone sub for the expected VI7, A7) → Dm7 (ii) → G7 (V).** Named directly as a "3-6-2-5" using a tritone substitution in place of the plain A7 — matches [[Turnarounds]]'s **Tritone subs** row (`Cmaj7 E♭7 Dm7 D♭7`) and the dim7/tritone-sub mechanics on [[Diminished Chords]] and [[The ii-V-I]], but applied specifically to the *6* chord of a 3-6-2-5 (an iii-launched turnaround unit) rather than the more commonly documented 1-6-2-5 launched from the I chord. Composed line: run the diminished-family arpeggio shapes available in the same position for E♭7, connect to a line resolving onto the 3rd of Em7. → [[Turnarounds]], [[Diminished Chords]], [[The ii-V-I]]
- **Chunk 4 — reusing a 251 lick shape a string set over, but retargeting the landing chord: the same Gm7-C7-Fmaj7 phrase shape from Chunk 1, moved down one string set, played instead over Dm7-G7-resolving-to-Em7 (ii-V-iii, not ii-V-I) — because Cmaj7 (I) and Em7 (iii) are interchangeable via the upper-structure-triad relationship.** Stated directly: "the upper structure triad of a C major seven chord is an E minor triad, so these two chords are almost interchangeable in this instance." This is the video's second distinct harmony-analysis move (after the tritone-sub 3-6-2-5) and isn't yet documented on [[Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords]] or [[The ii-V-I]] in this specific "borrow a I-chord line to land on iii instead" framing — most of what's already on those pages targets comping/soloing triad extraction from a single chord, not tonic-substitution via the shared upper-structure triad. → [[Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords]], [[The ii-V-I]], [[Chord Substitution]]
- **Chunk 5 (the turnaround/"first ending" of the A section) — a reusable "3-6-2-5-1" vocabulary line dropped in wholesale, then deliberately fragmented: "I could plug in just a piece of it, or I could take it apart and use it different ways."** Framed as the payoff of building up standard-vocabulary lines over common changes ahead of time: "if you play these types of songs a lot you'll kind of build up some vocabulary you can use" — the same "vocabulary transfers because the same changes recur constantly" logic already documented on [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]'s chunk-then-connect section and [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]. Practice tip embedded in the demonstration: deliberately practice using only a *fragment* of a memorized line, not just the whole thing verbatim, to build flexibility.
- **Chunk 6 — targeting a secondary dominant's chord tones on the way to its resolution: Em7 → A7 (V7/ii, a secondary dominant resolving to Dm) → Dm7 → G7.** Explicitly named as a functioning secondary dominant resolving to ii ("that A7 is going to a D minor, it's a secondary dominant chord... a functioning dominant five chord that's resolving"), matching [[Secondary Dominants]]'s V7/ii entry and [[The ii-V-I]]'s "insert a ii-V/secondary dominant wherever you're about to land" device. Composed line: play the A7 arpeggio/voicing available in position, target its 3rd, add a ♭9 specifically *because* it's a resolving secondary dominant (same reasoning pattern as Chunk 1's C7), then walk up to the 3rd of the following Dm7 and repeat sharp-9-to-flat-9 alteration logic into the final G7, resolving onto its 5th. → [[Secondary Dominants]], [[Altered Chords]], [[Guide Tones]]
- **The overall composing philosophy, stated as the closing takeaway: chord-tone-only soloing (the "basic guideline") works as a complete, usable solo on its own — the altered/outside notes are an optional layer added only once the underlying chord tones and arpeggio shapes are already secure.** "I could play a solo very inside the changes... just using the chord tones if I wanted to... once you can see those chord tones and see those arpeggio shapes, then you can start to add those altered notes when you understand the harmony." Mirrors [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]'s Step 4 ("outline the chord tones of every chord") as the prerequisite before its own Step 3 chromatic-tension layer. → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]
