# 6ths on Guitar: How to Map Them Across the Entire Fretboard

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dZGJaEeN5yE)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dZGJaEeN5yE)

## Synopsis

A single-interval deep-dive from Jared Borkowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared): rather than teaching a lick or a scale, the whole 10-minute lesson is a systematic method for taking one sound — the diatonic **6th** interval, played harmonically as a double-stop — and mapping it across the **entire fretboard** through all five CAGED scale-form positions of C major. The value here isn't the interval choice itself so much as the *process*: learn each position's plain scale fingering cold first, harmonize it into 6ths with a deliberately chosen (not default) fingering, improvise inside that one position, then connect positions together, alternating strict scale-fingering passes with loose improvising throughout. Jared frames the whole approach as generalizable — "extract a sound that I love and get control over it everywhere so I can be fully creative with it" — so the five-step build order here is reusable for any interval or lick a player wants to internalize across the whole neck, not just 6ths specifically.

## What's taught

- **[~0:00] The pitch — why bother mapping 6ths at all.** Jared plays a few 6th-interval examples up front and frames the whole lesson around one idea: if you like that harmonized sound, it's worth deliberately mapping across the fretboard rather than only playing it wherever it happens to fall under the hand.
- **[~0:40] The five positions to map: all five CAGED scale forms of C major, walked up the neck.** Uses his own "parent scales" printable PDF (free download, soundguitarlessons.com/scales) as the shape reference, but the C major forms are fully demonstrated in the video itself. The four named forms, in the order demonstrated, are **G, E, D, C** — preceded by one unnamed lowest position which, by standard CAGED sequencing, is almost certainly the **A-shape** (inferred from order/convention, not stated by name).
- **[~0:55] Step 1 of the drill — learn the plain scale-form fingering first, before touching the 6ths.** "Make sure you know this really well — just see it as a map on your fretboard." The harmonized-6ths work only starts once the single-note shape is solid.
- **[~1:05–2:20] Fingering is called out as the actual puzzle, not the shape.** Worked A-shape (first, unnamed) position example: beat 1 = finger 2 then 1; beat 2 = finger 3 then 1; beat 3 = finger 1 then 4; beat 4 breaks the expected symmetric pattern — **finger 3 then finger 2**, not 2-then-1 — deliberately hopping the first finger to the next spot rather than staying put, described as "an easy relaxed kind of just shift... not tense at all."
- **[~2:35] Ascending vs. descending bar asymmetry — intentional, not accidental.** The same two notes get barred going **up** the neck but are **not** barred coming back **down**, because ascending and descending feel physically different under the hand — "that is intentional."
- **[~2:55] G-shape position.** Named because the chord shape formed off the root at the bottom of the position is a G-chord shape (standard CAGED chord-to-scale naming logic).
- **[~3:35] G-shape "ideal" bar option.** Bar ahead of time at fret 5 to prep the coming finger change and avoid a hop — fingering with the bar held: last three beats = 3-2, then 3-1 (bar still held), then 4-1. Explicitly optional: "if you want to hop the finger and you're okay with a little gap there, that's fine."
- **[~4:15] E-shape position** — demonstrated with no special fingering caveats beyond "know this really well."
- **[~4:45] D-shape position — flagged directly as "kind of a trickier one for sixths,"** the hardest of the five to get clean; worth extra dedicated reps.
- **[~5:15] C-shape position (final, highest up the neck).** If it's physically too high on a given guitar, transpose the whole system to another key/position — it's a movable shape, "you translate the same thing to any position, changing keys, moving the shape to a different place." Bar fingering: 1-to-4, 3-1; again optional — "not essential but it's pretty slick feeling... ergonomic and nice."
- **[~6:20] Step 2 recap — the improvising phase, done position-by-position first.** Improvise independently *within each position* over a chord progression/backing track in the key (C major here), mixing in single notes and deliberately **targeting a melody note, then landing the 6th on/under it** — a "target first, then harmonize" approach rather than random double-stop noodling.
- **[~7:10] Step 3 — connecting positions together, once each is solid.** Crossing strings *within* one position is called out as the harder skill; moving *along* strings *between* positions is comparatively easy once each shape is known, because adjacent CAGED positions overlap physically — "it's not as hard as you think."
- **[~8:10] Step 4 — alternate strict and loose practice on an ongoing basis, not once.** Loosely improvise, then go back and strictly run the plain scale fingering up and down; the strict pass reveals what the loose pass is masking, and vice versa — cycle back and forth continually rather than treating it as a one-time sequence.
- **[~9:20] Homework, stated directly:** find your own backing track and repeat the whole five-step process (map the position → harmonize in 6ths → improvise per position → connect positions → alternate strict/loose) independently, in whatever key you're working in.
