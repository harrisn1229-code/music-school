# Disclosure: Faking "That Analog Sound" Using Automation & Variation (15 Tricks)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HEcJ8vHWk88)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HEcJ8vHWk88)

## Synopsis

An explicit, stated philosophy from Disclosure: "analog" character isn't gear, it's the *absence of repetition* — a mouse-programmed, all-software session can read as fully analog if nothing in it repeats identically. The whole video is a demonstration of that idea: a 909 hi-hat pattern with zero identical note lengths, several simultaneous micro-builds instead of one obvious riser, deliberately extreme and even accidentally-discovered distortion choices, and per-note-varying bitcrush. Their own framing: "I wanted the distortion, the delay, everything to sound like it's moving and like a jam... to me it sounds analog as hell."

## Techniques

- **No two hits identical, anywhere — the core "analog" trick.** Every note in a 909 hi-hat pattern has a different length (including triplets), with velocity mapped to volume — described as the single most time-intensive element in the whole track specifically because there isn't a single looped section anywhere in the song. The absence of any exact repetition, more than any single processing choice, is what reads as "alive"/analog. → [[Genre Drum Patterns]], [[Automation]]
- **Layer several small, independent builds simultaneously instead of one obvious riser.** In the same build section: a Ring Shifter building up on the hi-hat, a delay (Timeless-style, feedback/repeats increasing) building on the same hi-hat, a filtered/evolving reverb building on a clap, and two tambourines hitting randomly *before* the beat for no rhythmic reason — several different elements each doing their own small build at once, rather than one dedicated riser sample dropped in on top. The combined effect creates moments where the listener expects the beat to land and it deliberately doesn't. → [[Automation]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **Hide a literal reference to the vocal's main lyric/phrase inside the beat, purely for internal coherence — no listener needs to notice it.** A rhythmic or melodic echo of the song's key vocal phrase gets worked into the instrumental itself; stated motivation is purely that "everything serves a purpose," satisfaction from internal consistency rather than an audible payoff. Practical note: this kind of retrofit usually happens after the lyrics exist, so it means going back into an already-built beat to work it in.
- **Kick: very short/punchy body (fast release, minimal hold/decay) specifically to leave headroom for a large amount of sub-bass underneath**, layered with a separate "acoustic kick" sample on top for extra character — the short synth kick handles transient/impact, the sub handles weight, and the acoustic layer adds texture, each with a distinct job. → [[Kick Design]], [[Layering and Stacking]]
- **Automate a transient shaper's sustain across a drum hit's life, not just set it once** — pull sustain out on some passes, then bring it back in on others so "the space in the sample" reappears and disappears over time, then cut it entirely — a moving, rhythmic use of transient shaping rather than a static per-hit setting.
- **Treat the whole drum bus like a DJ effects mixer: build reverb up, cut bass, live-tweak EQ/reverb the way you'd ride a DJ mixer's filter/FX section** — explicitly credits a "DJ brain" mindset for treating mix-stage reverb/EQ as performative, momentary moves rather than fixed settings. Reinforced by **automating pre-delay to accelerate through a build** (pre-delay getting shorter/faster over the section) as an alternative way to create rising tension without an obvious sweep or riser. → [[Reverb]], [[Rhythm and Groove]]
- **"More distortion is more" — explicitly validated, not second-guessed, as a mixing philosophy for a maximalist drop.** An accidental distortion-style switch (meant to do something else) produced a much more chaotic drop character; rather than reverting it, the team ("Howard and my managers") agreed the more-extreme version was correct — restraint was the wrong call for that specific moment. Worth trying an "accident" before undoing it. → [[Saturation and Distortion]]
- **Decapitator reads as "analog" almost immediately, even before pushing it hard** — layering it with a bitcrusher (Decimort-style) on top compounds the effect further; a second Decapitator instance elsewhere in the same chain for a different distortion style. → [[Decapitator]], [[Decimort 2]]
- **RC-20's wobble effect, blended via its own internal Mix knob against the untouched signal, for a subtle pitch-wobble variation mostly audible on headphones** — rather than using the wobble at full wet, dialing its own mix control back keeps it as a barely-there character layer rather than an obvious effect. → [[RC-20 Retro Color]]
- **RC-20's bitcrusher "Flux" mode applies a different crush amount to every note automatically, instead of one static setting for the whole part** — described as functionally like having ten different bitcrushers each dialed differently, but happening per-note without manual automation — a built-in source of note-to-note variation for exactly the "nothing repeats identically" philosophy this whole video is built around. → [[RC-20 Retro Color]]
- **Duplicate a track purely to introduce variation between two similar parts, not to layer them together** — rather than reusing one instance for a repeated section, a second identical track gets its own independent tweaks so the "same" part differs slightly each time it plays.
- **A hardware-style Juno-106 has a punchier note-onset transient ("click" at the start of each note) than TAL's Juno emulation** — called out as a specific, if small, difference that matters to his ear; framed as a general pattern with vintage-style synths (Moog- and Juno-style instruments) having a better attack "entry point" than some software emulations. → [[Roland Juno-106]]
