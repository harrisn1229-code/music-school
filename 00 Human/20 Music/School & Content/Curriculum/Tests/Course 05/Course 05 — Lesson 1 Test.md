> **Q1.** **Two beats, and the dotted quarter gets the beat**
> Six eighth notes are written; they group into two dotted-quarter pulses.
>
> **Q2.** **The division count, not the beat count**
> Compound time signatures lie about this, which is the whole trap.
>
> **Q3.** **Divide by 3 — 9/8 is three beats, 12/8 is four**
> Both in dotted quarters.
>
> **Q4.** **Because each beat holds three divisions, and three of a value makes a dotted note**
> Three eighths is exactly a dotted quarter, so the beat unit can't be an undotted note.
>
> **Q5.** **Accenting a weak beat or an offbeat — emphasis where the meter doesn't expect it**
>
> **Q6.** **Accent a weak position · tie a weak-position note over the strong one · rest on the strong beat**
> Three routes to the same effect.
>
> **Q7.** **Beat 1 strongest, then 3, then the backbeats 2 and 4, then the offbeats between them**
> *Also accepted:* 1 > 3 > 2 and 4 > the "and"s
>
> **Q8.** **The meter has to stay in the listener's head — without it there's nothing to play against**
> *Rubric (5):* states the condition — an audible or implied steady pulse (2) · says syncopation is defined relative to expectation (2) · says that with no reference the same notes read as arbitrary rather than syncopated (1)
> Syncopation isn't a property of the notes themselves; it's a relationship between where a note lands and where the listener expected one. That expectation comes from the meter, so the meter has to be established and maintained — by other instruments, by the bass, or by sheer repetition. Remove it and the identical rhythm stops being syncopated and starts being formless, because there's no longer a grid for it to contradict.
>
> **Q9.** **Because the accent is what creates the syncopation, and velocity is what creates the accent**
> *Rubric (5):* says the first of the three forms is accenting a weak position (2) · says velocity is how a programmed part expresses accent (2) · notes that the notes' positions are unchanged, so the meter reference is untouched (1)
> Of the three forms, only two involve moving or removing notes. The first — accent a weak position — doesn't. In a programmed part, accent *is* velocity, so raising the velocity of the offbeat 16ths and lowering the on-beat ones puts the emphasis where the meter doesn't expect it while every note stays exactly where it was. It's arguably the cleanest case, because the underlying grid remains fully audible, which is precisely the condition Q8 says syncopation needs.
