# Songs that use the Wonderwall chord progression

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DfoM4EfHK0k)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DfoM4EfHK0k)

## Synopsis

One progression, several names (Wonderwall / Mad World / Imagine Dragons / "Plagal Cascade") — Bennett explains the descending-4ths mechanism behind the name, the Dorian borrowing that makes the IV chord major, and how harmonic rhythm alone (same chords, different pacing) changes whether the loop reads as a progression or a riff.

## Techniques

- **The progression: i–III–♭VII–IV** (Am–C–G–D, in A minor/Dorian).
- **Why "Plagal Cascade"**: after the opening i→III (a 3rd up), the rest of the loop is a chain of descending PERFECT 4THS — C→G, G→D, D→Am, three drops in a row. A plagal cadence (IV→I motion) is normally a single event; here it's cascaded, repeated across the whole loop.
- **The Dorian borrowing**: the IV chord (D) is major, not the diatonic D minor a plain natural-minor key would have — it requires the raised 6th degree (F♯) that Dorian has and natural minor doesn't, giving the progression its distinctive "bittersweet" straddle between major and minor.
- **Harmonic rhythm changes the character of the identical four chords**: one full bar per chord ("True Faith," New Order) reads as a straightforward progression. Half a bar per chord — double speed ("Run to You," Bryan Adams) — makes the same sequence read more like a riff/hook, with a sense of racing momentum; both fast examples also push the chord change slightly ahead of the barline for extra energy (syncopation/anticipation).
- **Songs**: "Mad World" (Tears for Fears / Gary Jules cover), "Wonderwall" (Oasis), "Radioactive" and "Monster" (Imagine Dragons — "Monster" reuses "Radioactive"'s progression, written for a video-game tie-in that likely wanted a similar feel without being a literal repeat), "True Faith" (New Order), "Run to You" (Bryan Adams).
