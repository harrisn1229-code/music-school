# Guide Tones

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Jazz Harmony]]. **The 3rd and the 7th carry the harmony. Everything else is bass, colour or doubling.**

This is the highest-leverage idea in the whole strand: two notes per chord are enough to state a progression, and following those two notes is how you voice-lead anything.

## Why the 3rd and the 7th

Take the four chord types you meet most:

| Chord | Notes | 3rd | 7th | What the pair tells you |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cmaj7 | C E G B | E | B | major 3rd + major 7th → **major** |
| C7 | C E G B♭ | E | B♭ | major 3rd + minor 7th → **dominant** |
| Cm7 | C E♭ G B♭ | E♭ | B♭ | minor 3rd + minor 7th → **minor** |
| Cm7♭5 | C E♭ G♭ B♭ | E♭ | B♭ | needs the ♭5 too — the exception |

**The root tells you nothing about quality.** The 5th tells you almost nothing — it's the first note dropped from a voicing precisely because it's the least informative. The 3rd says major or minor; the 7th says major, dominant or minor. Together they name the chord.

That's why a bass player and a horn can imply a full progression with no chordal instrument present at all.

## Guide tones move by step — that's the whole trick

Run the guide tones through a ii–V–I in C:

| Chord | 3rd | 7th |
|---|---|---|
| Dm7 | F | C |
| G7 | B | F |
| Cmaj7 | E | B |

Follow one line: **C → B → B.** Follow the other: **F → F → E.**

Nothing leaps. One voice holds while the other moves a half-step, then they swap. **The 7th of each chord becomes the 3rd of the next** — that's not a coincidence, it's what root motion by fifths does, and it's the mechanical reason ii–V–I sounds inevitable. → [[The ii-V-I]]

## Shell voicings

Play *only* the guide tones plus a root and you have a **shell voicing** — three notes, no 5th, no extensions:

- **Root – 3rd – 7th**, or
- **Root – 7th – 3rd** (7th below the 3rd; usually the better-sounding of the two)

Shells are the practical form of this idea. On guitar or piano they're easy to grab, they never sound muddy, and they leave the entire register above free for melody or for another player. On a dense arrangement they're often the *right* voicing rather than the simple one — a full 5-note chord under a busy vocal is usually the mistake.

Add extensions on top of a shell and you have the whole vocabulary: shell + 9th, shell + 13th. → [[Extended Chords]] · [[Chord Voicings]]

## Using it to write

**To voice-lead a progression:** write out the 3rds and 7ths of every chord first, as two horizontal lines. Fix any leap larger than a step by inverting the chord or choosing a different extension. Only then decide the full voicings. This is faster than voicing chord-by-chord and it produces smoother results, because you've solved the horizontal problem before the vertical one.

**To test a substitution:** a substitute chord that keeps the guide-tone lines stepwise is a good one. This is the concrete test behind [[Chord Substitution]] — the tritone sub works precisely because it *swaps* the guide tones rather than disturbing them.

**To thin an arrangement:** when a mix is crowded in the mids, cutting the chordal instrument to shells frees space without losing the harmony. The 5th and the doubled root were the notes doing the crowding. → [[Masking and Carving]]

## The one exception

**Half-diminished and diminished chords need the 5th.** Cm7♭5 and Cm7 have the same 3rd and 7th (E♭ and B♭); only the G♭ distinguishes them. Any voicing of a m7♭5 that drops the 5th states the wrong chord. → [[Diminished Chords]]

- **The guitar-mechanical proof of this whole page, worked as a moveable two-finger shape over a 12-bar blues: the "two-note wonder" — 3rd + ♭7th on just the D and G strings, no root, no 5th.** Danny Gatton's name for it; also called guide tones, diads, or chord-tone targeting elsewhere. Because every chord in a blues is a dominant 7 and a dominant 7's 3rd/♭7 sit a **tritone** apart, the whole I–IV–V of a blues in G collapses to **one shape sliding across three adjacent frets**: G7 (I) = F (♭7, D string fret 3) + B (3rd, G string fret 4); C7 (IV) = the identical shape slid **down one fret**: E (3rd, D string fret 2) + B♭ (♭7, G string fret 3); D7 (V) = the identical shape slid **up one fret**: F♯ (3rd, D string fret 4) + C (♭7, G string fret 5). Explicitly framed as a fix for "too much information" — a full bar chord has too many notes and eats space in a band mix, especially with keys or a second guitarist. **Chromatic slide-ins** (half step below, whole step below, or half step above) work as ornaments into the target diad. Stated directly as the guitar-mechanical version of this page's "a bass player and a horn can imply a full progression with no chordal instrument present at all" — the ear fuses the diad with whoever else (bass, keys) is covering the root. Song references for the same one-dominant-to-four-dominant motion: "Feelin' Alright" (Joe Cocker), "Mercy Mercy Mercy" (Joe Zawinul/Cannonball Adderley). **Soloing extension:** target whichever of the current chord's two guide tones ISN'T already inside the pentatonic box being soloed over — e.g. over G7 while playing G minor pentatonic, the ♭7 (F) is already in the box so it registers as nothing new, but the 3rd (B) sits outside it and is the note that actually announces "G7" to the ear; same logic gives E as the target over C7 and F♯ over D7. - **Chord-tone outlining, called "the be-all end-all" of knowing where you are in improvisation, worth weeks or months of dedicated practice, then deliberately forgotten.** Over a fast `Dmaj7 Bm7 Em7 A7` turnaround (two beats/chord), play only the chord tones of each chord in turn, phrased musically rather than mechanically — the physical, tempo-driven form of "aim at 3rds and 7ths, not roots": once chord-tone landing becomes unconscious, targeting stops being a conscious choice and starts being a reflex, which is what then makes the guide-tone-adjacent [[Chord-Tone Soloing|prolongation technique]] (collapsing several chords into one sustained function) sound intentional rather than accidental. Sequenced as one step in a five-step ladder (tonic-only → downbeat targeting → chromatic tension → this outlining step → continuous chord awareness) — full writeup on [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]. → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] — Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared, [How to improvise jazz guitar solos over a 1 6 2 5 chord progression](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7aQUQUGcUk)
- **Soloing over static-function chords: an AAAB phrasing structure that only requires targeting ONE chord's guide tone, not every chord's.** Over a I–vi–IV–V progression, treat I, vi, and IV as sharing "the same harmonic function" and play a short phrase idea loosely three times across them (the AAA part); only on the V — "the one that's actually different" — deliberately land a chord tone of THAT chord at the phrase's end (the B). Simplifies the more advanced version of the same idea: after mapping every chord's chord tones, solo completely freely (any scale — pentatonic, blues, major) and just make sure to resolve onto a chord tone at phrase boundaries, which is what keeps free/exploratory playing sounding intentional rather than random. → [[Chord Voicings]] — Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared, [Soloing, Theory, Fingerstyle, & More - My 10 Favorite Lessons of 2023](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xeFuUkMRBBM)
- **Extend the "track one line through the changes" idea from guide tones (3rds/7ths) to an altered-dominant's color tone — the extension itself can be the moving voice.** Worked over a major 2-5-1 in A (Bm7–E7alt–Amaj7): pick one altered extension of the V chord (♭9, ♯9, ♯11, or ♭13) as the top note, and it turns out to sit a half-step from a note in both the ii chord and the I chord, producing a built-in chromatic inner voice across all three chords — e.g. the ♯9 (G) gives an ascending line F♯ (Bm7) → G (E7♯9) → G♯ (Amaj7, the major 7th). Same device works for all four alterations (♭9 and ♯11 give descending lines, ♯9 and ♭13 give ascending ones). The tension of the altered chord in isolation only pays off because of this stepwise pull into the next chord — full extension-to-scale-degree mapping and all four worked lines on [[Altered Chords]]. → [[Altered Chords]], [[The ii-V-I]], [[Chord Voicings]] — Jack Ruch, [[Learn These Beautiful Altered Chords!]]
- **Identify (or communicate) a whole chord from a single top note by treating it as the leading tone — the practical, hum-it-to-a-bandmate version of guide-tone thinking.** Once a progression's shape is set, the fastest way to lock in each chord's voicing is to track ONE note — the leading tone, usually the top note — rather than the full stack; this is how one half of a songwriting pair actually communicates chords to the other, singing just that single top note and trusting it to imply the whole voicing underneath. → [[Chord Naming by Context]] — Chromeo (Sol State), [Chromeo Gives Chord Tips](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZYgam1P6QtQ)
