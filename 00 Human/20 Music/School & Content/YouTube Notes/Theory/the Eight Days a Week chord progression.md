# the "Eight Days a Week" chord progression

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5ANjO8ypYfQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5ANjO8ypYfQ)

## Synopsis

A deep-dive on one specific progression — I–II–IV–I with the second chord borrowed major instead of diatonic minor — tracing it through Beatles catalog and solo-career usage, then explaining the harmonic mechanism (temporary Lydian color / interrupted secondary-dominant cadence) and two contrasting voice-leading approaches to it.

## Techniques

- **I–II–IV–I, all four chords major** (D–E–G–D) — the borrowed II (E major instead of diatonic E minor) is the whole character of the progression: an unusually bright, uniform sound since every chord is major quality.
- **Two ways to hear the II chord:**
  - **Temporary Lydian** — E major contains G♯, which is D's raised 4th degree, so the progression briefly tips into D Lydian; the next chord (G, natural 4th) restores plain D major, giving a deliberate uplift-then-settle arc across the loop.
  - **Interrupted secondary dominant** — II functions as the V of V (E is a 5th below A, the key's real dominant), so the ear expects a II→V (E→A) resolution; instead the progression moves to IV (G), redirecting the expected pull rather than completing it — an interrupted cadence.
- **Voicing choice changes the character without changing the chords**: root-position throughout produces pure parallel motion (every note moves the same direction together) — reads as powerful/unified, more like one big riff than four distinct chords. An alternative voicing with a stepwise-descending top line (A→G♯→G→F♯) gives each chord more independent identity instead. Neither is "correct" — a deliberate choice between unity and voice independence.
- **Song examples**: The Beatles — "Eight Days a Week," "You Won't See Me," and "Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band" (title track, with dominant 7ths added to every chord for a bluesier variant); several Beatles solo-career tracks also reuse it. Queen's "It's a Kind of Magic" (the vocal melody leans directly into the raised-4th/Lydian color on the II chord). Also used in the *Undertale* and *Sonic* game soundtracks.
