# Breaking Up Voicings

Part of [[Jazz Harmony]] — a comping-texture device that sits next to [[Chord Voicings]] (which shapes to grab) and [[Jazz Comping — Rhythmic Motif and Walking Bassline]] (when to strike them): **once you've got a voicing, don't treat "strum all four notes together" as the only option.** A struck 4-note chord is really four separate notes you can deploy in any combination or order — treating the voicing as raw material for fragments and lines, not a single fixed event, opens up a lot of comping texture from shapes you already know.

## The core method
Take any known chord voicing — worked here on a **C major 7 drop-2 voicing rooted on the A string** (see [[Chord Voicings]] for the drop-2 family) — and instead of always striking all four notes at once, play subsets or orderings of them:
- **Outside notes, then inside notes** (or reversed) — split the voicing into its outer pair and inner pair, play one pair then the other.
- **Every other note** — skip through the voicing rather than reading it top-to-bottom or bottom-to-top.
- **Hold the top note as a pedal, play the remaining notes below it as a line** — turns a static chord into a moving inner-voice line under a sustained top note, a related idea to [[Chord Voicings]]'s common-tone-threading entry (Jack Ruch) but generated from breaking a single voicing apart rather than choosing extensions across a progression.

Once one drop-2 inversion is exhausted, **repeat the whole process on the chord's other inversions** — each inversion has its own four notes to recombine, multiplying the available material. **This generalizes to any voicing family**, not just drop-2 (shell, drop-3, quartal — see [[Chord Voicings]], [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]) — and is described as **especially effective on larger/denser chords**, where there are more notes and therefore more combinations to draw fragments from.

## Applying it to a progression
Run this over a basic progression — a **2-5-1** is given as the starting ground (see [[The ii-V-I]]) — in two stages:
1. **First pass:** pick the chord shapes for the whole progression, then find ways to break up *every* chord.
2. **Second pass:** dial it back — break up only a couple of the chords in the progression rather than all of them, which **increases the impact** of the broken voicings by contrast against chords still struck normally. Treat "which chords stay struck vs. which get broken" as its own arranging decision, not an all-or-nothing setting.

## Practice note: apply it to a narrow target, not everywhere at once
Rather than trying to internalize every combination across every voicing, pick one small fragment to drill — e.g. one specific way of breaking up chords, or one area of the neck — and hyperfocus on it for an extended stretch (a couple of months, sometimes closer to a year) until it starts coming out naturally in real playing, then move to the next fragment. A personal example given: applying the breaking-up idea specifically to **diminished chord inversions** (playing off each inversion of a diminished shape rather than always the same one) as a current focus area — same "isolate one narrow thing and grind it until it's automatic" logic as [[Etude-Based Vocabulary Practice]].
