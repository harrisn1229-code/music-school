# Songs that use the Backdoor Progression

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s1tDB79UEsQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s1tDB79UEsQ)

## Synopsis

Full treatment of the "backdoor progression" — iv7–♭VII7–I, a jazz-standard resolution that reaches the tonic via the minor iv and flat-7 dominant rather than the usual V7 — including its "backdoor dominant" shorthand form and its common pairing with a ii–V setup targeting the iv chord.

## Techniques

- **The core progression**: in the key of C — Fm7 (minor iv) → B♭7 (♭VII dominant) → C (tonic, often voiced Cmaj9 for a "soulful, flavorsome" color). A jazz-flavored alternative resolution to the plain V7→I perfect cadence.
- **Pop/R&B examples**: "Stay" (Rihanna) — Am7 (minor iv) → D (♭VII) in the key of E. "No Such Thing" (John Mayer) — the entire chorus is essentially one long backdoor progression. "Underground" (Ben Folds Five) — quick backdoor progression in the key of B. "I Want to Be Where You Are" (The Jackson 5) — Gm7→C7→Dmaj7 in D. "The Waiting Line" (Zero 7) — the song's *main* progression, in C, is a backdoor progression in full. "Lights Up" (Harry Styles) — chorus resolves what's otherwise an A♭ minor song to A♭ *major* via the backdoor move — effectively a minor-key song deliberately resolving major, backdoor-style.
- **Stevie Wonder's repeated use, with the ii–V-of-iv setup**: "Knock Me Off My Feet" (from *Songs in the Key of Life*) — backdoor progression (Fm7–B♭7–Cmaj7) in C, preceded by a **ii–V targeting the IV chord** (a 2-5 of F) that would normally resolve to major F — instead redirected to land on the *minor* iv (Fm) as the first backdoor chord, making the "2-5 of 4" flow directly into the backdoor progression's start. "Ordinary Pain" — same device again, in the key of F: B♭m7 (minor iv) → E♭9 (♭VII), preceded by the same ii-V-into-minor-iv setup.
- **Jazz standard usage**: "Misty" — backdoor progression guiding back to the tonic under the lyric "clinging to a cloud." "Just Friends" — Cm7→F7→Gmaj7 in G. "Stella by Starlight" — an unusual case where the backdoor move resolves not to the piece's ultimate tonic (C) but to a *local*, temporarily-tonicized G (the piece has modulated to G at that point in the form — common in older jazz tunes that wander through several keys) — Bennett labels this a "backdoor progression of five" (i.e., applied to the V chord as a local tonic rather than the true I).
- **The "backdoor dominant" shorthand — ♭VII7 resolving straight to I with no preceding minor-iv chord**: a lighter version of the same effect. Pop/rock examples: "New York State of Mind" (Billy Joel), "Layla" (Derek and the Dominos), "Again and Again and Again" (Wings), "You Are So Beautiful" (Billy Preston).
- **Major-iv variant**: "Midnight at the Oasis" uses the *major* IV chord (not minor) leading into the ♭VII7→I resolution — a related but distinct variant of the same backdoor effect, in the key of E♭ (A♭→D♭9→E♭maj7).
