# Songs that use 9th, 11th and 13th chords

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_GXLqQ6z6z8)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_GXLqQ6z6z8)

## Synopsis

A practical, notation-focused walk through upper chord extensions (9th/11th/13th) — the three quality flavors (dominant/minor/major), the "stack up, don't just add on" naming rule, and the single most useful reframe in the whole video: how 11th and 13th chords are almost always voiced with the 3rd and 5th dropped, which means they're really upper-structure triads over a bass note and are often easier to read, play, and remember as slash chords instead.

## Techniques

- **Three flavors of every upper extension** (9, 11, or 13): **dominant** (major 3rd + minor 7th — e.g. C9 = C7 with a 9th added), **minor** (minor 3rd + minor 7th — Cm9), **major** (major 3rd + major 7th — Cmaj9). Only the 3rd and 7th degree change between the three; the extension itself (9/11/13) is identical.
- **add9 vs. plain 9 — a real naming trap**: "add9" means literally add ONLY the 9th to a triad, no 7th (Cadd9 = C-E-G-D). A plain "9" chord (C9) requires stacking THROUGH the 7th to reach the 9th (C9 = C7add9, i.e. C-E-G-B♭-D). Same logic scales to 11 and 13: a true 11 chord stacks 7th-9th-11th; a true 13 chord stacks 7th-9th-11th-13th. **The rule has to be memorized, not derived from the name** — same category of convention as "m" = minor or "7" = dominant 7th. → [[Chord Naming by Context]].
- **Cadd9 as a common open guitar chord**: easily accessible on guitar as an open-position shape — "Wonderwall" (Oasis) is the reference example.
- **A fully-stacked 13 chord is dense and impractical** — 6 stacked notes create a muddy, hard-to-voice chord, which motivates the next technique.
- **Standard omission rule**: in real voicings, the 3rd and 5th of an 11 or 13 chord are routinely dropped, leaving mostly the 7th/9th/(11th/13th) stack. This means an 11 chord in practice often reduces to an upper-structure triad over a bass note.
- **Reconceptualizing extended chords as slash chords — the single most useful idea in this video.** A B♭11 with 3rd/5th dropped is functionally an A♭ triad over a B♭ bass — write/think "A♭/B♭" instead of "B♭11." "The Long and Winding Road" (The Beatles) — the actual voicing Paul McCartney plays is exactly this: A♭ triad, B♭ in the bass, no 3rd or 5th of the "true" B♭11 chord present. "Easy" (Commodores) — E♭11 similarly reduces to a cleaner slash-chord read. "Rock With You" (Michael Jackson, verse: E♭m9-A♭11-B♭11) — the PUBLISHED sheet music itself notates the 11 chords as slash chords (G♭/A♭, A♭/B♭) rather than "11," because it's easier to read and play. "Never Too Much" (Luther Vandross) — a whole run of adjacent 11 chords, also published as slash chords.
- **Slash-chord reframing can reveal a static right hand**: "Sunday Morning" (Maroon 5) loops Dm9–G13–Cmaj9; read as slash chords, Dm9 and G13 are BOTH an F major 7 chord (F-A-C-E), just over different bass notes (D, then G) — so a pianist's right hand barely moves between the first two chords, something that's invisible if you're reading "Dm9 → G13" but obvious once reframed as "Fmaj7/D → Fmaj7/G."
- **General songwriting-craft point**: the actual composer likely never thought "B♭11" — McCartney more plausibly thought "A♭ chord with a B♭ in the bass." Both framings describe the identical sound; knowing both vocabularies just means you can communicate with musicians who default to either one.
- **The 7♯9 "Hendrix chord"**: an altered dominant, a regular dominant 7 with a raised (sharp) 9th stacked on top. "Purple Haze" (Jimi Hendrix, E7♯9) is the namesake use, but predates Hendrix — "All Blues" (Miles Davis) uses the same chord, and is cited as the direct inspiration for Pink Floyd's Rick Wright using it in "Breathe" (Wright's own account: he heard and worked out the Miles Davis chord at home before using it in the song).
- **The 6/9 chord**: adds BOTH the 6th and 9th degrees to a triad, without the 7th — more accurately an "add6add9" than a true 9-family extension, since it skips the 7th-degree stacking rule entirely. "Horse With No Name" (America) opens on a D6/9.
