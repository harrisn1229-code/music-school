# Modulation (Key Change) (Changing Key)

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Keys]] / [[Chord Progressions]]. **Modulation** is moving from one [[Keys|key]] to another *within* a song. (Distinct from a one-chord [[Borrowed Chords|borrowing]], which colors the current key without leaving it, and from a [[Secondary Dominants|secondary dominant]], which only *tonicizes* a chord for a moment.)

## Common types
- **Parallel** — to the [[Parallel Minor|parallel]] key (same tonic, major↔minor): a mood flip that keeps the ear oriented. *(Beatles "Here There and Everywhere" bridge.)*
- **Relative** — to the relative major/minor (shares notes; see [[Keys]]): the smoothest change, often barely noticed.
- **Pivot-chord** — use a chord common to both keys as the hinge, then reinterpret it (the classical/jazz-smooth way).
- **Secondary-dominant / tonicization** — a V7/x that briefly makes x feel like home; string several together to travel around the [[Circle of Fifths]].
- **Direct ("truck-driver") modulation** — just jump up a step (semitone/whole-tone) with no preparation, usually for a final-chorus lift (the classic pop key change).
- **Common-tone** — hold one note and rebuild a new key's chord around it.

## How to hear/analyze it
- Watch for **new accidentals** that persist (not just passing color) and a chord that behaves like a **new V → I**.
- Map both sections in Roman numerals ([[Chord Progressions]]) and find the pivot: where does the same chord get two names?

## Diatonic vs. altered vs. enharmonic common-chord modulation
Three escalating versions of the pivot-chord technique:
- **Diatonic common-chord** — find a chord that's diatonic in BOTH the outgoing and incoming key (always an adjacent "related key" on the [[Circle of Fifths]]); the pivot is conventionally the last chord analyzable in the old key, and the first accidental appears in the very next chord.
- **Altered common-chord** — same pivot technique, but the pivot chord is itself an *altered* chord in the old key, new key, or both. The chord *type* doesn't change between keys, just its role.
- **Enharmonic modulation** — the chord's actual type changes via respelling. Only two ways this happens: (1) a **German augmented 6th** is enharmonically identical to a dominant 7th — approach it like a Ger.6, resolve it like a V7 (or vice versa; Beethoven used this). (2) A **fully-diminished 7th** is equidistant/symmetrical — inverting it yields another root-position fully-diminished 7th, so any fully-diminished leading-tone 7th is a pivot into **3 other possible keys** by respelling. → [[Augmented Sixth Chords]] — Toby W. Rush, [[Music Theory for Musicians and Normal People - Enrichment|Music Theory for Musicians and Normal People]], p.29, 44

## From videos

- **The core quantitative rule for how obvious a key change sounds: shared-note count between the two keys, not physical distance between the tonics.** Two keys sharing most of their notes → smooth, easy-to-miss modulation; two keys sharing few notes → stark, obvious shift, regardless of how close the tonics sit on the keyboard. Worked contrast: "Message in a Bottle" (The Police) moves C minor → A major sharing 6 of 7 notes — one of the smoothest possible key changes. "Love on Top" (Beyoncé) moves C major → D♭ major, a semitone apart on the keyboard but sharing only 2 of 7 notes (0 vs. 5 flats in the key signatures) — a stark, obvious jump despite the tiny physical distance. **Choosing a non-relative key deliberately for a lift**: "Zephyr Song" (Red Hot Chili Peppers) verse in A minor, chorus in D major — the "obvious" choice would be the relative major (C major, staying in one key throughout), but the actual modulation to D major gives the chorus a noticeable energy boost that the non-modulating version lacks. **Pivot-chord smoothing for a normally-obvious jump**: "Africa" (Toto) B major → F♯ minor via an A major chord that isn't diatonic in B major but is in F♯ minor. **Modulation as range management, not just color**: without its key change, "Message in a Bottle"'s pre-chorus melody would sit in a noticeably lower, lower-energy vocal range — the modulation partly exists to serve the vocal line's register. → David Bennett Music Theory, [[4 Songs you didn't notice change key]]
- **A chained sequence of modulations through closely-related keys, each pivoting on a single differing note.** "Happy Xmas (War Is Over)" (John Lennon) moves A major → D major → G major → back to D → back to A; each adjacent key pair shares all but one note, and the pivot happens right on that one differing note (e.g. G♮ replacing G♯ turns the A chord into an A7, cleanly pivoting into D). The whole chain reads as smooth precisely because no single hop is more than one note away from the previous key. → [[Chord Progressions]] (the 5-6-7 climbing-triad device from the same video) — David Bennett Music Theory, [[4 underappreciated John Lennon songs]]
- **A repeating chorus motif that never gets stale purely by re-modulating under it every 4 bars, PLUS the modulation that gets you back to the verse for free.** "Good Vibrations" (Beach Boys): the same 4-bar chorus motif restated three times, climbing G♭ major → A♭ major → B♭ major (a whole step each jump). The final key it lands on, B♭ major, is then re-heard as the **dominant (V) chord of the verse's key**, E♭ minor — so the climb doubles as setup for a clean perfect-cadence modulation straight back into verse 2, no extra transition material needed. A later chorus reverses the climb (B♭→A♭→G♭) instead, avoiding mechanical repetition of the earlier pattern. Verse itself modulates E♭ minor → its relative major G♭ (an Andalusian-cadence progression, i–♭VII–♭VI–V) at the end of every verse. → [[Chord Progressions]] § *The Andalusian Cadence*, [[Diatonic Modes]] (the chorus is in G♭ Mixolydian, not plain major — lowered 7th for a bluesier color) — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Analysing The Beach Boys' most ambitious song]]
- **"Clocks" (Coldplay): a downward key change, deliberately reversing the usual "modulate up for energy" convention, then banking that reversal for a bigger payoff on the way back.** Main progression is E♭ Mixolydian; the bridge drops a whole step to D♭ major — the move DOWN produces a muted, withdrawn feel rather than the lift an upward modulation usually adds, but that's the point: returning UP to E♭ at the bridge's end then reads as the exciting lift, because the energy was deliberately banked by descending first rather than spent immediately. → [[Diatonic Modes]] (Mixolydian) — David Bennett Music Theory, [How Coldplay use Music Theory](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1sL4KzKwwns)
- **Three George Harrison Beatles modulations, each a different mechanism.** "While My Guitar Gently Weeps": A minor verse → the SAME dominant chord (E7) that would normally resolve back to A minor instead resolves to its **parallel major**, A major, for the chorus — a brighter, more surprising choice than the more predictable shift to the relative major (C major); reverses the same way (E7 → A minor) going back into the next verse, the tonic-note stays fixed, only the tonality swings. "Something": C major verse modulates to **A major** for the chorus (a genuine surprise since the setup phrase had already established a C-major-resolving pattern the listener expects to repeat) — works because A major and C major are enharmonically close (C major is A minor's relative major, so this is functionally the same "add sharps" move as the Guitar Gently Weeps shift, from the opposite direction); the return trip is smoothed by a **shared melody note** (E, common to both the A major and C major chords) so the melody doesn't have to change shape at the pivot, plus a chromatic-vs.-diatonic descending line that "primes" the ear differently depending which key it's heading back into. "If I Needed Someone": verse in **A Mixolydian**, middle eight modulates to B minor — barely registers as a key change at all because A Mixolydian and B minor are modes of the same parent scale (D major) and share every note; the middle eight uses **B harmonic minor** specifically (raised 7th, F♯ major chord) for a clearer sense of "B minor is now home" than natural minor would give, then returns via a **pivot chord**: the B minor tonic doubles as the ii chord of the destination A Mixolydian, giving a genuine ii-V-I (Bm-E7-A) back into the verse key while simultaneously resolving the old one. → [[Modal Interchange]] (parallel-major swap), [[Harmonic Minor Modes]] (the raised-7th clarity mechanism) — David Bennett Music Theory, [[How George Harrison changes key in Beatles songs]]
- **Burt Bacharach's signature device across four songs: shift key by swapping exactly ONE note — the single note that distinguishes two closely related keys — rather than several notes/chords at once.** "Walk On By": A minor→D minor (B natural vs. B♭ is the whole modulation), reinforced by a borrowed D major chord that both colors the passage Dorian AND sets up a perfect cadence into the new key. "Do You Know the Way to San Jose": C major→E minor (F natural vs. F♯), ~6 bars, returning via a C half-diminished chord that leads back up to a diatonic-in-C D minor. "Close to You": the SAME one-note relationship (C major/E minor/shared-relative G major, all hinging on that one F) left deliberately ambiguous — no F sounds at all until the key is revealed — with the Carpenters' recording adding a piano intro specifically to make that ambiguity audible (absent from the original Richard Chamberlain version, where the ambiguity flies by in one bar). "This Guy's In Love With You": D♭ major→A♭ major mid-chorus (G♭ vs. G natural). → David Bennett Music Theory, [[Burt Bacharach's Clever Key Changes]]
- **Four pre-1980s "hidden in plain sight" modulations, chosen against the abrupt final-chorus cliché.** "Uptown Girl" (Billy Joel) modulates on EVERY chorus: verse→pre-chorus via chromatic modulation (V slides up a semitone into the new tonic), pre-chorus→chorus via a ii-V-I where the expected v chord resolves major instead, telegraphed by the melody moving D→D♯ (the destination key's leading tone). "Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds" (Beatles): pivot-chord modulation (a recurring D minor becomes the bridge) reinforced by a walking D-C-B♭ bass line, PLUS a simultaneous metric modulation (3/4→4/4) landing exactly on the new key. "We've Only Just Begun" (Carpenters): an otherwise-jarring E♭maj→Bm jump works because the melody holds one shared pitch (D, a maj7 in one chord and a chord tone in the other) across the transition — the ear tracks the note, not the chord. "God Only Knows" (Beach Boys): genuinely tonally ambiguous between A/D/E major for most of the song, resolved only briefly per section — the ambiguity is the point. Full mechanism detail on all four → [[4 Inventive Key Changes in Pop Music]]. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[4 Inventive Key Changes in Pop Music]]
- **A key change placed at the very START of a song (opening 8 bars), the inverse of pop's usual final-third placement — plus a worked example of the exact same chord flipping function entirely.** "If I Fell" (Beatles) opens D♭ major, uses a ♭II→I tritone substitution (D major resolving down to D♭ — see [[Tritone Substitution]]) twice in a row, priming the ear to expect that resolution both times — then the SAME D major chord betrays the pattern on its second appearance, pivoting UP via its own ii-V-I into D major as a genuine new key center instead of resolving down again. The modulation is "hidden in plain sight" specifically because the chord had already been trusted from its first (non-modulating) appearance. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[this Beatles song changes key in the first 8 bars]]
- **Five Queen modulations, each argued to reinforce the specific song's theme/mood rather than being complexity for its own sake — checked directly against the (weaker) same-key alternative.** "We Are the Champions": C minor → relative major E♭, then an abrupt **truck-driver modulation** up to F major right at the triumphant chorus. "The Show Must Go On": B minor → C♯ minor at the SECOND VERSE (an unusual placement), matching a "soldiering on" lyric. "Under Pressure": D major → G major (down, "more closed-in") for the reflective bridge, then back UP to D major at the bridge's climax for a triumphant lift — the down-then-up arc is the device. "Killer Queen": C minor → E♭ major, then TWO consecutive fifth-related jumps in the chorus (E♭→B♭→F), walking the circle of fifths — smooth because a 5th-related key change only alters one scale note. "Bohemian Rhapsody": the closing piano figure walks the same E♭→B♭→F circle-of-fifths path to land in a key never visited earlier in the song, exactly as the narrative's identity/belonging arc resolves. → [[Circle of Fifths]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[How Queen use key changes]]
- **Three Radiohead songs charting a band's modulation technique maturing across their catalog.** "Sulk" (1995) — a plain **truck-driver key change** (abrupt whole-step-up, no pivot, saved for the last third for an energy lift); explicitly a well-worn pop cliché ("nowadays... a cheap trick"), included as the contrast baseline. "Knives Out" (2001) — E minor 6 as a genuine **pivot chord**: foreign to the verse's key (C minor) but 3 of its 4 notes are a semitone from the diatonic chord it replaces AND simultaneously already fit the chorus's key (A minor) — so the "wrong" chord in the verse quietly prepares the ear for a key change between two keys (C minor → A minor) that otherwise share almost no notes. "Present Tense" (2016) — chained **common-tone modulation**: D♯ major → C minor → B major, each adjacent pair sharing 2–3 notes (reinforced by a sustained vocal note held across the first jump), landing on a B–E cadence that's genuinely tonally ambiguous (B Mixolydian vs. E major share the same 7 notes — see [[Modal Interchange]]/[[Diatonic Modes]] for the same "shared-notes, disputed-tonic" mechanism). — David Bennett Music Theory, [[How Radiohead use Key Changes]]
- **ii–V–I as the standard smooth-modulation tool**: play the destination key's ii-V-I and the ear reinterprets it as belonging to the new key, completing the move — the ii and I chords are flexible in quality, but the V must stay major/dominant to supply the leading-tone pull. Several Beatles/pop worked examples (including a pivot-chord case where the same two chords serve double duty as vi/II of the old key and ii/V of the new one) plus the jazz-standard chained-modulation use ("Giant Steps"). Full writeup and songs → [[The ii-V-I]] § *Using ii–V–I to modulate*. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Songs that use 2 5 1 chord progressions]]
- **A full modulation-type taxonomy drawn from a 186-song catalog study (every Beatles song checked for a key change — 54 qualified, 29%), each type anchored to 2-4 concrete songs.** Categories, by distance/mechanism: **up a semitone** (*If I Fell*, *And I Love Her*, *Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds*); **up a whole tone** (*Penny Lane* — three separate modulations in one song, *Day Tripper*, *I Am the Walrus*); **minor 3rd** (*Something*, *Here, There and Everywhere*, *The End*); **major 3rd** (*Sun King*); **closely related keys, a 4th/5th away, swapping only one sharp/flat** (*Strawberry Fields Forever*, *Help!*, *No One*) — flagged as the smoothest-sounding category since most notes stay shared; **tonicization**, a modulation brief enough (a couple of bars) that it arguably isn't a "real" key change (*From Me to You*'s middle 8); **parallel major/minor**, same tonic note, different key signature (*While My Guitar Gently Weeps*, *Michelle*) — the video's stance is this still counts as modulation even though the root doesn't move; **parallel mode shift**, same idea but between other mode pairs, not just major/minor (*Norwegian Wood*: Mixolydian A-section → Dorian, counted; *Hey Jude*: Ionian verse → Mixolydian coda, NOT counted — both "major-family" modes, judged too subtle to qualify); **relative major↔minor** (*Yesterday*, *We Can Work It Out*) — tested by whether the new minor key gets a *functional* V chord of its own, not just borrowed as vi; **relative mode pairs beyond major/minor** (*Lovely Rita*: Mixolydian↔major; *If I Needed Someone*: Mixolydian↔relative minor). Full song list per category, plus the video's reasoning on genuinely ambiguous cases (*Come Together*, *Cry Baby Cry*, *Piggies*) → [[Every Beatles key change EXPLAINED]]. — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Every Beatles key change EXPLAINED]]
- **For a tune whose key changes feel overwhelming to solo over: reduce each key zone to its root and drill the switch before drilling scales at all.** Precursor drill to [[Continuous Scale Exercise]]'s full position-mapped method, aimed at players who get lost in the changes before they're ready for that heavier system: find the key centers in the tune (e.g. "All the Things You Are"), then mentally collapse each multi-chord zone into one unit — "one piece of information" — regardless of how many chords it actually contains. Then, over a backing track or unaccompanied, **play only the root note (or a root/power chord) of each key center**, switching to the new key's root the instant the harmony changes — no scales, no licks, just the root pulse, root-root-root-root, then the new root at the change. This trains the ear/reflex to *feel* exactly when a key change happens and *hear* where the new root sits, independent of any scale-shape vocabulary — described as reliably unlocking the "I keep getting lost in the changes" problem. → [[Continuous Scale Exercise]], [[Vision-Driven Practice and the Overwhelm Reflex]] — Jared Borkowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared), ["How to not get overwhelmed with everything there is to practice?" • Q&A • 10 questions answered](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RncKYU_1M9o)
- **A brief, uncommon modulation UP to the fifth degree, pivoting through the IV-minor chord — "Stella By Starlight"'s only genuine key change in an otherwise non-modulating form.** Worked: **Fm → B♭m7 → E♭7 → F major** (B♭ up a 5th, functioning briefly as a new tonic) — mechanism stated directly: "four minor, when you go up [a 4th], is just the tonic of the new key as a minor chord," so the iv-minor chord doubles as the pivot into the visiting key, with the following dominant approaching the new tonic. Flagged as unusual ("not a very common thing to have a four-minor chord as a pivot in a modulation") but a real, named device — corroborated by a second standard doing the identical move: **"My Foolish Heart."** Named functionally-equivalent substitute for comping: a plain **Gm7–C7** (ii-V of the new key) works in place of the iv-minor pivot. The modulation is brief — the tune is "already back from the dominant" (cadencing home) almost immediately after arriving, which is why it reads as a passing visit rather than a real new section. **Same tune's opening also confirms the "sharp-4 diminished as a modulate-up-a-major-3rd device" already documented below** — cadencing on a ♯4° chord to jump a major 3rd into a new key (cited elsewhere doing exactly that: "I'm Old Fashioned," "I Love You" by Cole Porter) — direct genealogical link to the "modulate up a major 3rd" entry immediately below, from a companion Larsen video. Full chord-function map → [[🎵 VICTOR YOUNG - Stella By Starlight]]. → [[🎵 VICTOR YOUNG - Stella By Starlight]], [[Diminished Chords]], [[Secondary Subdominants]] — Jens Larsen, [[Analyzing a Standard - Stella By Starlight - Functional Harmony in Jazz -  Jazz Guitar Lesson]]
- **"Modulate up a major 3rd" named as a recurring, recognizable device across the standard repertoire, not unique to any one tune.** Worked on "All The Things You Are" (A♭ major's A section resolves up a major 3rd into C major via a G7 that's understood in A♭ as V7/iii — expected to resolve down to Cm — but redirected to resolve UP to C major instead); flagged as common enough to be worth recognizing on sight, with a short list of other tunes doing the same thing (rendered ambiguously in auto-captions, most plausibly a Cole Porter tune, Jerome Kern's own "I'm Old-Fashioned," and "Gone With The Wind" the standard — not all of those songs modulate the identical way, per the source). → [[🎵 JEROME KERN - All The Things You Are]] — Jens Larsen, [[Jens Larsen - All The Things You Are - Harmonic Analysis|All The Things You Are - Harmonic Analysis]]
- **When a pivot chord is genuinely ambiguous between two valid readings, the choice changes what you actually play over it — worked as a real either/or, not just an academic distinction.** Same A♭→C modulation as above: reading **G7 as the pivot** (still A♭'s own dominant right up to the moment of resolution) means playing it altered, from the target's harmonic minor (♭9/♭13) — "sounds the most smooth"; reading **D♭maj7 as the pivot** (the IV of A♭, reread as C's Neapolitan/♭II) means you're already "in" the new key by the time you reach the dominant, so it gets played as a plain major-scale V7 — called the less satisfying, "unclear" way to play the same transition. **General principle: save the modulation's "surprise" for the resolution, not the dominant** — an altered/tense dominant chord already telegraphs "this is modulating," so delaying the payoff to the landing chord reads better than tipping it early. Confirmed as the composer's likely intent by checking the melody: the tune's second occurrence of this exact move puts a ♭9 (leading tone) directly in the melody over the dominant, which would clash against a plain major reading. → [[🎵 JEROME KERN - All The Things You Are]], [[Secondary Dominants]] — Jens Larsen, [[Jens Larsen - All The Things You Are - Harmonic Analysis|All The Things You Are - Harmonic Analysis]]
- **A tonic-major-to-tonic-minor pivot used as connective tissue mid-phrase (not a full section-length modulation) — landing on a major tonic, then treating its parallel minor as an accepted "borrowed" color on the same root to slide into a new key a minor 3rd up.** General principle: once a key center is established, its parallel-minor tonic is available as a pivot chord, since "pieces with both major and minor progressions on the tonic" are common enough that the ear accepts the shift without it registering as a hard modulation. → [[Parallel Minor]], [[🎵 JEROME KERN - All The Things You Are]] — Jens Larsen, [[Jens Larsen - All The Things You Are - Harmonic Analysis|All The Things You Are - Harmonic Analysis]]
- **A deceptive ii-V that resolves to the expected minor chord's PARALLEL MAJOR instead — a reusable surprise-modulation device, used twice in the same tune.** A ii-V that primes the ear for a minor-quality resolution (vi, or a I→i target) instead lands on that chord's major-quality parallel — e.g. a G-major-key ii-V expected to resolve to E minor (vi) instead resolves to **E major**. General technique: hearing a minor-quality ii-V sets a strong expectation for a minor landing, so redirecting it to the parallel MAJOR of the expected chord is a reliable, transferable surprise-modulation move for original writing. → [[🎵 JEROME KERN - All The Things You Are]], [[Cadences]] — Jens Larsen, [[Jens Larsen - All The Things You Are - Harmonic Analysis|All The Things You Are - Harmonic Analysis]]
- **A one-note voice-leading pivot for a fast, low-machinery key exit — lower a single chord tone and a stable tonic-quality triad flips into a functioning altered dominant.** Worked example: lowering the 5th of an E-major triad (B→B♭) converts it into a chord functioning as **C7alt**, which then resolves to F minor — readable a second way as the tritone substitution of the "dominant of the dominant" (C7 subbing for the expected F♯7 → B7 → E chain). Either reading, the mechanism is the same: one altered/lowered chord tone converts a stable chord into an altered dominant with almost no harmonic "seam," useful whenever you need to exit a key in a single bar. → [[🎵 JEROME KERN - All The Things You Are]], [[Chord Substitution]] § *Tritone substitution* — Jens Larsen, [[Jens Larsen - All The Things You Are - Harmonic Analysis|All The Things You Are - Harmonic Analysis]]
- **The general modulation-analysis workflow, stated as a repeatable method rather than a one-off observation: find the pivot chord (the chord that reads sensibly in both the outgoing and incoming key), and treat a genuine modulation as something clearly audible** — contrasted directly with a tune that runs through a lot of different-LOOKING chords (borrowed chords, passing chords, substitutions) but never actually leaves its home key, i.e. harmonic busyness isn't the same thing as modulation. → [[Chord Progressions]] — Jens Larsen, [[Jens Larsen - All The Things You Are - Harmonic Analysis|All The Things You Are - Harmonic Analysis]]
- **A practice method for transposing a whole song by chord FUNCTION, never by blind interval — memorize the tune well enough to know each chord's scale degree and role, then rebuild the identical function sequence in the new key.** Worked example, C major → F major: original progression **[C] → (secondary cadence to IV) Gm7–C7–Fmaj7 → Fm6 → back to tonic C**; transposed by re-deriving the same functions in F major rather than shifting each chord by a fixed interval: **F → (secondary cadence to IV) Cm7–F7–B♭maj7 → B♭m6 → back to tonic F**. Explicit reasoning for why function-first beats interval-shifting: you rarely have "what a B♭maj7 sounds like" memorized by ear, but you reliably know what "the IV chord of a major key" sounds like — thinking in function keeps the transposition tied to your ear instead of to arithmetic. Same method then applied to the melody, not just the chords — analyze each melody note's interval against its own chord (not just the key) before moving it, since standards reuse the same melody-note/chord-tone relationship (e.g. the 3rd of the tonic, the 3rd over the IV) constantly once you're listening for it → [[Reharmonization]]. → [[Chords by Key]], [[Secondary Dominants]] — Jens Larsen, [[How To Practice And Use Music Theory in Jazz]]
- **A bridge modulating to the relative major, with a fretboard shortcut for finding it: three frets/a minor 3rd above the minor tonic.** Framed as the standard bridge move for a minor-key tune wanting real harmonic contrast ("we're so minor, we're so dark — let's go to the happy place") — the relative major shares the tonic-minor's key signature (same accidentals), so it's reached cheaply, via a 2-5 (`iv – V7 – Imaj`) built on the relative major's own ii-V rather than any distant pivot. A composition/analysis prompt pairs with it: check whether a bridge is visiting genuinely fresh harmonic territory, and whether the lyric mirrors that same shift in perspective before returning to the A section. → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Circle of Fifths]], [[🎵 SIGMUND ROMBERG - Softly As in a Morning Sunrise]] — Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared, [SOFTLY, AS IN A MORNING SUNRISE Guitar Lesson | #9 of Top 25 Jazz Standards for Guitar](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bO6QVbU32so)

Worked out over Miles Davis's "Solar" (five key centers across 12 bars: C melodic minor → F major → E♭ major → D♭ major → C harmonic minor). - **The dim7 as a modulation "wormhole" to the most distant possible key — worked live demo on guitar.** From C major, target the most distant key on [[Circle of Fifths|the circle of fifths]] (F♯, directly opposite C): start on the dim7 a half step below C (which would normally just resolve straight back to C) and instead resolve it to F♯/G♭ by choosing a different one of its four symmetric "exits." Same trick shown resolving to B major instead of an "expected" ii-chord target. This is a live worked run of the enharmonic-modulation math already documented above (the fully-diminished 7th's 4-way pivot) — land on any dim7, pick which note is the resolving tone and which direction (½ step up / whole step down) to send it, arrive smoothly in the distant key, then sit there before finding a way back. → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Circle of Fifths]] — Jared Borkowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared), [[The Most Mind Blowing Chord 🤯 How to use diminished chords — Guitar lesson on diminished 7th chords|The Most Mind Blowing Chord — How to use diminished chords]]
- **Borrow a dominant 7th to pivot into a new key a 5th away** — add a ♭7 to any diatonic chord in the current progression (e.g. the IV) to turn it into a dominant 7th; it now pulls down a perfect 5th into a genuinely new key instead of back to the original tonic, giving a smooth modulation with the added chromatic top-voice motion doing most of the transition work. → [[Secondary Dominants]] — You Suck at Producing, [You Suck at Music Theory No. 1: Dominance](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uaivGz5sryQ)
