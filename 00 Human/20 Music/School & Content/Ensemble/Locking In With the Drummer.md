# Locking In With the Drummer

BassBuzz's framing of what it calls "the biggest thing you can do as a bass player to make your band sound tight": consistently syncing with the drums, not just approximately or occasionally. Beginners and even intermediates commonly fail at this while believing they're succeeding, because they're only checking sync at the one moment that's easy to nail.

## Why beat 1 isn't enough

Everyone locks up on beat 1 without much effort, because that's the obvious reset point — it's the moment attention is naturally on the downbeat anyway. **The nuance beginners and intermediates miss is everything that happens *after* beat 1, inside the bar.** A player can be hitting the technically-correct notes and rhythms and still drift in and out of sync between downbeats — the listener can't necessarily say what's wrong, but it "gives the listener this feeling of like something's not quite right." Pro bassists stay locked to the drummer through the *whole* bar, not just its first beat.

## What "locked in" actually is (and isn't)

- **Not** perfect metronomic rigidity — this isn't about playing to an unchanging click. Not all music should be played to a click track, and a real drummer's time moves slightly over a song.
- **Is** tracking the drummer continuously, including through whatever natural tempo movement the drummer has, so bass and drums stay glued together even as the reference itself isn't perfectly static.
- **The payoff:** a locked band "feels solid, steady, and just like chugging along like a train on the tracks." **The failure:** when bass and drums drift apart it "feels disjointed" and "kind of train wrecks" — the listener can't relax into dancing or enjoying the song when the rhythm section isn't glued together, even if they can't articulate why.

## Reference examples: simple, on-the-beat lines as an ear-training tool

Basslines with simple, mostly-on-the-beat rhythms are the easiest place to actually *hear* drift, because there's nowhere to hide it in rhythmic complexity:
- **On the Road Again** (Willy Nelson) — in the original, every bass note lands right on the beat with the kick drum, with only slight natural human variation; a demoed beginner attempt (nominally the right notes/rhythms) audibly drifts in and out of sync across the bar despite nailing beat 1 every time.
- **Bad Moon Rising** (Creedence Clearwater Revival) and **Folsom Prison Blues** (Johnny Cash) — cited as further reference tracks showing the same tight kick-and-bass relationship.

## How to build the skill

- **Keep your ears/awareness open to the drums on every single beat** — not just beat 1 — is the actual mechanism. The goal is continuous listening, not periodic re-syncing.
- **Beginners fail this because all their attention is going into wrangling the instrument itself** — a completely understandable bottleneck, not a discipline failure. The fix is gradual: as playing the instrument itself gets less effortful, awareness naturally has more room to open up toward the rest of the band.
- **Start with simple, mostly-on-the-beat lines** (like the reference examples above) specifically so the instrument isn't the bottleneck — that frees attention to actually practice the listening skill itself, rather than the notes.
- Play along with a real drum track/recording while deliberately keeping ears open to the kick on every beat, not just checking in at the downbeat.
- **A dedicated isolate-the-kick drill, worked in graduated steps.** Play an open E string (or clap/slap if you don't have a bass) exactly matching just the kick drum's rhythm, across patterns of increasing difficulty: plain quarter notes (1‑2‑3‑4), a simpler syncopated pattern ("an easy start for a bass line"), then a busier one (**1, 2-&, 3, 1, &, 3, 1, &, 3**) — then apply the last pattern in a full rock groove play-along. **When timing feels shaky, listen to the drums instead of yourself** — specifically the hi-hat's steady quarter-note pulse as a timing anchor *and* the kick for the actual rhythm; between the two, "you're getting all the info you need to play along." → BassBuzz, [[How to Play Bass with a Drummer (Foolproof Beginner Blueprint)]]

## A different kind of departure: dancing around beat one, with support (Sting)

Distinct from this note's push/pull section above — push/pull is a timing *offset* while still hitting every beat; this is about **not hitting a beat at all**, and it's about placement within the bar, not microtiming. A "rules of bass" breakdown states the standard rule bluntly: "play on the one, for God's sake" — beat one is the anchor point the whole band locks to (the video points at BassBuzz's dedicated Bootsy Collins/"the one" video for the fuller case, now in the vault: [[Beginner Funk Bass Made Simple (Bootsy’s Funk Formula)]] / [[Bootsy's Funk Formula — On the One and the Funk Box Shape]]). **The break, worked on a real bass line:** The Police's "Roxanne" verse bass part largely avoids landing on beat one. That doesn't mean the one stops mattering — the **guitar** is hitting the one instead, so bass and guitar have effectively **swapped their usual reggae roles** (normally bass on the beat, guitar off the beat; flipped here). Sting is still *relating to* the one — the line is built to dance around a beat-one anchor that the guitar is supplying, not abandoning the anchor concept. **Verdict: hit the one most of the time**, same as this note's default lock-in target — but a deliberate off-the-one placement can work specifically **when another instrument in the arrangement is anchoring beat one for you**. That's the load-bearing condition: the effect depends on the supporting context (something else in the mix nailing beat one), not on the bass line working in isolation — a genuinely different case from this note's earlier "drift" failure mode, since here the departure from beat one is deliberate and covered, not an accident of losing the lock. → BassBuzz, [[The 10 Rules of Bass Playing (That Pros Break)]]

## Past consistency: intentional push and pull

Once lock-in is *consistent* — not occasional — the next-level move is to deliberately, slightly push the beat forward (more insistent, driving, "falling over the cliff" energy) or pull it back behind the beat (a laid-back, "fashionably late" feel), rather than always sitting exactly on it. This is a controlled, on-purpose deviation layered on top of a genuinely solid foundation — attempted without that foundation, it just reads as sloppy rather than intentional. Full mechanics of this move (usable offset ranges, why different band elements should disagree rather than shift together, worked genre examples) live on [[Pocket and Microtiming]] — this note covers the *prerequisite* skill (consistent lock) that has to be solid before push/pull is a usable tool rather than a mistake.
