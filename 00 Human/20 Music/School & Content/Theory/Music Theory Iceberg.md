# Music Theory Iceberg

Part of [[Music Theory]]. David Bennett's "iceberg meme" survey of theory concepts, ordered from universally-known (surface) to genuinely obscure (ocean floor) — a companion study-sequencing roadmap to [[Music Theory Concepts Tier List]], organized by *obscurity/depth* rather than by *importance*. Bennett's own framing: each layer assumes familiarity with everything above it, so this doubles as a rough learning order, deepest-last. Where a concept already has a dedicated vault note, this page links out rather than re-explaining; concepts with no existing home get a full paragraph here instead, since a survey video's individual "1-2 sentence" mentions don't each warrant a standalone page of their own.

## Surface layer — universal, near-invisible because everyone already knows them

Concert pitch (A = 440 Hz), the time signatures 4/4, 3/4, and 6/8, 12-tone equal temperament (tune the octaves, divide each into 12 equal steps — full writeup on [[Tuning and Temperament]]), the three standard accidentals (♮ ♯ ♭), the [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes|pentatonic scale]], treble and bass clef, major/minor tonality, and **power chords** (a root + 5th with no 3rd, so neither major nor minor — genuinely absent a 3rd rather than an ambiguous one).

## Tip of the iceberg — still mainstream, but a layer deeper

[[Harmonic Minor Modes|Harmonic minor]] (raise the natural minor's 7th degree for a stronger dominant), chord types beyond plain major/minor ([[Diminished Chords|diminished]], [[Augmented Chords|augmented]], [[Seventh Chords|7ths]], [[Chord Inversions|inversions]], [[Suspended Chords|sus chords]]), the [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes|blues scale]] (minor pentatonic + the ♭5 "blue note"), [[Circle of Fifths]], melodic **sequence** (repeat a phrase transposed up or down), and [[Cadences]] (perfect/authentic = V→I, plagal = IV→I).

## Under the surface — genuinely intermediate

Odd/non-4-4 time signatures, [[Diatonic Modes]] (Dorian, Mixolydian, Lydian, etc.), **modal interchange** (switching between parallel modes within a piece — [[Diatonic Modes]] § relative pairs covers a related idea), double accidentals (𝄫/𝄪 — flatten or sharpen twice), the whole-tone scale, **mixed meter** (a piece that regularly switches time signatures — "Good Morning Good Morning," The Beatles), [[Harmonic Minor Modes|melodic minor]] (raises both the 6th and 7th of natural minor), [[Extended Chords|upper chord extensions]] (9ths/11ths/13ths, altered or not), the alto/C clef (whichever staff line the clef's center passes through is "C" — same logic makes the treble clef a G-clef and the bass clef an F-clef), [[Neapolitan Sixth]], [[Augmented Sixth Chords]], the Hendrix chord and Picardy third (both already covered on [[Line Cliché]] / [[Cadences]] respectively from this batch), [[Altered Chords]], **polyrhythm** (e.g. 3-against-2, two consistent pulses at once), **tritone substitution** (swap a dominant chord for the dominant a tritone away — they share the same tritone, so the resolution still works), **quartal harmony** (chords built from stacked 4ths instead of 3rds — "Tarkus," Emerson, Lake & Palmer; the usual chord-naming system, built for tertian harmony, gets awkward here, hence lots of "sus4" labels), and **minor-scale modes** (modes built FROM melodic/harmonic minor rather than the major scale — e.g. Lydian dominant, the 4th mode of melodic minor, used in The Simpsons theme).

## Sinking deeper — where most players stop

**Metric modulation** — switching time signature while keeping a shared note value constant across the switch (e.g. 6/8 to 4/4, tying the two together by making an 8th note worth the same in both). **Polymeter** — two different time signatures playing simultaneously, so the parts drift out of sync and back into sync once a full rotation completes ("5/4," Gorillaz — 5/4 guitar riff against a 4/4 drum pattern). **Octatonic scale** — any 8-note scale, but most often one of two symmetric semitone/tone alternating scales; the tone-first version is also called the diminished scale (opens Radiohead's "Just"). **Double harmonic scales** — double harmonic major (major scale with a flattened 2nd AND 6th) and double harmonic/Hungarian minor (harmonic minor with the 4th also raised — also the 4th mode of double harmonic major). **Moo chord** — a major chord with the 2nd degree added (an add2), the name popularized by Steely Dan. **Polytonality** — genuinely more than one key sounding at once (Charles Ives — one voice pair in C major, another in B major, simultaneously). **Bebop scales** — a scale with one extra passing tone added, retroactively theorized from how bebop players (Charlie Parker, Miles Davis, John Coltrane) actually played rather than planned in advance by them; see [[Bebop Vocabulary]]. **The Tristan chord** — a specific half-diminished chord (spellable as F∅7) named for opening Wagner's *Tristan und Isolde* with tonal ambiguity instead of a clear key statement; widely cited as an early step toward atonality. **Atonality** — music that deliberately avoids any tonal center at all (Schoenberg is the standard reference point).

## Daylight doesn't reach down here

**Swing ratios** — the precise duration ratio between a swung 8th-note pair: 1:1 is straight (no swing); 2:1 reads as triplet swing; 3:1 as dotted-8th swing; 3:2 and 4:1 as two different flavors of quintuplet-based swing. Real players don't consciously target a ratio (and their ratio can drift mid-performance) — the concept is mainly useful for programming swing into a DAW. **The overtone/harmonic series** — any played note is actually a fundamental pitch plus a stack of quieter overtones above it, always in the same interval order (octave, 5th, 4th, major 3rd, minor 3rd...), which the ear fuses into hearing "one note"; deep enough into the series the overtones land on intervals (sub-minor 3rd, super-major 2nd) that don't even exist in standard 12-tone tuning. **24-TET** — 24-tone equal temperament, adding one microtonal note between every standard semitone (Ivan Wyschnegradsky wrote in this system); notated with half-sharp/half-flat accidentals. **A = 415 Hz** — "Baroque pitch," almost exactly a semitone below modern A440, used by period-performance players trying to approximate original tuning — NOT a genuine historical standard, just one of many tunings actually used. **Just/pure intonation** — tuning every interval to a pure simple-ratio relationship (see [[Tuning and Temperament]] for the full writeup, including why this breaks down across multiple keys on a fixed-pitch instrument). **Meantone temperament** — a historical alternative to 12-TET that preserves justly-tuned 3rds at the cost of a slightly compromised 5th; because each key's intervals were tuned individually rather than uniformly, different keys genuinely had different characters under meantone (unlike modern 12-TET, where every key sounds identical) — cited as the historical root of the folk belief that specific keys have specific "moods." **Nested tuplets** — a tuplet inside a tuplet (e.g. a triplet nested inside a quintuplet), and double-nested tuplets go a level further still; usually there's a simpler, more intuitive way to notate the same rhythm. **Neutral intervals** — a microtonal interval that sits exactly between two named intervals (e.g. a neutral 3rd, between a major and minor 3rd).

## The ocean floor — genuinely obscure

**Pitch = rhythm** — the mind-bending unifying idea: a pitch is just a rhythm too fast to perceive as a pulse (middle C = 261.53 Hz = 15,691.8 "beats" per minute); slow a pitch down far enough and it becomes an audible rhythmic pulse, and speed a polyrhythm up far enough (a 3-against-2 pulse = a justly-tuned perfect 5th's 3:2 ratio) and it becomes a pitched interval or chord. **A = 432 Hz** — explicitly flagged as pseudoscience (a claimed "resonates better with the universe" tuning standard with no legitimate acoustic basis), unlike the genuinely historical A415 above. **The "super ultra hyper mega meta Lydian scale"** — a joke-named but real construction: play Lydian, then upon reaching its 5th degree, start a NEW Lydian scale from there, and repeat — a scale that keeps getting brighter as it ascends. **Deutsch's scale illusion** — a stereo audio illusion where two different melodies, one per ear (same timbre required), fuse into a perceived THIRD melody neither ear actually heard alone. **Shepard tone** — the audio illusion of a pitch that seems to rise forever; achieved by stacking the same pitch class across many octaves as sine waves and continuously fading the top ones out as new ones fade in at the bottom, so the ear's attention just keeps handing off to a lower octave. **Irrational time signatures** — e.g. 7/12: since there's no such thing as a literal "12th note," the meaning is derived the same way any time signature works (divide a whole note by the bottom number, take that many per bar of the top number) — here, divide a whole note into 12ths (eighth-note triplets) and take 7 per bar. Never used as a whole piece's time signature — only meaningful mixed into a run of more ordinary meters. **Microtonal modulation** — a key change to a key a microtonal interval away from the starting key (e.g. E major to G-half-sharp major) — genuinely audible as "out of tune" against a fixed-pitch instrument like piano, since the destination key isn't reachable on a standard keyboard at all. **Pythagorean tuning** — a historical temperament (pre-dating meantone) that preserves justly-tuned 5ths instead of 3rds, at the cost of the 3rds. **Xenharmonic music** — music that divides the octave into some number of steps other than 12 (more, like 19 or 21, or fewer, like 7 or 9) — distinguished from ordinary microtonal music, which typically still treats 12-TET as the base and adds extra notes between the standard ones; xenharmonic music discards the 12-division premise entirely. **Negative harmony** — the idea that every chord in a key has a "negative" mirror chord carrying the identical amount of tension/resolution toward the tonic, reached from the opposite direction — e.g. in C major, the perfect cadence G7→C has a negative-harmony mirror in Fm6→C: both resolve with the same total amount of voice-leading motion into the tonic chord, just moving in opposite directions (G7's B resolves UP to C; Fm6's A♭ resolves DOWN to G).
