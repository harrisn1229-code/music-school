## What's taught

- **The planning sheet IS the tracking record — one spreadsheet, not two.** Directly answering a viewer question (Brad): his practice-routine planning sheet and his record of what he's actually practiced are the same document, not separate planning vs. logging systems.
- **Track milestones, never a practice journal/diary.** Explicitly rejects keeping a log of "what I practiced" — tried it before, found it useless because he'll never want to look back at a session-by-session diary. Instead the sheet tracks **milestone completion per exercise/vocabulary item** — a stated tempo target that's either hit (checked off) or not. The milestone *is* the record; there's no separate "what did I do today" entry.
- **Color-coded milestone grid, red = not started.** Cells that haven't been filled in/attempted yet are colored red, making it visually obvious at a glance which vocabulary items are still untouched vs. which have a checked-off tempo — this is what drives session-to-session choice: skip what's already mastered at its current tempo, go straight to what's still red or has the lowest completed milestone.
- **One clean pass at a tempo checks it off; the next visit must beat it, never repeat it.** E.g. major triad arpeggios checked off at 120 BPM — the next time that row comes up, the target becomes 125 BPM, not another rep at 120. Whatever tempo is currently listed in the cell is the active milestone being chased. (Same no-repeat-at-a-cleared-tempo discipline as the more detailed drill on [[Circle-of-Fourths One-Position Scale Drill]].)
- **Vocabulary grid structure: chord-tone/arpeggio types × circle of fourths, inside a fixed fret-position range.** The "Vocabulary" practice category (previewed in #1) runs "all arpeggios through the circle of fourths" as part of his **Single Location Improv Method** — playing all chord-tone formations and improvising within **three fret positions** to connect everything across the neck (the system underlying his course **"Nail the Changes"**). The chord-type list drilled this way: **major triad, minor triad, diminished triad, augmented triad, sus2, sus4, major 7, major 6, dominant 7, minor 7** — each type gets its own row/milestone-tempo tracked through all 12 keys via the circle of fourths.
- **Worked demo of the drill itself (on guitar, positions 4–5–6): major triad arpeggios through the full circle of fourths** — C → F → B♭ → A♭ (having just come from E♭) → D♭ (shapes start repeating here — there are only five distinct shapes across all 12 keys) → G♭ → back to C. Rules stated: keep it in time, track the tempo you clear it at.
- **Rebuild-from-scratch as a periodic practice-system habit.** Jared says he periodically tears his entire practice routine down and rebuilds it from zero, and is doing that now — treating the redesign process itself as worth documenting on camera, not just a private admin task.
- **"Finish the system before fully using it" — same discipline as #1.** Currently building the routine one piece at a time (guitar warm-up → 5-min vocal warm-up → vocabulary system work built first), letting the rest of the routine come online gradually rather than waiting for the whole thing to be perfect before starting.
- **Milestones over "get it down before moving on."** Direct answer to a viewer (Paulo) worried his practice approach — spending very little time on some things, hours on others — was wrong: it isn't, unless you're insisting on fully mastering something before moving on. Jared's stance: don't fully "get down" a song/course/skill before moving to the next thing — spread practice out to leverage **spaced learning and sleep-dependent consolidation**; small daily chunks beat marathon cramming. If a topic only gets its scheduled 5–10 minutes some days, treat that as a "minimum placeholder" (his analogy: texting a friend instead of a full dinner — still keeps the connection alive) rather than a failure.
- **Milestone-chasing as built-in novelty/motivation.** Because red/unfilled cells surface categories he rarely touches (his example: sus4 arpeggios), the tracking system naturally routes him toward understudied material instead of endlessly re-drilling what's already comfortable and starting to feel stale — framed as both a learning benefit (new neural pathways vs. re-ingraining) and a motivation/gamification effect.
- **Mindset framing (not guitar-specific, general practice psychology):** quotes *Creativity, Inc.* (Ed Catmull, on expecting creative work to be troublesome — "watch your butt, they're tricky") and *The War of Art* (Steven Pressfield's "the Resistance" — we feel resistance toward the very things we most want to do, because quality effort costs biologically-conserved attention/energy). Used to normalize practice sessions that feel like a slog even when the underlying system is sound.
