# Internalizing a Tune Through Repetition

Part of [[Song Practice]]'s practice-methodology material — a process for learning a new tune (a jazz standard from a fake book, or any song) that leans on sustained repetition over analysis-first study, with harmonic analysis and lyric-driven feel folded in as later passes rather than the starting point.

## The core process, in order
1. **Play the melody with the chords, off the page, over and over** until basic comfort sets in. No shortcuts — this is the same "melody + chords" starting point [[Chord Melody Arranging]] and [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] both assume as prerequisite knowledge of a tune.
2. **As comfort grows, start noticing different ways to play it** — alternate voicings, and where the passing chords in the tune actually are (the same passing-chord vocabulary [[Melodic-Harmonic Blending (Comping While Soloing)]]'s every-beat drill surfaces deliberately; here it emerges naturally from repetition instead).
3. **Listen to as many recorded versions of the tune as possible.** The stated payoff is specific: different versions carry a rough sense of the tune's own history/lineage, can be radically different from each other and genuinely inspiring, and are a real source of **passing chords and rhythmic approaches** that repetition alone won't surface — this step feeds back directly into step 2.
4. **Play it over and over until it becomes meditative** — described as needing **an hour or more** of sustained repetition before a tune genuinely "opens up." The framing is explicit: you have to unlock a tune's secrets, and the only way to do that is to actually internalize it through time spent, not through faster analysis.
5. **Once memorized (eyes off the page), bring in the lyrics as a separate, later pass.** Looking at the lyrics' phrasing and meaning is flagged as a genuine tangent away from harmonic thinking — it pulls attention from chords/notes toward the lyric's emotional content, which is a different creative source, not a distraction to avoid. A concrete effect: if the lyric's meaning calls for "opening up," that can translate directly into a musical choice — e.g. reaching for a more open-voiced chord at that moment — a vibe-driven harmonic decision arrived at through the words rather than through substitution theory. → [[Reharmonization]], [[Chord Substitution]]

## Why this order, not analysis-first
The process deliberately front-loads raw repetition (steps 1–2, mostly mechanical) before the two research-heavy inputs (recordings, lyrics) that require the tune to already be somewhat under the hands — comparing other versions or following lyric phrasing against a tune you can't yet play smoothly just adds cognitive load without payoff. Repetition itself is treated as the primary internalization mechanism; recordings and lyrics are additive color layered on afterward, not competing starting points.

## From videos

- **A transpose-to-C variant of tune internalization, offered as a general strategy and then shown producing an actual arrangement as a side effect:** transpose whatever tune you're learning to the key of C (a personal habit, stated as helpful independent of what key a resulting guitar arrangement lands in), play/learn the plain chords in that key, then **sing the melody over the chords** to internalize it — Jared's account of how his own "Georgia on My Mind" solo fingerstyle arrangement came to exist: he "simply was wanting to just strum around and play the song," moved it to C, "started playing the chords, really started understanding the chords under it and kind of singing the melody," and the arrangement "came to life on its own" from that process rather than being composed cold. Stated as mutually reinforcing in both directions: knowing a solo arrangement helps you remember the chords and the song better, and knowing the song itself (easy voicings + singing through it) helps you interpret a solo arrangement accurately. For choosing *which* key a resulting solo-guitar arrangement should actually be played in: pick whichever key makes the bass movements fall naturally under open strings/standard tuning, not necessarily the recording's key.
