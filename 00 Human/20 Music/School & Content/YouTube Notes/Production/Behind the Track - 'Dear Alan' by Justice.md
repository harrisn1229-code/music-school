# Behind the Track｜'Dear Alan' by Justice

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Nm--MSexnMY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Nm--MSexnMY)

## Synopsis

Justice (Gaspard Augé & Xavier de Rosnay) break down "Dear Alan," built around a Chris Rainbow vocal sample ("Dear Brian," a tribute to Brian Wilson), renamed as their own tribute to Alan Braxe. Emphasis throughout on additive layering where individual parts are deliberately meaningless alone, and a genuinely useful "happy accident" technique: pushing a synth-into-delay signal chain past the software's real-time calculation limit to generate real (not simulated) glitch artifacts.

## What's taught

- **Additive counterpoint layering where each individual part is deliberately uninteresting alone.** Multiple arpeggio layers are stacked one at a time, each one "so simple it doesn't say anything on its own" — the compositional payoff only appears once several deliberately-plain layers combine; explicitly framed as choosing simple/uninteresting parts on purpose because complexity emerges from the combination, not from any single part.
- **Keep an imperfect real performance over a "correct" programmed one, when the imperfection is what makes it groove.** The bassline was originally programmed, revealing that roughly half of what made it feel right was ghost notes; when re-recorded with a real bass player, the performance had small timing/feel imperfections the programmed version lacked — decision: keep the human take rather than "fixing" it back toward the programmed original, because "it sounds good."
- **Deliberately mismatched/improvised acoustic drum-mic technique for feel, not correctness.** Drums tracked to tape; the drummer used one stick for snare/some hats and his bare hand for the other hat, an ad-hoc technique adopted specifically because it produced the right sound in the moment — general philosophy stated: if a part needs something unconventional to sound right, do the unconventional thing (same drummer known for playing percussion with a bottle on other Justice tracks).
- **A genuine "happy accident" glitch technique: push a synth-through-delay chain past software's real-time calculation limit for real, unrepeatable glitches.** A hydrosynth (a mono synth) run through a digital delay, sped up in tempo until Ableton's delay could no longer calculate the delay taps in real time — the resulting glitches are a literal one-take recording of the software failing to keep up, not a simulated/designed glitch effect. Used deliberately for a specific dramatic goal: turning an otherwise "sunny" outro into something closer to "a feverish nightmare" — an example of using a genuine technical limitation as a compositional device once discovered, rather than treating it as a bug to avoid.
- **Remix-only-the-vocal composition method for discovering new chord possibilities.** When Justice remixes tracks, they often work from the isolated vocal alone, sometimes without listening to the original production at all — building entirely new harmony under a known melody this way repeatedly reveals how much a different chord progression changes the emotional read of the same melodic line, a discovery process they still lean on for original writing.
- **Resist over-conceptualizing a track that's already working.** Stated directly as a production/mixing philosophy: if a section already feels good, leave it — don't force additional justification, theory, or refinement onto something that doesn't need it.
