# The 10 Rules of Bass Playing (That Pros Break)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ul_93WTz_MQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ul_93WTz_MQ)

## Synopsis

BassBuzz's Josh runs a "rules of bass" format (explicitly credited as inspired by Mike at Art of Guitar's "10 rules guitarists break" video) — ten standard beginner-teacher rules, each paired with a named pro bassist who breaks it, followed by an honest verdict on whether breaking it actually helps or hurts. The format is consistently even-handed rather than "rules are dumb, break them all": every rule gets the *reason* it exists stated plainly, then the specific, narrow condition under which the named pro's exception works — and one rule (pinky fretting, Chris Squire) is flagged directly as the one genuine rule of the ten, where even the counter-example probably shouldn't be fully copied. The ten rules span technique (plucking force, one- vs. two-finger plucking, pinky use, thumb-over-neck, fingertip vs. flat-finger fretting), setup/posture (strap height), and musical judgment (staying in the low "money note" register vs. playing melodically higher, tone brightness/thinness, whether theory is required, and playing on vs. around beat one). Ten named pros anchor the ten rules: Geddy Lee (Rush), James Jamerson (Motown), Chris Squire (Yes), Louis Johnson ("Billie Jean"), Rocco Prestia (Tower of Power), Robert Trujillo (Metallica), Peter Hook (Joy Division/New Order), Victor Wooten, Billy Sheehan, and Sting (The Police). Runs light on production/gear content and heavy on right-hand/left-hand technique judgment calls plus a couple of musical-decision rules (register choice, theory necessity, rhythmic placement) — a companion piece, by format and level, to the vault's other BassBuzz rule-of-thumb compilations.

## What's taught

- **Rule 1 — don't pluck too hard, unless you want to sound like Geddy Lee.** The standard-teacher reason: over-plucking is tiring, gives a nasty tone, and can wreck rhythm — tense fingers get "stuck" on the string, turning clean eighth notes sloppy. The break: aggressive, hard "whack/attack" plucking is a real, deliberate tone choice for intense/aggressive music (Rush cited) — Geddy Lee's harder attack is part of his sound, not a technique flaw. **Verdict: context-dependent** — use normal plucking for a mellow sound, dig in harder specifically when the music calls for an aggressive tone, and stay aware of the rhythm-timing risk (tense over-plucking can throw off note length/timing) either way. → [[Bass Tone Chain Priority]] (Link 1 — fingers/attack is already documented as the single highest-leverage tone link; this rule adds the *when to dig in on purpose* half of that picture).
- **Rule 2 — pluck with two fingers (alternating index/middle), unless you want to sound like James Jamerson.** Standard reason: alternating lets you keep up with fast music. The break: some pros still favor one-finger plucking for its **consistency of attack** — every note gets hit the exact same way, since it's always the same finger. Stated caveat from the teacher himself: beginners still need to force themselves to practice alternating consistently, or they'll never get comfortable with it and will hit a speed ceiling — even moderately fast one-finger plucking is genuinely hard. **Verdict: alternating is still the default to build first**; one-finger plucking is a legitimate consistency-driven choice once you're not relying on it as a crutch to avoid learning to alternate. → [[String Crossing and Plucking Patterns]] (adds a fifth plucking-approach axis — finger *count*, not just alternate-vs-rake or index/middle-start — to that note's existing plucking-hand vocabulary).
- **Rule 3 — use the pinky to fret, unless you want to play like Chris Squire.** Standard reason: the earlier you build pinky use, the sooner it stops feeling hard, and it gives more reach with less energy and fewer fretting-technique issues. **The one rule of the ten flagged as a genuine rule, not just a stylistic option** — Chris Squire (Yes) was the best example found of a pro avoiding the pinky (favoring index-ring instead), but even he used it on more complex runs, and the video argues he likely could have played with *less* physical effort by using it more. Worked comparison: fretting 3rd-fret/5th-fret with index-ring stretches the hand one amount; fretting the same interval index-pinky stretches it less — meaning less cumulative work/fatigue over a full set. **Verdict: build pinky use early** — this is the one rule the video says to actually follow, even naming its own counter-example as suboptimal. → [[Fretting-Hand Strength, Stretch, Stamina, and Finger Control]] (reinforces that note's existing pinky-independence drilling with the reach/energy-savings *rationale* for why pinky use matters, not just how to drill it).
- **Rule 4 — no thumb over the neck, unless you want to play like Louis Johnson.** Standard reason: thumb-over-neck can crowd out pinky use (bad hand angle) and reads visually as "guitar player," described as the bass's worst look. The break: thumb-over gives you a free muting tool for the low strings when the plucking thumb is busy elsewhere (picking, slapping), and it opens up **secret low notes** you can add underneath a line — Louis Johnson's actual "Billie Jean" part has extra low notes under what most players learn as the bass line, added via the fretting thumb reaching over the top of the neck. **Verdict: situational tool**, not a technique to avoid outright — reach for it specifically for low-string muting or for adding a genuinely lower voice than the fretting fingers alone can reach. → [[Bass Note Muting Technique]] (a fretting-thumb-specific muting tool, distinct from that note's existing fretting-hand/plucking-hand and floating-thumb variants) and cross-linked from [[Bass Chords and Double Stops]] for the "extra note underneath the line" angle.
- **Rule 5 — fret with your fingertips, unless you want to sound like Rocco Prestia.** Standard reason: fingertip fretting avoids locking out the fingers, avoids accidentally muting adjacent strings, and gives cleaner, tighter notes. The break: fretting with **flat fingers** (Rocco Prestia/Tower of Power) deliberately mutes most notes slightly, since other parts of the flat fingers touch the string past the fretted point — producing a tone close to palm-muting or a bridge/Nordi-style mute. Direct A/B given: the same line fretted with fingertips vs. flat fingers is "less clean and way funkier" flat, without actually locking the fingers out (they're relaxed, not stiff). **Verdict: a deliberate funk-tone tool**, not sloppy technique — reach for flat-finger fretting specifically to get a muted, Rocco-style groove tone. → [[Bass Note Muting Technique]] (a second fretting-hand muting variant added alongside the thumb-over-neck one above — both are new "deliberate partial-mute-via-fretting-hand-contact" tools for that note).
- **Rule 6 — keep your bass above the waist, unless you want to play like Robert Trujillo.** Standard reason: a low-slung bass forces wrist torque that fights normal fretting, tends to push you toward thumb-over-neck and away from the pinky (the exact two techniques Rules 3–4 cover above), and if you switch between sitting and standing, a strap set for standing-low is useless once seated. The break: playing very low (Trujillo/Metallica) looks visually aggressive/cool on stage, and paradoxically it's *easier* to keep a straight plucking wrist because the arm sits so low. **Verdict:** unless stage look is the priority, keep the bass higher — a low slung bass creates real fretting-reach limitations (harder to get to upper frets) and tips forward when you sit down. → [[Bass Playing Position, Setup, and Tuning Basics]] (adds a "how low is too low" data point and mechanism to that note's existing strap-height guidance).
- **Rule 7 — stay down low on the "money notes," unless you want to sound like Peter Hook.** Standard reason: staying in the low register is common guidance from teachers, studio sessions, producers, and bandleaders alike — reinforces why the money-note anchor register (first few frets of each string) matters so much early on. The break: Peter Hook (Joy Division/New Order) treats bass as a **lead melodic voice**, playing well up the neck — "Love Will Tear Us Apart" is cited as unimaginable without its high-register hook. Trick for keeping low-end presence while playing high, in the right key: weave in **low open strings** underneath the higher melodic line. **Verdict: the low-register default is right for supporting a band, but it's a starting point, not a ceiling** — a strong melodic bass voice can go as high as the part calls for, especially with the open-string low-end trick to avoid losing bottom end entirely. → [[Bass Fretboard Note Memorization System]] (Step 1's money-note anchor register gets its explicit "why you'd ever leave it" counterpoint).
- **Rule 8 — bass tone should have lots of low end, unless you want to sound like Victor Wooten.** Standard reason: a bass needs enough bottom end to fill its actual role in a band mix, from either a teacher's or a sound engineer's perspective. The break: Victor Wooten deliberately runs a **thinner, more guitar-like tone** as a lead bassist, specifically so intricate, virtuosic lines stay pickable-out even for listeners without a trained bass ear. Worked demo on "Me and My Bass Guitar": a full, bassy tone makes the individual notes hard to distinguish; **rolling off the bass knob and rolling up treble/high-mids** makes the same line dramatically easier to pick apart. **Verdict:** keep a properly bassy tone as the default supporting-role sound, but deliberately thin the tone (less low end, more treble/high-mid) specifically when a line or solo needs to cut through and be heard as distinct notes rather than blend into the low end. → [[Bass EQ Frequency Zones — The Five Dwarves]] (a sixth artist recipe — Victor Wooten — for the cut-the-lows/boost-the-mids-and-highs lead-visibility move, distinct from that note's existing cutting-*tone* recipes) and [[Bass Tone Chain Priority]] (Link 2, the tone-knob lever this rule's "roll off bass" move uses directly).
- **Rule 9 — you must learn theory, unless you want to play like Billy Sheehan.** Standard reason: theory explains *why* things sound good. The break: Billy Sheehan is quoted describing his own playing as pattern/melody/move-driven with **no conscious knowledge of the underlying scale or note names**, and flunked music-rudiments class in high school. **Verdict:** an exceptional ear can substitute for theory — but for anyone without one, theory is presented as the more reliable path to *build* a better ear, plus the practical payoffs of being able to talk about music, understand what's happening in a song, and analyze other people's parts. Framed as the direct justification for teaching theory in context on the channel and in the paid course. → [[The Five-Tier Bass Theory Curriculum]] (a sixth, ear-driven data point alongside that note's existing tier-by-goal framework — Sheehan's approach maps closest to "minimum theory required" territory, but via exceptional ear rather than a deliberate low-theory-tier goal).
- **Rule 10 — play on the one, for God's sake, unless you want to sound like Sting.** Standard reason: beat one is the anchor point for the whole band — the video references its own prior Bootsy Collins/"the one" video for the fuller case, now in the vault: [[Beginner Funk Bass Made Simple (Bootsy’s Funk Formula)]] / [[Bootsy's Funk Formula — On the One and the Funk Box Shape]]. The break: The Police's "Roxanne" verse bass line largely avoids landing on beat one — but the guitar *is* hitting the one, so bass and guitar have effectively swapped their normal reggae roles (bass usually on-beat, guitar off-beat, flipped here). Sting is still relating to the one — the line is built to dance around a beat-one anchor that the guitar is supplying, not abandoning the anchor concept altogether. **Verdict:** hit the one most of the time, but a deliberate off-the-one placement can work specifically when another instrument in the arrangement is anchoring beat one for you — the effect depends on that supporting context, not on the bass line alone. → [[Locking In With the Drummer]] (a distinct case from that note's beat-1-plus-everything-after "lock" framing — a deliberate, *supported* departure from beat one itself, not a drift/timing failure).
