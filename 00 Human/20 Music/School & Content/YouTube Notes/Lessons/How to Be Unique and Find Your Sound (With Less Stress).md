# How To "Be Unique & Find Your Sound" (but With LESS STRESS)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mynUnKv9Qm0)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mynUnKv9Qm0)

## Synopsis

A synthesis of Fox Stevenson and Ian Kirkpatrick's takes on "finding your sound," plus Sol State's own practical fix for the anxiety the question tends to create. The reframe: uniqueness isn't a target to chase directly — it's the automatic byproduct of making decisions based purely on your own taste, since nobody else shares your exact combination of influences. The most actionable idea is procedural, not philosophical: keep the "does this sound like me" question completely out of the creative process, and only ask it after a body of finished work exists to look for patterns in.

## Techniques

- **Uniqueness comes from making decisions based purely on your own taste, not from deliberately trying to sound different.** Fox Stevenson's framing: nobody shares your exact combination of influences (what your parents played, what you had fond childhood memories of, what you personally discovered) — making what YOU want to hear is inherently unique because only you want to hear exactly that; chasing "uniqueness" as a separate goal is the wrong target, taste is the actual mechanism.
- **Avoid imitating one specific idol too closely — a close copy reads as derivative even when it's technically well made.** Direct example given: a track that would have sounded fresh six years ago (styled closely after Avicii) now just reads as an off-brand version of something better already established — "nothing wrong with the song, but I won't remember it." The lesson isn't "don't have influences," it's "don't let one influence become the whole identity."
- **Don't overthink how far uniqueness needs to go — just "give people a reason to care."** Ian Kirkpatrick's practical bar: stacking Splice loops together isn't enough to stand out anymore since the barrier to entry for production is so low; you don't need to reinvent a genre, you need one distinguishing element that makes a listener notice this track specifically. Push slightly past pure genre convention rather than making an interchangeable, typical-sounding track in that genre — balanced against staying "responsible" (i.e. not pushing so far it stops working as a song).
- **Keep the "does this sound like me" question completely OUT of the creative process — answer it only after the music is made.** Sol State's own fix for years of "find your sound" anxiety: focus entirely on creating during the creative process itself, and defer any sorting/categorizing/identity-analysis until afterward. Explicitly reduces self-doubt and overthinking mid-creation by removing a self-judgment question from the moment where it does the most damage. → [[Committing and Printing]]
- **It's fine to use "overused" sounds/presets if the end result is good — the audience doesn't know or care about insider stigma.** Explicit example: using Cymatics downlifters despite some producers considering them overused — the only thing that matters is whether the finished track sounds good to a normal listener, not whether a specific sound choice is currently fashionable among other producers to avoid.
- **Post-hoc pattern-finding method for identifying your own sound: after accumulating a body of finished work, ask three questions — what comes easy to me, what genres do I enjoy MAKING, and what genres do I enjoy FINISHING (a distinct question from enjoying the start).** Then look across that finished body of work for recurring patterns — genre tendencies, drum choices, sound-design habits — rather than deciding a "signature sound" in advance. The sound gets discovered retroactively from what you've actually finished, not designed upfront. → [[Committing and Printing]]
