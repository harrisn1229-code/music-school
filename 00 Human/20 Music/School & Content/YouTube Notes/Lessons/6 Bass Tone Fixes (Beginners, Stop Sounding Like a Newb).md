# 6 Bass Tone Fixes (Beginners, Stop Sounding Like a Newb)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0KgB7wIyTUw)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0KgB7wIyTUw)

## Synopsis

BassBuzz's Josh runs through six (plus a "fix zero") concrete, beginner-aimed reasons a new bassist's tone sounds off, each demonstrated with a live A/B — two takes of the same bass line where only the fix-in-question changes, so the viewer hears the failure mode before being told what caused it. The throughline, stated directly up front, is that tone comes from **a mix of gear and hands technique**, and the fixes are ordered roughly cheapest/most-fundamental first: tuning (free, daily habit) → plucking direction (free, technique) → nail length (free, maintenance) → string type/age (cheap, gear) → pickup choice (free, already-owned gear) → knob literacy (free, already-owned gear) → amp EQ (free, already-owned gear). Nothing here requires buying anything beyond strings. This is a narrower, more procedural companion to two other already-enriched BassBuzz videos covering overlapping ground from different angles: [[How to Fix Your Sh*tty Bass Tone]] (the blind-tested "which link of the chain actually matters" teardown, now on [[Bass Tone Chain Priority]]) supplies the *why* behind several of the same fixes (fingers > gear, rounds vs. flats), while this video supplies the concrete *how-to* mechanics and demonstrations — most valuable as the hands-on drill-through for someone who already knows the priority order and needs the actual knob-by-knob and A/B-by-A/B walkthrough. Two pieces of content are genuinely new to the vault and not just restated: **cutting fingernails** as a distinct tone-consistency fix (no prior BassBuzz video enriched here covers it), and a **walk-each-knob-to-its-extreme-then-back** beginner method for dialing in amp EQ by ear, which is a teaching *method* rather than a named-frequency framework.

## What's taught

- **Fix 0 — Tune every day, not just before playing (~0:50).** Demonstrated with an A/B of the same bass line, one badly out of tune — the badly-tuned take is immediately, obviously worse. Stated recommendation: tune daily, at least until you've developed enough of an ear to judge in-tune-ness without a tuner. Points to BassBuzz's own free Bass Basics series for the mechanics of tuning.
- **Fix 1 — Pluck across, not up (~1:40).** The single fix framed as most important ("the alpha and the omega of bass tone" elsewhere in this vault) — demonstrated on an open A string plucked two ways: **pulling up and away from the bass** (thin, weak) vs. **pulling across so the plucking finger runs into the adjacent string after every pluck** — here, across into the E string — which sounds fuller and rounder. Framed as a beginner default to drill deliberately (viewer is walked through doing both, back to back, to hear the difference on their own hands). This is the same mechanic already documented on [[String Crossing and Plucking Patterns]] (sourced from [[Beginner Bass Lesson No. 1 (Your Very First Lesson)]]) — this video is a second, independent A/B demonstration of the identical tip rather than new content, so it's cross-linked there rather than re-extracted; see that note for the "rest stroke" naming and the automatic-muting side benefit.
- **Fix 2 — Cut your nails (~3:10).** New to the vault. Demonstrated with a second A/B of the same bass line, the worse-sounding take caused by a fingernail catching the string on some (not all) plucks. Two distinct problems named: (1) nail hardness reads as a harsher pluck than flesh alone — bad for a lot of styles; (2) because a nail doesn't grab the string *every* pluck, it makes tone **inconsistent within a single line**, not just uniformly harsher — a different failure signature from Fix 1's up-vs-across positioning. Nails grown out far enough become a playability problem on top of the tone one. Full detail routed to [[Bass Tone Chain Priority]] (Link 1).
- **Fix 3 — Learn string basics (~4:00).** Two independent variables, demonstrated separately:
  - **Type — flatwound vs. roundwound.** Flatwounds (flat-wire wrap) are smoother under the fingers, darker/more vintage-sounding; roundwounds (round-wire wrap) sound brighter and more zingy. Demonstrated on two otherwise-identical Made in Japan Fender '62 reissue Precision Basses (same model, different paint/fingerboard wood) — one strung flat, one round — as a controlled type-only A/B.
  - **Age — same type, different lifespan.** Older strings sound darker/duller/more vintage-y; fresh strings of the exact same model sound brighter and more zingy. Demonstrated on a Squier Vintage Modified Jazz Bass: ~3-year-old D'Addario roundwounds vs. a brand-new set of the identical string.
  - **Recommendation:** if you mostly play one style, copy what bassists in that style use; if you play multiple styles, default to roundwounds — they can be pushed toward a flatwound-ish tone via knobs/EQ/string age, but flatwounds can't easily be pushed the other way to sound bright and zingy. Named caveat: outside session work, exact string choice matters less than it seems, because a real gig's room/PA/crowd noise swamps fine string differences anyway.
  - Full detail routed to [[Bass Tone Chain Priority]] (Link 2).
- **Fix 4 — Learn your pickups (~6:40).** A single-pickup bass (e.g. a Fender Precision split-pickup) has no pickup decision to make — it just sounds the way it sounds. A two-pickup bass (demoed on a Fender Jazz Bass) has a **neck pickup** (closer to the neck — growlier, bassier) and a **bridge pickup** (closer to the bridge — more mid-range-y/treble-y, and by itself often too thin for most bass playing). **Beginner default: use the neck pickup alone, or blend both** — covers "99% of bass lines"; reach for bridge-solo only when a deliberately thin/treble-y sound is actually wanted. Full detail routed to [[Bass Tone Chain Priority]] (Link 4).
- **Fix 5 — Learn your knobs (~7:40).** Three concrete, named layouts (own bass's specific layout may differ — look it up):
  - **Fender Precision (2-knob):** volume (counterclockwise = none, clockwise = full) + tone (counterclockwise = duller/darker "vintage" character, clockwise = clearer/brighter).
  - **Fender Jazz (3-knob):** one volume per pickup (neck volume up alone = neck pickup; bridge volume up alone = bridge pickup; both up = 50/50 blend) + one shared tone knob, same clockwise/counterclockwise behavior as the P-Bass.
  - **Peavey Cirrus (5-knob, active):** master volume, pickup blend (clockwise = neck, counterclockwise = bridge, center = 50/50), plus active bass/mid/treble EQ. **Beginner recommendation specifically for an active-EQ bass: leave bass/mid/treble centered and do all tone-shaping at the amp instead** — keeps the signal chain simple rather than fighting two EQ stages at once.
  - Full detail routed to [[Bass Playing Position, Setup, and Tuning Basics]].
- **Fix 6 — Nail the amp EQ (~10:30).** A **beginner method**, not just a settings table: set every EQ knob to noon first. Working one knob at a time — **bass knob** (pluck an open E as reference): roll all the way counterclockwise first (thin, "where did the bass go") and all the way clockwise (boom-y, "earthquake in your house") *before* settling, so both failure extremes are heard, then roll back to roughly the middle (exact spot depends on gear/room). **Treble/high knob** (switch to open D as reference, since treble's effect is easier to hear there than on E): same walk — full counterclockwise (muffled, dull, "like a blanket" over the tone) to full clockwise (nasty, overly bright/zingy), then back to roughly the middle. **Deliberately skip the mid knob as a beginner** — mids have too many "right answers" and behave differently across amps; leaving mid at noon while mastering bass/treble first keeps you "well within the safe zone." Full detail routed to [[Bass EQ Frequency Zones — The Five Dwarves]].
