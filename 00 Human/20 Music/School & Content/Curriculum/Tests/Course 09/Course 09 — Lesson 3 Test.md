> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **Because combining feeling states produces a genuinely new, higher-level experience**
> A chord isn't the sum of its parts to the ear, so adding the parts up is both slow and the wrong operation.
>
> **Q2.** **Syllables combining into a word you hear whole**
> *Also accepted:* a word rather than its letters/sounds
> You don't hear "c-a-t" and assemble it; you hear the word. Chords fuse the same way.
>
> **Q3.** **Each chord type × quality × position as its own feeling state, learned directly**
> *Also accepted:* one label per combination · "major-on-4" · "minor-on-6" · "dominant-on-♭7"
> Same approach as the twelve single degrees, applied one level up.
>
> **Q4.** **Major 7 bittersweet · dominant 7 light and wanting to move · half-diminished unsettled throughout**
> The major 7 carries the 7 feeling on top, sharpened by its half-step rub against the root. The dominant has a ♭7 on top: playful and going somewhere. The half-diminished is a diminished triad carrying that same ♭7, so it's unstable underneath *and* unresolved on top.
>
> **Q5.** **They're hearing that a seventh is present but not which one**
> The two answers are swapped relative to what played, which is the signature of a size-not-quality read — the same gap as 3 versus ♭3, one level up.
>
> **Q6.** **By the bass note's own scale-degree feeling**
> Root position has the "1" feeling in the bass — solid, landed. An inverted bass carries whatever that degree feels like.
>
> **Q7.** **Because the bass note's degree feeling is available before the chord is identified**
> If the bass doesn't feel like home, something other than the root is down there, and that's a complete answer to "is this inverted" on its own.
>
> **Q8.** **On an unfamiliar voicing you're transcribing**
> Decomposition is the tool for the case that hasn't been trained as a single flavour yet — not the default.
>
> **Q9.** **The ear is comparing the bass note to the tonic, and the answer tells you the chord's position**
> *Rubric (5):* says the comparison is between the bass note and the tonic, not between the bass and the rest of the chord (2) · says a root-position bass carries the "1" feeling of landedness (2) · says a non-1 feeling means inverted, with the specific degree naming which inversion (1)
> The ear isn't measuring the bass against the other chord tones. It's measuring it against the key's tonic, exactly the way Lesson 2 measured a single melody note. A bass note that feels like 1 is landed and settled, so the chord is in root position. A bass that feels like 3 gives that degree's warmth instead of landedness — which both tells you it's inverted and, once you can name the degree, tells you it's first inversion.
>
> **Q10.** **Because it's working against the fusing the rest of the method depends on**
> *Rubric (5):* says chord perception fuses notes into a whole automatically (2) · says hearing an inner voice means undoing that fusing, which is fighting the mechanism rather than using it (2) · notes the honest consequence — it stays effortful, so a brute-force fallback is legitimate rather than a failure (1)
> Everything else in the lesson works *with* the ear's tendency to fuse notes into a single object: that fusing is what makes a chord quality learnable as one flavour. Hearing an inner voice requires pulling a single strand back out of the fused whole, which is the same mechanism running in reverse. Practice improves it, but it never becomes automatic the way chord-quality recognition does — so outer notes first, then working inward, is the honest method rather than an admission of weakness.
