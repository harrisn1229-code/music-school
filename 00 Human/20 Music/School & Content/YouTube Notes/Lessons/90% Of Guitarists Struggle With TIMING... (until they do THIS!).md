# 90% Of Guitarists Struggle With TIMING... (until they do THIS!)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z9mKlFey3jA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z9mKlFey3jA)

## Synopsis

BERNTH diagnoses a subtle timing problem: players drill triplets in groups of 3, 8th notes in groups of 2, 16ths in groups of 4 so consistently that they end up feeling the *note-grouping pattern* rather than the actual underlying pulse/note value — and default to accenting the first note of every group out of habit, which restricts phrasing to the same handful of shapes. The fix/exercise: deliberately mismatch the grouping to the note value (e.g. groups of 4 played AS triplets), which forces the accent pattern to rotate and breaks the "always accent beat 1 of the group" habit.

## What's taught

- **Core diagnosis:** practicing triplets only in groups of 3 (and 8ths in groups of 2, 16ths in groups of 4) trains counting/feeling "ONE-two-three, ONE-two-three" — the FIRST note of the group always lands as the highest or lowest note of a fixed pattern — rather than actually feeling the triplet subdivision itself. This is named as one of the most neglected timing topics: players learn to count note-groupings, not pulse.
- **The exercise: mismatch group size to note value.** Play a repeating **4-note pattern using triplet subdivision** (so the 4-note group cycles against the underlying groups-of-3 pulse, a deliberate polyrhythmic mismatch) — immediately shifts which note in the pattern lands on the beat each cycle, since 4 doesn't divide evenly into repeating groups of 3. Explicitly generalizes: any mismatch works (groups of 5 played as 16ths, groups of 4 played as triplets, etc.) — the point is breaking the habit of grouping = note value.
- **4 rotational variations, each starting one note earlier than the previous.** Variation 2 starts the 4-note pattern one note into the previous cycle; variation 3 starts one note earlier again; variation 4 the same. Each rotation changes which string the pattern lands its extra/short group on, which in turn **flips the alternate-picking string-transition pattern** each time (e.g. 3 notes on the high string before moving down instead of 4) — so the drill forces adaptation to a genuinely new picking pattern on every variation, not just a new rhythm.
- **Technique requirements while running the drill:** strict alternate picking throughout (down-up-down-up, never two same-direction strokes in a row) even as the string-transition pattern keeps changing underneath; move fretting-hand fingers independently rather than leaving the index finger barred down the whole time (develops per-finger control and hand sync as a side effect); keep fingers close to the fretboard and motion small/economical/relaxed (avoid a "flying pinky").
- **Explicitly not a speed exercise** — the entire point is accuracy and control while tracking a genuinely disorienting shifting pattern; practice slow.
