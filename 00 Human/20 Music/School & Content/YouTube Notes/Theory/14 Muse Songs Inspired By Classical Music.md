# 14 Muse Songs Inspired By Classical Music

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7XycSsn9YyM)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7XycSsn9YyM)

## Synopsis

A single-artist deep dive (companion to [[28 Songs That Are Based On Classical Music]] and [[14 Songs That 'Rip Off' Classical Music]]) cataloging Muse's direct borrowings from Chopin, Rachmaninoff, Tchaikovsky, Saint-Saëns, Bach, Elgar, Barber, and Palestrina — notable because Matt Bellamy, despite the classical density of the influence, isn't classically trained and can't sight-read.

## Techniques

- **Modernize a classical vocal/orchestral piece by keeping its melody and structure but replacing the accompaniment texture with rock instrumentation, escalating dynamics where the original stays reserved.** "I Belong to You" lifts Delilah's aria from Saint-Saëns' *Samson and Delilah* nearly verbatim (transposed up a semitone) for its first half, then swaps the original's gentle string accompaniment for electric guitars and drums partway through — same melodic material, escalated arrangement.
- **Genre-mashup as deliberate thematic irony, worth keeping as a compositional idea in its own right, not just a borrowing technique**: "The Globalist" pairs Elgar's "Nimrod" (a piece culturally read as a patriotic British anthem) with lyrics about the dark side of empire and colonialism — the mismatch between the music's usual connotation and the song's actual subject IS the point. "Drones" sets modern lyrics about drone warfare to a Palestrina-style Renaissance sacred vocal idiom (plus a period-appropriate plagal cadence at the close) — a style historically reserved for devotional/liturgical subjects, deliberately mismatched against a dark, contemporary military topic for eerie contrast.
- **A worked instance of "borrow the arpeggio figure and dramatic character, not the literal melody"** (the texture-borrowing device already documented from Zeppelin/Bach's Bourrée in [[28 Songs That Are Based On Classical Music]]): "Space Dementia" opens on a sweeping piano arpeggio (Am add9) modeled on Rachmaninoff's Piano Concerto No. 2's arpeggio character and later lifts an actual vocal-melody fragment from the same piece (reharmonized with different chords). "Butterflies and Hurricanes"'s piano break plays like a sped-up version of Rachmaninoff's Prelude in G minor, same arpeggio-figure logic. "Hoodoo" reuses Tchaikovsky's Piano Concerto No. 1's dramatic piano figure with one chord swapped (D♭ major → C minor) and adjusted voicings.
