## What's taught
1. **Fundamental voicings first: drop 2 and drop 3, all basic qualities, before anything else.** Drop 3 = skip a string, root typically on the low E string. Drop 2 = no skipped string, roots typically on the A string and the D string. Borton frames these as the non-negotiable starting vocabulary — "these are your go-to guitar chords that you should have 100% down first" — for every basic chord quality, then move on. → [[Chord Voicings]] §The named voicing families (this page's own drop-2/drop-3 string-set data already covers this in more fretboard-specific detail)
2. **Build a rhythmic vocabulary by transcribing rhythms off recordings, then writing your own etudes from them.** Borton's own practice source: listen to Count Basie specifically to pull comping/soloing rhythms, then take a pulled rhythm and **write out your own etude** applying it (rather than only imitating passively) — mix in whole notes and half notes, not just straight eighth-note comping, so the etudes don't default to one rhythmic density. Once an etude's rhythm is set, apply the drop 2/drop 3 voicings to it, deliberately mixing which string set (E/A/D root) is used bar to bar rather than sticking to one. → [[Jazz Comping — Rhythmic Motif and Walking Bassline]] (adds a concrete *source* — Basie transcription — and a concrete *drilling method* — write your own etude from the pulled rhythm — to that note's existing rhythmic-motif framework)
3. **Extend the basic voicings: swap the 5th for the 9th on the same four-note shapes.** All the voicings taught so far were root-3rd-5th-7th; the next layer replaces the 5th with the 9th, described as adding "a warmer sound" — still drop 2 voicings, still practiced with roots on both the A and D strings, meant to be mixed in and swapped for the plain major-7 voicings rather than replacing them outright. → [[Extended Chords]], [[Chord Voicings]] §Which notes actually matter (matches this page's own priority-order logic — 5th is "the first thing to go, almost always" — from the opposite direction: here the freed slot is filled with the 9th rather than left empty)
4. **Chromatic approach: slide a whole voicing up or down one fret into the target chord.** Stated as adding "a lot of interest" on its own, then explicitly layered on top of the rhythm and 9th-voicing work already built — move a known voicing shape a half step above or below its target before landing on it. → [[Chord Voicings]] §Voice leading is the actual point (this page's own "approach chromatically... slide the whole shape in" rule, same device, no new mechanism)
5. **Anticipations: strike the chord before the barline, typically on beat 4 or the "and" of beat 4.** Named as the next layer after chromaticism — hit the upcoming chord early, ahead of the actual downbeat where the harmony changes, most commonly landing on beat 4 or the & of 4 of the preceding bar. Combine with the chromatic-approach device above (anticipate the target chord AND approach it chromatically) for a denser, more forward-leaning comp. → [[Non-Harmonic Tones]] (general definition: "a chord tone from the next chord, sounded early"), [[Jazz Comping — Rhythmic Motif and Walking Bassline]]
6. **Melody-building drill: find every voicing you know for one chord, then look only at the top note of each.** Worked on the first chord of "Tune Up" (Em7): gather all the Em7 voicings already in the vocabulary, isolate just the top note of each shape, and ask whether those top notes alone can be arranged into a simple, singable melody — a deliberate top-down (melody-first) way to pick which voicing to grab next, rather than choosing voicings by shape or ease alone. Once a top-note melody line is chosen, chromaticism can be added to the melody itself (not just to the underlying chord voicing), then the rest of each chord is added back in underneath the chosen top note to produce a full "melody with chords" comp. Stated to generalize to every chord of a tune, not just the opening one. → [[Chord Voicings]] §Voice leading is the actual point (this page's own "the top note is a melody" rule, here turned into a concrete practice drill: catalog every known voicing's top note for one chord, then compose from that note-set)
7. **Practice sequencing: master each layer individually before combining them.** Explicit closing advice — practice drop 2/3, rhythms, 9th voicings, chromaticism, and melody-making as five separate skills first; only combine them once each is individually solid, or "you'll just get frustrated."
