# 1st Look At MJ's "Smooth Criminal" Bass

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7HIEGTvKDLc)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7HIEGTvKDLc)

## Synopsis

Marinelli, recounting a six-month stretch creating sounds directly for Michael Jackson at his home Synclavier (post-*Thriller*, pre-*Bad*), recreates the actual "Smooth Criminal" bass patch — a pitched-up piano sample layered with a sampled Minimoog — and uses it to walk through a set of Synclavier sampling/sequencing techniques that were genuinely ahead of their time.

## Techniques

- **Pitch-shifting a sample far enough to change its perceived instrument character, not just its pitch**: the bass's core layer is a stereo piano sample ("immunity muted piano" — a muted grand piano hit) raised well above its recorded pitch — high enough that it stops reading as "a low A on a piano" and instead reads as a distinct musical/synthetic tone. A clean demonstration that aggressive sample pitch-shifting is a sound-DESIGN tool, not just a transposition tool.
- **Two-layer bass, sampled + sampled, not sampled + synthesized**: layer 1 = the pitched-up piano sample (stereo, 2 voices on the Synclavier's partial/timbre system); layer 2 = a monophonic sample of a Minimoog (sampled directly from George Duke's own Minimoog during a studio sampling session) — both layers are samples, combined as two partials of one Synclavier "sound."
- **Timbre/partial system**: a Synclavier "sound" can combine multiple independently-sampled or synthesized partials (here: 2 stereo piano voices + 1 mono Minimoog voice = 3 voices total) into one named, recallable patch.
- **Real-time sequencer as an early multitrack "session" recorder**: originally an 8-16 track real-time sequencer, expandable via 32 physical buttons that could each be assigned to any of up to 200 tracks; storing/recalling a "sound" via a named button/file recalls the entire designed timbre, not just a waveform.
- **The Synclavier samples ITSELF for iterative sound design**: you can layer new partials onto an existing sample (demonstrated: adding a kick to an existing snare sample by selecting just that partial), then re-sample the resulting COMBINED sound in stereo as a new single sample — collapsing a layered design into one printed sound.
- **A DIY chorus/flange technique**: "chorusing" a sound is done by duplicating the sample and detuning the copy slightly — the same unison-detune principle used on live synth voices, applied here to a fixed sample.
- **Real-time pitch/tempo transposition of an entire sampled sequence**, printable back into the sequencer as a new take — demonstrated live, changing the pitch of a full assembled track and re-recording the result.
