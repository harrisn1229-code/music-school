# Limiting

General best practice for limiting and the mix→master handoff. Specific tools: [[Pro-L 2]] / [[Pro-L 2]], [[Oxford Inflator]], [[Ozone 9 Maximizer]]. See also [[Clipping and Loudness]], [[Compression]].

## What it is
The ceiling — catching the last peaks for loudness. The recurring lesson: it's what you do *leading up* to the limiter that matters, and often the limiter comes off before you hand the mix to mastering.

## Owned (first-call)
[[Pro-L 2]] / [[Pro-L 2]] · [[Oxford Inflator]] · [[Ozone 9 Maximizer]] · [[Limiter]] (Ableton) · [[Ghz Faraday Limiter 3]]

## From videos

- **A mix-bus limiter run barely engaged for length/character, not loudness — left on from the very start of the mix, with a specific settings recipe.** Andrew Scheps' Oxford Limiter: input trimmed down **~0.25 dB**; output ceiling **−0.1 dB** ("just to keep the red lights off"); **slowest attack, fastest release**; **auto gain OFF, auto comp OFF** — pure fixed-behavior limiting, no adaptive circuitry; the plugin's own **Inflator/enhancer** circuit engaged for extra harmonic lift. Despite reading as nearly inaudible, bypassing it reveals **4–5 dB of gain reduction on kick/snare peaks** — "this is actually a big part of how I'm lengthening the kick and the snare... without the limiter, they just come poking out, and they're too loud, and they're too short." → [[UAD Pultec EQP-1A]], [[Master Chain]] — Mix with the Masters (Andrew Scheps), [[Mix buss & drum processing with Andrew Scheps]]
- **A limiter checked at the tracking stage itself, on the drum bus, before any mixing begins — and a room mic's own processing kept to compression only, deliberately with no reverb added.** While getting live drum sounds up, CLA runs a bus limiter check on the drum bus during the mic pass ("take our bus limiter, make sure — gotta make sure you're doing right") — a safety check made while printing, not deferred to mixing later. Separately, on the room mic specifically: "this is no reverb, just a little bus compression" — since the room mic already captures real room ambience, no artificial reverb gets layered on top of it; the only processing reached for is light compression (a hardware Fairchild) to control/glue the room signal. Full mic-technique context on [[Drum Mic Technique]]. → [[Drum Mic Technique]], [[Compression]], [[3 Drums Bus|Drums Bus]], [[Vintage Gear Worklist]] — Chris Lord-Alge (Mix with the Masters), [[Tracking live drums with Chris Lord-Alge]]
- **Limiting isn't only a master-bus job — run one directly on a lead vocal track with a deliberately slow Attack so phrase transients survive.** Illangelo (The Weeknd), on [[Pro-L 2]] mid-way through his lead-vocal chain (after a gate and a de-esser, before multiband compression): "just grabbing peaks, they're all usually at the beginning of the phrase because the compressor attack is a little bit slow because you want that energy to poke through. You don't want to have the vocal come in and it's already clamped down." Stated philosophy behind putting it here rather than saving it for the master: many gentle stages in series so the vocal never arrives "already clamped down." → [[Pro-L 2]], [[The Weeknd Vocal Chain]], [[1 Main Vocal Track]] — Illangelo (Mix with the Masters), [[Producing The Weeknd's vocals with Illangelo]]
- **Near-master every stem individually before the 2-bus, and reach for an adaptive limiter that reacts to program energy rather than hunting a fixed threshold.** Every section of a mix (drums, hats, instrumental, vocals) is processed as its own mini-master — "they've all been mixed and basically mastered individually" — so each stem barely nudges the 2-bus when it lands there, and the final limiter's job shrinks dramatically (a concrete real-mix corroboration of this page's existing "a limiter should end up doing almost nothing" entry below). The per-stem limiter used for this (the "God-Particle," unowned — see [[Plugin Wishlist]]) is framed as **adaptive** rather than threshold-based: "it doesn't find a static threshold in the mix... it adapts to whatever energy you're sending it and corrects it, gives it what it feels that it needs" — if it's engaging too hard, the fix is pulling back the **input** and raising the limiter's own makeup, not chasing a threshold number. Stated reasoning for letting original transients pass through freely rather than fighting to preserve them: "if you don't let your original transients through, you're never going to have an open and beautiful exciting mix." → [[Master Chain]], [[Compression Staging]] — Jaycen Joshua (Mix with the Masters), [Mixing Summits with Jaycen Joshua](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L6xx7DWKzng)
- **A limiter can — and, done right, should — end up doing almost nothing: a worked real-mix example from a personal 7-processor "why simple is better" mastering chain.** When the upstream stages already build loudness through upward maximization (a free maximizer plugin, then a compressor/expander-off "just insert it" upward-processing move, then an Enhance-style upward pass), the final limiter's job shrinks to "protect from overs and subtly shape peaks," not carry the loudness. On the demoed mix: GR landed **~2 dB or less**, at an **integrated LUFS of about −8** — meaning even less limiting would have worked. Paired settings: **channel linking kept high** for mono compatibility, **2x oversampling** for aliasing, and attack/release/shape only tweaked if a transient change is actually audible. → [[Master Chain]], [[Plugin Wishlist]], [[Clipping and Loudness]] — Sage Audio, [My Favorite Mastering Chain - Why Simple is Better](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bLVMsAwjRPw)
- **Don't ride a preview limiter on the master output through the whole mix session.** It's become common to leave a limiter on the mix bus from the start of a session to preview how the mix will sound once loud — but this leads to misinformed mixing decisions more often than not, because any master-bus processing colors every decision made underneath it while it's engaged. The moment that limiter comes off or gets changed, the mix's own timbre and dynamic range shift — meaning balance/tone calls made while it was on were made against a mix that no longer exists once it's removed. Same underlying phenomenon as this page's existing "re-judge every earlier decision once a limiter goes on" entry below, viewed from the opposite direction: that one says re-check your calls after adding a limiter at the end; this one says don't make your calls with one on the whole way through. Better path: mix start to finish with nothing on the master output, then the resulting master benefits because the underlying mix is better — see [[Mix Handoff]]'s own warning about taking the limiter off the 2-bus before sending. → [[Mix Handoff]] — Sage Audio, [How to Prepare Your Mix for Mastering](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vd81zTPvnMQ)
- **Why the ~50 ms release-time sweet spot elsewhere on this page isn't arbitrary — a worked wavelength/period derivation.** A frequency's period (time for one full oscillation) is `1 ÷ frequency`, ×1000 for ms: 20 Hz (the bottom of human hearing) works out to `1/20 = 0.05 s = 50 ms`; a 200 Hz wave completes one oscillation in 5 ms. Set a limiter's release *shorter* than the period of a frequency present in the master (e.g. 10 ms against 20 Hz content) and it can alter that wave's amplitude mid-cycle — audible distortion, not a subtle effect. So **50 ms is a floor, not a target**: at or above it, every frequency in the audible range (20 Hz–20 kHz) completes its cycle before the release engages. Don't run it too long either — an overly long release keeps attenuating well past the peak that triggered it, catching unrelated later transients and reading as lost definition/detail. This gives this page's own release-time ladder below (50 ms sweet spot / 250 ms+ smoother / 5–10 ms max-loud) its mechanical "why" for the first time. → [[Pro-L 2]] — Sage Audio, [How to Master Loud and Clear Without Sounding Harsh](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cy-S6YmVe2U)
- **Two distinct distortion risks behind pushing a limiter's ceiling hard, and why look-ahead + oversampling address both.** Inter-sample peaking: a signal can be so consistently loud it exceeds what the session's bit depth *and* sample rate can accurately represent — even while the channel meters show nothing wrong; a lower sample rate encodes less accurately, worsening it. More oversampling improves quantization accuracy (reduces inter-sample-peak distortion); more look-ahead gives the limiter more time to react before the peak arrives (reduces unwanted clipping). If neither is enough, true-peak limiting is the fallback — but this page's own contrarian stance elsewhere is to avoid leaning on it as the default. Separately, **aliasing**: at a low session sample rate (44.1 kHz cited), distortion generated by hard limiting can exceed the frequency ceiling that rate can represent — the excess doesn't vanish, it **reflects back down the frequency spectrum** as aliasing distortion, which reads as harsh and unpleasant almost every time. Oversampling fixes this the same way it fixes inter-sample peaking. → [[Pro-L 2]], [[Clipping and Loudness]] — Sage Audio, [How to Master Loud and Clear Without Sounding Harsh](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cy-S6YmVe2U)
- **A tool-agnostic reassessment habit: once a limiter goes onto the master, go back and re-judge every processing decision made earlier in the chain, because ears hear frequency balance differently at different loudness levels.** A touch of saturation or a high-frequency EQ boost that sounded fine while mastering quiet can read as too aggressive/harsh once the limiter makes the master louder — not because the earlier move was wrong, but because loudness itself changes perceived tone. Practical habit: don't finalize EQ/saturation calls until the limiter (or a stand-in gain boost matching its target loudness) is already in the chain, and re-listen to earlier stages after adding it rather than trusting judgments made at a quieter working level. → [[Gain Staging]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [How to Gain Stage Your Master](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WW4bnNi4DAA)
- **A basic three-function anti-distortion recipe for limiting — and, unusually for this page, one that recommends true-peak detection rather than arguing against it.** Framed as "use a combination of" all three: **oversampling** (more accurate quantization, catches overs/peaks that would otherwise read above 0 dB), **look-ahead** (measures the incoming signal ahead of time so the limiter has more time to react), and **true-peak detection** (automatically reduces the output by whatever's needed to avoid clipping distortion). *Tension flag: this page's other Sage Audio sources (above/below) argue true-peak mode specifically should usually be skipped in favor of a manually-pulled-down ceiling + oversampling + short lookahead — this entry-level source recommends using all three together instead, without engaging that nuance. Treat as the beginner-level version of the same topic, not a competing house recommendation.* → [[Pro-L 2]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [How to Gain Stage Your Master](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WW4bnNi4DAA)
- **Pull a master's output down to −1 or −2 dBTP before it goes anywhere lossy (YouTube, SoundCloud, Spotify), not just to 0 dBFS — corroborates this page's existing −1.0 dBTP ceiling target from a separate angle.** Lossy encoding (MP3 and similar) reduces the accuracy of a master's amplitude; content that's close enough to 0 dB can peak and distort once it's re-encoded, even if the source file measured clean. Reducing the output ceiling by 1–2 dB before export keeps the signal from crossing 0 dB when its amplitude shifts during the lossy conversion. → [[Master Chain]], [[Clipping and Loudness]] — Sage Audio, [How to Gain Stage Your Master](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WW4bnNi4DAA)
- **Ranked limiter picks, explicitly framed as opinion, not a definitive ranking.** Best free — Limiter No. 6; best introductory — Waves L1; best versatile — FabFilter Pro-L 2 (owned, see [[Pro-L 2]]); best for retaining detail — Newfangled Audio's Elevate; best overall-sounding — Voxengo Elephant (already flagged elsewhere on this vault, see [[Plugin Wishlist]]). → Sage Audio, [How to Limit Your Master](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SrFwQqDMJQM)
- **What a limiter's Attack control actually does — a mechanical clarification, not a setting.** Every limiter's true attack (fully engaging gain reduction on a peak) is near-instant/zero milliseconds regardless of what the Attack knob shows; what the control really sets is the time *before the release/recovery envelope begins*. Quicker "attack" = less distortion; longer = louder-reading master. Combine with the release-time ladder below for finer control of a master's timbre. → [[Pro-L 2]] — Sage Audio, [How to Limit Your Master](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SrFwQqDMJQM)
- **A concrete release-time ladder — a faster set of numbers than this page's own ">100 ms" tool-agnostic target below.** 50 ms is the sweet spot for a loud master without introducing distortion; faster than 50 ms mildly-to-moderately distorts (may or may not be wanted, genre-dependent); 250 ms+ for a smoother-sounding master; 5–10 ms for maximum loudness if some distortion is acceptable. *Tension flag: sits below this page's existing "release above 100 ms" target from a different Sage Audio source — worth A/B'ing both, not picking one as the house default.* → [[Pro-L 2]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [How to Limit Your Master](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SrFwQqDMJQM)
- **A looser attenuation ceiling than this page's own "max 3 dB" target: typically stay under 5–6 dB of GR at any point, more only when the genre/track calls for it.** If a loud master needs more, reach for low-level compression *before* the limiter rather than pushing it harder — it amplifies the quieter parts of the signal, raising perceived loudness without needing aggressive limiting. → [[Pro-L 2]], [[Compression Staging]] — Sage Audio, [How to Limit Your Master](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SrFwQqDMJQM)
- **A concise restatement of this page's existing contrarian true-peak stance: skip a limiter's dedicated true-peak detection mode in most cases** — it alters the master's transients for a less impactful sound. Instead: pull the ceiling down slightly, turn oversampling on, use a short amount of lookahead — the ceiling avoids overs, oversampling gives more accurate quantization (less aliasing), lookahead ducks ahead of a peak, all with a much less aggressive transient cost than true-peak mode itself. → [[Pro-L 2]] carries the deeper mechanical "why" and a numeric recipe (ceiling −0.5 to −1 dB, 0.1 ms lookahead, 4x+ oversampling) from a separate Sage Audio source — Sage Audio, [How to Limit Your Master](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SrFwQqDMJQM)
- **Always use oversampling when limiting a master — pushing a signal into a brick-wall ceiling generates harmonic distortion, and if those harmonics come from high enough frequencies they alias; oversampling avoids that.** Gives a more accurate representation of the signal's amplitude, which reduces peaking, with no cited downside. → [[Pro-L 2]] — Sage Audio, [How to Limit Your Master](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SrFwQqDMJQM)
- **A mid-side limiter as stereo expansion — attenuate the mid image more than the side — a distinct route from this page's channel-unlinking entries above/below.** Two named methods for stereo expansion via a limiter: this one (route to mid/side, limit the mid harder) and unlinking the channels so L/R are limited independently (already well-documented on this page). Unlinking won't widen every master, but can make L/R read as more unique/distinct as a pleasant side effect. → [[Width and Stereo]], [[Pro-L 2]] — Sage Audio, [How to Limit Your Master](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SrFwQqDMJQM)
- **Two limiters in series, with distinct roles per instance, not just split gain reduction: limiter 1 attenuates peaks by ~2 dB, limiter 2 pushes perceived loudness** — same ceiling/look-ahead/oversampling settings on both, and deliberately try different limiter *types* back to back to combine their distinct timbres rather than stacking two of the same. → [[Pro-L 2]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [Limiter Masterclass](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LZxglknf3xA)
- **"Soft limiting" is a named, ratio-defined category (>10:1), not just a gentler brick wall** — a soft/high-ratio limiter's attack time isn't instant like a brick-wall limiter's, so it can be shaped with normal Attack/Release controls. Recommended specifically as the *first* limiter in the two-limiter technique above — it controls peaks with less distortion and gives more control over timbre than putting a brick-wall stage first. → [[Compression Staging]], [[Pro-L 2]] — Sage Audio, [Limiter Masterclass](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LZxglknf3xA)
- **Reach for upward compression before reaching for heavier limiting** — since limiting only ever attenuates peaks, controlling dynamics upstream (upward compression specifically: bringing the quietest parts of the signal up, increasing detail while reducing dynamic range) lets you use less limiting, or skip brick-wall limiting altogether. Try various forms of compression ahead of the limiter and judge whether it gets you the control you needed before the limiter ever engages. → [[Oxford Inflator]], [[Compression Staging]], [[Clipping and Loudness]] — Sage Audio, [Limiter Masterclass](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LZxglknf3xA)
- **True-peak limiting's transient cost, mechanically: it works by *reforming* distorted waveforms, which necessarily changes the timbre/ADSR of the transient being reformed** — the mechanism behind why true-peak mode reduces perceived loudness and transient detail even though it does genuinely reduce inter-sample-peak distortion. Reinforces this page's existing contrarian true-peak stance below with the "why," from a different Sage Audio source; pairs with the concrete non-true-peak ceiling/lookahead/oversampling recipe on [[Pro-L 2]]. → [[Pro-L 2]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [Limiter Masterclass](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LZxglknf3xA)
- **Two limiters in series for a specific combined character — a named Oxford Limiter → Pro-L 2 pairing, plus a stance against true-peak mode.** The Oxford Limiter first (credited with a "thick and full" sound, mainly from its Enhance function — unowned, see [[Plugin Wishlist]]), then [[Pro-L 2]] second (reads "modern," its dynamic algorithm helps retain impact) — combined, described as full, aggressive, and upfront, a different result than either alone. Paired with a second stance: avoid true-peak limiting mode specifically, instead running oversampling with very low levels of lookahead — echoes the same true-peak-skeptical argument elsewhere on this page from a different Sage Audio video, applied here as a working default rather than a contrarian aside. → [[Pro-L 2]], [[Master Chain]], [[Plugin Wishlist]] — Sage Audio, [How to Master a Song Start to Finish](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aUygMNRx2yE)
- **Dual-mono (unlinked L/R) limiting for extra loudness headroom — split the signal into left and right channels, run identical limiters unlinked.** Each limiter then only attenuates when *its own* channel crosses threshold (similar to pulling Channel Link low/off), rather than both sides ducking together whenever either one peaks — a loud moment on one channel no longer forces gain reduction on the quieter side too, which pushes the achievable level noticeably higher before attenuation turns aggressive. Named specifically effective with the **Sonnox Oxford Limiter**, pairing it with the unit's **Enhance** function to amplify quiet detail alongside the peak-down limiting (unowned — see [[Plugin Wishlist]]). *Tension flag: contradicts the linked-channel research target elsewhere on this page and on [[Master Chain]]/[[Width and Stereo]] (commercial masters keep L/R RMS within 0.8 dB) — treat this as a loudness-first technique to A/B on [[Pro-L 2]]'s Channel Link, not a default swap.* → [[Master Chain]], [[Pro-L 2]] — Sage Audio, [Mastering Loud But Not Squashed](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YJVE4_CQ9Ts)
- **A contrarian take: avoid running a limiter's dedicated true-peak mode at all — meter true peaks separately and pull the output down manually instead.** Two named limiter mistakes for a loud master: look-ahead (captures peaks early, attenuating transients and reducing important dynamic high-frequency content, the same cost look-ahead has on a compressor) and — the less conventional claim — **true-peak limiting mode itself**, argued to reduce transient impact and read as quieter the same way look-ahead does. The suggested fix: monitor true peaks with a separate meter, use oversampling, and lower the limiter's output slightly to stay clear of overs, rather than trusting the limiter's own TP-mode attenuation. *Tension flag: this cuts against the owned [[Ozone 9 Maximizer]]/[[Pro-L 2]] chain settings and other Sage Audio sources on this vault, which explicitly recommend TP-mode limiting with a −1.0 dBTP ceiling as the safe default — worth A/B-testing on a loud master before adopting, not a settled recommendation.* → [[Master Chain]], [[Clipping and Loudness]] — Sage Audio, [Mastering Loud But Not Squashed](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YJVE4_CQ9Ts)
- **How a limiter actually works, mechanically — and why that mechanism, not misuse, is what blurs transients.** Modern (brick-wall) limiters use look-ahead: enabled, the added latency lets the processor see peaks coming before they arrive, so it reduces gain exactly at the moment of the peak instead of reacting like a normal compressor — it doesn't clip the peak the way a clipper does, and there's no attack-time compromise because it already knows what's coming. The only genuine benefit of that pre-mapped attenuation is that it lets you push more input gain into the limiter, amplifying the quieter parts and reducing masking of detail — that's the whole "loudness" trick. But by design a limiter always trims the top of every peak, and trimming peaks is exactly what reduces transient impact and reads as "blurred" — better limiter algorithms (release time/shape, attack shape, post-attenuation transient expansion, oversampling accuracy on peak detection, linked vs. independent channels) change the character but never remove that fundamental peak-shaving. → [[Clipping and Loudness]] — Sage Audio, [Stop Limiting Your Masters](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s_LDCzWx20g)
- **Three alternatives that hit the same "amplify quiet detail" loudness goal without a limiter's peak-shaving tradeoff.** (1) Parallel compression — compress hard, then blend to roughly 50/50 wet/dry so peak attenuation only lands on half the output ([[Pro-C 2]]'s built-in Dry Gain does this in one plugin; also open Attack/Release up for transient retention since the blend removes the clipping risk that forces a fast attack). (2) Gentle upward maximization via wave-shaping instead of brick-wall attenuation — [[Oxford Inflator]] pushes quiet content up then gradually rounds toward the ceiling rather than squaring it like a clipper; the unowned PSP Vintage Warmer 2 does the same via its Knee control, with the brick-wall stage optionally switched off entirely (see [[Plugin Wishlist]]). (3) Subtle multiband saturation — [[Saturn 2]]'s Subtle Tube/Tape types + Dynamic style get the same upward-detail effect the parallel-compression trick does, without attenuating first, adjustable per frequency band. → [[Parallel Processing]], [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [Stop Limiting Your Masters](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s_LDCzWx20g)
- **Tool-agnostic mastering-limiter target: release above 100 ms, channel link ~60–80%, max 3 dB of attenuation — and if more GR than that is needed to hit an LUFS target, fix it upstream, not at the limiter.** Framed as "whichever limiter you use is fine" — carry these settings into any limiter, not one specific unit. If 3 dB of GR won't get you loud enough, add more upward processing and/or peak attenuation *before* the limiter rather than pushing it harder. After limiting, judge by re-listening — does it still sound like what you want once the quiet detail gets pushed forward? — and go back to the tone-shaping EQ, not the limiter, if not. Demoed on Voxengo Elephant (not owned, see [[Plugin Wishlist]]) — owned equivalents: [[Pro-L 2]], [[Ozone 9 Maximizer]]. Sits last in a five-stage minimal mastering chain (EQ → RMS comp → upward processing → tone EQ → limiter), which is also where saturation, upward processing, and limiting are distinguished as three mechanically different tools — saturation reshapes peaks, limiting retains peak shape and uses clean gain, upward processing reshapes from the quietest points up. → [[Master Chain]], [[Saturation and Distortion]] — Sage Audio, [The Only Music Mastering Video You'll Ever Need](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=M88T8jFL2uU)
- **Only some limiters carry enough lookahead/oversampling to avoid waveshaping — most don't, so expect a limiter to add harmonics/alter frequency response as a side effect of doing its job.** Compressors can largely avoid the waveshaping that generates new, higher-frequency harmonic content by using lookahead (lets the oscillation pass through before gain reduction, so it's retroactively attenuated without cutting into the wave shape) plus oversampling — but this only fully applies to a compressor; most limiters don't carry enough of either to prevent it, so treat "if you limit, you're almost certainly adding harmonics that alter the frequency response" as the default assumption unless a specific limiter is confirmed otherwise. → [[Compression]], [[Clipping and Loudness]] — Sage Audio, [All Processing is Connected - Crucial Knowledge for Pro Mixes & Masters](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XV-0rdUrgHs)
- **Post-limiting amplification hits quiet temporal-effect tails (reverb, delay, modulation) harder than the dry signal — take the limiter off before mastering, or expect it to change how those tails read.** Reverb/modulation content sits lower in amplitude than the dry signal almost always (unless gated), so limiting that lifts quiet content lifts those tails disproportionately along with everything else — a mastering-stage limiter can make a mix's own reverb/delay read louder/more present than intended, independent of what the mastering engineer does deliberately. → [[Mix Handoff]], [[Reverb]] — Sage Audio, [All Processing is Connected - Crucial Knowledge for Pro Mixes & Masters](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XV-0rdUrgHs)
- **Pull the ceiling ~1 dB below 0 dBTP as insurance against intersample peaks on MP3 export, on top of the limiter's own GR target.** True-peak overs can appear on an MP3/lossy bounce even when the source file measures clean at 0 dBFS on a sample-peak meter — the lossy encode's reconstruction filter can overshoot between samples. Push the limiter's input gain until GR on the loudest hits lands ~2–3 dB (the target, not the ceiling), then back the output ceiling off an extra ~1 dB below true-peak 0 as a safety margin specifically for that conversion step, judging the result qualitatively ("I don't want to clip much more than three off the top of my peaks — I put those peaks there, that's where I want them"). → [[Clipping and Loudness]], [[Master Chain]] — Max Konyi, [Final Mix & Master | Studio One LoFi Tutorial | Part 9](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-OKUCuyLBSg)
- **Limit, don't compress (Lophiile's source-control hot take)** — "I use limiting 90% of the time on bass, keys, anything that needs to be controlled... compression is hard to control and you can always hear it." Waves L1-style limiter straight on sources; owned: [[kHs Limiter]], [[Limiter]], [[Pro-L 2]] (master only — it adds noticeable latency). Drums are the exception: he saturates those instead of limiting — Lophiile, [All About Basslines stream](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tnruvmw_OWc)
- **Produce INTO a limiter** — keep [[Pro-L 2]] (Dubstep style, punchy) on the master the whole time you beatmake so you hear the drums the way the finished record will hit; then "turn every single other thing down" — drums should be the loudest thing hitting the limiter — Lophiile, [Make Your Drums Slap](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v4ZhwlhF5yE)
- **Tape → limiter = the "FL bump"** — a tape stage (UAD Ampex ATR-102 default; owned: [[Kramer Tape]]/[[Cassette]]) feeding the master limiter makes kicks distort cleanly and the low end thicker, "like an FL beat"; free path: [[kHs Limiter]] — Lophiile, [All About Basslines stream](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tnruvmw_OWc)
- **Oxford Limiter as *glue with character*, not just a ceiling** — on a kick+clap it adds a pleasing distortion/overdrive that crunches the two together (limiters have a "feel"); without it the clap "sits above the mix." Reach for its color, keep GR light (~3 dB where they coincide). → [[Compression Staging]] — Disclosure, [Holding On](https://youtu.be/8H8U-WjvdVc)

A clipper with heavy oversampling beats a limiter with none (the old Oxford Limiter aliases across a full mix) — Disclosure, [NO CAP](https://youtu.be/kz5dmb3QdI8)

- **A limiter still earns its place on the drum bus** — the Oxford Limiter's slight distortion gives a forward push, even barely engaging (put it on for safety) — Disclosure, [Waterfall](https://youtu.be/sL5CG444qwQ) & [NO CAP](https://youtu.be/kz5dmb3QdI8)
