# How do vocal harmonies work?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Zf7Oh99Qp4k)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Zf7Oh99Qp4k)

## Synopsis

A thorough, example-driven survey of how vocal harmony lines are actually written and layered — interval choice, contour matching, oblique harmony, unison, and manual (performance-based) double tracking, as distinct techniques rather than one general "harmonize it" instruction.

## Techniques

- **Thirds are the default interval, with a stated reason to deviate.** Most 2-part vocal harmony sits a third apart (Beatles' "Two of Us," Simon & Garfunkel's "Mrs. Robinson," Celine Dion/Peabo Bryson's "Beauty and the Beast"). Deviation happens specifically when the "correct" third would clash with the underlying chord — "Two of Us" briefly drops the third at one point because it would have produced a note rubbing against the G major chord underneath.
- **Sixths as inverted thirds — same sweetness, wider gap.** "Islands in the Stream" (Dolly Parton/Kenny Rogers): the lower voice sings the exact same notes a third-above harmony would use, just shifted down an octave, turning the interval into a sixth below instead.
- **Fifths deliberately avoided, and why — precisely because they're TOO consonant.** The perfect 5th is the most consonant interval beyond the octave, but that's exactly why it's rare in vocal harmony: two voices a 5th apart blend so completely the ear can struggle to hear them as two separate voices, producing an uncanny "merged mega-voice" effect rather than a clear harmony — used deliberately for that otherworldly quality on Bowie's "Blackstar."
- **3-part harmony uses the same interval logic, with fourths as a practical fallback.** boygenius's 3-part harmony sits mostly in thirds/fourths between the parts; one moment deliberately drops to unison on a single word, breaking the otherwise-matched melodic contour for emphasis.
- **Contour matching as the default rule, and oblique harmony as the deliberate exception.** Harmony lines normally share the lead melody's up/down shape ("contour"). **Oblique harmony** breaks this: the harmony line holds a single fixed note while the lead melody moves freely above or below it — Green Day's "Basket Case," Sinatra duet "Something Stupid," and the Beatles' "Please Please Me" (Paul holds the tonic while John's melody descends).
- **Unison singing and the phasing/thickening effect it produces.** Two voices singing the exact same line never align in pitch/timing perfectly, and that tiny mismatch reads as a thickened, "chorused" sound rather than a doubled melody — audible on Elton John/Kiki Dee's "Don't Go Breaking My Heart." When it's the SAME singer performing the identical part twice, this becomes **manual double tracking**: two real performances, similar but not identical, layered for a fatter/more powerful lead vocal (Nirvana's "Smells Like Teen Spirit," the Beatles' "Back in the USSR" — a technique the Beatles used so often that one of their engineers built an automated way to fake it, i.e. ADT). Deliberately copy-pasting a single take does NOT work — the takes need to be genuinely different enough to phase against each other, or the thickening effect disappears.
