# 7 Easy Blues Bass Line Formulas (The Blues Box)

## What's taught

- **[Intro] Framing.** The Blues Box is pitched as the next step after the survival shapes — a single movable shape that, combined with the 1-4-5 root-finding already taught in the prior video, generates "tons of kick-ass blues bass lines" without more theory. Promises seven formulas from real songs plus a downloadable cheat sheet (link in description, not accessible from captions).
- **The Blues Box shape — exact fretting, given here for the first time in this vault.** Worked example rooted on D (5th fret, A string): index finger on the root, pinky two frets up on the next string (the 5), index back to the root's fret on the string after that (the ♭7), pinky two frets up on that same string (the octave). Playable with just two fingers (index-pinky-index-pinky). Note-degree makeup for players who want it: root, 5, ♭7, octave — "if that sounds like Klingon to you," just memorize the shape instead. Full fretting table and interval verification: [[Blues Box Bassline Formulas]].
- **Moving the box across a chord change.** Find the new chord's root on the E or A string (enough neck above it to fit the whole box), restart the identical shape there. Worked over a D→G→D→G comp: playing root notes only under the changes is called out explicitly as "the boring option" — relocating the full box is the actual payoff.
- **Genre-crossover claim.** The same four-note shape is also "the Funk Box" (from a separate, not-yet-vaulted BassBuzz lesson, "Booty's Funk Formula") and "the Jazz Box" depending on which lesson it's taught in — same notes, genre-agnostic.
- **12-bar blues recap** (quick refresher, full theory deferred to the prior Survival Shapes video and the Beginner to Badass course): three root notes per progression — 1, 4, 5 — named from the major scale; find 4 and 5 relative to 1 using either survival shape from the prior lesson.
- **The core insight tying the box to the 12-bar form:** "the survival shapes tell you where your root notes are, the 12-bar blues form tells you when to play them, the Blues Box gives you some tasty notes you can build a bassline with" — the final step is just picking a rhythm and a note order, which is exactly what the seven formulas below demonstrate.
- **Formula 1 — The Basic Box** (key D): box notes played in order, straight quarter notes, no shuffle. The "floor" formula everything else elaborates on.
- **Formula 2 — The Box Shuffle** (key A, from "Little by Little"/Junior Wells): same note order as Formula 1, shuffle-eighths rhythm. Includes an on-camera even-vs-shuffle ear-training explanation ("even" vs "lumpy" eighth-note feel).
- **Formula 3 — The Reverse Box Shuffle** (key C, from "I'm Tore Down"/Eric Clapton): same shuffle rhythm as Formula 2, but reversed note order — root, octave, ♭7, 5.
- **Formula 4 — The Funky Formula** (key C, from an Otis Redding cover — source song title lost to a caption gap): straight eighth notes, no shuffle feel; explicitly not from a 12-bar blues originally but adapted onto one here.
- **Formula 5 — The Finger Pop Formula** (key F, from "Shake and Finger Pop"/Jr. Walker & the All Stars): same straight-eighths rhythm as Formula 4, reordered notes.
- **Formula 6 — The Funky Trunk Formula** (key B♭, from "Leavin' Trunk"/Taj Mahal): steps up to sixteenth-note subdivision — flagged as genuinely harder for players new to 16ths.
- **Formula 7 — The Tasty Triplet Shuffle** (key/frets not given in the captioned portion, from "The Blues Is Alright"/Gary Moore): shuffle-family rhythm with an added triplet, saved for last as the hardest; at the end of the 12-bar form the pattern breaks into a scripted unison "chug" on the V chord with the rest of the band.
- **The 4(+1)-step build-your-own-bassline process**, demonstrated live over a D blues with a guitarist/drummer: (1) find the key — ask the guitarist rather than guess; (2) find the 1-4-5 with the survival shapes; (3) pick a rhythm that fits the drummer's groove; (4) pick a box note order — reuse a formula or invent one, **always starting on the root** ("you're always laying down the low end" — true of all seven formulas). Optional step 5: combine formulas mid-song — small rhythmic swaps any time, but a big jump in how busy the bassline is should land at the **top of the 12-bar form** (e.g. a new solo starting), never mid-phrase, or it "breaks the flow of the groove" and "upsets your bandleader."
