# Masking and Carving

**What it is:** frequency masking = two elements sharing a band at similar levels; the ear picks one and the other vanishes. Carving = deciding who *owns* each band and cutting the loser there. Most "my mix is muddy/cluttered/vocal is buried" problems are masking problems.

## The hierarchy (who wins a collision)
**Lead vocal → kick/snare backbeat → bass → hooks/leads → everything else.** When two elements collide, the lower-ranked one moves: dip, duck, pan apart, octave change, or arrangement edit. Cut the *loser*, don't boost the winner (boosting into a masked band just escalates the war).

## Diagnosis before surgery
- **Mute test:** mute the suspect group — whatever suddenly gets clearer is what it was masking (and vice versa: turn it up and hear what disappears).
- **Pro-Q collision detection:** [[Pro-Q 4]] on both buses, analyzer sidechained — colliding frequencies light up red while both play. Cut the red on the lower-priority bus. (Workflow in [[2 Inst-Synth Group|Inst-Synth Group]].)
- Solo lies: piano/guitar boosts at 1–2 kHz sound honky solo and correct in the mix — always judge carves in context.

## The carving toolkit (gentlest first)
1. **Arrangement** — two parts in the same register are an arrangement problem no EQ fixes. Change the octave/voicing.
2. **Panning** — elements can share a band if they don't share a position (guitar L, keys R).
3. **Static carve** — the loser gives up 1–3 dB where the winner lives (guitars dip 2–4 kHz for vocal; everything HPFs above what it needs — see each group note).
4. **Mid/side carve** — cut the loser's low-mids in the *mid channel only* (can go deep, −6–9 dB): the center clears for vocal/snare/bass while the sides keep the body. The element even gets wider. ([[1 Acoustic Piano Track|Acoustic Piano Track]], [[1 Synth Bed Track|Synth Bed Track]].)
5. **Dynamic carve** — [[Pro-Q 4]] dynamic node keyed from the winner: the dip exists only while the winner plays. Full sound in the gaps.
6. **Sidechained soothe2** — the surgical endgame: ducks exactly the colliding resonances, exactly when they collide ([[2 Guitar Group|Guitar Group]], [[2 Inst-Synth Group|Inst-Synth Group]]).

## Standing lane assignments (this template)
- Sub <100 Hz: 808/bass only, mono · kick vs bass pocket: pick per song (50–60 vs 80–120 Hz) · pads/FX/hats never below 150–300 Hz · vocal air 10 kHz+ beats hat sparkle · repeats/reverbs always duller and narrower than their source.

## From books

- **The drum kit's own internal lane ladder — four ascending high-passes that keep each drum out of the zone below it.** Kick **40–60 Hz** · toms **60–200 Hz** (per drum — floor toms low, rack toms high, "stop clashing" is the stated reason) · snare **100–200 Hz** ("more if necessary") · hats **200–300 Hz** or higher. This is the standing-lane-assignments rule above applied *inside* the kit rather than across the mix, and it pairs with a second rule: **cut below the fundamental, boost at it** — kick shelf 80–100 Hz, snare shelf 200–300 Hz, toms boost 100–300 Hz. Collision points to watch: the snare's 100–200 Hz HPF sits directly under its own 200–300 Hz body shelf (cut too high and the "thwack" goes with it — [[1 Drums Snare-Clap Track]] actually *boosts* +1–3 dB @ 200–250 Hz there), and the toms' 60–200 Hz cut overlaps their own 100–300 Hz power boost, which only resolves if the cut sits under that specific drum's fundamental. Presence lanes stay separated the same way: kick 2–4 kHz snap · snare 5–7 kHz crisp · toms 5–10 kHz punch · hats 7–10 kHz+ sizzle, with a tom boxiness cut at **300–800 Hz** (sweep a narrow boost to find the honk first). → [[EQ]], [[Pro-Q 4]], [[Kick Design]], [[Snare Design]], [[SUB Slot]], [[BASS Slot]], [[BED Slot]], [[MIDS Slot]], [[LEAD Slot]], [[AIR Slot]] — Whipped Cream Sounds, [[Drum Mixing EQ Guide - Enrichment|Drum Mixing EQ Guide]]

## From videos

- **Decide the song's single most important carrier first, then build the whole mix — panning included, not just EQ — around keeping it central, rather than treating hierarchy as a per-collision EQ decision only.** Giles Martin, on remixing "In My Life" (The Beatles): "what's the most important thing about this song? The most important thing about the song is actually the vocals... it's the message of the song, and so that's why what you're concentrating on, that's what you've put in the middle and everything else goes around it." A whole-song-level version of this note's own [[#The hierarchy (who wins a collision)|hierarchy]], applied once at the start of a mix rather than resolved collision-by-collision. Paired with an over-correction caution told in the same breath: remixing *All Things Must Pass* (George Harrison) for a film, too much kick drum in isolation made the whole song "immediately sound leaden and lumpy... doesn't float anymore" — a mix decision has to be judged against the full arrangement, never by how good one element sounds soloed. A second real example, same session: on "With a Little Help from My Friends" (Sgt. Pepper's), moving Ringo's originally hard-panned bass to the center changed the song's emotional read — his vocal, meant to sound lonely and friend-seeking, stopped sounding lonely once the bass filled the space beside it. A panning decision that changed the song's *emotional* narrative, not just its tonal balance. → [[Width and Stereo]] — Tape Notes (Giles Martin), [[TN 190 The Beatles (Giles Martin) - Full Video Episode]]
- **Tweak a synth patch's filter WHILE the track is running, not soloed — a soloed patch that sounds "right" is often wrong in context, and vice versa.** Steve Porcaro (MJ/Thriller sessions), on string patches: backing the filter off to sound realistic in isolation made the patch sound like "alto flutes" — too mellow — once heard alone, but that's precisely because a filter setting judged in a mix (with other elements already occupying the same band) reads differently than the same setting judged solo. Direct case study for this note's own [[Filters and Resonance#The mix-context rule|mix-context rule]]. Companion discipline: when a part still sounds "muddy" or "cloudy" after patch-level EQ/filter attention, hand it to the mixing engineer rather than chasing it further at the synth — naming the specific problem ("my low end's cloudy, having trouble with the sound speaking") gets a targeted EQ/compression fix and invests the engineer in that part's place in the mix. → [[Filters and Resonance]] — Anthony Marinelli & Steve Porcaro, [[Steve Porcaro In-Studio - Writing Human Nature and Unreleased MJ Songs Part 1]]
- **"Mirror EQ" — complementary boost/cut on lead vs. rhythm guitar so they carve space instead of competing.** Find the frequency where lead guitar naturally "speaks" — cited as roughly 900 Hz-1.5 kHz, the same range human speech intelligibility lives in — and boost it slightly on the lead guitar for extra bite/cut-through; then do the OPPOSITE (a cut) at a nearby but distinct frequency (~1.2 kHz cited) on the rhythm guitar. A complementary pair of moves, not an isolated boost. Paired tips from the same source: **double, don't duplicate** — record the same rhythm part as two independent takes for genuine stereo width, since duplicating one take does not produce the same result; and an **ear-first resonance-hunting method** — sweep a narrow EQ band by ear to find a boxy/spiky resonance and cut it a few dB, rather than trusting the visual curve alone, since a visually alarming EQ move is often exactly what the sound needs. → [[Pro-Q 4]] — BERNTH feat. Daniel Fellner, [[4 BEST HOME RECORDING TRICKS! - Dialing In Your Perfect Guitar Tone]]

- **Reduce kick/bass overlap by notching the kick's own fundamental out of the BASS, not the other way around — a static, always-on alternative to sidechain ducking.** Read the kick's fundamental frequency off an analyzer, then create a narrow bell dip at that exact frequency on the bass, carving room for the kick without touching the kick's own tone. For dynamic control of the same overlap: a multiband compressor on the bass, sidechained from the kick, ducking only a band centered on the kick's fundamental — versus standard full-band sidechain ducking, which attenuates the ENTIRE bass signal on every kick hit, including parts that aren't actually competing. Soothe2 offers a resonance-suppressor version of the same idea (external sidechain, lows-focused) as a third option. High-pass placement is a masking decision too: don't push a lead vocal's high-pass above its own fundamental; instead high-pass COMPETING elements (synths, guitars) to just above the vocal's fundamental. → [[Multiband Compression]], [[Sidechaining]], [[Soothe2]], [[Kick Design]] — Sage Audio, [How to Mix for Clarity](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=R-UjkDtleVU)
- **Temporal (time-based) masking — the sibling of simultaneous masking, and the mechanism behind why snare ring is disproportionately hard to mix.** Simultaneous masking = two things loud at the SAME time, one buries the other. Temporal masking = masking across DIFFERENT times, centered on a loud transient: **pre-masking** extends roughly **20 ms before** the loud sound, **post-masking** extends up to **~100 ms after** it, falling off exponentially the further a quiet sound sits from the transient. A snare's ring sits right in that post-masking window of its OWN transient while ALSO competing with everything else playing simultaneously — a double masking problem. Fix: upward processing/maximization on the masked (quiet) content itself — bringing up a signal's own quiet details relative to its peak ([[Oxford Inflator]]'s mechanism) raises the masked material's amplitude relative to the transient burying it, reducing both temporal and simultaneous masking at once, rather than trying to carve the masker. → [[Oxford Inflator]], [[Snare Design]], [[TRANSIENT Slot]] — Sage Audio, [3 Advanced Audio Concepts You've (Probably) Never Heard Of](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cjwBvTtE5-I)
- **200–400 Hz is the primary masker of a vocal's 3–5 kHz clarity formant — name the specific frequency pair before reaching for a general "make it clearer" boost.** The vocal's third formant (vowel/consonant articulation, the range most responsible for intelligibility) sits mostly in **3–5 kHz**; **200–400 Hz** is its primary masker, whether the excess energy is in the vocal itself or in surrounding instrumentation. 200–400 Hz is also one of the most populated ranges in any mix (many instruments' fundamentals or low-order harmonics live there), so it's usually the first suspect for a "muffled" or "buried" vocal. Fix: find the loudest instrument(s) occupying 200–400 Hz and attenuate subtly THERE, spread across multiple tracks rather than hitting one hard; also subtly attenuate 200–400 Hz on the lead vocal itself and/or boost its own 3–5 kHz formant. Watch reverb/delay returns — they can reintroduce energy in this same masking range and may need the identical carve. → [[EQ]], [[MIDS Slot]], [[De-essing]] — Sage Audio, [The 5 MOST IMPORTANT Vocal Mixing Tips](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rXk1lvT6AhU)
- **The mid-range is the busiest, most masking-prone zone in almost any mix — not because it carries the most energy, but because nearly every instrument's overtones (kick, bass, vocal, guitar, synth) converge there regardless of where each instrument's fundamental sits.** A kick's fundamental is low but most of its overtones are in the mids; bass guitar's harmonics (the actual timbral character) sit in the mids despite a low fundamental. Practical read: when carving competing elements apart, treat the mids as the default collision zone to check first, even for instruments whose fundamentals don't overlap — their overtones probably do. → [[EQ]], [[MIDS Slot]] — Sage Audio, [Mids - the Most Important Frequency Range](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=23pezFNXW_4)
- **A well-arranged mix should already read close to a pink-noise-shaped frequency response (amplitude generally decreasing lows→highs) before any processing — arrangement, not EQ, does most of the balancing.** If instruments are chosen for distinct overtones/timbres and notes are arranged to avoid heavy overlap, the frequency response arrives close to correct; EQ still fine-tunes, but a conflicting arrangement (e.g. kick and bass emphasizing the same frequency zone) produces mud that EQ alone struggles to fully undo. Fill a genuinely thin range by generating new content there (saturate a low/low-mid source so its harmonics land in and fill the mids, or reverberate a mid-heavy source so its early reflections do the same) rather than defaulting to an EQ boost on a range with nothing there to boost. → [[EQ]], [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[Reverb]] — Sage Audio, [Arrangement and Music Mixing: Understanding the Relationship](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_--yEbcNjAs)
- **The cochlea only resolves ~24 critical bands (Barks) total, far fewer than an EQ/FFT's thousands of bins — two sounds sharing a critical band mask or amplitude-modulate against each other and are hard to hear as distinct.** Worked example: Bark band 4 centers at 350 Hz with a 300–400 Hz bandwidth (100 Hz wide) — a 310 Hz and a 330 Hz tone are difficult to tell apart, and playing them together produces audible beating/pulsing rather than two clean pitches. Bandwidth widens with frequency (a 20 Hz gap is noticeable in the lows, but takes 200–300 Hz to notice in the highs), matching the ear's own reduced high-frequency pitch discrimination. **Concrete rule: keep the kick's and bass's fundamentals — and their harmonics — separated by at least an octave, so their content never lands in the same critical band; the same applies to any two instruments with close fundamentals** (e.g. vocal vs. guitar/synth). This is the psychoacoustic mechanism underneath why kick/bass separation and general lane assignment work at all, not just a rule of thumb. → [[EQ]], [[SUB Slot]], [[BASS Slot]] — Sage Audio, [Psychoacoustics Pt.2 - the Logarithmic Ear](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Aha_7purQQo)
- **The "freight train" mix philosophy: match the low-end AND high-end WEIGHT of every element (kick, bass, snare, guitars) so none of them dominates — dense and powerful, but streamlined, nothing sticking out.** Andy Wallace, describing the goal visually: "a big old-fashioned steam engine... a lot of weight and mass and power, but fairly streamlined... nothing is really sticking out." Concrete mechanism: he doesn't want the bass, kick, or guitars carrying noticeably more low end than each other ("I want that low end to be about the same impact on you"), and applies the identical logic to the high end — the guitars' "clank," the bass's "clank," and the snare/kick's "whack" should all read together rather than one element poking out. Achieved partly by trimming a specific un-musical low-end frequency (~40 Hz, "dirty") off the guitars specifically so the kick/bass could be heard clearly — see [[EQ]] (effect) for the exact move. A snare doesn't need real sub content to satisfy this rule — it needs to *feel* thick relative to the other elements, a perceptual-balance goal rather than a literal frequency-matching one. → [[SUB Slot]], [[BASS Slot]], [[EQ]] (effect) — Mix with the Masters (Andy Wallace), [[Mixing Avenged Sevenfold - Andy Wallace]]
- **High-pass a layered element to resolve a low-end collision with the bed underneath it, rather than EQ-cutting the bed to make room.** Andrew Scheps, on a bridge fuzz guitar layered over rhythm guitars: "the left side is pretty small, the right side actually has a bunch of low end, so I'm gonna try passing that just a little bit." Also found bass presence in the 700 Hz–1 kHz range specifically because the low end was already full — added presence there instead of competing for space below. → [[EQ]], [[6 Guitars/3 Guitar Bus|Guitar Bus]], [[4 Bass/2 Bass Group|Bass Group]] — Andrew Scheps (Mix with the Masters), [[Mixing Guitars & Bass - Andrew Scheps]]
- **Cede a whole frequency region to whichever element already owns it, rather than trying to make two competing low-end sources coexist untouched — cut a percussion loop's low end because the 808 already occupies it, then reclaim cut-through room higher up instead.** Jaycen Joshua, on a tambora/percussion loop sharing space with an 808: "you're taking a little bit of the low end off because obviously the 808 is taking, or occupying, all of that. And you're adding a little bit of what you need — that upper high-mid that just lets that cut through." Stated as a governing philosophy, not a one-off move: "I always try to remove stuff so things can be on its own and separated — the bass has its frequency region, the loop has its frequency region, and so on and so forth, and when they all work together it absolutely sounds beautiful." → [[Pro-Q 4]], [[SUB Slot]], [[BASS Slot]], [[Parallel Processing]] — Mix with the Masters (Jaycen Joshua), [[Jaycen Joshua on creative drum mixing]]
- **Roll the bass off a jangly rhythm-guitar layer so it stops fighting a second, heavier guitar sharing the same low end — the second part named directly as the reason for the cut, not a generic HPF habit.** Eddie Kramer, on Dave Mason's 12-string against Hendrix's 6-string on "All Along The Watchtower": "certainly a lot of bass has been rolled off, in order to let it sit in the track and not interfere with the heavy bass end of Jimmy's guitar." *(general starting point — no exact frequency/dB given; classic pre-emptive low-end carve between two same-register instrumental parts.)* → [[EQ]], [[BASS Slot]], [[Layering and Stacking]], [[1 Acoustic Guitar Track|Acoustic Guitar Track]] — Mix with the Masters (Eddie Kramer), [[Eddie Kramer's story behind "All Along The Watchtower" by Jimi Hendrix]]
- **Complementary EQ carve between two harmonized/doubled melodic parts (a lower and higher string line singing the same phrase) so their boosts never land in the same band, plus a pre-emptive high-pass that clears room for an instrument that hasn't even been mixed yet.** Marcella Araica routes the pair through a shared sub-mix Aux first, then EQs each individually: the low string gets high-passed **60–90 Hz** specifically "because I know my bass, which I haven't done anything with yet, is going to be somewhere in that world, and I don't want it to get interference" — carving for a collision that's anticipated, not yet audible — then has its body restored with a **300–400 Hz** boost. The high string instead gets a **cut around 300 Hz** in the same zone, "because I'm boosting [elsewhere]... there's no need for me to have any type of overlapping boosts in the frequency where I'm actually trying to take away" — one part in the pair owns the boost, its partner gives up a cut in that same band rather than both pushing at once. A static carve (toolkit item 3) applied specifically to two related, same-register parts rather than a soloist vs. the rest of the mix. → [[BASS Slot]], [[BED Slot]], [[EQ]], [[Session Templates]] — Mix with the Masters (Marcella Araica), [[Blending melodies with Marcella Araica]]
- **Two co-lead vocalists sharing register is a masking problem best solved with an automatic, frequency-selective sidechain duck rather than manual fader automation at each overlap point.** James Blake's vocal ducks a second singer's dedicated 1–6 kHz "cut-through" zone automatically, only at the moments they sing together, via a 32-band dynamic EQ keyed off the other voice — the stated failure mode without it is "mess in the middle" wherever the two overlap. A standing, always-on carve triggered by presence rather than a one-off automated dip; full mechanism and owned-equivalent build on [[Sidechaining]] and [[Pro-Q 4]]. → [[Sidechaining]], [[Pro-Q 4]], [[Width and Stereo]], [[James Blake Vocal Chain]] — Mix with the Masters, [[MSM Mixing James Blake's Vocals]]
- **The single principle named as "the key to pretty much all great mixes": never more than one or two audible elements at any given moment, and they must be the RIGHT two.** Bruce Swedien's work on the Michael Jackson records, singled out as the record that "changed everything" for a working mixer's own ear: even with "two [or] 300 tracks going on," Swedien "could just make it sound [like] at any one point you never heard more than one or two elements" — and those elements have to be "the essence of what that section is," changing identity from verse to bridge to chorus. Everything not in that slot isn't silent, it's *support*, felt rather than consciously heard. Stated bluntly as the failure mode of ignoring the hierarchy: "if you hear all 100 [tracks], you can hear nothing." A restatement, from direct listening rather than an EQ workflow, of this page's own hierarchy concept above — worth keeping as the plain-language version to reach for when a mix feels cluttered and the fix isn't obviously an EQ move. → [[Referencing and Translation]] — Mix with the Masters (Michael Brauer), [[Michael Brauer's mixing inspiration]]
- **A first-person demonstration that "loud everything" isn't additive — pushing one element up just moves the masking fight somewhere else.** Chasing Andy Wallace's Nirvana *Nevermind* sound (guitars, snare, kick, and bass all reading loud and huge at once — "how does every one of those get so loud, it just sounds so simple"), the actual attempt backfired: pushing the guitars louder made the snare and drums audibly recede ("what happened to my snare, what happened to my drums now that the guitars are loud"). A concrete, admitted failure case for the instinct to just raise a fader when an element needs to compete — the fix this page's hierarchy/carving toolkit already prescribes (cut the loser, don't boost the winner) is the thing that was missing on the first pass. → [[Referencing and Translation]] — Mix with the Masters (Michael Brauer), [[Michael Brauer's mixing inspiration]]
- **Width as a de-masking tool, not just a frequency cut: spreading a synth out of the mono center is what un-buries the vocal.** A keyboard part sharing low-mid content with the vocal reads as "overloaded" while narrow/centered; widened out with an SRS-like stereo effect (see [[Width and Stereo]]), the same element stops competing and starts "framing the vocal" instead — the masking symptom (brain can't separate the vocal from the competing part in the center) resolved by moving the competitor's *stereo position*, not by carving its frequency content. Reapplied to a guitar element for the same result, confirming it's a general move rather than one-instrument-specific. A pan/width-based entry for this page's carving toolkit, alongside the existing static-panning item above. → [[Width and Stereo]], [[EQ]] — Mix with the Masters (Tchad Blake), [Shaping synth sounds with Tchad Blake](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mf8iWnF0JXQ)
- **A stated mix philosophy: support and push a vocal so it "competes" against a dense arrangement WITHOUT ever changing what it fundamentally is — carving/arrangement over tonal alteration.** On Billie Eilish's "Birds of a Feather," her voice is unusually airy, described as "not that we were adding it as much as it was there the whole time" — and the explicit warning against over-processing is that altering that natural quality reads immediately as inauthentic ("that's not the person I know and love"). The stated goal is getting the vocal to sit against a dense "symphonies of synths and piano" backdrop through arrangement/level support (carving space for it, per this page's hierarchy) rather than EQ/saturation changing its tone. A second, related observation from the same breakdown: a single balance change anywhere inside that tightly interlocking synth/piano stack can visibly break the whole arrangement ("it all falls apart") — evidence of how load-bearing every carved-out lane in a dense mix actually is. → [[Referencing and Translation]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]] — Finneas, [[Behind the Track - 'BIRDS OF A FEATHER' by Billie Eilish]]
- **A static, heavy low-cut EQ on a second pad layer, applied purely so it doesn't clash with a first pad already occupying that low-end space — a simple static carve by layer role rather than frequency-analysis.** A hook layer reuses the exact same chords as an earlier verse pad, on a different preset; rather than any dynamic/sidechain trick, a straightforward EQ cuts "quite a lot of the lows" out of the second layer specifically because the first pad is already sitting there — same-role layers get the newer one carved back, not the established one. → [[EQ]], [[Pitch and Formant]], [[Rap Subgenres]] — loner, [how to make hard drill beats with synths from scratch (headie one, drake etc.)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rcJ-j6X9bAU)
- **"Augment" a vocal by dynamically cutting the SIDE image instead of boosting the vocal itself — masking reduction read as a loudness gain.** A dynamic bell on the side image at **2 kHz**, range **2 dB**, only engages while the vocal is being sung — reducing the side image's content at that frequency reduces how much it masks the mono-centered vocal, which increases the vocal's *perceived* amplitude without ever touching its own fader or EQ. A worked example of "cut the loser" (the toolkit's static-carve principle) applied dynamically to a stereo-image channel rather than a competing instrument. Same source also details a low-mid muddiness carve (a dynamic ~200 Hz band, isolate/solo the band to find the exact culprit frequency by ear before attenuating) using the same dynamic-carve mechanism. → [[Pro-Q 4]], [[EQ]], [[Width and Stereo]] — Sage Audio, [How to Use Dynamic Eq During Mastering](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tvuBplNrYMs)
- **Vocal-priority dynamic carve on an instrumental, via a vocal-triggered external sidechain — the hierarchy enforced automatically instead of by ear.** Rather than a static carve, build multiple bands on the instrumental centered on the frequencies that clash with the vocal, set each **Dynamic**, and key each band's threshold off an **external sidechain from the vocal** — the instrumental only gives up those specific bands while the vocal is actually singing, full tone back in the gaps. A frequency-precise sibling to this note's Dynamic-carve entry (item 5 in the toolkit above), using per-band external sidechain instead of a single dynamic node. → [[Pro-Q 4]], [[2 Inst-Synth Group]], [[Sidechaining]], [[Width and Stereo]] — Sage Audio, [EQ Masterclass](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iPBnL6mx9c0)
- **A perceived-loudness EQ move built on masking: boost ~3–5 kHz (ear sensitivity), cut ~250 Hz (its masking effect on that same band).** Ears are most sensitive in the ~3–5 kHz range, so a subtle boost there reads as louder without any added gain — but 250 Hz has an outsized masking effect on exactly that 3–5 kHz range, so a subtle *cut* at 250 Hz reduces that masking and lets the 3–5 kHz content come through more clearly, compounding the same perceived-loudness effect. Both moves stacked together (small boost up top, small dip at 250) audibly increase perceived loudness with zero added level — a loudness-via-masking trick rather than a corrective EQ move. → [[EQ]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [Mastering Loud But Not Squashed](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YJVE4_CQ9Ts)
- **EQ retroactively turns another processor's own effect up or down without touching that processor's parameters — it changes perceived character, not just tone.** Since reverb, clipping, saturation, and other processing all add or amplify content in specific frequency ranges as a byproduct, EQing that range afterward doesn't just brighten or darken the mix — it amplifies or attenuates the perceived amount of that processor's own effect (worked example: boost the ~2–5 kHz band a reverb's reflections occupy and the tail reads as more present; cut it and the tail recedes) even though the reverb's decay time or the clipper's threshold/ratio never changed. The same clipper or reverb setting can read completely differently depending on the EQ placed after it in the chain — useful when a processor "isn't doing enough" and the actual fix is EQ in the band it's already working in, not more of the processor itself. → [[EQ]], [[Reverb]], [[Clipping and Loudness]] — Sage Audio, [All Processing is Connected - Crucial Knowledge for Pro Mixes & Masters](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XV-0rdUrgHs)
- **Reach for a multiband exciter instead of an EQ boost to bring up a problem frequency — it adds new harmonic content an EQ physically can't create.** Find the frequency zone that needs to come forward (demoed on a vocal), and drive an exciter there instead of raising the band's level — A/B'd with it bypassed, the vocal audibly disappears back into the mix, and even after compensating for the resulting volume difference the exciter version stays more present than a plain EQ boost, because it's generating genuinely new harmonics rather than just amplifying what already exists. — Lenno (Sol State), [IN THE STUDIO w LENNO](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9DhEPnRaKKw)

- **The core mixing question is "what can I afford to get rid of," not "how do I fit everything in."** Not every element can be loud/prominent simultaneously — deliberately keeping some things quiet or short is what makes the remaining loud/long elements read as bigger by contrast. Soloing a kick always makes you want it longer, but in the context of a busy bass part that same long kick stops being valuable. **"Clarity is what you don't hear more than what you hear"** — clarity comes from the absence of junk, not from adding more processing. → [[Kick Design]] — Steve Duda & deadmau5 (Sol State), [How Duda Changed Deadmau5 Kicks FOREVER](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3rDduXys5I)
- **A final low-end cleanup pass, instrument by instrument, once the arrangement is otherwise settled — cut hardest where the least is lost, cut almost nothing where the low end actually lives.** Go through every non-bass, non-kick element in turn and decide how much sub it can afford to lose: a piano that already had some highpass got noticeably more (accepting a slight bite into usable low-mid as the tradeoff); a guitar whose two primary notes for the part sit well above the sub zone got a much bigger cut since nothing valuable lives down there; a lead synth already handled at the sound-design stage needed no further cut; the bass — the element that actually owns this range — got only a token ~20 Hz/24 dB-per-oct highpass, purely to shave inaudible super-low mud, not to shape its real low end; drums were already handled on the drum bus's own dedicated top/bottom EQ cut. Reinforces the "kick owns sub-100 Hz" rule below with a worked multi-instrument example of *how much* each loser gives up, rather than a flat blanket cut. → [[Master Chain]] — Max Konyi, [Final Mix & Master | Studio One LoFi Tutorial | Part 9](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-OKUCuyLBSg)
- **Choose static EQ vs. multiband dynamics for mud by how much the source can tolerate a permanent cut** — muddiness is too much low-mid content pooling in a mix (loosely 100-200 Hz). On a bass, a straight EQ cut there risks reshaping the whole character of the sound, so reach for multiband dynamics instead: a 2-band split (kill the unused high band), solo the mid band and narrow its range down around the muddy zone (~100 Hz), then pull the threshold down so only the muddy notes get compressed when they get loud — the sub band underneath stays mostly untouched, then nudge the output back up. On a less identity-sensitive source (a small supporting synth), a static EQ notch is fine instead: sweep a narrow soloed band to find the exact muddy frequency (commonly clustering 100-200 Hz) and pull the gain down (~−5 dB in the worked example) with a narrow Q right on that point. → [[Multiband Compression]], [[EQ]] — You Suck at Producing, [You Suck at Mixing No. 8: Fixing Muddiness](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ISqEnvAq2ao)
- **Low-end ownership applies inside a single stacked instrument, not just across the mix** — with two kick layers, or a sub-bass layer plus an upper/harmony bass layer, decide which layer owns the low end and cut the low end out of the *other* layer(s) instead — the same "who owns this band" rule from the mix-wide hierarchy above, applied one level down inside a single stacked sound. → [[Layering and Stacking]] — You Suck at Producing, [You Suck at Mixing No. 6: Layering Sounds](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DgXB2t0tNHU)
- **Stack a harmony bass over a sub/wobble bass by cutting the harmony layer's low end** — when a second, melodic/harmony bass sits above a dedicated sub-wobble bass, cut the harmony bass's low end so it makes room for the sub underneath rather than fighting it for the same band — the two-bass version of the standard "who owns sub-100 Hz" rule, applied inside a single instrument's stacked layers rather than across kick vs. bass. → [[BASS Slot]] / [[SUB Slot]] — You Suck at Producing (Underbelly ft. San Holo), [How To Make WHOLESOME RIDDIM with San Holo](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sVpQB8yh9xs)
- **Audit a house-music habit: pull the drum bus down 3–4 dB and A/B it** — "drums should pop" instincts from house music specifically can push the whole drum bus too loud relative to the mix; test by pulling 3-4 dB off the drum stem on an older track and comparing — the quieter version often holds up as well or better. A genre-specific blind-spot check, not a universal rule. — Synthet, [all edm people make this mistake](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ondFW7rXz38)
- **Pick where everything sits (the shaker move)** — he noticed his lo-fi hi-hat had no high-highs, so the shaker got boosted exactly there to poke through: find the empty zone and assign it, "you don't want things fighting with each other" — Lophiile, [All About Basslines stream](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tnruvmw_OWc)
- **Judge layers in context, never solo** — his fake jazz-archtop guitar "sounds kind of fake by itself, but in the mix I barely notice it"; conversely a busy new part only worked once simplified into the dense mix — Lophiile, [Alina Baraz breakdown](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mdfdbKBmY_A) & [Fixing an Old Beat](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qJJZHz5D3C4)
- **The kick owns sub-100 Hz — evict everyone else** — hard low-cut guitars/keys/synths (even hats) at ~100 Hz: they sound identical, the low band belongs to kick/808 alone, and the beat takes more level. Re-find the number per song — "your EQ is not the same every time" — Kenny Beats, [808 & Kick + Subtractive EQ](https://youtu.be/4iAKvWWlCvY)
- **Resonance workflow: play → watch spikes → hover-freeze → click → bring them EVEN, not to the floor** — Pro-Q's analyzer holds peaks under the cursor and hands you the band; level the pokes against each other instead of deleting character. Same loudness, dramatically easier to listen to — and every evened resonance is free headroom — Kenny Beats, [808 & Kick + Subtractive EQ](https://youtu.be/4iAKvWWlCvY)
- **Volume before plugins** — the "that sucks" loop: try the fader at every level first; only when a sound fails loud AND quiet is it a frequency problem (then EQ/[[Soothe2]]) — Kenny Beats, [Beat Mix & Creation Process](https://youtu.be/FC__hbvnETY)
- **Note-/chord-following resonance carving replaces "one static notch per chord."** Before Soothe/Pro-Q 3, carving a moving part's resonances across a 4-chord section meant *four separate EQs*, each notching one chord, automated in turn (a real Disclosure kludge). Now a **dynamic/note-tracking cut** ([[Soothe2]], [[Pro-Q 4]] dynamic, Surfer EQ) does it in one — reach for those the moment a resonance moves with the harmony. → [[Soothe2]] — Disclosure, [Where Angels Fear To Tread](https://youtu.be/8nMXSU_K_IM)
- **Cut low-mids *hard* on stacked vocals.** A single vocal's low-mids sound natural, but stack many and they pile into a big boost that fights the bass/kick/synths. Kendrick (GKMC) and Drake solo vocals sound "missing" low-mids on purpose — the air/top carries them over a busy low end. Decide who owns the low-mid lane: the vocal or the synths. → [[EQ]], [[LEAD Slot]] — Disclosure, [Birthday](https://youtu.be/b-6x_4XRwOQ)
- **Filter synths above 7-7.5 kHz so hats and white noise stop fighting them for space** — a blanket high-shelf cut on synth layers around 7k clears the top end for hi-hats and noise elements without changing the synth's core character below the cut point; the same move reused at mastering to keep a loud master "pristine" rather than harsh up top. → [[Clipping and Loudness]] — Jauz (Sol State), [8 Tips for PRO Synths, PUNCHY Drums, & LOUD CLEAR Mixdowns](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pn3Izw3-Qmw)
- **Note-following low-mid carve on a moving vocal (Surfer EQ)** — instead of many static notches, a pitch-tracking −5 dB low-mid cut that stays locked wherever the melody goes; bounce it and move on. "Better than FabFilter *for this one job*." → [[Plugin Wishlist]] — Disclosure, [Birthday](https://youtu.be/b-6x_4XRwOQ)
- **A chord voicing itself can be a masking decision, not just an EQ move.** Dense 10-note chord stacks (the kind chased from gospel-piano tutorials) eat every frequency a vocal would want to sit in; going simpler on the voicing (fewer notes) opens the room up before any carving is even needed. → [[Chords]] — Oliver / Pat Lok (Sol State), [7 Small But Big Music Ideas](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wjGd4m5E1y4)
