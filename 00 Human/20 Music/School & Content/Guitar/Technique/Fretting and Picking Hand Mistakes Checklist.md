# Fretting and Picking Hand Mistakes Checklist

A 14-point checklist of small physical-technique errors, ordered roughly common-to-rare, covering both hands. Complements [[Grip Tension Release and Strumming-Hand Basics]] (tension release + strumming specifically) and [[String Bending Technique]] — this page is the general fretting/picking-hand fundamentals list those pages assume.

## Fretting hand

1. **Thumb placement.** Thumb rides behind the neck, not wrapped over the top, for single-note/lead playing (see grip split below for the open-chord exception).
2. **Finger angle.** Fingers should meet the fretboard close to perpendicular, not angled sideways ("Italian style" cramped grip) — angling limits reach.
3. **Grip choice is context-dependent, not universal.** Two named grips: **"Hendrix" (baseball) grip** — thumb hooked over the top of the neck — is better for **open chords**. **"Italian" grip** — thumb behind the neck, fingers more perpendicular — is better for **single-note lines and soloing higher up the neck**, because the Hendrix grip's over-the-top thumb severely limits reach in that context. Rule of thumb: open chords → Hendrix grip; solo lines → Italian grip (fingers not angled).
4. **Don't fret directly on the fret wire** (the metal fret itself) — fret just behind it, in the space between frets, for a clean note.
5. **Bend fingers outward, not inward**, for both chords and single-note lines — an inward finger curl is a common beginner-era default that's harder to correct the longer it's left uncorrected.
6. **Minimum-pressure test for grip tension.** Touch a string with a fretting finger, then slowly increase pressure while picking the note repeatedly — stop increasing pressure the instant the note rings clean. That's the actual pressure needed; anything beyond it is wasted tension. Applies to pick grip too — squeeze only as hard as needed to keep the pick from slipping, no harder (a too-loose grip that lets the pick slip is a rep-count problem, not a squeeze-harder problem — it self-resolves with practice).
7. **Economy of motion — keep fingers close to the fretboard.** Minimize how far fingers lift off the strings between notes rather than flailing them upward; some lift is unavoidable and fine, but excess lift wastes motion and time. Caveat: if a passage is being played successfully already, economy of motion doesn't need to be policed in that specific context — it's a fix for passages that *aren't* working, not a rule to obsess over once something already sounds right.
8. **Bend with multiple fingers, not one.** Reinforcing a bend with 2–3 fingers behind the bending finger adds strength and control versus bending with a single isolated finger.
9. **Bend from the wrist, not just the finger joint.** The bending motion should rotate through the wrist, not just flex the fingertip — described as mechanically stronger and more controlled. The Hendrix-style grip (thumb over the top) also suits bending better than the Italian grip, for the same reach/leverage reasons as point 3.
10. **Fix "double notes" (hand-sync issues).** A note that sounds twice — once from a hammer-on/pull-off, then again when the pick strikes — is a left/right-hand synchronization problem: the fretting hand and picking hand must land on the note at the exact same instant, not fractionally apart.
11. **Curl fingers for stronger hammer-ons**, not flat/straight fingers — applies to fretting-hand technique broadly, not just hammer-ons specifically.
12. **Pull-offs need an active flick, not a passive lift.** A weak pull-off comes from simply lifting the finger off the string and expecting it to sound; the fix is actively flicking/plucking the string sideways with the fretting finger as it releases, which is what actually sets the string in motion.

## Picking hand

13. **Move the picking hand across the strings, not just the wrist/fingers in place** — especially on passages that cross all six strings; most players already do this somewhat but under-move relative to what the passage actually needs.
14. **Don't lift picking-hand fingers.**
15. **Stop watching your picking hand.** Staring at the right hand while playing is a normal beginner reflex but worth deliberately breaking — needing visual confirmation of pick placement is a crutch that muscle memory should eventually replace.
16. **Don't use a lack of picking-hand references (Matteo Mancuso, Mark Knopfler) as an excuse to avoid the pick.** Basing a technique decision on one or two fingerstyle outliers isn't a good reason to skip pick technique — a fair exception is deliberately chasing *their specific* fingerstyle sound, not general pick-avoidance. A pick slipping out of the hand when starting out is normal and self-resolves with reps, same as the grip-pressure point above (point 6) — it isn't a reason to quit using a pick. Keep the hand relatively limp/relaxed rather than splaying fingers outward as you pick — an anchor point (e.g. resting the pinky lightly on the guitar body without gripping down) is fine, but the fingers themselves shouldn't fly open. Framed as the picking-hand version of economy of motion (point 7).

## From videos

- **Index-finger pre-mute on the neighboring string during scale runs.** While fretting a note on one string, lightly rest the index finger against the adjacent string even though it isn't being played — if an accidental pull-off motion or other noise happens on that neighbor, it's already muted. A preemptive, always-on habit rather than a reactive fix. Separately: fingers/knuckles physically rubbing against the strings (not the pick itself) is named as a distinct noise source from palm muting — eliminating that contact removed the need for a fretwrap/string dampener entirely. — BERNTH, [[10 Life-Changing Guitar Technique HACKS!]]
- **Pain/fatigue/burning as a technique diagnostic, not a toughness test.** An extreme fretting-hand-angle habit is named as a direct cause of wrist pain, fatigue, or a burning sensation while playing — treat those sensations as a signal something in the technique is wrong, not something to push through. — BERNTH, [[This Exercise Solved My BIGGEST Technique Frustration - Guitar Lesson]]
- **A "no wrist assistance" refinement to the open-string finger-independence drill, plus a decreasing-grip companion to point 6's minimum-pressure test.** Run the open-string chromatic workout (forces every finger to lift between notes, since there's no fret to rest on) with the wrist held still — isolate the motion to the fingers alone rather than letting a wrist rock assist them. Separately: a decreasing-grip variant of point 6's test — instead of starting light and increasing pressure until clean, start at a normal grip and deliberately reduce it rep by rep until notes start to die, calibrating the minimum-pressure sweet spot from the other direction. Also: don't over-anchor the index finger as a default hand-stabilizer — lift it the instant it isn't the finger doing work, same as any other finger, and don't let it always lead/anchor licks either (practice licks where it deliberately can't stay planted on one string) — both cost real independence in the other fingers when defaulted to. — BERNTH, [[This ONE Technique Mistake KILLS Your Guitar Progress!]]
- **A concrete before/after reach comparison for point 3's grip split, from a 10-year retrospective.** With the thumb hooked over the top of the neck, a modest stretch (frets 5-7-9 on one string) was physically impossible past the 8th fret; moving the thumb to the area behind the middle finger removed that ceiling entirely and added meaningfully more snap/power to hammer-ons and pull-offs — cited as the direct cause of years of "flying pinky" once resolved. Reinforces, doesn't replace, the existing grip split: thumb-over is still the right call specifically for expressive bends/wide vibrato, where the extra leverage genuinely helps. — BERNTH, [[10+ Year Guitar Progress - 10,000 Hours Later (DON'T Make These MISTAKES...)]]
- **"Hover, don't fly away" pinky cue, on a harmonic minor shape.** When the pinky isn't fretting, keep it hovering close over the strings instead of letting it bend away/upward — same economy-of-motion principle as point 7 above, with a concrete failure image (pinky bending away, feeling glued to the other fingers) that makes it easier to self-check while playing. Resist the instinct to widen fretting-hand motion as tempo rises — named as the hardest part of the drill. — BERNTH, [[This Finger Exercise SOLVED My Biggest Technique Problem!]]
- **The fretting thumb should track the fingers across strings during a legato line, not stay locked in one spot.** Positioned in the area behind the middle finger, the thumb visibly slides along as the fretting hand moves from the low E string toward the high E string on a full-fretboard legato run, rather than staying anchored at its starting position. Two stated benefits: **consistency** — a moving thumb keeps the same wrist angle/finger feel on high strings as on low strings, where a locked thumb forces a different angle per string (a real source of "clean on low strings, sloppy on high strings" inconsistency); and it **structurally enforces a light grip** — a tight fretting-hand grip physically can't let the thumb travel, so the motion itself prevents over-gripping rather than requiring separate discipline. The same light-grip benefit transfers to sweep-picking arpeggio shapes even though the thumb usually stays more fixed there — it's the grip lightness, not the literal travel, that carries over. Distinct from point 9's wrist-rotation-for-bending rule — this is thumb position along the neck, not wrist angle for a bend. — BERNTH, [[TECHNIQUE MISTAKES 89% of Guitarists make & how to fix them!]]
- **Chromatic scale chosen specifically to force equal use of all four fretting fingers for legato, plus a no-wrist-rotation rule for hammer-ons/pull-offs.** Heptatonic/pentatonic fingerings (2-3 notes per string) under-exercise the pinky and ring finger relative to index/middle; the chromatic scale's even note distribution forces genuinely equal hammer-on/pull-off use across all four fingers. Separately: unlike bending/vibrato (which benefits from wrist involvement, point 9), legato hammer-ons/pull-offs work better with the wrist **locked** and the motion isolated to the fingers alone — a named personal correction that improved control once adopted. — BERNTH, [[The BEST Daily Finger Exercise! - Guitar Lesson]]
- **A deliberate light-curl exception to point 11 for string-skipping material, plus a floating-wrist contrast case for the picking-hand anchor.** For string-skipping arpeggios specifically, curl fretting fingers *less* than usual on purpose so they lightly touch and mute the strings being skipped over — active muting takes priority over full articulation-curl in this one context. Separately: for chordal rhythm/strumming patterns, an un-anchored, floating picking-hand wrist (off the bridge) unlocks a more natural motion than the usual bridge-anchored resting position used for rock/metal picking — named as a genuine fundamentals gap even for players with strong shred technique. — BERNTH, [[3 EXERCISES I Wish I Knew As A Beginner Guitarist!]]
- **Total picking-hand relaxation as a hard requirement, not a nice-to-have, for 2-note-per-string arpeggio work — plus a concrete curled-fingers failure mode for point 11.** Any added tension/attack in the picking hand doesn't just degrade a 2-notes-per-string arpeggio exercise, it makes the hand touch multiple strings and collapses the exercise outright — worth using as a self-check ("can I even complete this relaxed?") rather than powering through tense. Separately: poor fretting-hand angle/flat fingers on the same exercise cause overlapping and missed notes from inefficient position-switching — proper curl keeps arpeggiated notes cleanly separated, called the single biggest factor in how clean the exercise sounds. — BERNTH, [[The Exercise That SOLVED My Biggest Picking Problem - Guitar Lesson]]
- **Open-string diagnostic for hand sync.** Add open strings into a scale pattern to isolate picking/fretting-hand synchronization: with a fretted note, a slightly-off sync can hide inside the pitch; an open string has no fretting-hand cover, so any timing mismatch is immediately audible. Practiced as a daily 5-minute workout, one pattern moved to four fretboard positions with tempo climbing. — BERNTH, [[Best 5 MIN Alternate Picking WORKOUT! (Hand Synchronization, Speed, Endurance)]]
- **Two more concrete fixes for points 5/11/7 above.** Fret near the **fingernail**, not the fleshy fingertip pad — easier to avoid touching neighboring strings and to build speed/cleanliness; named as the direct fix for "flat fingers." And don't leave an unused fretting finger pressed down mid-lick (point 1 territory) — lift it the instant it isn't hammering/pulling, drilled specifically on a "sandwiched" middle string (e.g. G) where both neighbors can ring if a finger lingers, not just the outer strings. — BERNTH, [[6 Steps To Amazing LEGATO Technique! - Guitar Exercises + Tabs]]
- **The chromatic "spider" exercise's overlap check, as a built-in diagnostic for point 5/11's curled-finger rule.** Most players get the finger-lift discipline right (don't lift a finger until it's needed elsewhere) but miss that the outgoing and incoming notes are SUPPOSED to overlap/ring together briefly as fingers change strings — that overlap is only physically possible with properly curled fingers that don't block the string in between, so failing to hear it usually means the fretting technique is wrong, not the exercise. — BERNTH, [[6 Guitar Exercises You're Probably Doing WRONG]]
- **"Flying pinky" named directly as a speed-killing instance of point 7's economy-of-motion rule.** Letting the fretting-hand pinky (or any finger) lift far off the fretboard between notes in a fast picking pattern is called out as a common, specific bad habit — keep it close to the strings between notes, same fix as the general economy-of-motion point above, just named for the finger it shows up on most. — BERNTH, [[Learn To SHRED With Just 6 Patterns]]
- **A third source, contributing a deliberate real-time exception to point 3's grip split, plus two diagnostic reframes.** States the Hendrix/Italian grip split as an active, in-song switch (not just a fixed per-context rule): thumb tucked behind the neck for fast technical passages, thumb hooked over the top specifically for expressive bends/vibrato, trading strict technique for the more physically free motion on purpose. **Hand-angle, not hand size, is usually the real stretch limiter** — a failed 3-note-per-string extension from a 2-note-per-string pentatonic shape is fixed by correcting the fretting hand's approach angle, not by "having bigger hands"; misdiagnosing this as a size problem is common. **Flat (uncurled) fingers block adjacent strings and prevent the snap motion hammer-ons/pull-offs need** — ties point 11's curled-finger rule to a concrete downstream technique failure rather than just tone. Also includes a bend-tuning check (play both target pitches separately before bending, verify against a backing track) and a vibrato-dynamics note (match width/speed to context — rock lead vs. acoustic ballad — rather than one fixed intensity). — BERNTH, [[The 6 WORST Guitar Technique Mistakes (and 6 exercises to FIX them)]]
