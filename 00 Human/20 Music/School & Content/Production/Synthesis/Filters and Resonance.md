# Filters and Resonance

The second block in the [[Signal Path]], and the one that gives subtractive synthesis its name. The filter removes harmonics the oscillator generated. It cannot add any.

## Filter types

| Type | Passes | Removes | Use for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Low-pass (LPF)** | Below cutoff | Highs | The default. Warmth, distance, closing a pad down |
| **High-pass (HPF)** | Above cutoff | Lows | Thinning a pad out of the bass's way, telephone effects |
| **Band-pass (BPF)** | A band around cutoff | Both ends | Nasal, radio, focused mids — good for ear candy |
| **Notch** | Everything except a band | A narrow band | Phaser-adjacent movement, removing a resonance |

Low-pass is the one you'll use 80% of the time, because it maps onto how real sound behaves: distance, absorption and dullness all read as *fewer highs*.

## Cutoff and slope

**Cutoff** is the frequency the filter acts around. Everything else is relative to it.

**Slope** is how steeply it acts, in dB per octave:

- **12 dB/oct (2-pole)** — gentle. Leaves some of the removed material audible. Warmer, more "analogue."
- **24 dB/oct (4-pole)** — steep. The Moog ladder sound; more dramatic sweeps, more obviously synthetic.

A 12 dB filter closed all the way still lets some brightness through; a 24 dB one really shuts the door. Neither is better — 12 for warmth, 24 for drama.

## Resonance

**Resonance boosts a narrow band right at the cutoff frequency.** It doesn't change what's removed; it emphasizes the edge of the removal.

Musically this is what makes a filter sweep *sing* rather than just get dull. Low resonance = a tonal change. High resonance = a pitched, vocal quality that follows the cutoff as it moves.

**Push it far enough and the filter self-oscillates** — it starts generating a pure sine tone at the cutoff frequency, with no oscillator input at all. That's the half the placement (SYN-05) missed, and it's genuinely useful: a self-oscillating filter is a free sine oscillator you can play with the cutoff knob. Many classic laser, zap and kick-drum sounds are exactly this.

> [!warning] Resonance and level
> High resonance adds real energy at the cutoff. Sweeping a resonant filter can produce sudden loud peaks — especially in the low end, where a resonant sweep through 60 Hz will slam a limiter. Watch levels when automating cutoff with resonance up.

## Filter drive

Most modern filters have a drive or saturation stage. This is not a bonus feature — analogue filters distorted as a matter of physics, and that distortion is a large part of why they sound "warm." Driving a digital filter puts some of that back. Small amounts are almost always an improvement on basses and leads.

## The filter envelope

Covered in [[Envelopes and ADSR]], but the essential pairing lives here: **cutoff + envelope amount** is where character comes from.

- **Pluck** — filter envelope with fast decay, sustain 0, high envelope amount. Bright on the attack, dark immediately after.
- **Pad swell** — slow attack on the filter envelope so the patch opens over seconds.
- **Wub / bass movement** — an [[LFOs and Modulation|LFO]] on cutoff, tempo-synced.
- **Negative envelope amount** — the filter *closes* on attack. Uncommon and distinctive.

**Key tracking** is the other control worth knowing: it makes cutoff follow the note pitch, so high notes stay proportionally as bright as low ones. Without it, a patch that sounds right in one octave sounds muffled two octaves up.

## The mix-context rule

The most common filter mistake isn't technical. A patch soloed with the filter wide open sounds impressive; in a mix it eats the same space as the vocal and the hats.

**Filter to fit, not to impress.** Most synth parts in a finished record are duller in isolation than you'd expect. Closing the filter is usually a better fix than EQ, because it removes the harmonics at the source rather than carving them out afterwards — see [[Masking and Carving]].

## On your synths

[[Model 84 Polyphonic Synthesizer|Model 84]] has the Juno filter — a 24 dB low-pass with the resonance character the whole bedroom-pop and synthwave lane is built on. [[Analog]] and [[Drift]] both offer switchable 12/24 dB. [[Serum 2]] and [[Wavetable]] carry large filter menus including comb and formant types worth exploring once the basics are automatic.

## Drill

On [[Analog]] with a saw: sweep cutoff with resonance at zero, then at 50%, then near maximum until it self-oscillates. Then kill the oscillator entirely and play the self-oscillating filter as a sine. Five minutes and resonance stops being abstract.

## From videos

- **Use an envelope follower fed by your own live VOICE (into a mic, preamp, then the follower) as a filter-cutoff modulation source, for irregular/"emotional" sweeps a knob or slider can't reproduce.** An envelope follower converts an external signal's loudness into control voltage — louder in, more voltage out — and that voltage can drive filter cutoff the same way a hand on a slider would, except the shape is whatever irregular, jittery dynamic contour a spoken/vocalized performance has, not a smooth manual sweep. Full chain from the worked example (Tyler, the Creator's "Noid"): mic → preamp (for a hotter signal) → mult, splitting to (1) a ring modulator for a robotic texture and (2) the filter directly; blend in noise and a keyboard-played pitched oscillator to taste; the same envelope-follower voltage also gates a VCA so the filter/oscillator content only sounds while you're vocalizing. Worth reaching for specifically when a part needs a sweep that reads as spontaneous/emotional rather than mechanically smooth. → Anthony Marinelli, [[Tyler The Creator Noid from Cromakopia Breakdown with a Mic]]
- **Full worked example of inverted keyboard tracking, extending the entry below**: sending negative keyboard CV to an oscillator's pitch input (not just the filter) makes low notes sound high and high notes sound low — Zawinul's ARP 2600 "backward keyboard" on Weather Report's "Black Market." A side effect worth knowing on its own: with oscillator tracking inverted, the FILTER's normal tracking also effectively runs backward relative to what's heard, so high-sounding notes (played from low keys) get progressively duller instead of brighter — "extra dull," beyond even a bass instrument's normal upper-register dulling — which reads as a deliberate mysterious/opaque character rather than a mistake. Diagnosing the patch by ear: listen for beating (none = likely one oscillator, not layered/unison), listen for filter movement over a note's sustain (a fixed, un-swept filter with resonance for "grab" reads as nasal/vocal rather than a moving sweep), and a slow LFO-to-pitch vibrato was the final layer added on top. Full melodic/compositional consequences of playing an inverted keyboard are on [[Melody Writing]]. → [[Melody Writing]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[Life Changing Synth Sound by Weather Report]]
- **"Reverse glide" for a downward pitch-slide entry, plus why a bass-register filter sweep reads richer than a high one, plus keyboard tracking amount trading off against initial cutoff.** Turn glide OFF, set/play a HIGH note first, then turn glide ON and play the actual target LOW note — pitch audibly slides DOWN into the low note rather than the more common up-glide, combined with a slow filter-envelope open for an "opening up from below" effect. Separately: a filter sweep on a low note keeps more of its swept harmonics inside audible range for the whole sweep than the same sweep on a high note (whose harmonics push further above hearing as it opens), which is why bass-register sweeps read as richer. And: keyboard tracking amount and initial cutoff frequency directly compensate for each other — tracking OFF needs a higher starting cutoff for the same brightness tracking ON would reach at a lower cutoff, worth knowing when a synth only offers fractional tracking options (1/3, 2/3, full) rather than a continuous knob. → [[Bass Design]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[MJ's Thriller Monster Growl Bass Synth Breakdown]]
- **A second real-world example of filter TYPE (not just cutoff) as a continuous, envelope-modulatable CV parameter.** A boutique Oberheim-style synth (the Shear Electronics Relic) blends continuously from 4-pole bandpass down through 2-pole lowpass to 4-pole lowpass on one knob, and because the filter's profile itself is voltage-controlled, an envelope can sweep the filter's shape over a note's duration (demoed on an e-piano patch: bandpass at the attack, settling to lowpass by the decay) — the same underlying idea as the Sherman Filterbank's Minirestyler mode below, on different hardware. Same demo also showed deliberately pushing per-voice VCA gain well past 0 dB (to +24 dB) as a controlled distortion source. → [[Saturation and Distortion]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[Oberheim Inspired Analog Synth - Shear Electronics Relic]]
- **Two more deliberate uses of resonance beyond "sweep for character": as a tunable pitch source, and as a zero-modulation formant generator.** (1) Push resonance far enough to self-oscillate, then tune that self-oscillating pitch to unison with the main oscillator for a resonant "whistle" layered on top — or drop the main oscillator entirely to hear the filter as a bare sine source. (2) Set cutoff and resonance manually to park the resonant peak at a fixed, vowel-like frequency with **no envelope-generator amount into the filter at all** — a static formant rather than the more common swept "wah," movable live by hand for an expressive, human-timed talking effect an automated envelope can't reproduce exactly. Separately, self-oscillation plus a very short EG decay routed to the filter gives each note a Hammond-style percussive pluck; softening that EG's attack time turns the same patch from a hard pluck into a softer "talking" character. → [[Envelopes and ADSR]], [[Bass Design]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[The Most Misunderstood Knob On Your Synth]]
- **Self-oscillating filter as a full percussion instrument — "talking drum" technique.** Push resonance high enough that the filter generates its own pure tone with no oscillator input at all (resonance off = silence, resonance on = a clear pitched tone from the filter alone). Layer three things on top: (1) a FAST sine-wave LFO on the self-oscillating pitch for a growly "rub" — notably faster than musical vibrato; (2) a clock generator firing a fast-decay-only ADSR into the filter for a separate rhythmic "tick" layered against the sustained tone; (3) route the result through a VCA with its own AR envelope on the exponential input, tuned for tightness (fast attack/release) vs. looseness (slower attack, more release) — this is what turns a raw oscillating tone into a percussive hit. Then physically "play" the filter's initial cutoff/center frequency live as the melodic control, sweeping it by hand for a chromatic, drum-like performance — playable as a duet against a real drummer. Edgar Winter's "Frankenstein." → [[Parallel Processing]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[How to Make Edgar Winter's Frankenstein on Synth]]
- **The general rule the OB-X entry below is an EXCEPTION to: raising resonance emphasizes whatever frequency the cutoff currently sits at, at the direct expense of low-frequency fundamental — a circuit-design tradeoff, not a side effect to fight.** As cutoff sweeps (via envelope) the ear tracks the moving resonant peak, but the more resonance added, the thinner the tone gets underneath it. A downward-sweeping filter envelope (cutoff set HIGH/open initially, then closing down over the decay stage) is a distinct sweep direction worth choosing deliberately for a "swirling"/resonant identity, as opposed to the more common closed→open-on-attack pattern. Two oscillators in UNISON (same octave, not layered apart) with matched PWM adds phasing/chorusing depth from doubling, a different tool than octave-layering for thickness. → [[Layering and Stacking]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[How I Programmed The Synth Opening On Thriller Jupiter 8 Pt2]]
- **A specific vintage filter design (Oberheim OB-X era) keeps the bass fundamental intact even at high resonance — most filters lose it.** Rush's "Tom Sawyer" bass-synth intro needs heavy resonance for its character; on most synths, pushing resonance that far thins out and loses low-end fundamental, but the OB-X's filter is a rare exception — resonance climbs while the sub/fundamental stays "boomy." **Workaround on a synth without this trait**: blend in a clean sine wave (into the filter input, or post-filter into the mixer/VCA) to restore lost fundamental when pushing resonance hard for character. Same patch's other filter-envelope trick: no sustain level on the filter envelope at all — decay time alone (stretched to roughly a bar) shapes the whole audible sweep, while the VCA envelope sits at full sustain the entire time and contributes nothing to the sweep shape. → [[Voice Architecture]], [[Sound Selection Guide]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[Tom Sawyer Legendary Synth Intro]]
- **Keyboard tracking can be INVERTED, not just turned on/off/partial — so the filter gets duller as you play higher instead of brighter.** On synths that route key-CV through an invertible stage (e.g. ARP 2600), flipping tracking upside down is a real, useful option whenever a part needs the opposite of the usual brightening-with-pitch behavior, rather than only choosing between off/partial/full. Same source also restates the reference-chain tuning method (oscillator 2 and 3 both tuned against oscillator 1, never against each other) for keeping a multi-oscillator patch locked. → [[Synth Patching and Editing Method]], [[Voice Architecture]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[5 Mistakes People Make With Synths]]
- **Keyboard tracking (key follow) on the filter isn't a binary on/off choice — set it to a deliberate partial amount when the register a part is actually played in needs it, distinct from bass's usual "turn tracking off" rule.** On an ARP Odyssey lead doubling ABBA's "Gimme Gimme Gimme" strings, key tracking set to roughly half: zero tracking dulled out the upper register, full tracking made it too bright, half made the patch "speak" evenly across its actual playing range. Contrast with [[Bass Design]], where tracking is normally turned OFF because real bass gets duller (not brighter) going up the neck — the right setting depends on what the source instrument being emulated actually does across register, not a fixed default. → [[Voice Architecture]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[How Did ABBA Create THIS on Gimme Gimme Gimme]]
- **Push resonance just short of self-oscillation, then let the ENVELOPE'S RELEASE time (not the resonance amount) shape the audible falling-pitch tail on note release.** With resonance high enough to nearly ring, modulating filter cutoff on release is effectively modulating a pitch — the filter briefly becomes a secondary pitched sound source as it decays, heard as a falling "woo" tail. Pair with a VCA envelope set as a simple gate (no attack, long release) so that tail is actually allowed to sound instead of being cut off by the amp stage. → [[Voice Architecture]], [[Reverb]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[Cosmic Synth Slide - Everybody Loves the Sunshine by Roy Ayers]]
- **Running two filters in a fixed low-integer ratio (1:2, 1:4, 1:7) is itself a distortion/coloration source, distinct from detuning them freely for a chorus-like spread — and the filter-type selection itself can be made a single continuous, envelope-modulatable knob rather than a switch.** The Sherman Filterbank's "harmonic mode" runs Filter 2 at a fixed sub-ratio of Filter 1 for a specific coloration; a "range extension" mode transposes both filters together, shifting that ratio further and pushing into heavy distortion at extremes. Separately, its Minirestyler variant collapses LP/BP/HP into one continuous crossfade knob that can itself be modulated by an envelope follower on the incoming signal, so the filter type morphs in real time with the source's dynamics rather than sitting fixed. → [[Saturation and Distortion]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[The SCREAMING Filter - How Herman Gillis Created the Sherman Filterbank]]
- **Dual-filter (MS-20-style HP+LP in series) "shredding" for aggressive, textured tone**: heavy resonance on both filters plus light distortion for extra growl gives a more aggressive, harmonically complex character than a single filter alone — this is the same series-HP-then-LP architecture behind the band-pass-pedal workaround entry below, but built natively on a two-filter synth rather than emulated with an outboard pedal. → [[LFOs and Modulation]] — Anthony Marinelli Music, [[How I Create Dubstep on Analog Synths (without a computer)]]
- **Run an outboard filter pedal in BAND-PASS mode on a synth that already has its own onboard filter — deliberately, to get a two-filter (MS-20-style) character the synth's single low-pass/high-pass section can't produce alone.** Even on a synth with a perfectly good built-in filter (demonstrated on a Jupiter-8), routing the output through a separate pedal set to band-pass mode adds resonant interest specifically in the MIDDLE of the frequency range, rather than only sweeping brightness from the top (low-pass) or thinning from the bottom (high-pass) the way the synth's own single-mode filter does — the combination reads closer to a classic dual-filter architecture (like an MS-20's series LP+HP) than either filter alone. Worth trying whenever a patch's onboard filter feels one-dimensional even with resonance pushed up.
- **A press-and-hold poly-chord voicing technique**: on a synth with a poly-chord/chord-memory function (demonstrated on an Oberheim OB-X), build a chord from a bottom triad plus separate "color" notes added above it — e.g. a minor 11th voicing built as a C-G-E♭ bottom triad, then the 5th/m7/9th/11th layered on top as the color extensions — so pressing a single key plays the whole pre-designed voicing. General voicing discipline that carries over regardless of whether the instrument has chord memory: keep the LOW register to plain triads/roots for a strong harmonic foundation, and reserve 7th/9th/11th color extensions for the upper register only — stacking extensions down low muddies the foundation rather than adding color. → [[Extended Chords]], [[Chord Naming by Context]]
- **For bass specifically, keyboard filter tracking should usually be OFF/minimal, not on — the opposite of this page's general "keep it bright across octaves" default.** This page's key-tracking entry above is written for leads/pads, where you want brightness proportional across the register. Bass behaves differently: a real bass guitar gets *duller*, not brighter, as it plays higher (upper notes have less string length/energy), so tracking the filter cutoff to pitch on a synth bass makes upper notes unnaturally thin and bright instead — the opposite of how bass is supposed to read, and it costs low-end power/weight going up the neck. Practical setting: little to no keyboard tracking on the filter for a bass patch (compensate any lost brightness on high notes with filter envelope amount instead, not tracking) — reserve tracking-on for parts that move around a lot in the upper register specifically. Applies on any synth with a keyboard-tracking/key-follow control on the filter (Ableton [[Analog]], [[Wavetable]], [[Serum 2]] all expose one). → [[Bass Design]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[10 Tips For Synth Bass]]
- **Follow-up nuance: a *little* keyboard tracking on a bass patch, deliberately attenuated to roughly half, can sound great — the entry above ("off/minimal for bass") is the safer default, not an absolute rule.** In an A/B bass-patch shootout, Marinelli's ARP 2600 patch used a workaround to get partial tracking (the ARP's keyboard control voltage comes out inverted with no built-in attenuator, so he flipped it back and manually halved it via patching) specifically because zero tracking read "a little dull" up top on that particular patch — a small amount added brightness in the upper range while keeping the low end full, and he called the result "amazing." Read both entries together: default to off/minimal, but if a bass patch's upper register sounds dull rather than appropriately duller-than-the-lows, a small (not full) amount of tracking is a legitimate fix — taste and the specific patch decide, not a fixed rule either way. → [[Bass Design]] — Anthony Marinelli & Rob Rosen, [[Battle of the Modular Giants Moog vs ARP - Clip]]
