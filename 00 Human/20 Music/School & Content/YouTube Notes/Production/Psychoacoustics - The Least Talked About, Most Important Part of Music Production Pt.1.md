# Psychoacoustics: The Least Talked About, Most Important Part of Music Production Pt.1

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SZ_2yIPp4g4)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SZ_2yIPp4g4)

## Synopsis
Part one of Sage Audio's psychoacoustics series, covering the four cues the ear uses to localize a sound source (amplitude, timing, frequency, environment/reverb) and auditory scene analysis — the five cues the brain uses to decide whether multiple sound sources belong together (the "cocktail party effect"). The practical payoff is symmetrical: the same four localization cues that make panning realistic can be used deliberately to separate two similar-sounding instruments (like a doubled guitar melody an octave apart), and the five grouping cues can be used deliberately to glue two very different-sounding sources together (like two different singers on a duet) when cohesion is the goal instead of separation.

## Techniques
- **Four cues drive sound localization, and combining all of them produces more realistic panning than amplitude panning alone.** (1) **Amplitude** — basic pan-pot panning; panning doesn't amplify the target side, it turns DOWN the opposite channel. (2) **Timing/inter-aural delay** — a sound from the left arrives at the right ear ~3 ms later; DAWs don't do this automatically, it needs a stereo delay. (3) **Frequency** — a sound bending around the head to reach the far ear loses some high-frequency content (higher frequencies are weaker and get absorbed/diffused more), so the far ear receives a signal with slightly attenuated highs; even a subtle high-frequency reduction on one channel alone still reads as panned. (4) **Environment/reverb** — the most important cue: since a real sound interacts with its environment BEFORE reaching the ears, reverb placed before amplitude/delay/frequency panning creates the most realistic sense of placement; the minute reflections give the ear a complex space to estimate size, shape, and source placement from. → [[Width and Stereo]], [[Reverb]], [[Depth and Space]]
- **Use the localization cues (not just amplitude panning) to deliberately SEPARATE two performances that would otherwise be perceived as one grouped source.** Worked example: two guitar parts playing the same melody an octave apart will likely be grouped together (shared harmonic content, onset, frequency/amplitude co-modulation — see auditory scene analysis below) if both are panned dead center. Applying distinct localization cues to each — different pan position, different inter-aural delay, different high-frequency content, different reverb — makes it far more likely a listener perceives them as two distinct sources rather than one thick one. → [[Width and Stereo]], [[Layering and Stacking]]
- **Auditory scene analysis ("the cocktail party effect"): five cues the brain uses to group or separate sound sources — harmonic content, onset, frequency co-modulation, amplitude co-modulation, and location.** If multiple sources share these cues, the brain perceives them as coming from one source. **This is the actual mechanism behind doubling** — a doubled take is different enough not to be phase-identical, but similar enough across all five cues to be perceived as "one voice," not two. Conversely, keeping any one cue distinct (most easily: location/panning) is enough to make two similar sources read as separate. → [[Artificial Double Tracking (ADT)]], [[Layering and Stacking]]
- **Deliberately GROUP two very different-sounding sources (e.g. two different singers on a duet, or a lead + counter-melody) by matching them across the five auditory-scene-analysis cues, one processing move per cue.** Worked example, two distinct vocal takes from different singers in different rooms: (1) **compress both similarly** → causes amplitude co-modulation; (2) **de-ess both** → attenuates high-frequency transients similarly, reducing onset differences; (3) **apply similar EQ** → makes their frequency responses read more alike; (4) **saturate both with identical settings** → generates overlapping harmonic content even though the underlying melodies differ (works better the more aggressive the saturation); (5) **send both to the same reverb/delay and pan both to the same location** → matches the location cue. Result won't read as identical as a true double, but meaningfully increases the odds the two voices are perceived as belonging together. Any combination of these five moves can be used to intentionally group OR separate performances — practical or creative either way. → [[Artificial Double Tracking (ADT)]], [[De-essing]], [[Compression]], [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[Width and Stereo]]
