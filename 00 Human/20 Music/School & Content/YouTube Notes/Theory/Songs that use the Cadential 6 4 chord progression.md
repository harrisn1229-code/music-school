# Songs that use the Cadential 6 4 chord progression

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jU-1N9sJ2f4)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jU-1N9sJ2f4)

## Synopsis

Full treatment of the cadential 6/4 — the classical-music device of stretching out a perfect cadence by inserting the tonic chord in second inversion before the dominant — including two ways of further extending it (a sus4 insertion, and a voice-led approach via stepwise-descending inversions) and its use as "musical punctuation" at phrase/section endings across pop, video-game, and classical repertoire.

## Techniques

- **The core device**: a plain perfect cadence is V(7)→I. A cadential 6/4 inserts the tonic chord in **second inversion** just before the V chord — I⁶⁄₄ → V(7) → I (root position) — stretching out and emphasizing the arrival. Named for the interval numbers (6th and 4th above the bass) that describe a second-inversion triad.
- **Pop examples, each at a phrase/section ending ("musical punctuation")**: "Manic Monday" (The Bangles) — ends the verse. "Our Stand by You"-type ballad phrasing similarly used elsewhere. "We Are Young" (fun.) — caps the chorus's central motif each time it repeats. "The Luckiest" (Ben Folds) — closes the chorus, in the key of D. "A Little Bit of Everything" (Dodie) — the "satisfying, drawn-out, grand" conclusion to the chorus. "New" (Paul McCartney) — closes the chorus, with an even more emphasized version at the very end of the whole song. "You're the Inspiration" (Chicago) — a *fast* cadential 6/4, one beat per chord, reading as a quick turnaround rather than a dragged-out cadence — demonstrating the device's timing is flexible, not just the slow/grand version.
- **Extension 1 — inserting a sus4**: splicing an Esus4 (or equivalent) between the I⁶⁄₄ and the V chord breaks the cadence's two simultaneously-moving voices into two separate steps (one note resolves at a time instead of both at once), further dragging out the harmonic tension. Example: the Rainbow Road theme (*Mario Kart*) — the game's grand-finale race track, fittingly capped with an extra-drawn-out cadential 6/4-plus-sus4 for climactic weight.
- **Extension 2 — voice-led stepwise approach**: "Edelweiss" (*The Sound of Music*) — the cadential 6/4's first chord is approached via a full descending bass-line walk (each preceding chord independently inverted so the bass steps down scale-degree by scale-degree) rather than arriving on the 6/4 chord directly — connects this video's device to the [[Chord Progressions]] descending-stepwise-bass entry already documented from an earlier video in this batch.
- **Classical/Baroque home turf**: "Skyline Pigeon" (Elton John) — explicitly cited as sounding Baroque-influenced partly *because* of the harpsichord timbre and partly because of the cadential 6/4 itself, a staple of Baroque/Classical-era harmony. A Bach chorale example; several instances in Schubert's "Ave Maria," including one "interrupted cadential 6/4" — the first two expected chords of the cadence appear (I⁶⁄₄ → V7), but instead of resolving to I, the V7 chord jumps to vi instead — a **deceptive/interrupted cadence**, described as a "supercharged" version of the device precisely because the drawn-out 6/4 buildup makes the redirect land harder.
