## Techniques

**Per-song riff/chord content (master record — not duplicated elsewhere):**
- **Seven Nation Army (The White Stripes)** — single-note riff on the 5th string (E), taught in 5 escalating layers: single note → 2-note power chord (add a note 2 frets up, 1 string up) → 3-note power chord (add the octave with the pinky) → same riff on the top string at the 12th fret → barred top-two-strings version. Fretting-hand low-E touch-mute demonstrated here (see routed technique below) to let the pick strum harder without ringing the open low string.
- **Free Fallin' (Tom Petty)** — capo 3 for the recorded key. Chord shapes: **Dsus4** (not the commonly-taught plain D→G, but the exact recorded voicing — a "color note" a fret above the D triad's 3rd, which is actually the same note present in the underlying G chord when a bassist plays G under it) → **Asus4** (same color-note logic). A simpler in-between version substitutes a partial-G chord for the sus voicings; full open-chord version (D–G–A) also shown for comparison — all three variants sound "correct," differing in brightness/airiness.
- **Smoke on the Water (Deep Purple)** — blues-scale-derived riff starting on G (3rd fret, low string), same power-chord-layering progression as Seven Nation Army (1 note → 2-note → 3-note power chord).
- **Wagon Wheel (Old Crow Medicine Show)** — capo 2 for the recorded key. Progression: **G (with added color note, third finger) → D → Em → C**. Practice tip: keep the color-note finger down as a physical anchor while switching between G and D shapes. Strumming caution: avoid the low strings on the D shape (top-strings-only strum).
- **Wild Thing / Louie Louie** — same progression, same key, taught as a matched pair (single-note → power chords → full triads: A–D–E).
- **Sunshine of My Love (Cream/Clapton)** — single blues-scale-shape riff; the recording's "attitude" comes from a slight bend/release on the second-to-last note (a taste/ear call, not a fixed amount).
- **Ode to Joy (Beethoven, arranged for guitar)** — melody in D major, single-note technique piece; taught landing on the root and playing the notes surrounding it (root-centric scale visualization, matches [[Root-to-Root Scale-Form Mapping Method]]'s own root-anchoring principle).
- **Knockin' on Heaven's Door (Bob Dylan)** — **G–D–Am–G–D–C** (color-note G voicing as in Wagon Wheel/Free Fallin'). Dynamics tip: alternate harder/softer strums for emotional expression ("orchestrating" the chords).
- **Wonderwall (Oasis)** — flagged as the hardest song on the list: capo 2, fingers 3+4 anchored on strings 1–2 throughout (same shape as the color-note G used elsewhere in this lesson), producing **Em7 → G → Dsus4 → A(sus4♭7, functionally ambiguous)** — the anchored ringing top notes are the song's signature character; playing plain E–G–D–A "doesn't feel right" by comparison. Strumming caution: top-three-strings only on the D-shape chord.
- **Stand By Me (Ben E. King)** — key of G: **G → Em → C → D**, with a strum-then-palm-mute technique on the G. Note on key choice: G is repeatedly the default beginner key across this whole video because most of its chords are open-string shapes with minimal technical difficulty — capos let any song be played with the same easy shapes regardless of its actual recorded key.

**Genuinely reusable technique (not song-specific):**
- **Fretting-hand first-finger touch-mute of an unwanted open low string, for pick-strummed rhythm parts.** Lightly touch (don't fret) the low string with the fretting hand's first finger at the moment of strumming — produces an inaudible click buried under the full strum, letting the player strum harder/more freely without the open string ringing out of key. Distinct from the palm/bridge muting already documented on [[Guitar Strumming Patterns]] (picking-hand technique for a chugging/muted-chug tone) and from the classical/fingerstyle thumb-hop and thumb-side muting on [[Classical Guitar Expression & Muting]] — this is a third variant: fretting-hand finger-touch muting for standard strummed rhythm parts.
- **A sus4 (or sus2) open-chord voicing can BE the "color note" version of a plain triad, not a separate chord to learn** — when a recording's chord actually contains an extended/suspended tone (because of layered guitars, bass, or synth in the full arrangement), substituting the sus voicing for the plain open triad reproduces the recorded sound more accurately than the "textbook" plain chord most beginner tutorials teach; both are "correct" to play, they just carry different amounts of color.
