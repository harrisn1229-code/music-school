# 7 tricks that will make you sound good at piano

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dYY14xjWvkI)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dYY14xjWvkI)

## Synopsis

Seven concrete pianistic flourishes that read as impressive with relatively little technical difficulty — each explained with the "why it works" mechanism, not just "play this."

## What's taught

1. **Black-note glissando**: the piano's black keys form a pentatonic scale (E♭ minor pentatonic or F♯ major pentatonic depending on chosen root) — pentatonic scales have no semitone intervals, so a fast slide across ALL the black keys can never produce a harsh clash, only mild tension (whole-tone gaps at worst). "Foolproof" specifically because the interval floor (a whole tone) rules out the harshest clash a scale run could produce. **Anchor it**: end the run by striking the root note (E♭ or F♯) so it reads as a deliberate musical gesture rather than a random slide. Cited example: the intro of "I Don't Like Mondays" (Boomtown Rats).
2. **White-note glissando**: the white keys are a C major scale — sliding across them WILL clash on the semitone gaps (E-F, B-C), unlike the black-key version. Two mitigations: **go upward** (semitone dissonance reads as less harsh higher on the keyboard than lower), and **always resolve onto C** at the end for the same "deliberate, not random" reason as trick 1. Cited example: Louis Armstrong's "La Vie en Rose."
3. **Grace notes (acciaccatura) sliding into a melody note from a semitone or tone below** — instead of striking the target note directly, slide up onto it from an adjacent (often black) key just before it. Mimics how a vocalist approaches a pitch rather than attacking it cleanly, adding character/character without altering the underlying melody. Easiest version: sliding from a black key onto the white key immediately above it.
4. **Arpeggiated octave runs**: a single chord (e.g. C major: C-E-G-C) played as a fast ascending/descending arpeggio across multiple octaves. Sounds acrobatic/impressive but is mechanically just ONE chord shape repeated in a fixed hand pattern — the "trick" is that the perceived difficulty (fluid movement across a wide range) is higher than the actual conceptual difficulty (one repeated shape). Works in any key/chord.
5. **Chromatic runs that resolve onto a chord tone**: a run through every semitone (all 12 notes) isn't tied to any key, so practice moving fluidly through all 12 semitones transfers to every key — unlike a diatonic scale run, which only fits its own key. The rule that keeps it sounding intentional rather than random: **always land the run on a note that belongs to the underlying chord.**
6. **Pedal point**: hold one bass note fixed in the left hand while playing ANY chords in the right hand, including chords quite distant from the current key (the video's example: C pedal under a C chord, then an F♯ chord, then back to C) — the sustained bass note "pulls the whole thing together" even under a harmonically wild chord choice, reading as sophisticated/cinematic rather than random. As with tricks 1-2, **resolving back to the tonic chord at the end** is what makes even a wild pedal-point excursion read as intentional rather than aimless.
7. **Chromatic voice-led arpeggio wandering**: start on any chord arpeggio (root-3rd-5th broken pattern), then after a few repetitions, move exactly ONE note of the chord by one semitone — repeat, moving one note by one semitone each time. Because only one voice moves, and only by a semitone, the resulting chord-to-chord progression is automatically smooth/well-voice-led even without planning which chords result — a way to generate interesting, cohesive-sounding chord movement (the video's example lands on a B augmented chord) without needing to consciously choose the harmony in advance.

**Common thread across all seven**: each trick reads as "random" or "showing off" unless it resolves back to something stable (the root note, the tonic chord) at the end — the resolution is what converts a flashy gesture into something that sounds deliberate and musically intentional.
