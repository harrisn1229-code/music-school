# Spinnin' A&R: How To ACTUALLY Get Heard & Signed (16 Insider Tips)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z9Alop7n5EA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z9Alop7n5EA)

## Synopsis

An interview with Spinnin' Records' head of A&R on how label signing decisions actually get made. Industry/business content rather than production technique — useful mainly if pursuing a label relationship rather than self-releasing. Core message: decisions are gut-feeling-first ("the stomach feeling"), demo submissions genuinely matter (roughly 50/50 split between cold demos and connections), and the label's real philosophy is "extend the current trend, don't outrun it" — records that innovate too far ahead of what an audience already understands get passed over in favor of ones that push a recognizable trend slightly further.

## Techniques

- **A&R decisions are gut-first ("the stomach feeling"), then checked against market fit — not personal taste alone.** The described process: an initial visceral reaction (playing a record loud in the office to see who reacts) followed by a deliberate check against what the *audience* currently wants and what's trending, explicitly separate from what the A&R personally likes.
- **"Extend the current trend, don't outrun it" — the core risk-calibration advice for demo submissions.** Too innovative/unfamiliar and an audience "doesn't understand it anymore"; too safe and it doesn't stand out. The sweet spot is a record that pushes an already-established trend slightly further (cited example: 2020's slap house sound, extended into a "little bit darker, still super melodic" variant a year later) rather than either copying it directly or replacing it with something unrecognizable.
- **Cold demo submissions are taken seriously — roughly 50/50 with connection-based signings, not a formality.** Directly contradicts the assumption that only industry connections matter; demo inboxes and submission pools get checked daily, and can genuinely lead to a signing on their own.
- **Avoid leading with an entirely new/unfamiliar vocal on a submission — rework something recognizable instead.** DJs and audiences respond much faster to a familiar hook (an old or forgotten record given new production) than a completely new vocal, because there's no recognition moment for a brand-new voice on first listen; reworking something familiar speeds up acceptance from A&Rs, DJs, and audiences alike.
- **A record needs a built "story" before it can go to mainstream/daytime radio — it can't launch straight there.** The staged path described: build buzz at a specialist/niche level first (get a credible, specific story — "top DJs are playing this at a track-list-1000 chart position"), let that level validate the record, and only then attempt to cross over to bigger platforms; going straight to daytime radio with no story behind the record doesn't work given the competition from established names.
- **A strong social following helps but isn't essential to get signed** — explicitly downplayed as a "nice plus," not a requirement, despite being a common assumption among producers trying to get noticed.
- **Labels increasingly sign artist profiles for multiple releases rather than single tracks**, specifically to avoid a scenario where a breakout single benefits a different company that opportunistically rushes out a follow-up to profit from the momentum (a "cannibalization" risk of one-off deals).
- **The one trait cited across every successful artist the A&R has worked with: passion for making the music itself, not the trappings of being a DJ/artist** — explicitly contrasted against wanting fame/status as the primary driver.
- **What a label actually adds over self-release: an established promotional network, not a magic quality bar.** DJ promotion relationships built over years, territory-specific radio promotion teams, and brand recognition ("it's signed by [label], so it's a good record" as an instant credibility shortcut) — infrastructure a bedroom producer doesn't have access to on their own, distinct from any claim that label-signed music is inherently better.
