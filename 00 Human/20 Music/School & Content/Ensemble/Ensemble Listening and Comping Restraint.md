# Ensemble Listening and Comping Restraint

Part of [[Music Theory]] — the **sideman/accompanist skillset**: what to do (and, more often, what *not* to do) while someone else is soloing. Distinct from [[Jazz Comping — Rhythmic Motif and Walking Bassline]] (a specific rhythmic comping pattern to play) and [[Solo Dynamics and Arc]] (shaping your own solo) — this note is about listening, balance, and restraint as a comper, the largely-untaught skill of *supporting* rather than *playing*.

## Connect to the rhythm section, not just the changes

- **A comping instrument's rhythmic connection point in a jazz context is the drummer's snare** — a piece of secondhand-but-repeated advice: "your chords have to connect with the snare drum," meaning the rhythmic placement of a comped chord should lock to what the drummer's snare is doing, not just land wherever feels natural against the harmony. *(general starting point: listen to snare comping/kicks for a chorus before you start comping, and place your first few hits directly on ones you hear.)*
- **The bass player's equivalent connection point in a jazz feel is the hi-hat** (vs. the kick drum in a more pop/rock feel) — the same "lock to a specific drum voice" logic applied to the bass chair. Relevant when comping alongside a bassist, or when a guitarist is covering the bass role: know which drum voice the genre's groove actually organizes around before trying to "connect with the drums" in the abstract.
- **Listen for what the soloist's intention actually is before reacting to it** — and know you don't have to follow it; you can also deliberately pull the music in another direction and see what happens. Both are valid responses; the failure mode is neither following nor contrasting, just comping on autopilot without reference to what's happening around you.

## Balance and blend

- **The comping-vs-soloing volume trap: guitarists who raise volume to solo often drop back to comping *louder* than their pre-solo level**, because "back to normal" doesn't recalibrate correctly by ear once you've been playing loud. Concrete fix: don't trust your ear to reset — actually check your comping volume against a reference (a recording, or asking the band) after a solo, not just by feel.
- **Test your amp/instrument's volume taper before a gig, not during one.** An amp with a non-linear volume response (quiet through most of the dial, then a sudden jump to painfully loud within a fraction of a turn) is a real practical hazard, not just a mixing habit to fix in the moment — soundcheck specifically for where that jump happens if you don't already know your gear.
- **Room acoustics change what "blended" means — soundcheck for it, and know how to fix it fast.** A guitar amp's low end is disproportionately easy to let bury a double bass in a room with poor bass response; a mid-focused tone with aggressively cut low end is one concrete fix. The instruction isn't "always cut lows," it's "actually listen for whether you're covering the bass player in this specific room, and adjust."
- **Know your structural role before adjusting your sound to it.** "Make your sound blend" is generic-sounding advice on its own — the concrete version is knowing where you sit in the song's structure and the band's hierarchy at any given moment (comping under a solo vs. being the lead voice vs. trading), and adjusting tone/volume/density to that role rather than to a single fixed "good" sound.

## Restraint as comping, not absence of playing

- **"If you don't know what to play, just don't play" is a real, underused option — not a cop-out.** It takes real discipline to reach for, because most improvisers' instinct pulls the opposite way (fill uncertainty with *something*) — but an uncertain phrase almost always reads worse than a rest. Monk and Dave Brubeck (comping behind Paul Desmond specifically, described as "barely comping") are reference points for how sparse effective comping can be while still supporting a soloist fully.
- **Use mimicry sparsely — mostly create phrases that contrast with and develop what the other players are doing.** Continuously "parroting" a soloist's rhythm or line reads as insecure rather than supportive, and denies the soloist the contrasting space that actually helps them think. Occasional mimicry (picking up and running with a soloist's rhythmic figure, e.g. a 2-against-3) can work as a one-off device — the failure mode is doing it as a default habit, not doing it at all.
- **Closely tracking a soloist's harmonic movement can actively work against a soloist who's deliberately playing "outside."** If a soloist is creating tension by deviating from the changes, a comper who follows that deviation note-for-note removes the tension the soloist was building — sometimes the more supportive move is to hold your own harmonic ground rather than chase theirs.
- **Play to make the other musicians — and the overall music — sound good; leave your ego at the door.** The most bluntly-stated version of the sideman mindset: if a rhythmic or harmonic idea you're pushing isn't landing with the rest of the band, the instruction is to stop forcing it and adapt to where the band actually is, rather than continuing to impose an idea nobody else picked up.

- **A style/density spectrum for comping behind a soloist, given as two contrasting named recording pairs, plus a "lean on energy vs. lean on groove" framing this page didn't yet have.** **Busy-but-not-in-the-way (hard bop):** Wes Montgomery with Wynton Kelly's trio, comping behind Cannonball Adderley — genuinely a lot of rhythmic activity, but skillful enough that it never crowds the soloist; contrasted with **minimal, harmony-only, groove-left-open:** Bill Frisell with Paul Motian and Charlie Haden behind Joe Lovano on the "On Broadway" record series — very few notes, long sustained tones (pushed further by Frisell's compressor, at times reading as barely guitar-like at all), laying down the harmony without trying to convey groove — deliberately handing that job entirely to the rhythm section for more freedom. Stated personal position: closer to the Frisell camp, alongside Herbie Hancock and John Scofield as biggest comping influences. **New "lean on energy" framing**, distinct from this page's existing "give the soloist something to lean on" idea: some soloists want a stable groove to lean on, others want to be *challenged* — pushed against, given energy to play against rather than a fixed feel — and the right read is worked out by ear from playing with people repeatedly, not decided in the abstract ahead of time. **Additional named reference recordings for comping specifically (not soloing):** Herbie Hancock with Tony Williams behind Miles Davis (high interaction, sometimes reactive to the soloist, sometimes internal rhythm-section conversation); Wynton Kelly on *Smokin' at the Half Note* (less overtly interactive than Hancock, very solid/tasteful); John Scofield on Joe Henderson's *So Near, So Far* (called "insane" comping and soloing in an all-star context). **Behind a bass soloist specifically: play less and play softer/plainer chords** — a bass player is unusually easy to bury sonically, so restraint matters even more there than behind a horn or vocal; add rhythmic activity only when the time itself needs clarifying.
