# Learn a Valuable Arpeggio Exercise to Map Out the Fretboard

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0rM7svWLaKA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0rM7svWLaKA)

## Synopsis
Jack Ruch's lesson on a five-position arpeggio-mapping exercise built to teach the fretboard through the CAGED system, worked over a 12-bar blues in **A** (A7–D7–E7, I7–IV7–V7 — the exact key/chords used as the worked example on this vault's [[Blues Form and Harmony]]). The premise: fundamentals like triads, scales, arpeggios and chord inversions are blueprints for *finding* notes on the neck, not the goal of playing itself — but a solid grasp of them is what lets a player make deliberate, intentional choices instead of noodling on autopilot. The exercise: in each of the five CAGED-derived positions, map out the dominant-7 arpeggio (root–3rd–5th–♭7, in whatever order the position's lowest available string puts first) for **all three** chords of the blues — A7, D7, E7 — using the CAGED bar-chord voicing that sits in that position as the visual/fingering anchor for each chord shape. Because there are only five CAGED shapes total, each position reuses shapes already learned for the *other* two chords, just repositioned — the same G/C/D/A/E-shape vocabulary keeps recurring, only relocated and reassigned to a different chord each time it appears. After mapping each position's three arpeggios, Ruch plays a short demonstration solo confined to that one position, using the arpeggio notes as target/outline tones for the changes — five solos total, one per position, working from the 2nd–5th-fret zone up to the 12th-fret zone (the open-position shapes repeated an octave up). Explicitly framed as a template method: swap in any tune's own chord sequence and qualities and the same position-by-position arpeggio-mapping approach still works, not just this one 12-bar blues.

## What's taught

### The exercise, generally
- **Fundamentals are blueprints, not the goal.** Chord inversions, triads, scales and arpeggios exist to teach the fingers *where the notes are*, not to be played verbatim as licks — the actual goal is playing soulful, melodic, intentional lines; fundamentals earn that by making note-finding automatic. *(opening framing, stated as the single most important thing to work on as a guitarist, regardless of style or skill level)*
- **Why start with a blues:** all three chords (I, IV, V) are the same chord quality — dominant 7th — which keeps the exercise simple while still covering a full, real progression. Ties directly into [[Blues Form and Harmony]]'s "why every chord is a dominant 7th" section — the same I7-IV7-V7-as-home-sound fact this lesson leans on pedagogically.
- **The core drill:** for each of five CAGED-derived positions, find and play the dominant-7 arpeggio (root, 3rd, 5th, ♭7, in whatever order the position's lowest note dictates) for **each of the three blues chords** in turn, using that position's CAGED bar-chord voicing as the fingering/visual anchor for the chord shape underneath the arpeggio. Because there are only five CAGED shapes (C-A-G-E-D), and three chords need a shape each in every position, shapes get reused and reassigned across positions rather than each position needing new material — "there's actually only five shapes, so you're going to repeat yourself a lot."
- **After each position's three arpeggios are mapped, play a short solo confined to that one position** over the 12-bar form, using the position's arpeggio notes as target/outline tones for each chord change — the same "outline the changes with chord tones" goal as [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] and [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]], but taught here as a full-neck, position-by-position system rather than a chunk-to-chunk connecting method.
- **Six-string completeness matters:** learn each position's arpeggio shapes across all six strings, not just the top strings, so that from any string you're soloing on, you can find the nearest chord tone to the next chord in the progression without having to shift position.
- **The method generalizes to any tune:** swap in a different chord sequence and different chord qualities (not just dominant 7ths) and the same five-CAGED-position arpeggio-mapping approach still applies — this 12-bar blues in A is a worked example, not the limit of the technique.

### Position 1 — frets 2–5 (explicitly stated fret range)
- **A7** — CAGED **G-shape** (an open G chord voicing moved up a whole step, barring where open strings would otherwise ring). Arpeggio starts on the **♭7 (G)**, then climbs: ♭7 – root (A) – 3rd (C♯) – 5th (E) – ♭7 – root – 3rd – 5th.
- **D7** — CAGED **C-shape** (an open C "cowboy" chord voicing moved up a whole step) in the same position. Lowest note is the **3rd (F♯)** on the low E string; arpeggio: 3rd (F♯) – 5th (A) – ♭7 (C) – root (D) – 3rd – 5th – ♭7 – root – 3rd.
- **E7** — a **D-shape moved up a whole step**, played with the 3rd voiced in the bass. Arpeggio starts on the **3rd (G♯)**: 3rd (G♯) – 5th (B) – ♭7 (D) – root (E) – 3rd (played either an octave up or back down near the starting note — Ruch prefers the lower option).
- Once all three shapes are under the fingers in this position, practice moving directly between them (A7→D7, A7→D7→E7, ascending and descending) before playing the position-confined demonstration solo.

### Position 2 — over the "A-shape / D-shape / E-shape" grips
- **A7** — CAGED **A-shape**, arpeggio starting on the **root**: root – 3rd – 5th – ♭7 – root – 3rd – 5th – ♭7 – root. The notes fall inside the chord shape but are played around/through it rather than block-strummed.
- **D7** — starts on the **lowest available note on the low E string in this position**, which is the **5th (A)**: 5th – ♭7 – root – third – fifth – ♭7 – root – 3rd – 5th – ♭7.
- **E7** — same physical shape as this position's D7 shape, just relocated up: starts on the **3rd**, ending up covering fifth – ♭7 – root – third – fifth – ♭7 – root – third – fifth.
- **Practical payoff of learning full six-string coverage here:** wherever you're soloing within the position, you can find the nearest chord tone for the *next* chord without leaving the position — demonstrated by playing directly between two adjacent chord tones across the position rather than jumping out.

### Position 3 — over the "A-shape / D-shape / E-shape" grips, higher up the neck
- **A7** — a shape similar to the E-shape used lower on the neck; starts on the **3rd**: 3rd – 5th – ♭7 – root – 3rd – 5th – ♭7 – root – 3rd. Flagged as a fluency goal, not just a mapping exercise: "you really want to get this down to the point where you can play it very fluidly... without having to think about which note comes next" — speed of recall matters for actually using the shapes while improvising.
- **D7** — the same **open-G-chord-style shape** moved up, now assigned to D7 in this position: ♭7 – root – 3rd – ♭7 – root – 3rd – 5th – ♭7 – root.
- **E7** — same shape as previously used elsewhere, now relocated: 5th – ♭7 – root – 3rd – 5th – ♭7 – root – 3rd – 5th – ♭7.
- Practice suggestion at this stage: vary the drilling pattern — go up one chord's arpeggio then down the next, rather than always ascending both — "there's a million ways you can practice these things," but the beginning goal is just mapping where each chord's tones sit in one position.

### Position 4 — over the "A-shape / D-shape / E-shape" grips, further up
- **A7** — starts on the **3rd**: 3rd – 5th – ♭7 – root – 3rd – 5th.
- **D7** — starts on the **root**: root – 3rd – 5th – ♭7 – root – 3rd – 5th – ♭7 – root.
- **E7** — starts on the **♭7 (D)**: ♭7 (D) – root (E) – 3rd – 5th – ♭7 – root – 3rd – 5th – ♭7 – root.
- Same reuse-and-reassign logic continues — shapes already drilled in earlier positions reappear here, just repositioned and reassigned to a different chord.

### Position 5 — 12th fret (the open-position chord shapes, played an octave up)
- **A7, D7, E7** all use the shapes that, in open position, would simply be the open A, D and E cowboy chords — but played up at the 12th fret instead, an octave above where a beginner first learns them.
- **A7** arpeggio: 5th – ♭7 – root – 3rd – 5th – ♭7 – root – 3rd – 5th – ♭7.
- **D7**: 3rd – 5th – ♭7 – root – 3rd – 5th – ♭7 – root – 3rd.
- **E7**: root – 3rd – 5th – ♭7 – root – 3rd – 5th – ♭7 – root.
- **The closing insight of the whole exercise:** once all five positions are learned, the same physical shape keeps recurring for different chords in different spots — "this is going to be the same shape here for A as it is here for D and as it is here... for E" — so the real skill isn't memorizing 15 separate shapes (5 positions × 3 chords), it's recognizing and recalibrating the same 5 CAGED shapes wherever they land. Takes time to get comfortable if new to arpeggios, but speeds up with repetition.

### Practice framing
- **Practice goal, stated explicitly:** get comfortable enough moving through A7→D7→E7 (and back) that it stops requiring conscious thought, while still staying aware of exactly which note you're on at any moment (3rd, 5th, ♭7, root) — automaticity of execution combined with retained harmonic awareness, not just muscle memory alone.
- **The exercise's real purpose is chord-tone target practice for soling, not shape memorization for its own sake** — every position's arpeggios exist to be used as target notes in the position-confined demonstration solos, giving solos "a more deliberate and intentional sound."
- **Portability:** explicitly stated to generalize beyond this one blues — change the chord sequence and chord quality (not just dominant 7ths) and the same five-position CAGED arpeggio-mapping method applies to any tune.
