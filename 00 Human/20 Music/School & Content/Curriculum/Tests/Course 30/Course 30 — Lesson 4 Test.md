> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **The rhythm, not the notes — so drill the rhythm alone on one static shell voicing first**
> Metronome on 2 and 4, one voicing, no changes. Only once the rhythm is automatic does it go through a progression with real voicings.
>
> **Q2.** **Beat 1 and the "and" of 2 — it is the reference groove, and it sits there without going anywhere**
> Foundational and static: nothing in it pulls toward the next bar.
>
> **Q3.** **The push, or anticipation — a sustained chord on the "and" of 3**
> It pulls forward into the next bar's change, arriving before the barline, so the harmony leans ahead instead of waiting.
>
> **Q4.** **The downbeat-anchored one — it avoids syncopation, so it never competes**
> Mostly straight downbeats tied together by a simple two-note melody. Deliberately unobtrusive.
>
> **Q5.** **Playing too much — chords on 2 and 4, or one per bar, nearly always sit better**
> Leaving space is the fix, and it is a decision rather than a limitation.
>
> **Q6.** **Repetition is what makes a part read as cohesive and intentional**
> Reinventing the rhythm bar to bar sounds scattered even when every individual choice is defensible, because nothing establishes a baseline.
>
> **Q7.** **Lock the motif for a full pass through the form, then depart from it, then return**
> The departure only reads as deliberate because the baseline was established first. Vary after locking, never before.
>
> **Q8.** **The drummer can catch a hit and double it**
> *Also accepted:* the soloist gets a stable surface to build on · a bassist or second comper can join the call and response
> Interaction runs in every direction through the rhythm section, and it needs something predictable to latch onto.
>
> **Q9.** **Root on beats 1 and 3 · an approach note toward the next root on 2 and 4**
> The approach can be a half step below, a half step above or a whole step above — the interval between the two roots decides which sounds best.
>
> **Q10.** **Only when the soloist or singer has actually stopped — check whether anyone else is playing**
> A fill under an active melody is not a fill, it is clutter. Genuine space is the cue, not a gap in your own part.
>
> **Q11.** **Where does it lead — which rules out a fill that decorates the chord it starts on**
> The job is to move the music forward into the next bar or the next section of the form, so the destination is decided before the notes are.
>
> **Q12.** **Play it only where the vocal rests, size it to the space, and build it simple line first, colour second**
> *Rubric (5):* places the fill in genuine space left by the singer (2) · says the fill has to lead somewhere, ideally into the next chord or section (2) · describes building it bottom-up, a plain line first with colour added as a second pass (1)
> The cue is silence from the singer, so the fill lands between vocal lines rather than under one. How much room the form leaves decides the length: a few notes at the tail of a bar behave like a passing tone at the phrase level, while a turnaround or a full open bar supports a fill that stands on its own as a melody. Construction goes simple first — a plain blues or diatonic line that already connects to the next chord — and colour is a second pass on top of it, a slid-into major 7th, a passing chord, a run down to a rootless voicing of the destination. Trying to compose something harmonically dense from the first note is what makes fills unplayable in real time.
