# Analysis of "Just" by Radiohead

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LGcH6I0Y_10)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LGcH6I0Y_10)

## Synopsis

A single-song deep dive packing modal interchange, an octatonic/diminished scale, blue notes and false relation, a tritone tension chord, and a mid-song Dorian key change into one riff-driven Radiohead track — framed as reputedly the product of Thom Yorke and Jonny Greenwood competing to cram in the most chords possible.

## Techniques

- **Modal interchange stacked chord-by-chord within a 4-chord riff, made audible specifically because the chords use full major/minor triads instead of power chords.** C major (home) then E-flat major (borrowed from parallel C minor) then D major (borrowed from parallel C Lydian, a rarer interchange source than the usual major/minor pairing) then F major (back to plain C major diatonic). Stated mechanism: a power chord has no 3rd, so it can't distinguish major from minor; swapping in full triads is what makes each borrow audible as a genuine modal shift rather than an ambiguous 5th. The riff still reads as cohesive despite the chromatic churn because it's a melodic sequence (the same phrase, a chord moving up a minor 3rd, repeated at the next starting pitch, see [[Songs that use Sequence]]) rather than four unrelated chords.
- **An ascending tremolo line built on the octatonic/diminished scale (tone-semitone-tone-semitone, the "whole-half" variant, not the alternating "half-whole"), each scale note landing on a chord tone of the riff below it** — a genuine composed use of the scale as a connective/ornamental line rather than a static texture.
- **A false relation as the vocal's blue-note mechanism, verse section**: the underlying chord (A minor) carries an E natural, while the vocal simultaneously sings E-flat against it — the classic blues device of major-and-minor 3rd (here, effectively the 3rd of the implied tonality) sounding in close proximity, generating tension that resolves on the next chord (A-flat major, which itself contains E-flat, absorbing the vocal note into the harmony).
- **A "blue chord" — a flattened-5 blue note promoted from a single note into a full chord**, sliding G-flat major to F major (5th degree down to 4th) in the pre-chorus; reused later in the chorus as one leg of a C major to G-flat tritone (one of the most dissonant intervals available, "the devil's interval"), which then resolves down onto a consonant F major 7.
- **A structural payoff for withholding a note**: the vocal sings E-flat (the blue note) against major harmony for the entire verse/pre-chorus, then switches to E natural for the first time exactly at the chorus — the color change itself (leaving the blue-note tension behind) is what makes the chorus read as a release, independent of any chord-progression resolution.
- **A bridge key change into E Dorian** (raised 6th, C-sharp where plain E minor would have C natural), paired with a second, "morphed" diminished-scale line: it starts on the same tone-semitone diminished pattern as the earlier tremolo line, but the 5th note (expected B-flat for a strict diminished scale) bends up to B natural instead, drifting the scale toward a normal minor sound mid-phrase, deliberately echoing the earlier diminished line to tie the new key area back to the rest of the song rather than reading as a clean break.
