# Secondary Dominants

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Chords]] / [[Chord Progressions]]. A **secondary dominant** is a dominant-7th chord borrowed from *another* key to temporarily point at (tonicize) a chord that isn't the real tonic. The most common way to add pull and color to a diatonic progression without leaving the key for long.

## The idea
- Any major or minor chord can be treated, for a moment, as a temporary "I" — and you approach it with **its own V7**.
- Notation: **V7/x** ("five-seven of x") = the dominant of the chord x. E.g. **V7/vi** = the dominant that resolves to the vi chord.
- In **C major**, the diatonic chords are `C Dm Em F G Am Bdim` (I ii iii IV V vi vii°). The secondary dominants are the V7 of each:
  - **V7/ii** = A7 → Dm
  - **V7/iii** = B7 → Em
  - **V7/IV** = C7 → F
  - **V7/V** = D7 → G
  - **V7/vi** = E7 → Am
- Each one contains a note **outside the key** (its leading-tone), which is the "lift" you hear — e.g. E7 → Am borrows G♯, the leading tone of A.

## Why it works
- A dominant 7th has a **tritone** (3rd ↔ ♭7) that strongly wants to resolve. Aiming that pull at a non-tonic chord makes that chord feel, briefly, like home — then the progression moves on. Instant forward motion + a splash of chromatic color.
- The most common by far is **V7/vi**, which pulls a major-key loop toward its **relative minor** ([[Keys]]) — the source of a lot of mellow/introspective R&B and pop feels.

## Hear it
- **[[🎵 BABY KEEM - 16]]** — `Fmaj7 – C – E7 – Am` = `IVmaj7 – I – V7/vi – vi`; the E7 tonicizes Am (the relative minor) before landing there.
- "Five Foot Two, Eyes of Blue" and countless jazz standards ride a chain of secondary dominants (V7/V → V7 → I).

## The full dominant-function roster
V7/x is only the most common case — there are actually **5** dominant-function chords usable as secondary dominants: **V/x, V7/x, vii°/x, vii°ø7/x, vii°7/x**, all approached/resolved with the same root-movement rules as any other chord. In major keys, "x" can be any diatonic chord except tonic and the leading-tone triad itself; in minor keys, composers generally restricted secondary dominants to only "of iv" and "of V." — Toby W. Rush, [[Music Theory for Musicians and Normal People - Enrichment|Music Theory for Musicians and Normal People]], p.42

- **A general-purpose songwriting device, distinct from every analytical entry below: deliberately respell the I chord as its own dominant 7th, 3rd inversion, as a pivot between vi and IV inside the ubiquitous I–V–vi–IV loop.** Over I–V–vi, instead of resolving straight to IV, insert **I7 with its own ♭7 in the bass** (worked in five keys around the circle of 4ths — E, A, D, G, C): the bass rises a half step out of vi's root into the new chord's bass note (the ♭7), then falls into IV — a **V7/IV**, functionally identical to the standard V7/IV entries elsewhere on this page, but framed here explicitly as a *tool to reach for while writing*, not a chord already sitting in a chart to be analyzed. Directly A/B'd against "Let It Be" (same key, same I–V–vi opening) which resolves plainly to IV with no substitution — "it just delays it by giving us this tension chord first, giving it a little spiciness." Paired teaching method: drill the identical substitution across all five keys to internalize it as a **scale-degree function** rather than one memorized shape. Worked example (recorded key, A major): `A – E – F♯m – A7/G – D(→Dm)` — chord/voicing detail and the song's own borrowed-iv follow-up → [[🎵 THE BEATLES - In My Life]]; the same chord as a *functional-inversion* case study (second, independent source) → [[Chord Inversions]]. → [[Chord Inversions]], [[Chord Progressions]], [[Borrowed Chords]] — Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared, [[Songwriting Tip from In My Life by The Beatles (songwriting lesson)]]
- **A moving-progression companion to this page's own i–iv-vamp entry below (from a different Jack Ruch video) — the same A7-as-V7/iv idea, but here inserted into a real chord change inside a full 12-bar minor blues rather than alternating over a static two-chord vamp, plus two additional layers (diminished substitution, altered voicings/scale) stacked onto the identical one-bar transition.** Over an A minor blues (Am/Dm/Em), substitute **A7 in the bar immediately before the Am→Dm transition** — A7 = A major triad + ♭7 (A C♯ E G), the V7 of D minor — everything else in the solo stays strict minor-pentatonic/blues phrasing; the substitution works whether the band actually voices the A7 or stays on Am the whole time (the tension is the point, and it resolves when Dm lands). **Arpeggio target vocabulary:** the A7 arpeggio (A-C♯-E-G) played from different starting chord tones, relocatable across positions/octaves once comfortable in one spot. - **A named Joe Pass reharmonization trademark: leave a diatonic minor chord (most often the iii) reinterpreted as its own dominant seventh as a standing color choice, not requiring resolution to what it would tonicize — distinct from every V7/x entry already on this page, all of which resolve to their target.** Jens Larsen states directly that Pass "will very often turn the three chord into a dominant chord" (iii → V7/vi, e.g. Em → E7 in C) purely "because he loves that pull forward," and that Pass deploys it **"at random" across tunes, including inside blues turnarounds**, landing on the third degree as a dominant instead of resolving plainly back to the tonic — i.e. the borrowed dominant is used as flavor on the chord it replaces, not as a pivot toward vi. - **A systematic drill for finding the secondary (ii–V) cadence that resolves to EVERY degree of a key, not just the well-known V7/vi and V7/V cases, then a worked reading of the same pattern inside a real tune.** Full table built in **B♭ major** (diatonic chords B♭maj7 – Cm7 – Dm7 – E♭maj7 – F7 – Gm7 – Aø7 – B♭maj7): **I** is the key's own ii–V–I (Cm7–F7–B♭maj7); **ii** (Cm7, minor-quality target → minor cadence) ← **Dø7–G7♭9–Cm**; **iii** (Dm7) ← **Eø7–A7**, both chords entirely outside the key; **IV** (E♭maj7) ← **Fm7–B♭7–E♭maj7**; **V** (F7) ← **Gm7–C7–F7**; **vi** (Gm7) ← **Aø7–D7♭9–Gm7**; **vii** (Aø7, minor-quality → minor cadence) ← **B♭ø7–E7–Aø7**. Drilled as a call-and-response quiz with a practice partner: name a key + scale degree, the answer is the diatonic chord AND its secondary cadence. **Applied reading, "I Should Care":** Eø7–Dm7 (secondary cadence to the ii, present); Fm7–B♭7 does NOT resolve to E♭maj7 where the key would expect — flagged as *not* a functioning secondary cadence despite the shape; Bø7–E7 likewise fails to resolve to Am, also flagged as non-functioning; Gm7–C7–Fmaj7 (secondary cadence to the IV, present); Bø7–E7–Am later in the form (secondary cadence to the vi, present). - **A worked test for spotting when a "ii–V" is actually a tonic chord plus a secondary-dominant embellishment, not a real 2-5.** `Gm7 – A7 – Dm7 – G7` superficially resembles a reducible 1-6-2-5, but reducing it the same way that works on `Cmaj7 Am7 Dm7 G7` (which safely collapses to C→G7, since Cmaj7/Am7 are both tonic) fails here — mechanically stripping the first two chords gives `A7♭9 → G7`, which "doesn't sound like a turnaround anymore." The reason: **Gm7 is itself tonic-function in this progression, not a ii chord — so the A7 immediately after it is only a secondary dominant (V7/ii) decorating the tonic**, not the structurally important move. Correct reading: Gm7(T) → G7(D). **General rule: before analyzing a minor-seventh-then-dominant pair as a real functional 2-5, check whether the minor seventh chord is actually tonic-function in context — if it is, the dominant chasing it is very likely just a secondary-dominant embellishment.** → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Chord Progressions]] — Jens Larsen, [[Jens Larsen - Why You Want To Think in Functional Harmony|Why You Want To Think in Functional Harmony]]
- **A full worked example of finding secondary dominants inside a real standard, including the test for telling a real one from a false positive, plus a clean two/three-case rule for which scale to solo with.** Analyzed on the first half of "There'll Never Be Another You" (key of E♭ major): list every dominant-7th-quality chord in the chart (G7, E♭7, D♭7, F7, B♭7), discard the chord that's just the key's own plain V (B♭7), then test each remaining one against whether it actually **resolves down a fifth** — G7→Cm7 passes (**V7/vi**), E♭7→A♭maj7 passes (**V7/IV**), but D♭7 fails the test (it moves to E♭, not G♭) and turns out to be a non-functional dominant-quality chord, not a secondary dominant despite its spelling; F7→B♭7 is flagged as "the dominant of the dominant" (**V7/V**), noted as a very common move that recurs in the same slot across many standards. - **Two ways to insert a secondary dominant that isn't already written into the chart — one for comping, one for a long static vamp.** (1) Drop a secondary dominant as a passing chord directly in front of an existing target inside a progression, stackable: over a plain Dm7-G7-Cmaj7 (ii-V-I in C), add A7 before Dm7, and/or turn Dm7 itself into D7 to pull toward G7, and/or add E7 before that A7 — explicitly framed as *not* something a soloist needs to treat as a real target, just a comping color. (2) Over a long static vamp (his example: a 4-bar Gm7 stretch, the kind found in "Airegin" or "April in Paris"), insert the vamp chord's own dominant (D7) on the weak part of the bar and resolve back — extendable into a full mini ii-V (A7 → D7 → Gm) for extra pull. The same vamp trick works in soloing too, substituting the **F♯ diminished arpeggio for D7** (they share three notes) as the line played over the inserted dominant before resolving back into Gm6/Gm7. → [[Turnarounds]], [[Diminished Chords]] — Jens Larsen, [[Secondary Dominants - What You Want To Know]]

- **A recurring V7/ii turnaround inside a real song, used as the target for a diminished-chord substitution — Stevie Wonder's "Isn't She Lovely" (key of E).** The verse (C♯m – F♯7 – A/B – E) sneaks in an A♭7/G♯7 secondary dominant on the way back to C♯m, and the chorus (Amaj7 – A♭7 – C♯m) restates the same move — a **V7/ii** (G♯7 resolving to C♯m, the ii of E major) recurring twice in the same tune's form. Framed as a general-purpose substitution spot: since G♯7 already contains a diminished triad (see [[Diminished Chords]] for the full derivation), the whole beat can be replaced with a movable dim7 rhythm grip or targeted with a symmetric dim7 arpeggio in a lead line, then generalized to *any* functioning dominant, not just this one song's secondary dominant — including a plain blues V7 (e.g. B7 in a blues in E). → [[Diminished Chords]] (the substitution mechanics + movable grips/arpeggio shape), [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]] — Jack Ruch, [[How To Use Diminished Chords For Rhythm and Lead Guitar!]]
- **A jazz blues's VI7 (the "6" of the 1-6-2-5 turnaround) explicitly named as V/ii, with the practical payoff that any altered-tension vocabulary usable over a plain V7→I resolution transfers to it unchanged — "the chord movement is the same," since C7→F and A7→D are the identical interval relationship.** Demonstrated by reusing the same ♭13→chromatic-descent→♭9 line shape over both the I→IV transition and the VI7→ii transition of the same 12-bar jazz blues, worked from three transcribed bebop saxophone licks (Cannonball Adderley, Charlie Parker, Jimmy Forrest). → [[Turnarounds]], [[Bebop Vocabulary]], [[Altered Chords]] — Jack Ruch, [[How to Add Bebop Jazz Vocabulary to the Blues]]
- **Use a secondary dominant as the analytical unlock for breaking a scary-looking, non-diatonic progression into small recognizable pieces.** Worked over C7 → E7 → Am7 in the key of C: E7 isn't diatonic to C major, but it's **V7/vi** — a functioning dominant whose whole job is to create tension/release resolving specifically into Am, not just "a chord that sounds a little off." Framed as the general first move for reading any hard chart (standards, Ray Charles-style material): spot the secondary dominant, name what it resolves to, and that one confusing chord becomes a known, three-chord unit instead of a mystery. - **Worked minor-key V7/iv example inside a real pre-chorus, with the ♭13 color tone that goes with it.** Building a G minor pre-chorus already sitting on ♭VImaj7 (E♭maj7), turns the chord about to lead into iv (Cm) into a dominant 7th on iv's own root — **C7 = V7/iv**, resolving straight into Cm — his plain-language description: "that's called a secondary dominant, a chromatic chord" that leads to something that's not the tonic. Immediately layers a **♭13 extension onto that same secondary dominant (G7♭13 in a later restatement of the same move over the V, i.e. V7♭13/iv)**, reasoning the extension note by scale membership: it has to be the ♭13 (not the plain/major 13) specifically because the plain 13 would be a note outside the underlying minor key's own scale, while the ♭13 stays inside it — a concrete illustration of "still relating back to the tonal center's scale, not the individual chord's own major scale." Full worked pre-chorus: **♭VImaj7 → C7(V7/iv) → Cm(iv) → V7(D7) → i**, with the ♭13 version layered onto the dominant chord for extra push right before the minor resolution. → [[Extended Chords]] (the ♭13 mechanics), [[Chords by Key]] (the full minor-key ladder this pre-chorus is built from) — Jared Borowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared), [[Writing a Complete Song in a Minor Key (Part 1) - Chord Progression]]
- **Use a secondary dominant as a transition/approach chord between the two chords of a minor i–iv vamp, then combine it with a relative-major arpeggio substitution** — over a D minor/G minor (minor-blues-style) progression, insert D7 (V7 of the upcoming G minor) between D minor and G minor, and A7 (V7 of the upcoming D minor) between G minor and D minor — "the dominant five chord of wherever you're heading," used as a melodic/arpeggio target rather than just a harmonized chord change. Full connected sequence: relative-major arpeggio over the minor chord (F maj7 over D minor, B♭ maj7 over G minor — see [[Chord Substitution]]) → secondary dominant (D7 / A7) → resolve onto a chord tone of the next chord. Once comfortable, layer the **harmonic minor scale of the target chord** onto the secondary dominant for extra pull (G harmonic minor over the D7, D harmonic minor over the A7) — see [[Scale Modes Reference]] § Harmonic Minor. → [[Chord Substitution]], [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § Minor blues — Jack Ruch, [[Think Different with Chord Substitutions]]
- **Worked tritone-substitution cadence into a minor tonic, voiced deliberately "wrong-looking"** — targeting A minor, use **B♭9 in 3rd inversion (B♭9/A♭)** instead of the expected E7 (the real V7): B♭ sits a tritone from E, so B♭9 does the same dominant-resolving job while giving a smoother chromatic bass rise (D → A♭ → A) into the tonic and a "hipper," rootless-G7♭13-flavored top voice. Framing principle it's used to illustrate: keep most of a progression diatonic and land only one or two non-diatonic/tritone-sub chords — the contrast against an otherwise-diatonic backdrop is what makes the substitute chord register as special. → [[Chord Progressions]] (the general tritone-substitution rule this is a worked case of), [[Chord Inversions]] — [[How to Write Neo-Soul and R&B Chords - A Simple Method|Max Konyi]]
- **Alternate stable, extended diatonic "target" chords with altered-dominant "approach" chords that resolve into them, over and over across a whole progression** — the target chords stay diatonic but get extended (9ths, 11ths, 13ths); each approach chord is an *altered* dominant (never a plain, unaltered dominant 7th) built on a root related to its target — most simply the target's own 5th, a straightforward generalized/altered secondary dominant (e.g. an altered G resolving to Cm9, since G is the 5th of C). It's the alternation itself — not any single chord — that produces a "spine-tingling," jazz-pop tension/release quality; plain dominants in the same slots don't do it. Two other approach-root relationships (half-step-above/tritone-adjacent, and a whole-step-below "backdoor" resolution) are documented on [[Altered Chords]] and [[Chord Progressions]]. → [[Altered Chords]] — Max Konyi, [The Jazz-Pop Trick for Beautiful Passing Chords](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6IFNWeQf3WU)
- **Turn any diatonic chord into a dominant 7th to modulate a 5th down, as an applied V7 pivot** — inside an existing progression, take a non-tonic chord (e.g. the IV in the key of C, F major) and add a dominant ♭7 to make it F7; that F7 now behaves exactly like a V7 and resolves down a perfect 5th to a genuinely NEW key (B♭ major) instead of back to the original tonic — an instant, smooth modulation, with the added 7th creating chromatic top-voice motion into the new key almost for free. → [[Modulation (Key Change)]] — You Suck at Producing, [You Suck at Music Theory No. 1: Dominance](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uaivGz5sryQ)
- **A secondary dominant doesn't need root position to work, and stacking a 9th on it is a free color upgrade** — worked across four targets in one key-of-C progression: G7 → C (V7/I, plain), E7 → Am (V7/vi), C9/G (its own 5th in the bass) → F (V9/IV), D9/F♯ (its own 3rd in the bass) → G (V9/V) — the inverted, extended versions pull into their targets just as strongly as the plain root-position case. → [[Chord Inversions]], [[Extended Chords]] — Max Konyi, [Classic Chords That Strike the Heart | Secondary Dominants](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ClY0nuiGHak)
- **Reharmonize one bass note per repeat with a secondary dominant, instead of the whole progression** — over a repeating descending-bassline progression (e.g. `E – B/D♯ – C♯m7 – G♯m/B` in the key of E), swap in a secondary dominant ON ONLY ONE of the later repeats: keep `E – B – C♯m` identical each time, but on (say) the third pass replace the diatonic chord under that bass note with a `V7/vi`-style dominant 7th (E9 in 2nd inversion, functioning as V/vi since E is the 5th of A) leading into the vi chord — the ear has heard the "normal" version enough times that the ONE altered pass reads as a deliberate, tasteful lift rather than a random substitution. Works especially well combined with a chromatic descending bass line ([[Chromatic Bass Lines]]) since the secondary dominant's added chromatic tone (here, a D natural against the prevailing D♯) gives the ear something concrete to latch onto. → [[Loop-Based Harmony]] (the "reharmonize the last bar" loop-refresh technique this is a specific case of), [[Chromatic Bass Lines]] — Max Konyi, [[As I Hear It - The Secrets of Great Music Ep. 02]]
