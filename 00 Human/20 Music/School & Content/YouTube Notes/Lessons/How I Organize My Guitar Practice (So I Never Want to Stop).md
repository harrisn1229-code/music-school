## What's taught

### The Practice Priority Pyramid (the philosophy layer)
Five stages, each unlocking the next, presented explicitly as a pyramid/priority order — full extraction now lives on **[[The Practice Priority Pyramid]]**:
1. **Consistency** — show up, any amount of time, before worrying about content.
2. **Enjoyment** — once consistent, optimize for what you'll actually enjoy, or consistency won't hold.
3. **Goals** — once enjoying it, get clarity on outcomes (what music, what repertoire, what technique).
4. **Design/definition** — build the literal system/curriculum that bridges where you are to each goal; explicitly counts as real practice time, not avoidance, as long as it's honest and moves you toward practicing sooner.
5. **Deliberate/challenging practice** — practice at the true edge of ability; only works reliably once stages 1–4 are in place, because jumping straight to "hard" without direction just makes people quit.

### Milestones, Rotation, Variation (the operating mechanic)
The three-part discipline that runs *within* the pyramid's top stage, applied to every exercise in the spreadsheet — full extraction on **[[The Practice Priority Pyramid]]**:
- **Milestones** — a defined "done" state for a segment/exercise (a clean take, a target tempo, N in a row) — without one you're "flying blind" on when to move to the next thing.
- **Rotation** — cycle between multiple exercises/categories (within a session, across days, or across the whole system) rather than grinding one thing to completion before ever returning to it — built-in spaced review.
- **Variation** — when a milestone-checked item comes back around in rotation, don't repeat it identically; vary it (simplest form: faster tempo; many other forms possible) so return visits still push growth rather than just re-confirming what's already known.

### The spreadsheet's category structure (his "menu")
A single spreadsheet, organized as tabs/sections, each fully self-contained with exercise name → description/rules → tracked milestone. He stresses repeatedly this specific tool (a spreadsheet) is incidental — the organizing *structure* is what matters, and he expects to keep changing tools over time (name-checks going back to a paper notebook someday). Categories, roughly in the order he works through them in a session:
1. **Warm-up & Technique** — "always, always, always do this first, no matter what else." Contains: a short visualization (~30 sec, imagining how he wants to feel during/after the session), guitar tuning, nail polishing, a physical stretch routine, then the technique exercise queue itself.
2. **Vocals** — a vocal warm-up, treated as its own tracked category because singing matters directly to his songwriting.
3. **Guitar Vocabulary** — distinct from technique: scales, modes, chord structures/inversions tied to actual musical material rather than pure mechanics (currently working "Segovia scales"; this is the same category demonstrated in full via the circle-of-fourths drill on [[The Scale Practice Method That Forces You to Really Know Your Fretboard]] → [[Circle-of-Fourths One-Position Scale Drill]]).
4. **Ear Training** — his "Learning to Listen" system: play an interval or chord structure, sing back a specified note within it (e.g. isolate and sing just the bottom note of a whole-step dyad), timed and metronome-driven, working toward streak targets (worked example in the video: chasing 20 in a row on a structure he'd already partly drilled). Built from scratch to solve a specific problem — "splitting the stack," i.e. being able to mentally isolate individual notes inside a stacked chord voicing heard on a recording, rather than hearing the chord only as an undifferentiated blob. **The category's own foundation lesson (2.9) is singing-first, not app-first**: sing the major scale with scale-degree numbers then solfège, then apply both to known melodies, with a guitar-aided bootstrap (open G string = degree 1, open B string = degree 3) for singers starting from zero — full extraction on [[Guitar Ear Training Essentials - The Missing Link for Guitarists]] → [[Scale Degree Ear Training]].
5. **Transcription** — described as "the most alive version of practicing... it is just electric." Worked example in the video: transcribing Barney Kessel's guitar intro to "I'm in the Mood for Love" (the Julie London recording) note-for-note, chord-melody style — stopping on each stacked chord and asking "what other note is in that stack?", directly applying the ear-training "splitting the stack" skill from category 4 to a real recording. Says the ear-training system he built is what made this kind of transcription tractable for him where it wasn't before.
6. **Classical Guitar Repertoire** — currently under-worked relative to the other categories because he's still mid-rebuild of the whole system, but described as "ideally my focus repertoire because it's the real music — it's what all the other stuff is for." Uses a **measure-by-measure spaced-repetition approach**: get one measure solid, then move on and don't return to play it again (implying the next-measure combination carries the previous one forward, similar in spirit to but described differently from the fully-detailed pyramid-pairing approach on [[The Check-Off Method for Repertoire Practice]] and the end-to-start approach on [[Backward Chaining Practice]] — Jared says a dedicated video on this exact classical-guitar method is coming "very soon," so the mechanics beyond "one measure down, move to the next" aren't in this transcript yet; flag for a future enrichment pass once that video posts).

*(He also has non-guitar always-items further down the menu — drum set, a newly bought clarinet — kept low-priority/optional, plus a planned integration point for pop/rock cover learning under the name "Jared Blankets," carried over unchanged from [[This might ruin my channel Practice Log No. 1|Practice Log #1]]'s preview.)*

### Warm-up & Technique — worked exercises
- **1-2-3-4 finger-permutation warm-up** — his long-standing daily always-item (predates this spreadsheet rebuild). Free written-out PDF at `soundguitarlessons.com/warmup`. One pass per session (deliberately capped — "it's for warming up," not a full drill). Currently at 85 BPM, 16th notes. Uses **two different picks** for different variations — a Jazz III (Dunlop) and a Dunlop 73 — because the feel changes noticeably between them. Variations also include alternating down-up picking vs. all-downstrokes, and, for the classical-guitar version, right-hand finger-combination patterns (p-i-p-i and others) with the left hand doing its normal warm-up shape while the right hand works the pattern. **Milestone gate: one perfect pass, zero mistakes, checks it off and advances to the next variation the following session** — deliberately not requiring multiple clean reps in a row for this particular exercise (contrast with the higher-stakes clean-streak gates on [[The Check-Off Method for Repertoire Practice]] and [[Backward Chaining Practice]]). He also logs, per exercise, his best (fewest) and worst (most) mistake counts across attempts as an informal secondary progress signal, not a strict milestone requirement.
- **Angular reach / alternate-picking exercise** — full rules and extraction now on **[[Angular Reach Exercise]]**. Offbeat-click 16th-note pattern starting on the 9th fret, angling up and back down across string groups (bottom four, middle four, top four, then repeating with a position/finger shift), with chord and sweep-picking variations of the same shape. Milestone: one perfect pass at any tempo, zero mistakes, checks it off.
- **"Snake" exercise (economy-picking/sweep drill)** — 16th notes at a metronome tempo, built to work on sweeping evenness and time-feel with economy picking; already checked off once at 110 BPM and now rotating into a different version of the same shape. Thin on rule detail in this transcript (unlike the angular exercise, no click-placement or fingering spec given) — noted here rather than spun into its own page; revisit if a future video details it further.
- **Giuliani's 120 Right-Hand Studies** — a real historical resource (Mauro Giuliani, 18th–19th-century classical guitarist), used as a checklist of right-hand plucking-pattern permutations — Jared frames the appeal explicitly as the same instinct behind his own finger-permutation exercise: "what are all the different combinations?" Working through them slowly, checking off individual patterns as milestones are hit (several already checked off out of ~120); the point isn't finishing the list, just having every option visible and organized so returning to it months or years later picks up exactly where it left off ("I don't really care if I never get to this one — if I write it down or not, it still exists").
- **Physical stretch routine** — a short, deliberately quick sequence run every session as a ritual/mental-entry point, not just physical prep: hand/finger stretches, targeted massage of the areas where he's previously had tendonitis (his fretting-hand thumb-web area and picking-hand top-of-hand/wrist), done *lightly* — he specifically warns his own past habit of massaging too hard made his tendonitis worse. Ends with a light shake-out and a few breaths before starting.
- **Nail-care update** (extends [[Guitar Nail Care]]): zero nail issues since the start of the year specifically because he's polishing and using his nails **every single day** now, playing both an electric and a classical guitar kept within reach — daily use prevents the small imperfections that build up with inconsistent play. Superglue is still the fallback for a hard chip, per the method already documented on [[Guitar Nail Care]].

### Meta-advice: organizing scattered material across multiple courses/books
For anyone sitting on many disconnected courses, books, or a teacher's scattered YouTube exercises and not knowing where to start:
- **Don't try to finish one course before starting the next.** Each course/book becomes its own rotation category (same "menu" logic as his own spreadsheet) — work a bit on one, check off what you check off, then rotate to the next category next session, rather than working linearly through a single course front-to-back before touching another.
- **This only applies to skill-based courses, not sequenced beginner curricula** — Jared draws the line explicitly: his own beginner course (a full ground-up foundation) should be finished start-to-finish first if someone is a true beginner; his other courses (chord melody, single-note improvisation, chord-tone soloing, etc.) are each self-contained skill tracks with no required order between them.
- **A good exercise is one someone already designed for a specific result — don't reinvent it.** Giuliani's 120 studies are the model: someone already solved "what are the useful permutations," so use the existing resource rather than re-deriving it. Applies equally to any YouTube teacher's exercise — take the raw idea and impose your own organized milestone/rotation structure on it if the source teaches it loosely ("here's this thing," with no built-in practice structure).
- **Any minimal tracking method works** — a stack of PDF books plus a plain checklist, or literally pencil and paper with checkmarks, is enough; the spreadsheet is not required.
