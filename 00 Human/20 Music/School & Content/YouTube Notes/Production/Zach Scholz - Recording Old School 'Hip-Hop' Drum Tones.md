# Zach Scholz - Recording Old School 'Hip-Hop' Drum Tones

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=R14N3T5AikU)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=R14N3T5AikU)

## Synopsis

Third in Zach Scholz's TASCAM M-30 series (after bass and guitar), his first-ever pass running live drums through the mixer, chasing a lo-fi/experimental drum sound and deliberately trying to do as much of the character-shaping through the M-30 as possible, saving as little as he can for "the computer." He walks through a genuinely detailed mic setup — a dual-SM57 snare trick, a hard-clipped kick, a mono overhead he can't run through the mixer (no phantom power) so instead emulates the M-30's breakup with a driven UAD 1073 plugin, a cheap-pencil-condenser stereo pair hung from the ceiling for subtle width, and a spare mic thrown in an adjacent closet purely for a processed-ambience trick — then closes with the light in-DAW cleanup: expansion (not compression) on the bussed snare mics to fight hi-hat bleed, and minimal drum-bus EQ/compression since the M-30 already did most of the work. This is the most directly applicable video in the whole batch to Nate's own hip-hop/trap production, even though the specific mixer isn't owned.

## Techniques

- **Chase the lo-fi/experimental drum character mostly through the analog mixer, minimize in-DAW processing** — his stated goal for the whole session: do as much of the sound-shaping as possible on the way in through the TASCAM M-30, leaving the computer stage light. Same "commit early" philosophy as his other M-30 videos.
- **Dual-SM57 snare trick: a standard top mic + a second angled "almost front-kick-mic" position** — mic 1 is the typical top snare position, a few inches off the batter head; mic 2 is a second [[Shure SM57]] positioned slightly above the shell (not quite a side-snare position) and a bit further back — chosen specifically because, in his setup, it captures the kick's batter head almost like a front kick mic, plus extra kit ambience. Both mics run into independent M30 channels (1 and 2), each with its own EQ, then get **summed to a single interface input** — a hardware pre-sum before the signal ever reaches the DAW. → [[TRANSIENT Slot]]
- **Push mic input hard into the M30 for deliberate clipping** — both snare mics driven "really hot" for heavy M30 clipping, producing "a very distorted snare" that reads as an intentional lo-fi choice in the context of the full kit rather than a mistake. → [[Saturation and Distortion]]
- **Kick: EQ boosted hard around 60 Hz until it clips, for a deliberately weird/lo-fi low end** — described as a "very weird low end" on its own, but "sounds really cool" once blended with the overhead. → [[SUB Slot]]
- **Overhead can't go through the mixer (no phantom power on the M30) — so the mixer's character gets emulated in-the-box instead**: his mono overhead (a Warm Audio WA47 Jr, not gear Nate owns) runs directly into the interface, then he drives a **UAD 1073** plugin hard for breakup/saturation to match the vibe of the already-M30-processed kick and snare — a concrete example of matching an ITB stage to an analog stage's character rather than letting one track sound cleaner than the rest. He notes he's open to adding a phantom-power box down the road specifically to get condensers through the M30 too.
- **Budget stereo pair trick: cheap pencil condensers (Rode M5) hung directly from the mic cable instead of mounted on stands** — because they're so light, you can skip buying (often expensive) mic stands entirely; he calls this his go-to move from when he was first starting out, not just a one-off. Used here as a subtle stereo-widening pair, kept very low in the mix (the kit stays mostly mono, this just adds width) — same processing philosophy as the other mics (drive for breakup) plus extra correction since pencil condensers "tend to sound a little bit brighter": low end pulled down and top end attenuated to sit the pair inside the mix rather than sticking out. → [[Stereo Field]], [[AIR Slot]]
- **Ambience trick: a spare mic thrown in an unrelated adjacent room, run through an amp sim purely for its reverb** — a condenser sitting in a small tiled closet to his side (off-camera, always live) captures ambient kit sound at a distance; instead of using it directly in this mix, he processes it through an amp simulator to get "cool reverb" on it — an odd signal path he says he uses often because a room-captured, amp-simmed ambience mic reads "more natural" for a bigger drum sound than just slapping reverb on the drum bus. **General takeaway: if you have a spare mic during a drum session, throw it in an adjacent room or far corner and process it separately** — cheap extra size/ambience that a bus reverb doesn't replicate. → [[Depth and Space]], [[Reverb]]
- **Snare bleed fix: expansion instead of compression on the bussed snare pair** — because the second snare mic directly faces the hi-hats, bleed is close to unavoidable through mic placement/playing alone; rather than compress (which would pull the hi-hat bleed UP toward the snare), he runs a touch of **expansion** so the snare hits push further out and the hi-hat bleed between hits drops down — expanding the dynamic range instead of squashing it. → [[Sidechaining]], [[Masking and Carving]]
- **Once separated via expansion, the snare gets pushed through additional distortion for extra lo-fi grit** — a second saturation pass specifically enabled by having isolated the snare from the hi-hat bleed first (sequencing: separate, then distort further).
- **Drum bus stays light on purpose: just subtle EQ and a little compression** — because the M30 already did most of the character work at the source, the bus doesn't need to do much; his explicit point is that most of the "sound" here was decided at the mic/mixer stage, not the mix-bus stage. → [[3 Drums Bus|Drums Bus]]
- **First-time verdict: he liked running drums through the M30 and plans a lot more drum-specific content with it** — this is his first drum pass with the mixer (after two prior bass/guitar videos), framed as the start of an ongoing series rather than a one-off experiment.
