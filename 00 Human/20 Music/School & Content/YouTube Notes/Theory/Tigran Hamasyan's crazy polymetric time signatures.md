# Tigran Hamasyan's crazy polymetric time signatures

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EWA1_XthYGE)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EWA1_XthYGE)

## Synopsis

A case study on Armenian jazz pianist Tigran Hamasyan's "Entertain Me" (from *Mockroot*), which he himself describes as being in 256/16 time — Bennett breaks down what that actually means, how to make an absurd unified meter performable by subdividing it, and the genuine polymeter running underneath (piano in the odd meter, drums locked to plain 4/4, both in the same tempo but out of phase).

## Techniques

- **The core technique for taming an absurd time signature: subdivide top-down until it's performable.** 256/16 is really 7 repetitions of a 35/16 phrase plus one bar of 11/16 (7×35 + 11 = 256). That 35/16 bar itself naturally falls into **4 groups of five + 5 groups of three** — so the practical, playable transcription is 4 bars of 5/16 followed by 5 bars of 3/16, repeated, rather than one theoretically-"correct" but useless 256/16 bar. Each subdivision level trades theoretical unity for practical readability — pick the level that suits the purpose (one unified bar for theory, the subdivided version for an actual performer to read).
- **Polymeter, defined and demonstrated**: two different time signatures played simultaneously at a unified tempo, in time but out of sync with each other. Here, the piano runs the odd 256/16-family meter while the **drummer simply plays straight 4/4** underneath — the hi-hat marks a plain quarter-note pulse throughout, meaning the "insane" meter is really only insane from the keyboard's perspective; the rhythm section doesn't need to think in it at all.
- **Practical takeaway for a composer/arranger**: an extreme meter doesn't have to be performed as extreme by every player in the ensemble — assigning one instrument the odd subdivision while an anchor instrument (drums, bass) holds a simple meter is a legitimate way to make otherwise unplayable material work in a real band.
