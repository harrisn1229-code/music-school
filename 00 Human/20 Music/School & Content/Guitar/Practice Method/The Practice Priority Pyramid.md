# The Practice Priority Pyramid

Jared's (Sound Guitar Lessons) top-level framework for **what order practice fundamentals need to be established in**, plus the three-part discipline he runs within it — the connective philosophy behind the concrete category/spreadsheet system he walks through in [[How I Organize My Guitar Practice (So I Never Want to Stop)]], the payoff video that [[This might ruin my channel Practice Log No. 1|Practice Log #1]] first previewed and [[The Scale Practice Method That Forces You to Really Know Your Fretboard|Practice Log #5]] partially demonstrated. **Jared's dedicated deep-dive on Stage 1 specifically** — the "cut it in half" minimum-commitment rule, the missed-day quota system, and the "runner's itch" framing — lives on [["how much should i practice guitar?" (the surprising answer that will keep you going)]], folded into the Stage 1 bullet below. Same channel/author as [[The Check-Off Method for Repertoire Practice]] and [[Backward Chaining Practice]], both of which are applied instances of the "milestone" mechanic below. Where [[The Five Pillars of Jazz Guitar Practice]] answers *what topics* a complete practice system needs to cover, and [[Interleaved Practice, Chunking, and Deliberate-Practice Drills]] answers *how to drill a single piece of material*, this note answers a third, earlier question: **what has to be true before "hard practice" is even a good idea, and in what order do you build it.**

## The five stages

Presented explicitly as a pyramid — each stage is a precondition for the next, not a parallel option to pick from:

1. **Consistency.** Show up, any amount of time — Jared's own baseline example: "5 minutes a day, 10 minutes a day, whatever... I don't care if it's 20 minutes three times a week." Content doesn't matter yet at this stage; showing up does. **The concrete mechanism for actually hitting this stage — deliberately explained as the "surprising answer" in Jared's dedicated deep-dive on the question "how much should I practice":** take whatever amount of time you *think* you want to practice, then **cut it in half**, and commit to *that* half as your daily minimum (2 hrs you want → 1 hr commitment; 1 hr wanted → 30 min commitment; 30 min wanted → 15 min commitment). The reasoning ties directly back to this stage's priority ordering: an ambitious target is easy to blow past on a good day but easy to break on a hard one, and breaking the streak costs more than an ambitious number is worth, since Consistency outranks everything else in the pyramid. Critically, **the minimum is a floor, not a ceiling** — you can always do more when you have time or energy (Jared's own current minimum is 1 hour/day of jazz guitar, despite training in an 8-hrs/day conservatory environment, and he regularly plays well past it). A **missed-day "quota" system** pairs with the halved minimum: if a day is missed, make it up by roughly doubling on a later day rather than treating the gap as a failure — flagged by Jared himself as optional/"a little hardcore," not a requirement. Sustained enough, consistency produces a **"runner's itch"** — it starts to feel wrong *not* to practice, the signal the habit has taken hold independent of willpower. — Jared, [["how much should i practice guitar?" (the surprising answer that will keep you going)]]
2. **Enjoyment.** Once you're consistent, optimize for what you'll actually enjoy doing — without this, consistency won't hold, because you won't want to come back tomorrow. *(general starting point on how to find it: ask directly "what's going to make this fun/nourishing" — e.g. working with recordings you love, or sight-reading fresh material if that's what's satisfying)*
3. **Goals.** Once you're consistently enjoying it, get real clarity on outcomes — what music you want to play, why, and where/how you want to play it (a specific piece finished and recorded, a repertoire list, a technical benchmark). Framed as often "tricky" and genuinely personal — Jared's own goals split into technique goals (pursued partly as their own game, independent of any specific piece) and repertoire goals (get *this* piece down, played all the way through, recorded).
4. **Design/definition.** Once goals exist, build the literal bridge from where you are to each one — the actual curriculum, exercise list, and rules. **Explicitly counts as real, productive practice time, not procrastination** — Jared describes deliberately allowing himself to spend a chunk of official practice time just designing/refining the system itself ("even if it takes me half an hour... that is when things really shifted for me"). The one hard boundary he draws against this becoming avoidance: it has to be honest, ongoing work that's actually moving you toward practicing, not a permanent substitute for practicing (see also the "finish the system, then practice it" rule on [[This might ruin my channel Practice Log No. 1|Practice Log #1]], which is the same discipline stated as a hard rule rather than a guideline).
5. **Deliberate/challenging practice.** Practice genuinely at the edge of your ability — the stage most people never actually reach, per Jared, precisely because they try to jump straight to it without stages 1–4 in place first. His claim: with the other four stages solid, deliberate practice stops feeling like grinding and starts feeling like "an endorphin workout" you can't wait to repeat, because every session has clear direction and a known target. Without the earlier stages, hard practice with no visible destination just produces quitting. **The research case for why this stage matters at all** — Anders Ericsson's *Peak*, cited directly: progress halts the instant a skill stops being challenging (not gradually — right at that point), and a doctor-performance study showing skill can *decline*, not just plateau, once a task stops demanding anything new. Full extraction, plus the named "repetition with manipulation" method and parameter-locked exercise design, on [[Repetition with Manipulation (Getting Past a Plateau)]]. — Jared, [[How to Get Better at Guitar - The science of getting past a plateau]]

## Milestones, Rotation, Variation — the operating mechanic

The three-part discipline that runs *inside* stage 5, applied uniformly across every exercise in his system (and, per Jared, "imagine that with every exercise and every category of practicing" — this is meant to generalize, not stay confined to guitar technique):

- **Milestones.** Every exercise needs an explicit "done" condition before you can know when to move on — without one, "we're truly just flying blind." The gate itself varies by exercise: sometimes a single perfect pass ([[Angular Reach Exercise]], the finger-permutation warm-up), sometimes a clean-streak count ([[The Check-Off Method for Repertoire Practice]]'s escalating rep target, [[Backward Chaining Practice]]'s 3-or-5-in-a-row token system), sometimes a checked-tempo-then-required-increase rule ([[Circle-of-Fourths One-Position Scale Drill]]). The common thread across all of them: a real, defined bar, not "practice until it feels done."
- **Rotation.** Cycle between multiple exercises and categories — within a single session, across days, or across the whole system — instead of grinding one thing to full completion before ever coming back to anything else. This is the same underlying idea as interleaving on [[Interleaved Practice, Chunking, and Deliberate-Practice Drills]] (Daniel Seriff, same "don't mass-practice one thing" logic, different channel), and it's what keeps a large system's early material fresh without a separate spaced-repetition pass bolted on — later-stage rotation naturally re-exposes earlier-checked-off items (documented concretely on [[The Check-Off Method for Repertoire Practice]]'s "refresh is built in" section). **Jared's own dedicated deep-dive on why this works names the mechanism directly**: interleaving (mixing item order) plus spaced repetition (widening review gaps on a forgetting curve) — cited research on [[Interleaved Practice, Chunking, and Deliberate-Practice Drills]] puts interleaved practice at roughly triple the test performance of blocked practice (63% vs. 20%), at the cost of visibly messier in-the-moment accuracy (60% vs. 89%) — and gives the concrete scheduling form of Rotation as a **10–12 item "batch/collection"** rotated as one project rather than finished item-by-item, worked out fully on [[Interleaved Practice, Chunking, and Deliberate-Practice Drills]]. — Jared, [Spaced Repetition Method & Interleaving Practice: Boost Your Music Retention 2-3x!](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v-kaN9ViRCw)
- **Variation.** When something checked-off comes back around in rotation, don't repeat it identically — vary it so the return visit still pushes growth instead of just reconfirming what's already secure. **Simplest form named: faster tempo** (matches [[Circle-of-Fourths One-Position Scale Drill]]'s "never repeat a checked-off tempo" rule exactly), but Jared is explicit there are "so many variations" beyond just speed — a different pick, a different right-hand pattern, a different fingering approach ([[Angular Reach Exercise]]'s chord/sweep variants of the same base shape are a concrete example of this in practice).

## Why the "video game" framing matters

Jared repeatedly describes a fully-designed system (stages 1–4 solid) as feeling like "my video game" — not because the practice gets easier, but because ambiguity disappears: sit down, see the highlighted cell/next item, and dive straight in with zero decision-making overhead. The claimed payoff is emotional as much as technical — showing up becomes something you crave rather than something you have to talk yourself into (his own anecdote: not missing a single practice day through two consecutive colds, purely because he didn't want to miss it, not out of discipline).

## Applying this to scattered material across multiple courses/books

For anyone with many disconnected courses, books, or loosely-taught YouTube exercises rather than one coherent curriculum:

- **Don't finish one course before starting the next.** Treat each course/book as its own rotation category (same logic as the categories above) — work a bit on one, check off whatever gets checked off, then rotate to the next course/category next session, rather than working straight through one course front-to-back before touching another.
- **Exception: true sequenced-beginner material should still be finished in order** — a ground-up beginner foundation course is the one case where linear completion is still correct; skill-specific courses (chord melody, single-note improvisation, a technique deep-dive) have no required order relative to each other and should rotate.
- **A good exercise is one someone already designed for a specific result — don't reinvent it.** Cites Giuliani's 120 Right-Hand Studies (worked through as part of the Warm-up & Technique category, see [[How I Organize My Guitar Practice (So I Never Want to Stop)]]) as the model: someone already solved "what are the useful permutations here," so use the existing resource instead of re-deriving it from scratch.
- **Minimal tracking is enough.** A stack of PDF books plus a plain checklist, or literally pencil-and-paper checkmarks, does the same job as a spreadsheet — the tracking tool is incidental, the milestone/rotation/variation structure is what matters.

- **A 5-step personal routine, four points worth carrying forward.** (1) **Fix a consistent daily practice TIME chosen for personal energy/focus** (own choice: ~4:30 AM, no interruptions) — the specific time matters less than picking one sustainable enough to survive long-term, since forcing a mismatched schedule burns out within weeks. (2) **Three tempo zones for technique work, with MID-TEMPO flagged as the most neglected:** very slow (technique focus — hand sync, fretting-hand curvature, actually locking to the click even slow), mid-tempo (~100-130 BPM — playing tight/locked at a realistic performance tempo; named as the zone most players skip by jumping straight from learning-tempo to max-speed, despite it mattering more for real studio/live work than pushing 16ths past 200 BPM), and high-speed (technique-ceiling pushing, only after the first two are solid). (3) **Add a "theory twist" to pure technique drills** — run picking-technique/scale-sequence exercises through harmonic minor or a mode (Dorian/Phrygian/Lydian) instead of always the same major/minor in the same fretboard area, or include diminished/augmented shapes in sweep-picking drills — same technique practice time, reinforces theory/fretboard knowledge as a free side effect. (4) **Log sessions in a written notebook** (date, topics, exercises, tempos achieved) specifically to build the practice habit in its first few weeks — visible BPM progress is motivating, and the act of logging reinforces consistency until the routine runs on autopilot. → BERNTH, [[The ULTIMATE Guitar Practice Routine! - **An exercise-category tier list (daily / often / occasional), ranked by real long-term impact rather than novelty or enjoyment.** Daily tier: finger-independence/economy-of-motion drilling (measurable multi-year payoff, "not a waste of time" even at 5-10 min), a 7-notes-per-string odd-grouping warmup (endurance + string transitions + timing in one pattern), and a legato workout with a straight-16ths-into-16th-triplet timing twist (named one of the most transformative categories of the last two years, 20-30 min/day). Often tier: classical pieces (Paganini specifically) as picking-transition workouts — valuable because non-guitarist composers wrote naturally non-uniform note-per-string patterns most guitar-native material doesn't — and a direction-changing sweep-picking pattern (genre-specific, so not universal-daily).
