# The Art of Finishing & Releasing Songs (Ft. Fox Stevenson) [4 Key Insights]

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JcI3F0ia8WQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JcI3F0ia8WQ)

## Synopsis

Fox Stevenson's finishing-and-releasing process, framed around four questions: should you finish this song or a different one, what percentage of ideas should you actually finish, what to do with the rest, and how to decide what actually gets released. A strong complement to [[Duke Dumont's 3x30 Method (Deceptively Simple)]] — where Duke Dumont's lesson is about generating enough raw material, Fox Stevenson's is about the judgment calls on the far end: which ideas deserve finishing, and which finished tracks deserve release.

## Techniques

- **The "best track on the album" test for whether an idea is worth finishing:** ask "if this was one of 13-14 tracks on an album, would it be everyone's favorite — or could it be?" If the honest answer is no, that's the signal to move to a different idea instead of finishing this one out of momentum or sunk cost. → [[Committing and Printing]]
- **Reset finishing-rate expectations using a batting-average mindset, not a school mindset.** School conditions "never fail"; music (like sports or startups) doesn't reward that framing — a .300 batting average (failing 2 out of 3 times) is considered *great* in baseball, and win-more-than-you-lose is the real bar, not win-almost-always. Concrete numbers from his own catalog: roughly 23% of project files have a usable "vibe" worth developing further, but only around 5% of ideas actually get finished and released — both numbers are treated as healthy, not as a problem to fix. → [[Committing and Printing]]
- **"Distribute for parts" — mine an abandoned idea for its best few seconds instead of scrapping the whole thing.** When a track isn't working as a whole but has one genuinely good moment (his example: a "kind of shit" jump-up track whose intro he loved), export just that moment into a personal sample pack rather than deleting the project — the goal is capturing only the bits likely to spark future inspiration, not preserving the whole idea. Explicitly the same philosophy as deadmau5's "Vault Method" (a large idea pool, a small release percentage). → [[Sample Browser and Clips Workflow]], [[Committing and Printing]]
- **Releasing is packaging, not just finishing — the same song released differently can land completely differently.** References artists deliberately splitting one body of work across multiple singles rather than dumping everything at once; the reasoning given is scarcity and narrative control — releasing everything the moment it's ready removes the "moment" a release is supposed to create ("it cheapens the stuff I do want to matter"). Framed as being the curator of your own catalog, a distinct skill from finishing tracks in the first place.
