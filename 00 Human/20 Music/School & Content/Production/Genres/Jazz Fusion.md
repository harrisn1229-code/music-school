# Jazz Fusion

**What makes it the genre:** jazz harmony and improvisation played with the instrumentation and production of a band record. The two things that separate it from straight jazz are **contrast** — sections that genuinely change character rather than one groove with solos over it — and **realism**, since most of it is now written into a DAW with virtual instruments and has to sound like humans in a room.

## The pillars

### 1. Contrast as the composition principle
- Write sections that differ in density, register and rhythmic feel, not just in who is soloing. The genre's interest comes from the switch.
- **The full stop as a fill.** Cutting everything dead for a beat reads as a bigger event than any drum fill, and costs nothing to write. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]

### 2. Harmony — the three moves that sound like fusion
- **Parallel sus chords moved chromatically** — the voicing stays fixed and slides, so the color changes without the function changing. → [[Suspended Chords]]
- **A descending chromatic bass under changing chord qualities**, landing on a **Lydian dominant**. The bass walk is the engine; the Lydian-dominant arrival is the payoff. → [[Chromatic Bass Lines]]
- **Modal interchange** as a staple rather than a special effect. → [[Borrowed Chords]]
- **Dorian as the fusion default.** A useful writing order: voice the chords with no bass at all, then add the root last — it keeps the voicings from collapsing into root-position stacks. → [[Diatonic Modes]]

### 3. Instruments — physically modeled over sampled
- Physically-modeled virtual instruments (Acoustic Samples' V-Horns, V-Reeds, Rhodes, Wurlitzer, guitars, J-bass) beat pure sample libraries here because they're playable and flexible, at the cost of slightly less perfect individual notes.
- **Research the real instrument before writing for it** — range, idiomatic gestures, what a player physically does. Non-negotiable for horns.
- **Automate every exposed parameter.** A real instrument's tone, dynamics and vibrato are never static. → [[Rhythm and Groove]]
- A piano layered with a Rhodes patch makes a good compound "key-bass" texture. → [[Sound Selection Guide]]

### 4. The three-tier reverb system
The mix technique that does most of the realism work:
1. **Dry everything out first.** Every library ships with baked-in room; left alone you stack a dozen incompatible rooms on top of each other.
2. **One shared sound-stage room** every instrument sends to in differing amounts — send level plus pan is what places players front-to-back in one believable space.
3. **One larger shared hall** anything can additionally reach into for scale, still coherent because it's shared.
4. **Flavor reverbs per special case** (a guitar, a vocal, one synth) — these can be completely unrealistic; their job is separation, not blend.

→ [[Reverb]] for the full treatment. FabFilter [[Pro-R 2]] on a modified "Studio B" is the worked example of the sound-stage room.

## Arrangement

Pillar 1 already names contrast as *the* composition principle. This is what that means bar by bar.

| Section | Bars | Slots filled | Density |
|---|---|---|---|
| Head | 16–32 | full band stating the melody in unison or harmony | 6 |
| Solo 1 | open | rhythm section only under the soloist — **register drops** | 4 |
| Interlude | 8–16 | written, not improvised — the section that changes feel | 3–5 |
| Solo 2 | open | different instrument, different density than solo 1 | 4–5 |
| Head out | 16–32 | as the head, often a tag or a vamp out | 6 |

**Entrances & exits**
- **Sections must differ in density, register AND rhythmic feel** — not just in who's soloing. A record where every section is the same groove with a different soloist over it is straight jazz, not fusion.
- Solo sections should *thin*. The comping instrument choosing to lay out for eight bars is a real arrangement decision.
- **The full stop as a fill** — cut everything dead for a beat. Reads bigger than any drum fill and costs nothing to write.
- The interlude is where the composition happens. It's the section that stops the record being a blowing session.

**The contrast move — change the feel, not the parts.** Straight to swung, double-time to half-time, 4/4 to a 7 or a 6. Fusion is the one genre here where the *rhythmic* axis carries the section contrast that other genres put on density or filtering.

**Density rule: 3–6, deliberately variable.** Variability is the point. Where lo-fi holds flat and pop swings on a schedule, fusion changes density unpredictably, which is what makes it read as played rather than assembled.
