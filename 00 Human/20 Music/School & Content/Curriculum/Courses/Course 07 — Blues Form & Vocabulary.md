# Course 07 — Blues Form & Vocabulary

The 12-bar form, the vocabulary that goes over it, and the one idea that makes both make sense: **blues doesn't obey major-key functional harmony.** Everything strange about it follows from that.

**What it assumes**

- [[Course 02 — Chord Function]] — you need the functional rules before the course that breaks them means anything. Blues is interesting precisely because it declines to resolve the way Course 02 says chords want to.

| 3    | The Vocabulary                    | 2        | [[Course 07 — Lesson 3 Test]] |

**What this unlocks**

- The jazz material. A jazz blues is this form with substitutions layered over it, so the substitutions are unreadable until the plain form is automatic.
- [[Joe Pass Guitar Chords - Enrichment]] p.23 — a 12-bar in C with six progressively denser reharmonisations. Good material, and the second tier rather than the first.
- Practically: blues is the shared ancestor of rock, soul, funk and — through sampling — most of hip hop. The ♭3/♭7 vocabulary is already in the music; this makes using it a choice.

> [!info] Twelve bars is the easy half
> Knowing a blues is twelve bars long is where most people stop. The form, the three dominant chords, the scale, the turnaround and the blue notes are five separate pieces of knowledge, and each one is doing a job the others can't.

## The lessons

### Lesson 1 — The Form

**Read**
- [ ] [[Blues Form and Harmony]] → *The 12-bar form*, *Why every chord is a dominant 7th*, *The variants worth knowing*

**The material**
- **[[BLU-C01 — The 12-bar form|Twelve bars, three chords, three four-bar phrases.]]** In A the chords are **A7, D7 and E7** — I7, IV7, V7.
- `A A A A / D D A A / E D A E` — bar 12 is the turnaround back to the top, which is Lesson 2's material.
- Then the shift the whole strand rests on: **[[BLU-C02 — Why every chord is a dominant 7th|all three chords are dominant 7ths, including the tonic]]**, and the major scale doesn't give you that. Blues isn't operating in major-key functional harmony at all.
- **The dominant 7th is the home sound here, not a tension needing resolution.** The ♭7 is colour. That single idea explains why the blues scale works over everything, why the ♭3 isn't wrong, and why the form circles rather than resolving.
- **[[BLU-C03 — Quick change, 8-bar and minor blues|Three variants worth knowing]]:** a **quick change** moves bar 2 to the IV instead of holding I. An **8-bar blues** is a separate form in its own right, not a truncated 12-bar. A **minor blues** uses i7 iv7 v7, often with a ♭VI at bar 9 — but the **V usually stays dominant**, because the leading tone is still wanted.

**Concepts**
- [[BLU-C01 — The 12-bar form]]
- [[BLU-C02 — Why every chord is a dominant 7th]]
- [[BLU-C03 — Quick change, 8-bar and minor blues]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — write out a 12-bar in A, then E, then G. Then the quick-change version of each, and say which bar changed.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — play all three to a click at 90 BPM, saying the bar number out loud at every chord change until you stop needing to count. Then play a minor blues in A and find the point where the V is still dominant.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 07 — Lesson 1 Test]]

### Lesson 2 — Turnarounds and the Lyric Shape

**Read**
- [ ] [[Blues Form and Harmony]] → *Turnarounds*, *Call and response*, *Feel*, *Where it went*

**The material**
- **[[BLU-C04 — Turnarounds|A turnaround is the last two bars]]**, and its job is to *break* the resolution so the ear expects bar 1 again — usually by landing on V. In A: `A7 – F♯7 – B7 – E7`, or a chromatic line descending over an A pedal.
- Without it the form stops instead of circling, which is the difference between twelve bars and a loop.
- **[[BLU-C05 — Call and response is the form|The form is call and response — AAB.]]** Bars 1–4 state a line, bars 5–8 repeat it, bars 9–12 answer it. The repeat is the point, not padding.
- In performance it plays out between the voice and an instrument, in the gaps the form deliberately leaves. **The space is where the response lives**, which is why a blues arrangement that fills every bar sounds wrong.
- **Shuffle feel** is a triplet with the middle note removed, long–short — the same fact as [[RHY-C03 — Swing is a triplet with the middle note gone]], arriving from a different direction.
- **[[BLU-C08 — Blues into rock and hip hop|Where it went.]]** Rock took the form and the pentatonic vocabulary almost wholesale. **Hip hop inherited it through sampling** — soul and funk records built on blues harmony got sampled, so the ♭3/♭7 vocabulary and the call-and-response phrasing survive in the loops even where the 12-bar doesn't.

**Concepts**
- [[BLU-C04 — Turnarounds]]
- [[BLU-C05 — Call and response is the form]]
- [[BLU-C08 — Blues into rock and hip hop]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — play a turnaround in A and in E at the end of a full chorus. Then take a blues lyric and mark its AAB, saying which bars carry the call, the repeat and the answer.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — record a 12-bar in A and leave the gaps empty. Sing the AAB over it, then go back and fill only the gaps with a guitar answer. Nothing in the called bars.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 07 — Lesson 2 Test]]

### Lesson 3 — The Vocabulary

**Read**
- [ ] [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]] in full

**The material**
- **[[BLU-C06 — Blue notes and the blues scale|Blue notes are the ♭3, ♭5 and ♭7]]**, played against a major or dominant background. The **blues scale is minor pentatonic plus the ♭5** — in A: **A C D E♭ E G** — and the ♭5 is a passing tone, never a resting note.
- Which raises the question the strand hinges on: the scale has a ♭3, the chord has a major 3rd, and a semitone apart is normally a clash. **[[BLU-C07 — Why the ♭3 isn't wrong|Three reasons it isn't an error]]:**
1. **The friction *is* the sound.** The grind between ♭3 and ♮3 is what "bluesy" names.
2. **Blues isn't functional major/minor** — the scale is imposed over the chords, not derived from them. Lesson 1's dominant-7th tonic is the same fact.
3. **The note is a region, not a pitch.** Players bend the ♭3 up toward the ♮3 and sit between them; the blues has microtonality built in, and notation can't show it.
- The underrated half: over a dominant, three of minor pentatonic's five notes are the chord's own tones — root, 5th and ♭7 — so the ♭3 is the only note in it that deliberately fights the chord.
- It does **not** dodge the natural 4th, which is the one note that genuinely clashes against a major 3rd: in A that's the **D** in A C D E G, and it gets treated the way the ♭5 is, passed through rather than landed on. The scale that leaves the 4th out altogether is **major** pentatonic.

**Concepts**
- [[BLU-C06 — Blue notes and the blues scale]]
- [[BLU-C07 — Why the ♭3 isn't wrong]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — spell the blues scale in A, E and G, and say which note is the ♭5 in each. Then name the three blue notes in A without looking.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — over an A7 vamp, play the A blues scale and bend the ♭3 toward the ♮3 until it sits right. Then play A Mixolydian over the same vamp. Record both so you can hear the difference cold rather than while your hands are busy.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 07 — Lesson 3 Test]]
