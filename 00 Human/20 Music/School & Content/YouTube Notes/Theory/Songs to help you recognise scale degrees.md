# Songs to help you recognise scale degrees

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ppfTDU8dfiw)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ppfTDU8dfiw)

## Synopsis

The song-anchor method applied to scale degrees — a melody's exact key/note names matter less than which scale degree it's sitting on, since a degree (tonic, dominant, leading tone, etc.) has a recognizable feeling regardless of key. Covers all 7 major-scale degrees plus 5 more chromatic/minor degrees, for 12 total — every possible scale degree in 12-tone Western music.

## Techniques

- **Tonic (1st degree)** — "Mr. Brightside" (The Killers) verse sits almost entirely on the tonic note. "Baby You're a Rich Man" (Beatles) chorus, mostly tonic.
- **Supertonic (2nd degree)** — "Can't Stop the Feeling!" (Justin Timberlake) chorus, largely on the 2nd degree.
- **Mediant (3rd degree)** — "Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds" (Beatles) pre-chorus, drones on the 3rd degree.
- **Subdominant (4th degree)** — "Purple Rain" (Prince), just before the chorus.
- **Dominant (5th degree)** — "Don't Look Back in Anger" (Oasis) chorus opens on it; "Julia" (Beatles) verse sits on it for most of its length; "Swan Lake" (Tchaikovsky) — a minor-key example, since scale degrees can sound different in minor vs. major context, famous melody opens on the 5th degree.
- **Submediant (6th degree)** — named for being a 3rd *below* tonic (mirroring the mediant's 3rd *above*). "Bohemian Rhapsody" (Queen) piano riff, the high note it jumps to is the 6th degree, then resolves to the 5th.
- **Leading tone (7th degree)** — resolves up to the octave. "Wherever You Will Go" (The Calling); "Let It Go" (Disney's *Frozen*) — same underlying melodic device, 7th degree resolving to the octave.
- **♭3 (minor 3rd degree)** — "Riders on the Storm" (The Doors) — tonic then up to ♭3. "Come Together" (Beatles) verse oscillates between ♭3 and tonic.
- **♯4/♭5 (tritone from tonic)** — described as having a "jarring," "stark" sound. "Waltz #1" (Elliot Smith, major-key context) verse melody opens on it. "Für Elise" (Beethoven, minor-key context) — pivots between the 5th degree and ♭5.
- **♭6 (minor 6th degree)** — theme from *The X-Files* — a riff outlining a minor triad (1-♭3-5) that adds ♭6 on top; credited elsewhere as the same note that gives both *The X-Files* and *Stranger Things* themes their "spooky, unsettling" character (cross-referenced from a separate video already on the vault, see [[Chord Naming by Context]] for the Cmaj7/Em♭6 mechanism behind that specific spookiness).
- **♭7/subtonic (7th degree of natural minor)** — has its own name, "subtonic" (the note below tonic, as opposed to the leading tone's pull toward it). "You Really Got Me" (The Kinks) riff, ♭7→tonic. "(I Can't Get No) Satisfaction" (Rolling Stones) riff, top note is the ♭7.
- **♭2 (flattened 2nd degree, "the rarest scale degree")** — the note that turns natural minor into Phrygian. "Set the Controls for the Heart of the Sun" (Pink Floyd) — tonic then up to ♭2.
- **12 total scale degrees covered** — every possible degree in 12-tone equal temperament, regardless of major/minor/mode.
