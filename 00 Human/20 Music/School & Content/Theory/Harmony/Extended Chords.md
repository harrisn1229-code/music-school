# Extended Chords

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Chords]]. Extended chords keep stacking 3rds past the [[Seventh Chords|7th]]: 9th, 11th, 13th. The common shorthand — **9 = 2, 11 = 4, 13 = 6, an octave up** — is a *usually-true* simplification, not the actual rule. The real rule (chord-tone role, not octave distance): a 2/4/6 only gets renamed 9/11/13 when it's stacked on top of a **complete** lower structure — a full triad (1-3-5) for the 9/11 case, a full seventh chord (1-3-5-7) for the 13 case. If the corresponding lower tone is **missing** (no 3 present, or no 7 present), the added note keeps its plain scale-degree name instead, **regardless of what octave it's actually voiced in** — extensions don't have to be an octave-plus away from the root at all; they can sit right next to the root and still be labeled 9/11/13. Full breakdown → *From videos* below.

## The dominant series (built on C7)

| Chord | Notes | Built from |
|---|---|---|
| C7 | C E G B♭ | dominant 7th |
| C9 | C E G B♭ D | C7 + major 3rd on top |
| C11 | C E G B♭ D F | C9 + minor 3rd |
| C13 | C E G B♭ D F A | C11 + major 3rd |

Major and minor versions exist too (Cmaj9 = C E G B D; Cm9 = C E♭ G B♭ D) — full half-step formulas in [[Chords]]. Alternative names: Cdom9/Cdom11/Cdom13.

## What to actually play (standard omissions)
Full stacks get muddy — omit freely:
- **9th chords**: drop the 5th
- **11th chords**: drop the 3rd (the 3rd clashes with the 11) → sounds like C9sus4 ([[Suspended Chords]])
- **13th chords**: drop the 5th, 9th, and especially the 11th
- Drop the root too if a bass player has it

## Voicing (two-hand / polychord approach)
- C11: LH `E G B♭` + RH `B♭ D F`
- C13: LH `E B♭ D` + RH `A D G` — or LH `B♭ E A` + RH `C E A`
- More on stacking hands: [[Chord Inversions]]

## Usage
- Jazz substitutions for plain triads and dom 7ths ([[Chord Progressions]])
- Altered extensions (♭9, ♯11…) → [[Altered Chords]]

## Classical usage note
Common practice used extended harmonies (9ths/11ths/13ths) almost exclusively as diatonic chords built on the **dominant** (V9/V11/V13) — the 11th/13th barely appear before the Romantic era. The 4-part voicing logic matches the omission rules above: keep root + 3rd (tertial identity) + 7th (bridges to the extension, keeps it reading as one chord instead of two stacked triads), drop the 5th — the extension itself is the one note that's actually required. Almost always found in **root position**; inverted extended chords aren't really a thing in this style. — Toby W. Rush, [[Music Theory for Musicians and Normal People - Enrichment|Music Theory for Musicians and Normal People]], p.34

- **The slash-chord trick for voicing 9/11/13 chords, plus dominant-function placement guidance.** Any upper extension reduces to a simpler chord over a bass note: **G13** = F major 7 over G (drop the 5th, often the 3rd, from the literal 7-note stack); **G13♭9** = F minor-major 7 over G; **C dominant 9** = E minor 7♭5 over C. - **The actual rule for when a 2/4/6 is called an extension (9/11/13) vs. its plain scale-degree name — a chord-tone-role test, not an octave-distance test.** If a **complete triad (1-3-5)** or **complete seventh chord (1-3-5-7)** is present, any additional 2/4/6 added on top is an extension: 2→**9**, 4→**11**, 6→**13**. If the corresponding lower tone is **missing**, the added note keeps its plain name instead: 3 missing → an added 2 stays **2** (not 9), an added 4 stays **4** (not 11); 7 missing → an added 6 stays **6** (not 13, since 6 is "taking the role" of the absent 7). Crucially, this is independent of octave placement — an extension can be voiced right next to the root (or below it) and is still labeled 9/11/13, it never reverts to being called a plain 2/4/6 just because it's voiced close in. Two worked examples: **Cmaj7♯11** (1-3-5-7-♯11) — the ♯11 sits a half-step above the plain 4th and by ear-counting off the major scale would read as "♯4," but because it's stacked on a complete 7th chord it's always named ♯11, never ♯4; **Cadd9 (1-3-5-9) vs. Csus2 (1-2-5)** — same near-identical shape, different label purely because the 3rd is present (→ 9, "add9") or absent (→ 2, "sus2"). Also nails the terminology split: a "10th"/"12th"/"13th" as a plain *interval* term (compound 3rd/5th/6th, i.e. that interval + an octave) describes literal distance and has nothing to do with chord-tone-extension naming — core chord tones (1/3/5/7) never get renamed for octave distance either (a 3rd voiced a literal 10th above the root is still just called "the 3," never "the 10"; there's no such thing as a chord tone called "12"). → [[Add Chords]], [[Suspended Chords]], [[Intervals]] — Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared, [[Guitar Theory! - Demystifying Compound Intervals vs Chord Extensions]]
- **A short dominant-resolution line stacking three extensions in a row (9th → 13th → ♭9), read on guitar as a borrowed minor-chord arpeggio shape that only "works" once one note is reframed as a passing tone.** From a Wes Montgomery solo on Nat Adderley's "Work Song": over a V-I resolution (E♭7 → A♭), the line hits the **9th**, jumps to the **13th**, then uses the **♭9** to resolve down into the **5th of the tonic** — three stacked extensions in one short phrase rather than one at a time. On guitar the shape is an **F minor(major7) arpeggio**, which "doesn't technically work" over the underlying E♭7 in isolation (played alone it just sounds like plain F minor) — it only reads correctly once the shape's ♭3 (A♭) is treated as a **passing tone resolving to the 9th (G)** rather than a static chord tone, and only once a real V-I progression is actually sounding underneath it. General practice takeaway: judge an extension-heavy shape against the real backing chords, not a cappella. - **A systematic root→9th / 5th→11th-13th substitution ladder for building extended chords out of plain Drop 2 voicings, worked incrementally across the first 8 bars of Autumn Leaves (Cm7-F7-B♭maj7-E♭maj7-Aø7-D7-Gm-G7) through five increasingly colored passes.** Core mechanism: for any 4-note chord, keep the **3rd and 7th fixed** (the guide tones that define quality/function) and treat the **root and 5th as the two free slots** — substitute the root for the 9th first (Cm7→Cm9, F7→F9…), then once that's comfortable, also substitute the 5th for an 11th or 13th (Cm9→Cm11, D7♭9→D7♭9♭13…). Two payoffs beyond the mechanical rule: (1) **one substitution can produce a genuinely different chord's shape "for free"** — E♭maj7 with the root replaced by its 9th is note-for-note the same shape as a rootless Gm7; (2) **which extension is "correct" isn't fixed by the chord symbol alone** — an Aø7 sitting inside a ii-V cadence gets an 11th instead of the diatonic 9th specifically because that 9th (B♭) clashes with the cadence's own resolution, a functional check that overrides the generic scale-tone choice. A later pass swaps the same Aø7's 3rd for a **natural 9th sourced from C melodic minor** instead, deliberately trading melodic accuracy (the tune's real melody note clashes) for harmonic color — the same "pick the extension for the color, check it against context" theme carried further. - **Which upper extension a chord gets can be chosen mechanically — by which note a held or moving voice lands on — rather than picked by ear extension-by-extension, worked across one Dm7-G7-Cmaj7 (ii-V-I) skeleton voiced ~15 different ways.** **Common-tone method:** hold one note on top through all three chords and let its relationship to each chord's root supply the extension automatically — B stays a plain chord tone throughout (6th of Dm, 3rd of G, major 7th of C), while D, E, or G each generate a different extension per chord (E on top gives Dm9→G13→Cmaj7, since E is the 9th of D, the 13th of G, and the 3rd of C in turn). **Voice-leading method:** instead of a fixed common tone, move ONE voice by a single half step from chord to chord — eight full lines worked (four descending, four ascending), e.g. descending A→A♭→G gives Dm7 (5th) → G7♭9 (♭9) → Cmaj7 (5th), and ascending G→A♭→A gives Dm11 (11th) → G7♭9 (♭9) → Cmaj13 (13th/6th). **Contrary motion**, the most advanced worked example: two voices move in opposite directions inside the same three chords — Dm9→G♯9♭13(rootless)→Cmaj9 has an outer voice ascending A→B♭→B while an inner voice simultaneously descends E→E♭→D. Full worked catalog on the video's own page. → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Guide Tones]], [[Chord Voicings]] — Jack Ruch, [[IMPROVE your chords with COMMON TONES and VOICE LEADING!]]
- **Get a 9th or 13th from a 7th-chord shape by re-reading the SAME static open string that was previously an add9/6, once the chord underneath actually has its 7th present.** In a classical guitar progression, a standard root-position C dominant-7 shape gets its 9th "for free" by letting the same open high E string that colored earlier triads as an add9/6 ring against it instead of the shape's usual 3rd — giving **C9**. Later in the piece, the identical trick applied to a minor7 shape (open E rung against a Gm7) gives **Gm13** (the E reads as the 13, not the 9, because it's the chord's 6th-degree equivalent over a m7 rather than a 2nd-degree equivalent). On a repeat pass, that same C9's open-string extension is altered down a half step (fretted instead of open) to **C7♭9** for extra tension before resolving. General technique: an open/drone string can supply a 7th chord's extension the same way it supplies an add-chord's color tone (see [[Add Chords]]) — what changes is only whether the chord underneath already has its 7th. → [[Add Chords]], [[Chord Voicings]], [[Altered Chords]] — Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared, [Gorgeous Classical Progression | Milonga Guitar Chord Theory Analysis](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YH6qnXR7LvI)
- **Double an entire practiced set of voicings at once by substituting the 9th for the root in every shape — not just one shape.** Same "shift a known shape's root a whole step to build the 9th" logic as the bullet below, but applied across a whole memorized path of inversions in one pass rather than to a single voicing: take every voicing already learned off one chord (root/3rd/5th/♭7 inversions) and replace each one's root note with the note a whole step up. On one D7 chord across two string-set roots, this took an ~11-voicing set to **~22 distinct voicings** total, several reading as favorites — 9-on-the-bottom shapes, and a 9-substituted "off the 3rd" shape that visually resembles a half-diminished chord while still functioning as the dominant. - **Replacing a chord tone with the 2 or 4 gives a sus/add chord; stacking that same note ON TOP of an already-complete chord gives the 9 or 11 instead** — same note (D over a C chord, or F over a C chord), two different results depending on whether it substitutes for the 3rd or adds above a complete root-3rd-5th-7th stack: swap the 3rd for the 2 → Csus2/C2 (not a 9-chord); swap the 3rd for the 4 → Csus4; keep the 3rd AND add the 4/11 on top of an already-complete chord → an 11 chord (dominant 11, minor 11). The distinguishing test: is the 3rd still present underneath? If yes, the added upper note reads as a true extension (9/11/13); if the 3rd was replaced, it's a sus/add chord instead, not an extension. → [[Suspended Chords]], [[Add Chords]], [[Intervals]] — Jack Ruch, [[Write Great Chord Progressions and Reharmonize Melodies by Understanding Intervals!]]
- **A ♭13 extension on a secondary dominant, derived from the KEY's minor scale, not the chord's own major scale — worked example G7♭13 → Cm.** Writing a minor-key pre-chorus (G minor, V7/iv = C7 → Cm), adds a ♭13 on top of the dominant chord leading into the minor target as a melodic color tone right before resolution — mechanically, it's a half step above the chord's own 5th (a shell voicing — root/3rd/7th — plus that one added note). The reasoning for WHY it must be flat: checked against a parallel worked example (D7 → Gm), the chord's own **plain/major 13th is the major 3rd of the destination key** — a note genuinely outside G minor's own scale (1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7) — while the **♭13 IS a scale tone of the key** the progression is actually in. General rule stated explicitly: extensions on chords anywhere in a progression still have to relate back to the tonal center's actual scale, not get derived generically from each individual chord's own (unrelated) major scale — worth remembering any time an extension is chosen on a non-tonic chord inside a key, not just this one voicing. → [[Secondary Dominants]] (the V7/iv secondary dominant this extension rides on), [[Chords by Key]] — Jared Borowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared), [[Writing a Complete Song in a Minor Key (Part 1) - Chord Progression]]
- **Build a dominant 9 or 13 by shifting ONE note of a shape you already know a whole step, rather than learning a new fingering.** On a rootless top-4-string dominant-7 guitar voicing (bass covers the actual root), moving the shape's own "root" note up a whole step to the 2nd scale degree gives the **9** (A7's root, on the high E string, moved a whole step up → A9); moving the shape's **5th** up a whole step instead gives the **6th**, which reads as the **13** (A7's 5th, on the B string, moved up a whole step → A13). Same move applies identically to every chord in a progression once learned on one shape. For comping motion, don't park on the extended voicing — add and remove the extension note while playing (cycling plain-7 → 9 → 13 on the same shape) so the chord itself creates movement, rather than holding one static color through the bar. Worked on a 12-bar blues in A: A7/A9/A13, D7/D9/D13, E7/E9/E13. → [[Chord Voicings]], [[Blues Form and Harmony]], [[Seventh Chords]] — Daniel Seriff, [[The SIMPLE Chord Shapes That Every PRO Guitarist LOVES]]
- **Choose each extension for its specific emotional coloring, not for density — and drop the 5th by default on guitar.** Rather than grabbing the fanciest-sounding extension, know how each one actually feels (the 9 reads differently from the 7, the 11 differently from the 9) and pick on purpose per chord — e.g. rejecting the "obvious" dominant 7th on a V chord as "too hokey" for a given vibe and using an add11/add4 instead, or choosing an 11 over a 9 on a minor chord specifically because the 9 reads darker than wanted. On guitar, dropping the 5th of an extended chord (four fretting fingers, six strings) is the default move, not a compromise — **the 5th adds little distinctive color and is already implied by the root note's own overtone series**, echoing the general omission rule above. Worked across a I–V–vi–IV progression: Cmaj7 (1st inv.), G add11 (2nd inv., 3rd and 4th deliberately a half step apart for bite), Am7add11 with no 5th, Fmaj7sus2. → [[Chord Inversions]], [[Chord Voicings]] — Max Konyi, [[Beautifying Simple Chords]]
- **Treat every melody note as a deliberate chord-tone-vs-extension choice when harmonizing to a fixed top line** — rather than grabbing extensions indiscriminately for density, decide whether the melody note above a chord is functioning as a primary tone (1/3/5) or as an extension (7/9/11/13), and pick the chord quality that gives it that role on purpose. Used as the core decision-making step of a melody-first harmonization workflow (write the melody, then choose chords underneath it note by note). → [[Chord Progressions]], [[Chord Inversions]] — [[How to Write Neo-Soul and R&B Chords - A Simple Method|Max Konyi]]
- **Voicing rule for a clashing 3rd and 11th: put the 3rd ABOVE the 11th, not below it.** When a chord's 3rd and 11th (=4th) sit a half-step apart, voicing them as 1-11-5-3 (3rd on top) forms a major 7th interval between the two — the common, usable version of that clash. Reversing it (3rd below the 11th) produces an octave-plus-a-half-step interval that reads as an ugly flat-9-type clash instead. Worked on a Gadd11 voicing inside a Mixolydian progression. → [[Chord Inversions]] — Max Konyi, [Beautiful Mixolydian Chord Progression | Modal Harmony Tutorial](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N2fxXcpVU8M)
- **Extended chords blur major/minor identity and read as their own "pastel" flavor by ear** — a 9th/11th/13th chord doesn't sound like [base triad quality] + [extension] layered on top; it becomes a distinct meta-experience of its own (minor-11 ≠ minor-7-add-11 ≠ minor-9-no-11, as separately memorized sounds, not calculated from parts). Practical training order: isolate one extension type at a time on one chord type at a time (major9, major-add9, major-6/9, major-9-♯11…) before trying to mix them — this is explicitly a years-long vocabulary-building project, not a quick drill. → [[Scale Degree Ear Training]] — Max Konyi, [[Answering Your Ear Training Questions (Part 3) Chords and Progressions]]
- **Extend a 7th chord to a 9th by leapfrogging one more note, then balance the voicing across the whole progression** — skip and add once more past the 7th (same leapfrog logic as building the triad/7th in the first place) to stack the 9th on top. Once several chords in a progression have been extended this way, check they're roughly even in note-count — if one chord ends up noticeably denser than its neighbors (e.g. a 5-note 9th chord next to a 4-note 7th), trim a less-important inner note from the denser chord rather than leaving the progression lopsided. → [[Seventh Chords]], [[Chord Progressions]] — You Suck at Producing, [How to write lofi hip hop chords WITHOUT samples](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=djQlIr1ZYt8)
