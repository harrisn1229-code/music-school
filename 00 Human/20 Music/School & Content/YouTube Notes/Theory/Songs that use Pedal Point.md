# Songs that use Pedal Point

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-uckt1o_EUM)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-uckt1o_EUM)

## Synopsis

A full construction-and-application breakdown of pedal point — a held bass (or treble) note under moving chords — from its pipe-organ etymology through tonic/dominant/inverted variants to a John Williams film-scoring use case.

## Techniques

- **Core mechanism**: hold one note (almost always the tonic) constant while chords change above it — this reframes every chord above as a slash chord over that fixed bass. A plain G triad over a held C bass isn't heard as "G/C," it's heard as C major9 (missing the 3rd) — the pedal turns a simple chord shape into an implied extended harmony without the player having to voice the extension directly.
- **Tonic pedal**: "Jump" (Van Halen) — the intro holds C in the bass under G/C, C, F/C, G/C plus some suspended chords; reharmonizing the bass to follow each chord root instead "completely loses the character." "Eye of the Tiger" (Survivor) — verse 1 pedals the tonic under moving chords for sustained tension; verse 2 switches the bass to follow chord roots, and that switch alone reads as release, "like the song was being held in place against its will and now it's been let loose."
- **Inverted pedal**: the held note sits in a treble/upper voice instead of the bass — same effect, different register. "Eye of the Tiger" layers two pedals simultaneously: the tonic bass pedal above, plus an electric guitar chugging on the same tonic note in the treble range.
- **Film-scoring application**: John Williams' Indiana Jones score plays a sequence of chords that wouldn't naturally relate to each other, all over one consistent pedal tone — the shared pedal note "forcibly marries" the unrelated chords together, letting the cue move through harmonically distant material without sounding random.
- **Etymology, useful for remembering the concept**: named for the organ pedalboard — an organist sustains low bass notes with their feet while playing changing chords with their hands, so "keeping a note going with the pedal" while harmony moves above it is literally where the term comes from.
