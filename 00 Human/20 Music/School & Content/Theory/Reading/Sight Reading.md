# Sight Reading

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Notation]]. **The skill, not the symbol system.** [[Notation]] is what the marks mean; this is how you turn them into sound at tempo, on the first pass. It's the backing note for the `theory` lane in [[Practice System Config]] ("Theory & Sight Reading").

**The central reframe:** sight reading is not "reading fast." It is **prediction**. Fluent readers are two to four beats ahead of what their hands are playing, recognising *groups* — a scale fragment, an arpeggio, a familiar rhythm — rather than decoding note by note. Everything below is in service of moving the eye ahead of the hands.

---

## The five rules that do most of the work

1. **Read below your playing level.** Sight-reading material should look *easier* than what you can perform. If decoding takes all your attention there's nothing left over to look ahead with, and looking ahead is the entire skill being trained. Playing hard music slowly is a different exercise.
2. **Never stop, never go back.** The pulse is the one thing you may not break. A wrong note is a wrong note; a stop is a failure of the actual skill. If you fall behind, drop notes — keep the beat and rejoin. This is the hardest habit to build and the most important.
3. **Frequency beats intensity.** 5–10 minutes daily beats an hour on Sunday. Short, low-stakes, frequent exposure is what the pedagogy research consistently supports; a 30-second read at the top of a session is worth more than an occasional high-pressure test.
4. **Rhythm first.** Reading breaks down at rhythm far more often than at pitch. Drill rhythm on its own — clap or count a line before playing it, and give it dedicated minutes rather than hoping it comes along for free.
5. **Interleave, don't repeat.** Alternate short reads of *several different* pieces rather than repeating one. Repeating a passage stops being sight reading on the second attempt — by definition you're no longer reading at sight.

## The 30-second pre-scan

Before the first note, every time. Costs half a minute, prevents most derailments:

| Look at | Ask |
|---|---|
| **Key signature** | Which sharps/flats, and where do they fall in this position? |
| **Time signature** | What's the beat unit? Where does the pulse sit? |
| **Roadmap** | Repeats, 1st/2nd endings, D.S., Coda — where does this actually go? |
| **Range** | Highest and lowest note → which **position** covers both? *(guitar-specific and load-bearing — see [[Reading Notation on Guitar]])* |
| **Rhythm hotspots** | Any tuplets, syncopation, dotted figures? Count them silently now. |
| **Accidentals** | Circle the ones that will surprise you. |
| **Tempo** | Pick one you can hold **through the hardest bar**, not the easiest. |

Then set the pulse — count a full bar in, out loud or with a metronome — and start.

## Practice modes

Rotate these; they train different halves of the skill.

- **Cold reads.** New material, once through, no stopping. The core exercise. A duet part, an etude, a page you've never opened.
- **Rhythm-only.** Clap or tap the line while counting aloud. Removes pitch entirely so the rhythm is the only variable. Leavitt's own instruction on p.21 is blunt about this: **count aloud as you play.**
- **Away from the instrument.** Read a page and hear it, or name notes aloud, with no guitar in hand. Trains recognition speed, which is the actual bottleneck.
- **Slow-motion reading.** Half tempo, but *strictly* in time and eyes always ahead. Not the same as playing slowly to get notes right.
- **Position drills.** Same short line, read in three different positions. Attacks guitar's one-pitch-many-places problem directly. A more aggressive variant of this same idea: instead of reading in *several* positions, freeze **one** fret/string spot per note name in one register and commit to it with zero exceptions — trades flexibility for speed while the pitch map is new, then you can always widen back out to multiple positions later. Claimed payoff: ~2 weeks to fluent melody-plunking vs. years working out from open position book by book. — Jared Borkowski, [[Shortcut to Reading Sheet Music for Guitar - Read Guitar Lead Sheets]], see [[Reading Notation on Guitar]] §7a
- **Metronome on 2 and 4.** Once basic reading is steady — keeps time honest without spoon-feeding the downbeat.

## Measuring it

Sight reading improves invisibly, so track something. Once a month, cold-read a page you've never opened at a fixed tempo and note: did you keep the pulse, how many stops, how far ahead were your eyes.

One finding worth knowing: a *Psychology of Music* study found students given growth-mindset training improved their sight reading significantly more than those who weren't. The skill has an unusually large "I'm just not a reader" self-belief component, and it isn't true.
