# Songs that use Chromatic Mediant chords

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PtISkOeSz08)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PtISkOeSz08)

## Synopsis

A precise definition of the chromatic mediant (a chord a 3rd away from the tonic, chromatic to the key, AND the same quality as the tonic — which guarantees a shared common tone) followed by film and pop examples, then an honest close: chromatic mediant, modal interchange, and secondary dominant frequently describe the exact same chord progression, and which label is most useful depends on the wider musical context, not on some single "correct" analysis.

## Techniques

- **Full definition, three requirements**: (1) a 3rd away from the tonic chord. (2) chromatic — not diatonic to the key. (3) **same chord quality as the tonic** (both major or both minor) — this is what guarantees a shared common tone between the two chords, which is what lets the "foreign" chord still feel connected rather than random. (A looser, common definition drops requirement 3, but then the common-tone glue disappears.)
- **Named vs. plain mediant**: the regular diatonic mediant (iii) and submediant (vi) are the in-key thirds-relationships; a chromatic mediant is what you get when a same-quality third-relationship chord falls OUTSIDE the key instead.
- **Film score example, plus a Picardy-adjacent detail**: "The Imperial March" (*Star Wars*) is largely built on G minor → E♭ minor — a chromatic mediant (E♭ minor isn't diatonic to G minor; both chords share the common tone B♭). Later in the theme, a brief switch to E♭ MAJOR (the diatonic ♭III, not the chromatic mediant) is read as hinting at remaining good in Darth Vader — echoing the same reading given to the Picardy-third-adjacent moment in [[Why are Minor Chord Songs so rare?]]'s discussion of the same piece.
- **"Enjoy the Silence" (Depeche Mode)**: verse moves C minor → E♭ minor (iii, chromatic-mediant version, in C minor) for an eerie mood.
- **Seventh chords strengthen the bond, "supercharging" the relationship**: "Light My Fire" (The Doors, verse) vamps A minor7 → F♯minor7 — as 7th chords rather than plain triads, the two chords now share TWO common tones (not just one), which measurably softens/tames the chromatic-mediant weirdness while keeping an exotic quality.
- **Common in film scoring generally**: Howard Shore's *Lord of the Rings* score uses chromatic mediants extensively — the Aragorn/Arwen love theme moves tonic → ♭VI (two major chords a 3rd apart, chromatic to each other), read as epic/inspiring.
- **The same I–♭VI move surfaces in pop too, with a different emotional read**: "It Won't Be Long" (The Beatles) uses the identical chromatic-mediant relationship as the LOTR example, but reads as merely "foreign/intriguing" in a pop context rather than epic — same harmonic device, different emotional weight depending on genre/arrangement context.
- **Version-dependent chromatic mediant status — a genuinely subtle case**: "Morning Bell" (Radiohead) is a true chromatic mediant (Am→C♯m) on the *Amnesiac* version, but the *Kid A* version changes the underlying bass note so the same chord instead reads as Amaj7 — NOT a chromatic mediant at all, despite being closely related material. Analysis has to track the actual recording, not just "the song."
- **Explicit overlap with modal interchange**: "Head Over Heels" (Tears for Fears) moves A major → C major — describable equally validly as a chromatic mediant (C is a 3rd from A, chromatic to A major) OR as modal interchange (mixing chords from A major and A minor) — Bennett states directly both readings are "perfectly valid," since music theory is "a collection of different lenses... which often overlap," not one uniform system.
- **Explicit overlap with secondary dominants, plus Bennett's own tie-breaking rule**: "Happier Than Ever" (Billie Eilish) moves C major → E major — a valid chromatic mediant reading (E is a 3rd from C, chromatic to the key) AND a valid secondary-dominant reading (E major = V/vi, resolving to A minor, which is exactly what happens in the song). **Bennett's rule for choosing between the two labels**: when the chord actually resolves via a textbook secondary-dominant motion (as here), prefer "secondary dominant" as the more useful description; if the same chord just alternated back and forth without ever resolving to the implied target, "chromatic mediant" would be the better description instead. → [[Secondary Dominants]].
