# Are You an Intermediate Bassist? (17-point Checklist)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XHpzoGSshYQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XHpzoGSshYQ)

## Synopsis

A self-assessment checklist rather than a technique deep-dive — BassBuzz's Josh (explicitly inspired by Nate Savage's "Are You an Intermediate Guitar Player" video, plus the recurring "noob Josh" comic-relief bit doing everything wrong on camera) runs through 17 concrete yes/no benchmarks a player can test themselves against to find out whether they're actually intermediate, rather than guessing. The genuine value here isn't new technique content — it's **coverage and calibration**: a single compact checklist spanning technique (tuning speed, fretboard note memorization, four-finger fretting, alternating-plucking speed, metronome time-keeping, legato, muting), theory (five basic scales, rhythm/time-signature literacy, gear knowledge), and musicianship (notation reading, playing with a drummer, jamming, active listening/ear training, 12-bar blues, bassline construction from roots/fifths/octaves/triads, and playing full songs) — with a **concrete pass/fail test attached to nearly every point**, several tied to specific named songs (AC/DC's "Touch Too Much" for alternating-plucking speed, "Come As You Are" for legato, "Sunshine of Your Love" for muting, Red Hot Chili Peppers' "Otherside" bridge for syncopation) and one worked live on camera (a bassline built from scratch over a G–Dm–C–C progression, demonstrating roots then fifths). Every one of the 17 points maps directly onto material the vault already has deep, dedicated BassBuzz technique notes for — this video's contribution is less new mechanism and more **new benchmarks, tempos, and test-song pairings** to attach to material that in most cases was previously documented without a concrete self-check attached. Two points are genuinely new territory for the vault: **jamming mechanics** (starting a jam with a one-chord vamp vs. a chord progression, finding the root note when joining a jam already in progress) and **knowing the names of drum kit pieces** as a prerequisite to locking in with a drummer — both folded into existing notes below since neither reaches the ≥3-tips bar for a standalone page on its own. Runs light on qualifiers throughout ("as an intermediate bassist you might not have this perfect, but it should be in your awareness") — the checklist is framed as directional, not a hard pass/fail gate.

## What's taught

The full 17-point checklist, grouped into the video's own three chunks (basics, theory, "everything else"):

**Chunk 1 — Basics**

1. **Tune with a tuner in 15 seconds or less.** Stated reason: a slow tuner-fumble during a show tests the crowd's patience, and other musicians read a slow tuner as a mark against you. → [[Bass Playing Position, Setup, and Tuning Basics]]
2. **Money notes memorized (the natural notes on the first four frets, E F G A B C D E F G) — plus neighboring sharps/flats.** Test given: play the money notes low to high with a metronome at **50 BPM**, saying each note name out loud as you play it. Stated reason: without them you get lost when someone calls out chords at a jam, and you can't read a chart. Also: find a neighboring note by ear/fretboard (F → F♯, B → B♭). → [[Bass Fretboard Note Memorization System]]
3. **Fret with all four fingers, including the pinky.** Barring injury or an atypical hand, all four fingers should be in active use — pinky included — to have the full range from simple to technical lines available. Named reassurance: pinky fretting feels awkward at first but becomes comfortable with practice ("you and your pinky will become close friends"). → [[Fretting-Hand Finger Independence Drill]]
4. **Alternating-pluck eighth notes at 120 BPM** — on an open string as the base test, fretting + string crossing as the harder version. Stated reason: alternating that isn't up to speed caps what tempos/lines you can actually play or write. Test song named directly: **"Touch Too Much" (AC/DC)**. → [[String Crossing and Plucking Patterns]]
5. **Play along with a metronome at a slow tempo (50 BPM) without getting lost rhythmically** — the notes/material can be trivially simple; the point being tested is purely whether the groove holds. Stated reason: bad time means nobody wants to play with you, and even solo practice against a recording won't lock in if your internal rhythm drifts. → [[Ultimate Groove Workout — Fade the Beat]]
6. **Play legato — long, connected notes with no gap between them.** Named as one of the fastest tells of a beginner: short, choppy notes where connected ones would support the groove better. Explicitly framed as a spectrum, not pass/fail: "as an intermediate bassist you might not have this perfect, but it should be in your awareness." Worked comparison: "Come As You Are" (Nirvana) played choppy (beginner) vs. connected (intermediate target). → [[Connected vs. Disconnected Bass Phrasing]]
7. **Mute the strings you aren't playing, consistently on the low strings using the plucking-hand thumb.** Stated reason: without muting, the audience hears ringing-string noise instead of the intended line. Test song: **"Sunshine of Your Love" (Cream)** — can you play it clean, with no rumbling in the background? → [[Bass Note Muting Technique]]

**Chunk 2 — Theory (short by design)**

8. **Know the five basic scales: major, minor, major pentatonic, minor pentatonic, blues.** Test given as a cold-recall check: "play me a D minor pentatonic scale" or "C major scale" should come out immediately, without conscious derivation. Stated reason: nearly every bassline is built from some scale, even ones written by non-theory musicians. → [[Bass Scale Shapes — Major, Minor, Pentatonic & Blues]]
9. **Know basic rhythms and time signatures.** Count and play whole, half, quarter, and eighth notes accurately; know common time signatures (4/4, 3/4, 6/8, 12/8); play syncopated (offbeat/"and") rhythms accurately, not just count them. Stated reason: musicians communicate in these terms. Test song: the bridge of **"Otherside" (Red Hot Chili Peppers)** for syncopation — "full of offbeat eighth notes." → [[Time Signatures & Note Values]], [[Syncopation and Metric Displacement]]
10. **Know your gear — what the knobs on your bass and amp actually do, and can you dial in a decent basic tone for the music you play.** Framed as adjacent to theory ("understanding how stuff works, which is what music theory is, so close enough"). Stated stakes: right notes/right time can still be ruined by a bad tone — "cringe-ably horrible," or inaudible/boomy/rumbly. → [[Bass Tone Chain Priority]]

**Chunk 3 — Everything else**

11. **Read some kind of notation** — sheet music, chord charts, or tab-with-rhythm (or all three). Stated reasons, stacked: take better notes at rehearsals, learn songs faster, read other people's charts to play their songs, write your own music. → [[Reading Bass Clef]]
12. **Play with a drummer.** Framed as particularly important because bass and drums form the rhythm section's core and "communicate in a special way." Two concrete sub-skills named: know the names of the pieces of a drum kit, and know how to build a bass rhythm that gels with a drum beat — either by **following the kick drum**, or by falling back on a simple supportive rhythm like **chugging quarter or eighth notes**. → [[Locking In With the Drummer]]
13. **Jam with other instruments.** Two named sub-skills: (a) if you're the one starting a jam for a guitarist/keys player to join, can you create a **one-chord vamp** as opposed to a full chord progression; (b) if someone else already started the jam, can you **find the root note(s)** and play from there. → [[Root-Fifth Bass Lines and the 3-Step Style Rhythm Formula]]
14. **Listen to who you're playing with — real people or a recording — and train your ear.** Framed as opening your attention outward to the band rather than staying locked on your own playing; stated payoff is both social (nobody wants to play with someone who isn't listening) and musical (listening generates better ideas for what/how to play). Two concrete sub-skills: play with **dynamics** (loud/quiet, responsive to what you're hearing), and **ear-train enough to tell major from minor chords apart by ear**. → [[Steal Like a Bassist — Ear-Training from Known Melodies]]
15. **Play a 12-bar blues in any key.** Stated reason: it's the single most common jam-worthy song form — a lot of non-blues rock/pop still uses it. Test: call a key ("a blues in E") and find your three root notes — the **1, 4, and 5** — then play a basic line from them. → [[Blues Bass Survival Shapes and Vocabulary Ladder]]
16. **Create basslines from roots, fifths, octaves, and triads.** Live worked test: over **G major → D minor → C major → C major (one extra bar)**, build a bassline. Demonstrated approach: start with plain root notes locked to the kick drum, then layer in fifths (major 5th over G, minor-context 5th over Dm, major 5th over C). → [[Root-Fifth Bass Lines and the 3-Step Style Rhythm Formula]], [[Jamerson's Root-Fifth-Octave Exercise]], [[Bass Chords and Double Stops]]
17. **Play along with full songs, not just riffs/scales/exercises.** Stated reasons beyond repertoire: builds endurance, ear, and the ability to remember/track long song forms — "crucial skills for any musician." Direct instruction to anyone stuck on isolated drills: "just knock it off for a bit... and take the time to learn some full songs." → [[Goal-Tiered Practice and the Self-Taught Bass Commandments]]
