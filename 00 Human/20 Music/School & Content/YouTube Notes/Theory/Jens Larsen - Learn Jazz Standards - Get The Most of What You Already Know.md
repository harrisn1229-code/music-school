# Learn Jazz Standards - Get The Most of What You Already Know

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gJb9IQDxwJc)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gJb9IQDxwJc)

## Synopsis
A methodology lesson rather than a licks-and-voicings video — Jens Larsen steps away from his usual format to argue for two habits that make learning jazz repertoire dramatically faster. First: **learn the songs**, quoting Peter Bernstein directly ("learn the songs") as the single piece of advice he values most — repertoire depth is what actually makes someone a better improviser, because it stocks a library of real progressions to draw on. Second, and the video's real payload: **learn a song's form before its chords, and think in chord functions/small progressions rather than individual chords.** He demonstrates both by analyzing five 32-bar standards back to back — "All The Things You Are," "It Could Happen to You," "But Not for Me," "Out of Nowhere," and "Just Friends" — three of them deliberately chosen in the same key (E♭ major) specifically to remove the transposition step and let the *shape* of the form become obvious on repeat exposure. The payoff is a small set of recurring structural devices (a secondary ii–V to vi, a secondary ii–V to IV, a "IV → IV-minor → I" cadence, a "dominant of the dominant" V7/V near the end of the first half) that show up in the same *slot* of the form across unrelated tunes by different composers — once you can recognize the slot, a new standard stops looking like 32 bars of arbitrary chords and starts looking like a known shape with a few tune-specific variations. **Caption-quality note:** this transcript is a music-heavy spoken lesson with sung/played chord names, and YouTube's auto-captioner garbles a large fraction of the chord-letter vocabulary badly (see Source below for the specific substitution pattern) — the functional/conceptual teaching (which is the actual point of the video) survives cleanly, but individual chord-letter spellings in the worked analyses below are reconstructed carefully from context and flagged as lower-confidence where the garbling is severe. Nothing below was invented from general knowledge of these standards' published changes — only used to help interpret an otherwise-unreadable caption artifact.

## What's taught

### The two core habits (the real subject of the video)
- **"Learn the songs"** — direct Peter Bernstein quote, framed as Larsen's most-valued single piece of advice for anyone learning a style of music, jazz specifically: knowing a large number of real progressions is what makes someone a better improviser, more than isolated technique work.
- **Learn the FORM before the chords, top-down rather than chord-by-chord.** Stated blues analogy: all 12-bar blues progressions split into two families — ones that move to IV in bar 2, and ones that don't; once you know that one binary fact about a new blues, "you already know the song" because everything else is shared vocabulary. Applied to transcribing a standard by ear: write out the **form only** first (no chords), optionally note the key if it's already clear, then fill in chords while listening — leave anything unclear open rather than guessing, and exploit **repetition**: once a section (e.g. the first half) has been transcribed, a later repeat of that same section usually doesn't need to be re-worked from scratch, just confirmed. → [[Transcribing and Playing by Ear]]
- **Think in chord FUNCTIONS and small progressions, not individual chords.** The stated reason: many progressions that look different as chord symbols are doing the same functional job (a ii chord and a IV chord can both be sub-dominant, and a soloist plays the same vocabulary over either regardless of the literal chord symbol) — recognizing the function, not the label, is what lets a player see how different songs/progressions are "actually the same." Practical corollary: **always group a ii–V into one mental unit** ("a 2–5 to vi" / "a 2–5 to IV"), never as two separate chords to memorize independently — this is the single biggest chunking win in the video, repeated across every worked example. → [[The ii-V-I]]
- **Recognize recurring devices by the SLOT they sit in, not just by name.** A "IV → IV-minor → I" move and a "dominant of the dominant" (V7/V) both get flagged as occupying a specific, predictable *position* in the 32-bar form across unrelated tunes — once a player has internalized "this is roughly where the four-minor usually sits," spotting it (or correctly guessing it's there) in a brand-new chart gets much faster, described directly as "statistically the biggest chance you have that it'll be there."

### Worked analysis — "All The Things You Are" *(key of E♭ major; garbled in captions as "del mvw" / "they live in are you")*
*(32-bar form, split into two 16-bar halves — the second half largely repeats the first with a different ending)*
- Tonic **E♭maj7** opens the tune.
- A **secondary ii–V to the 6th degree** (→ Cm, vi) follows, immediately grouped by Larsen as "one thing" rather than two chords — → [[Secondary Dominants]], [[The ii-V-I]].
- A **secondary ii–V to the 4th degree** (→ A♭maj7, IV: B♭m7 → E♭7 → A♭) — the same "treat the 2-5 as a single unit" framing repeated. → [[Secondary Dominants]]
- On the IV chord (A♭maj7), the progression moves to a **D♭7**, described as functioning like A♭ minor's own dominant-adjacent move back to I — analyzed functionally as **IV → IV(minor-flavored) → I**, called out explicitly as an important, generalizable shape (not just specific to this tune) → [[Borrowed Chords]] (the vault's existing IV→iv→I "heartbreak cadence" entry — this is a jazz-standard confirmation of the identical functional move).
- Back to E♭ (tonic), then to the **6th degree** again, then an **F7** near the end of the first half — identified as **"the dominant of the dominant"** (V7/V), flagged as very common in major-key standards and specifically common **in this exact position of the form** (last line of the first half). → [[Secondary Dominants]]
- Second half is largely a repeat of the first until the second-to-last line, where an **A half-diminished → E7-family reharmonization** appears (described on-camera as a reharmonization of a diminished/augmented sound, not gone into in full chord-letter detail in the transcript — flagged low-confidence).
- Closing chain: **E♭maj7 → [tritone-related chord] → a "secondary dominant" C7 → ii–V → I** — the exact intermediate chord is too garbled in the caption to spell with confidence; the functional shape (a tritone-flavored move followed by a secondary dominant, then a closing ii–V–I) is clear.
- Explicitly used as the reference/baseline tune for the other four — "It Could Happen to You" is introduced as sharing a "roughly the same" form and being in a related key on purpose, to build recognition before transposing.

### Worked analysis — "It Could Happen to You" *(key of E♭ major)*
- Also 16+16 bars, same key as "All The Things You Are" (deliberately, per the video's framing).
- Tonic, then a **secondary dominant to the ii** (a diminished-quality chord functioning as C7 → Fm7, the ii).
- A **"sharp-4" chord appears** (captioned "shampoo"/"shower for" — Larsen's own shorthand for ♯4, an F♯ diminished chord) — flagged as occupying **the same kind of slot as the IV-minor move in "All The Things You Are," but placed differently in the form** — the ♯4 sound "doesn't have a steady place in the form" the way the IV-minor cadence does, i.e. it's a recognizable *sound*, not a fixed *structural slot*, unlike several of this video's other devices.
- Back to I, then IV, then a **secondary ii–V to the 2nd degree** (G half-diminished → C7 → Fm, i.e. a minor ii–V into the ii).
- **The IV-minor cadence appears again, in the same relative slot as "All The Things You Are"** (Fm7 → G♭7 → E♭maj7, i.e. iv → ♭VII7-flavored move → I) — Larsen states directly that this is **statistically the most common place in the form to expect a IV-minor move**, generalizing the observation across the whole 32-bar-standard category rather than treating it as tune-specific. → [[Borrowed Chords]]
- Last line of the first half: instead of the expected V7/V, a **plain ii–V** closes it (D half-diminished → G7 → vi), noted as a variation on the "dominant of the dominant" slot rather than the device itself appearing.
- Second half repeats the shape through the IV-minor slot, then diverges into a different closing turnaround/cycle (**tritone → dominant → ii–V → ii–V–I**, chord letters too garbled to spell confidently).
- Larsen flags explicitly that the song **ends differently** than "All The Things You Are" — most 32-bar standards in this shape end with a standard **2-bar turnaround**, but this one uses only a **1-bar turnaround**, called out as the atypical case.

### Worked analysis — "But Not for Me" *(Gershwin — garbled in captions as "girl swing"; key of E♭ major)*
- Opens on a **turnaround** rather than the tonic outright (first two bars are commonly played by other musicians as sitting on the dominant-of-the-dominant, but Larsen states the actual written progression starts with a genuine turnaround).
- ii–V into the dominant of the dominant (V7/V), then resolving down through it back to I — matching the "dominant of the dominant" slot from the E♭ tunes above.
- **The IV-minor cadence appears again in the same relative slot** — but this time as **IV → IV7 (not the diminished/minor-adjacent version, an actual IV-minor chord with the minor 3rd audible in the melody)** — flagged as a specific point of difference from how other musicians/recordings (cited: the Chet Baker version with Russ Freeman, and older Chet Baker recordings generally) play a plain dominant IV7 instead, because those versions don't carry the same melody note.
- Second half is close to identical structurally to the first, differing mainly in the final cadence/turnaround, which shares the video's other examples' shape of a secondary-dominant-into-ii-V-into-I close.
- Used explicitly as the third confirmation that "IV → IV-minor → I is in the same place" across three different composers writing unrelated tunes in the same key/form family — Larsen calls this out directly as evidence of a real, shared songwriting tradition rather than coincidence.

### Worked analysis — "Out of Nowhere" *(key of G major — first of two "looser variation" tunes)*
- Chosen specifically as a **looser variant** of the strict 16+16 shape — introduced as having "a lot happening" harmonically, useful precisely because it stretches the pattern rather than confirming it cleanly.
- Opens on I, held two bars, then moves through **B♭m7 → E♭7** (Larsen notes he doesn't know the tune's actual original changes here, but analyzes the functional type as "a sharp-4 sound").
- A named, fully-worked chord-naming digression: **"C♯ double-diminished"** — described as a C♯ diminished chord with a doubly-flattened 3rd, which resolves out to sound like an inversion of an E♭7. Larsen states this is the **same chord commonly called a "German augmented sixth"** in classical-theory naming, and explicitly prefers his own "sharp-4 diminished" name because **it names the chord by how it actually sounds in context** (a ♯4 sound) rather than by an unrelated historical label — a stated naming philosophy, not just a one-off aside. → [[Diminished Chords]]
- IV chord, back to I, then a **secondary ii–V to vi (Dm7♭9-quality dominant → Am)**.
- Last line of the first half: instead of the expected plain V7/V, an **E♭7 appears — identified as a tritone substitute of A7**, i.e. still functionally the dominant of the dominant, just voiced as its tritone sub — Larsen states "out of nowhere" as a tune "really revolves a lot around" this E♭7/tritone-sub-of-V7/V sound.
- **Missing the IV-minor cadence entirely in its usual slot** — attributed directly to the melody being constructed differently in this tune (an 8+8-feeling first half rather than a symmetric one), tying the harmonic-slot theory to melodic phrase construction rather than treating it as a fixed rule.
- Second half: **A minor (sub-dominant function) → IV chord** (harmonizable as a minor-sixth chord or as a secondary dominant of the ♭VII, described as not mattering for the melody either way), then a plain turnaround/three-six-two-five with the same ♯4 device closing back to I.
- General framing stated for this tune specifically: **you should learn the melody alongside the harmony**, because the harmonic slot choices (why the IV-minor is absent here) trace directly back to how the tune's melody is built, not to an independent harmonic rule.

### Worked analysis — "Just Friends" *(key of F major — second "looser variation" tune)*
- **The first tune in the video that does NOT start on the tonic** — opens on the **IV chord (B♭maj7)** instead, immediately moving into a **IV-minor cadence (B♭ → B♭m7 → E♭7, i.e. iv → its own dominant) back to the tonic (Fmaj7)** — the IV-minor device appearing at the very START of the form rather than mid-first-half, a structural variation worth noting against the earlier examples.
- A **♯4 diminished chord** (E♭ diminished, same device-family as "Out of Nowhere," Larsen's own shorthand again) — the practical guidance given: track whether it resolves back to the tonic or down to the ii, since that's the only detail that changes how it's used.
- Resolves down to sub-dominant (G minor 7 → C7, essentially a full **ii–V–I** compressed into a turnaround).
- Last line of the first half: a **plain V7** rather than a dominant-of-the-dominant — flagged as predictable/unremarkable in this slot for this tune.
- Second half: **G minor 7 → C7**, with two named alternate harmonizations circulating for the following bars — Larsen's preferred version moves quickly to **F7** (setting up the IV, a "smoother" melody-driven choice) versus the more literal-looking direct move to C minor — both explicitly stated as working equally well against the melody, a real-world case of the [[Reharmonization]] "melody note must be a usable tone" flexibility principle in action.
- **Neither the IV-minor slot NOR the dominant-of-the-dominant slot appears in this tune's second half**, in contrast to the E♭-key tunes — attributed again to a melody/harmony contrast: less harmonic movement in the first half, considerably more in the second, which the video frames as a legitimate alternative to symmetric 16+16 construction (more of an 8+8-and-8+8-different feel) that still uses several of the same recognizable component devices.

### General framing / takeaway (closing section)
- **Think top-down** when analyzing, transcribing, or learning any standard: get the big picture (form, then function-level shape) before filling in chord-by-chord detail — stated directly as the more efficient way to work than starting from individual chords.
- **The same chord can carry different meanings depending on context/placement** — the video's own example: in E♭ major, Fm7 can be a plain ii (in a ii–V–I) or a sub-dominant chord elsewhere in the same tune's form; recognizing which function is active in a given spot is what lets a player transfer vocabulary between progressions that look different as chord symbols but are functionally the same.
- **This transfers directly to improvising with other musicians** — if a progression is internalized functionally rather than as a fixed chord list, a player adapts smoothly when a different musician plays a slight variation on "the same" changes, because the variation is usually just a different literal realization of the same function.
- Direct call to action for anyone applying this: **don't try this cross-genre** — Larsen states explicitly this functional/repertoire-density approach is specific to learning material that's actually part of the jazz tradition (his own example of what NOT to substitute: a Steely Dan, Duran Duran, or Coldplay song), not a general-purpose analysis trick for any song.
- Video closes pointing to a companion video on the underlying **chord-function "building blocks"** in a major key (not further identified/linked in the transcript — flagged as a related Jens Larsen video not yet in the vault) and a stated intent to cover the AABA form (the vault already has [[Rhythm Changes]] for the AABA/32-bar-with-bridge case) in a future video.
