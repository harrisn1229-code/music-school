# Synthet - The Melody Trick to Make It Catchy

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-eOcIR0igdQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-eOcIR0igdQ)

## Synopsis

A call-and-response primer: two melodic parts (instruments, voices, or a vocal ad-lib) "having a conversation" by trading phrases rather than moving in lockstep. Synthet's own worked example uses a lead vocal answered by a female choir built via a voice-conversion tool, and generalizes the device down to its simplest form — even a slight melodic variation on a repeated phrase counts as a lightweight call-and-response, not just a full alternating-vocalist arrangement.

## What's taught

- **Call-and-response = trading phrases between two parts instead of moving together** — one melodic line "asks," another "answers," whether that's two instruments, two vocalists, or a lead vocal and an ad-lib response; described as a whole compositional device in its own right, worth remembering as a catchiness tool. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **An "AB" structure is inherently memorable** — the back-and-forth itself creates a mental hook independent of the actual notes chosen; cites two-vocalist trade-off songs and rap ad-libs as familiar examples of the same underlying device.
- **A synthesized response voice is a valid substitute for a second real singer** — Synthet's own example uses a voice-conversion/reharmonize tool to generate a "female choir" response to his own lead line rather than recording a second vocalist, i.e. the AI step is a production shortcut, not a requirement of the technique itself. → [[Vocal Harmony]]
- **The response doesn't need to be complex** — a simple repeated melody line, a slight variation on it, or a plain rhythmic answer all count; the device scales from an elaborate two-choir trade-off down to a one-note nod.
