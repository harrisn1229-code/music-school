# 10 Oasis songs that 'Rip Off' other songs

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yDQqKtYLNG4)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yDQqKtYLNG4)

## Synopsis

A single-artist deep-dive into Noel Gallagher's openly-acknowledged habit of building Oasis songs on top of older tracks — ranging from cases he freely admits to in interviews to two that actually resulted in lawsuits and forfeited royalties.

## Techniques

- **Combining two riff elements from one source into a single new riff**: "Cigarettes and Alcohol" fuses T. Rex's "Get It On" chugging E chord AND its descending blues-lick guitar part into one riff.
- **Full melody/chord/key match, resulting in a lawsuit and credit**: "Whatever" vs. Neil Innes' "How Sweet to Be an Idiot" — identical opening melodic phrase, same descending chord progression, same key (G major); Oasis was sued by Innes' label and Innes received a songwriting credit. Innes later got a cheeky retaliation in on his own Rutles track "Shangri-La," quoting both "Whatever" and "A Day in the Life."
- **Note-for-note Beatles melody lift, openly acknowledged**: "She's Electric"'s chorus melody matches "While My Guitar Gently Weeps" in places; its outro directly references "With a Little Help from My Friends."
- **Simplified-riff homage, source acknowledged**: "Don't Look Back in Anger" opens on a simplified version of the piano riff from Lennon's "Imagine."
- **Unconscious absorption, explicitly described that way by the songwriter**: Gallagher says the "Supersonic" guitar solo lifting George Harrison's "My Sweet Lord" riff wasn't a deliberate choice — "it's just a natural part of how he writes his songs," i.e. internalized influence surfacing without conscious intent, distinct from the deliberate combination technique above.
- **Chord-progression-only likeness, resolved amicably without a lawsuit**: "Half the World Away" vs. Burt Bacharach's "This Guy's in Love with You" — Gallagher himself calls them "exactly the same," but the actual shared element is just the I→IV(maj7) chord move; Bacharach invited Gallagher to perform his song live two years later rather than pursuing action.
- **A genuine lawsuit loss with financial consequence**: "Step Out" vs. Stevie Wonder's "Uptight" — chorus similarity was close enough that Oasis forfeited 10% of the song's royalties and had to share songwriting credit, and pulled the track from the intended album release entirely.
- **Advertising-jingle origin traced through a second lawsuit**: "Shakermaker" resembles "I'd Like to Teach the World to Sing" — itself originally a Coca-Cola ad rewrite of an earlier song called "True Love and Apple Pie" — and Oasis was sued directly by Coca-Cola over the likeness.
- **A source acknowledged as directly available via the band's own production team**: "Don't Go Away" closely follows The Real People's "Feel the Pain" (lyrical idea and melodic phrasing, shifted from a minor to a major key) — plausible not as coincidence but direct access, since The Real People's own members helped produce Oasis's early demos.
- **The songwriter's own stated attitude toward the whole pattern**: Gallagher, quoted directly, treats the borrowing as an open, almost defiant part of his process rather than something to conceal.
