# Songs that use the Neapolitan Chord

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cKpA16YjSKk)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cKpA16YjSKk)

## Synopsis

The most rigorous of Bennett's chord-type videos: a precise two-part test for what actually counts as a Neapolitan chord (major triad on ♭2 AND leading to the dominant — not just any ♭2 chord), why it fits more naturally in minor keys than major, real pop/rock/film examples, the classical-era first-inversion convention that gives the chord its "6th" name, and — most valuably — three explicit worked counter-examples showing chords that LOOK Neapolitan but aren't, because they fail the functional test or are better explained by a different theory lens entirely (tritone substitution, Phrygian borrowing).

## Techniques

- **The two-part definition, stated precisely**: (1) a major triad built on the LOWERED 2nd scale degree (♭II). (2) it must have **pre-dominant function** — it has to LEAD to the dominant (V) chord. A ♭II chord that doesn't resolve to V is NOT automatically Neapolitan, no matter how it's built.
- **Classical origin example**: Beethoven's "Moonlight Sonata" (C♯ minor) — D major (♭II) leading to G♯ (V).
- **Pop/rock examples, all passing the full two-part test**: "Hello" (Lionel Richie, A minor) — B♭ → E7 (V). "The Globalist" (Muse, A minor, same key/chords as "Hello") — Muse cited directly as a classically-influenced band where this device is more likely to surface. "Going to a Town" (Rufus Wainwright, F minor) — G♭ → C (V). "Do You Want to Know a Secret" (The Beatles, E minor) — F → B (V).
- **Why the Neapolitan fits minor keys more naturally than major**: in a minor key, only ONE note needs altering to build ♭II (the other two notes are already in the natural minor scale); in a major key, building the same chord requires altering an additional note, making it more chromatically "foreign." Still usable in major, just less natural: "Indiana Jones" theme (John Williams, C major) — D♭ → G (V) → resolves home to C.
- **Film-scoring usage beyond Williams**: "Sally's Song" (Danny Elfman, *The Nightmare Before Christmas*, E minor) — F → B (V) → I. "Who Wants to Be a Millionaire?" theme — D♭ → G7 (V).
- **The classical-era additional rule that produces "Neapolitan SIXTH" specifically**: early (17th-century) uses required the ♭II chord to ALSO be in first inversion — putting the chord's 3rd in the bass creates a minor 6th interval between the lowest and highest notes, hence "sixth." "Moonlight Sonata" is a textbook Neapolitan 6th (both criteria plus first inversion). Nearly all the pop/rock examples above use ROOT-POSITION ♭II — technically "Neapolitan chords" but not "Neapolitan SIXTHS," since the inversion requirement isn't met. A genuine modern first-inversion (true Neapolitan 6th) example: "Wolf at the Door" (Radiohead, D minor) — E♭maj7/G (♭II in first inversion) leading to A (V).
- **The Neapolitan 6th's origin as a chromatic variant of a common progression**: historically arose as a one-semitone alteration of the very common i-iv-V-i minor-key progression — raise/lower a single note in the iv chord's voicing and you get the Neapolitan 6th's distinctive bass-climbing voice-leading (4th degree up to 5th degree, then resolving home) instead.
- **Later Romantic-era usage shifted to ROOT position**: Chopin's Prelude in C minor uses root-position ♭II → root-position V, creating an angular tritone bass motion rather than the smoother stepwise climb of the inverted version. Traced forward into pop: "Could It Be Magic" (Barry Manilow) opens with a direct excerpt of that same Chopin prelude, carrying its Neapolitan chord along with it.
- **Etymology is shakier than the name implies**: named for the "Neapolitan school" of composers (Scarlatti, Pergolesi, Durante) credited with popularizing it — but earlier examples exist well before that school and outside Naples, so the geographic/historical association is described as "slightly dubious."
- **Roman-numeral labeling convention**: the chord gets its own dedicated symbol, capital **N** (rather than the more generic ♭II), and **N6** specifically for the first-inversion Neapolitan 6th.
- **Three explicit counter-examples — chords that look Neapolitan but fail the test, each explained by a different concept**: (1) "Because" (The Beatles, C♯ minor) — D major (♭II), but resolves to D DIMINISHED, not the dominant G♯ — fails the pre-dominant-function test, so it's NOT a true Neapolitan despite matching the chord-construction half. Ironic backstory: John Lennon wrote it after hearing Yoko Ono play "Moonlight Sonata" (the video's own opening Neapolitan example) — direct inspiration, but the borrowed device doesn't itself qualify. (2) "Things We Said Today" (The Beatles, key of A) — a ♭II chord (B♭) that is DOMINANT-functioning itself (a tritone substitute for the actual V) rather than pre-dominant — the distinguishing test: pre-dominant (leads TO the dominant) = Neapolitan; IS-the-dominant-itself (substitutes FOR it) = tritone sub. → [[Chord Substitution]] (tritone subs). (3) "Nights in White Satin" (The Moody Blues, E minor) — an F chord (♭II) with no clear pre-dominant or dominant function at all — best explained as **borrowing from the Phrygian (or Phrygian dominant) scale**, which naturally contains a major triad on ♭2, rather than forcing a Neapolitan or tritone-sub label onto it. → [[Diatonic Modes]] (Phrygian).
- **Explicit meta-point on analytical honesty**: the Neapolitan-sixth label was coined in the 19th century to retroactively describe 17th-century music the original composers never called that — stretching a period-specific classical label onto 20th/21st-century pop is "debatable," and whichever lens (Neapolitan, tritone sub, Phrygian borrowing) is most useful depends on context, not on one chord having one "correct" universal name.
