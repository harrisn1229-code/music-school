# When did the Axis chord progression become so popular?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U8ImkF7FMPw)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U8ImkF7FMPw)

## Synopsis

A historical/statistical study of the I–V–vi–IV "Axis Progression" — Bennett collected 446 songs using it and plotted release year, tracing its rise from an occasional choice in the late 1950s–60s to the dominant, then over-saturated, pop cliché of the late 2000s/early 2010s, and its subsequent decline. Useful as dated evidence for how a progression's *cultural* meaning (fresh vs. cliché) shifts over decades independent of its harmonic properties.

## Techniques

- **Earliest examples found**: "To Know Him Is to Love Him" (Phil Spector, 1958) — the earliest confirmed use; the traditional tune "Auld Lang Syne" is a commonly cited but **incorrect** earlier example — only modern reharmonizations fit it to this progression, not the original melody/harmony. "Don't Think Twice, It's All Right" (Bob Dylan, 1963) — a fleeting instance with a passing chord between vi and IV. "One Fine Day" (Carole King/Gerry Goffin, 1963) — near-miss: uses I–V–vi–IV but with a *minor* iv chord rather than the canonical major IV, so not a strict match.
- **Decade-by-decade popularity curve**: 1970s — ~23 examples found, progression starting to catch on but still "just another" choice. 1980s — ~30 examples; also the point where songs start being built *entirely* as a loop of this progression rather than splicing it among others (early example: "So Lonely," The Police, 1978; "With or Without You," U2). 1990s — becomes a cliché specifically in pop-punk, sometimes nicknamed the "pop punk chord progression"; possibly popularized in that scene by The Clash, The Only Ones, then Misfits. 2000s — the true boom; became "the go-to progression" the way 12-bar blues was the go-to in the 1950s. **Peak: ~2011** (30 songs found that year) — not coincidentally just after the Axis of Awesome comedy sketch (c. 2009) that gave the progression its "Axis" nickname by mocking its ubiquity. Post-2011 — a sharp falloff as the progression became "sonically dated" to 2000s pop; by the 2020s usage is back down near 1990s levels.
- **Cyclical-fashion framing**: Bennett draws an explicit parallel to the 1970s revival of 1950s 12-bar-blues rock and roll, predicting the Axis progression will likely see its own nostalgia-driven resurgence eventually — genre/progression fashions cycle roughly on a generational (~25-year) timescale.
- **Repeat-user statistics**: some artists have used the progression so often they could fill an album with it — 9 Ariana Grande songs, 19 Taylor Swift songs, and at least 15 Blink-182 songs (consistent with its pop-punk association) all identified as using this exact chord progression.
