# Songs that use Secondary Dominants

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pMopMBiHKfQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pMopMBiHKfQ)

## Synopsis

A pop-song-grounded tour through all five secondary dominant targets (V/vi, V/V, V/IV, V/ii, V/iii) — each with multiple real examples across genres and decades — plus two nuances worth keeping: secondary dominants don't strictly need their 7th to function, and they don't always resolve to the chord they tonicize.

## Techniques

- **V/vi (major III → vi)**: "All You Need Is Love" (The Beatles, key of G: B7→Em). "Happier Than Ever" (Billie Eilish, key of C: E7→Am). Also: "First Day of My Life" (Bright Eyes), "Rabbit" ("Abba's Queen" per transcript). This is likely the most common secondary-dominant type in pop.
- **V/V (major II → V)**: "9 to 5" (Dolly Parton, key of F: G7→C). "Piano Man" (Billy Joel). "That'll Be the Day" (Buddy Holly). Also works in minor keys: "Time Is Running Out" (Muse) uses the major-II chord as V/V in a minor-key context.
- **V/IV (dominant I → IV)**: "Hey Jude" (The Beatles) — the bridge opens on IV (B♭); at the verse-to-bridge seam, Paul switches the tonic F major to F7, pointing toward that IV. "Delilah" (Tom Jones, minor key: A minor's i-chord, A, becomes A7 to point to iv, D). "Don't Think Twice, It's All Right" (Bob Dylan) — uses BOTH V/V (major II) and V/IV (dominant I) in the same song.
- **V/ii (major VI → ii)**: rarer, mostly pre-1990s. "Big Girls Don't Cry" (Frankie Valli & The Four Seasons, key of G: E7→Am). "Maxwell's Silver Hammer" (The Beatles). "How Deep Is Your Love" (Bee Gees).
- **V/iii (major VII → iii)** — the rarest type: "Bohemian Rhapsody" (Queen, key of E♭: D major = V/iii → G minor). "Mr. Sandman" — notable for a **chained sequence**: the G♯7 secondary dominant resolves to iii (C), which is ITSELF a secondary dominant (V/vi) resolving to vi, which continues the chain further — "a long chain of secondary dominants."
- **Chained secondary dominants as a technique, beyond the single Mr. Sandman example**: a classic device in older music. The bridge of "I've Got Rhythm" (Gershwin standard) is a chain of secondary dominants each resolving neatly into the next; "Be My Baby" (The Ronettes) reuses the identical progression, transposed, in its own bridge.
- **The dominant 7th is what makes the pull unmistakable, but it isn't compulsory**: most secondary dominants in this video carry a 7th, which sharpens the tension toward resolution. Two examples that omit it and still function, just with a softer pull: "Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds" (The Beatles, pre-chorus, key of B♭: plain C major = V/V→F, no 7th). "Heart of Glass" (Blondie) — an F♯ major chord (no 7th) as V/V at the end of the bridge.
- **A secondary dominant doesn't have to resolve to the chord it tonicizes** — same non-resolution logic as a plain V not always going to I. "Creep" (Radiohead) — the B chord is V/vi (should resolve to vi, Em) but instead goes to IV (C) — a **deceptive/interrupted cadence**, providing partial but incomplete resolution. → [[Cadences]].
