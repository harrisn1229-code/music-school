# How I Approach Practicing

## Synopsis
Jack Ruch lays out his current practice philosophy: learning a solo by ear is necessary but not sufficient — real growth comes from getting "under the hood" and analyzing *why* the vocabulary works over the harmony, then reducing each idea to a reusable "concept" that becomes a template for your own lines. He demonstrates the whole method on a real transcription — a bluesy jazz solo over **[[🎵 CLIFFORD BROWN & MAX ROACH - Delilah|Delilah]]**, an AABA tune in F♯ minor originally recorded by Clifford Brown and Max Roach. He breaks the solo into chunks, names the harmonic concept behind each chunk (a blues phrase, a relative-major arpeggio, an upper-structure diminished arpeggio, several melodic-minor-family substitutions), then plays two full passes of his own solo over the same changes using those concepts rather than the original licks — proving the point that a concept, not a memorized lick, is the actual payoff of transcription. The most valuable part of the video isn't any single scale choice but the repeatable **process**: transcribe → chunk → analyze against the chord → name the concept → template a new solo from the concept set. Full extraction of that process is on [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]]; the tune-specific chord-by-chord concept map is on the [[🎵 CLIFFORD BROWN & MAX ROACH - Delilah|Delilah]] song note.

## What's taught
- **Learning solos by ear builds ear and vocabulary, but analysis is what makes it transferable** — "it's not enough to just simply learn a solo by ear, you've got to get under the hood and analyze what's happening in the music so you can learn from it and make the ideas your own." (~0:40)
- **The chunk-analyze-template method**, demonstrated live on a transcription: break a solo into small sections, work out what's harmonically happening in each section against the underlying chord, reduce it to a single "concept," then use the full set of per-chord concepts as a template to build an original solo over the same changes. Full write-up: [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]]. (~1:00–2:30)
- **The tune used as the vehicle:** "Delilah," an old jazz standard covered by Clifford Brown and Max Roach — an AABA form, 8-bar A sections + 8-bar bridge, in F♯ minor (transcript says "F minor" at one point but every subsequent chord degree — i / iv / V — is consistent with F♯ minor; flagged as a likely caption error). (~2:30–3:10)
- **A section, pass 1 (bluesy concept set):**
  - Over the i chord (F♯m): repetitive bluesy phrases leaning heavily on the ♭5 — "a very signature kind of characteristic of this sort of jazzy blues type phrasing especially over a minor chord." (~3:30)
  - Over the iv chord (Bm7): a **D major 7 arpeggio** — the relative major of Bm7 — "it's really common to play an arpeggio for the relative major chord over a minor chord, it gives you kind of a minor nine sound." (~4:10)
  - Over the V chord (C♯7, altered dominant): an **upper-structure diminished arpeggio built off the 3rd, 5th, ♭7th, and ♭9th** of the dominant chord — "a really signature common thing in jazz and in bebop... and we can slide it up in minor thirds because it's a symmetrical chord." (~5:00)
  - "Once you've been playing for a long time and have fundamental stuff under your fingers, that's kind of all you need — a push, a nudge, a suggestion of where to go with something." (~4:40)
- **A section, pass 2 (melodic-minor concept set) — same underlying chords, different vocabulary:**
  - Over the i chord (F♯m): **F♯ melodic minor**, leaning on the natural (major) 7th over the minor chord — "in a minor 7 chord you'd have a flat seven, so it's a little different sound, it pulls your ear a little bit to have that major seven." (~6:30)
  - Over the iv and V chords: **C♯ altered scale** (a mode of melodic minor), pushed to "push the limit of the harmony to get some sort of interesting outside sounds." (~7:00)
- **Bridge section** (iv → V → ♭VI dominant, a half-step above V → V):
  - Over the iv chord (Bm7): same D major 7 arpeggio device as the A section. (~9:30)
  - Over the V chord (C♯7): **F♯ harmonic minor** — described as an alternate way of thinking about the same territory as a C♯ Phrygian-dominant scale; the natural 7 against the ♭6 gives a dramatic "Spanish" color. (~10:00)
  - Over the ♭VI dominant chord (D7/D9, the half-step-above approach chord back to V): **Lydian dominant** (dominant scale with a ♯11/♯4 and ♭7, another melodic-minor mode) — "one of my favorite kind of scale sounds... it works perfectly over this kind of chord change." (~11:00)
- **The concept, not the lick, is the reusable unit** — Ruch explicitly reframes a transcribed lick as one example of a broader concept ("that's the original lick, but once we have that concept then you can move that all over the place") that can be relocated, reharmonized, or applied to new material entirely. (~5:20)
- **Practice payoff framing:** the video closes by playing his own full variation solo over the whole form back-to-back with the concepts named throughout, then points to tabs (his own variation + the original transcription) and the backing track being posted to his Patreon, and recommends listening to the original Clifford Brown/Max Roach recording directly. (~17:00–19:07)
