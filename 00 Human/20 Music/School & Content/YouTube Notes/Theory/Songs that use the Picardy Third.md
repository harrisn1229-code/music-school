# Songs that use the Picardy Third

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jGaNdKabvQ4)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jGaNdKabvQ4)

## Synopsis

The Picardy third — a minor-key piece or section that resolves onto the MAJOR version of the tonic instead of the expected minor — traced from Baroque-era classical convention through several Beatles uses and into modern pop/rock, including one clever functional application: using a Picardy-third major tonic at the end of a chorus specifically because it sets up a real perfect cadence into the next section.

## Techniques

- **Definition**: a passage or piece in a minor key resolves to the MAJOR I chord instead of the expected minor i — historically common in the Baroque era, giving a minor-key ending a triumphant/grand quality, but still found throughout pop and rock.
- **Whole-song endings**: "Time of the Season" (The Zombies, E minor throughout, ends on E major — Bennett's textbook example). "Roundabout" (Yes, largely E minor, ends on E major).
- **The Beatles were "particularly keen" on the device in 1964 specifically — 3+ examples on *A Hard Day's Night* alone**: "And I Love Her" (final verse, D minor → D major). "Things We Said Today" (verse in A minor resolves to A major). "I'll Be Back" — a denser usage: the Picardy third resolves at the end of EVERY line in the verse (not just once at the end of a section), each line starting in A minor and resolving to A major before jumping back to minor to repeat.
- **The Beatles' most famous use**: "A Day in the Life" — the closing piano passage establishes E minor, building into the orchestral climax that resolves onto a massive E major chord (three pianos playing it simultaneously) as the final chord of *Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band* — explicitly more triumphant than the E minor resolution would have been.
- **Combined with a sus4 for extra ambiguity before resolving**: "Exit Music (For a Film)" (Radiohead, B minor) resolves to B major, made more effective by passing through a sus4 chord first — since a sus4 chord's suspended 3rd is neither major nor minor, it holds the ear in ambiguity right before the Picardy resolution, sharpening the payoff.
- **More examples across genres/eras**: "Hello" (Lionel Richie, A minor → A major at the end — made cohesive because the A major chord had already been heard earlier in the song). "Summertime" (Janis Joplin's version, G minor → G major). "Under the Bridge" (Red Hot Chili Peppers, outro repeats an A minor passage that keeps resolving to A major each time). "Kiss From a Rose" (Seal) — swings between G minor and G major throughout the whole song, so ending on G major is a fitting, well-prepared close rather than a surprise.
- **A functional, not just coloristic, use — the cleverest example in the video**: "Killing Me Softly with His Song" (Roberta Flack) is largely in F minor, but each chorus resolves to F MAJOR rather than F minor. This isn't just for triumphant color — it sets up a genuine **perfect cadence (V-I) from the chorus's F major into the next verse's B♭ minor** (F major functions as the V of B♭ minor). If the chorus had resolved to F minor instead, that V-I pull into the next verse wouldn't exist, and the transition would have noticeably less forward momentum.
