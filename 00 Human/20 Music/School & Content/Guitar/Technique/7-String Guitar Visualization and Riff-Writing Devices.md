# 7-String Guitar Visualization and Riff-Writing Devices

Part of [[Guitar Positions]]. A fretboard-mental-model for extended-range (7-string) guitar, plus a set of concrete riff-writing devices for avoiding the common "just play power chords on the low string" trap that extended-range instruments invite.

## Core visualization: six-string-plus-one, not a shifted six-string
Tuned a whole step down (so the guitar reads as standard D tuning across its top six strings), a 7-string should be visualized as **an ordinary 6-string guitar with one extra LOW string added below**, not as a 6-string shifted up with an extra high string. Every scale, arpeggio, and chord shape already known on 6-string applies unchanged on strings 2–7; only the new 7th (lowest) string is genuinely new territory. This reframing removes the intimidation of "learning a whole new instrument" — nothing existing needs to be relearned, only extended downward.

## Extending known shapes onto the extra low string via the octave trick
When a scale fragment runs off the bottom of the known 6-string shape, find the missing low note by **thinking in octaves backward** from a note already known higher up, rather than treating the low string as unfamiliar territory requiring a fresh count-up-the-alphabet lookup. Same [[Notes & Pitches|octave-relocation]] logic already used for extending 6-string shapes, just pointed one string lower than usual.

## Riff-writing devices for using the low range without defaulting to bare power chords
- **Extended arpeggios instead of power chords, even on the lowest strings.** Arpeggios aren't just a high-fretboard sweep-picking device — a root-3rd-5th-7th(-9th) arpeggio played low and slow reads as far more harmonically interesting than a plain 5th-based power chord, especially with an added color tone: a C major arpeggio (C-E-G) extended to Cmaj7 (add B) then an added F♯ (the Lydian ♯4/♯11) produces real tension against a low root, worked with a slide (not straight 8th notes) and resolving up to a 9th.
- **Perfect-4th dyads instead of a power chord's perfect 5th** for a more dissonant, open sound — e.g. F♯ and B (a bare 4th, no root sounding) followed by a second 4th dyad over the same held bass note, for a stacked, ambiguous harmony rather than a plain power-chord's stability.
- **A held minor 2nd against the bass** as a deliberate dissonance — sliding into a half-step clash against the low root, flagged as a favorite device for injecting tension into an otherwise diatonic riff.
- **Let an open low string ring, lightly muted, under a fretted arpeggio** — pick the open low string (A or D depending on the phrase) with the fretting hand's palm resting just enough to soften but not fully mute it, so it rings as a sustained pedal tone under the arpeggio rather than a clean sustained bass note or a fully choked mute — a deliberate middle setting between "full ring" and "full mute."
- **Reharmonize a plain triad arpeggio by raising one note** — instead of a plain D major arpeggio, raise a chord tone (the video works a raised note that produces a suspenseful, unresolved color rather than the plain major sound) for a more interesting shape built from the same fingering location.
- **Mirrored-shape tapping trick.** Fret a basic chord shape (power-chord-derived major shapes worked here) with the left hand as usual, then **tap the identical shape one octave higher** with the picking hand and pull off back down to the fretted notes — visualized as "mirroring" the same chord shape rather than tapping unrelated notes, which keeps the tapped part harmonically connected to what the fretting hand is already playing. Works at a range of tempos, slow for learning, fast for the shred payoff.
- **Close a phrase with a swept arpeggio plus a tapped minor-2nd clash** — sweep up through a major arpeggio, then tap the note one half-step below the top note reached, for a dissonant "wrong note" flourish that resolves the phrase with tension rather than a clean landing.
