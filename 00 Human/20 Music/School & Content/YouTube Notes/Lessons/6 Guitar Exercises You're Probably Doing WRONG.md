# 6 Guitar Exercises You're Probably Doing WRONG

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bcC5uZMxj1w)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bcC5uZMxj1w)

## Synopsis

Six commonly-misused practice exercises/topics, each with the specific mistake named and a concrete fix — the densest single-video "how to actually practice" content in this batch, closing out the technique-lessons playlist.

## What's taught

1. **The "spider" chromatic exercise — most players get the finger-lift discipline right but miss the overlap check.** Correct technique: fingers stay down until needed on the next string, AND the outgoing note and incoming note should audibly overlap/ring together briefly (e.g. a pinky-fretted note on one string still ringing as the index finger frets the next string's note). That dissonant overlap is the actual point — it's only physically possible with properly curled fingers that don't block the string in between, so the exercise doubles as an automatic curled-finger-technique check. Playing it "clean" (no overlap) usually means the technique, not the exercise, is wrong.
2. **Improvisation practice: stick to one fixed rhythmic pattern for the whole take, but never repeat the same melodic phrase within it.** Named fix for "I always play the same licks" and "I don't improve from daily improv" — random noodling over backing tracks doesn't target either problem; forcing a fixed rhythm while banning phrase repetition trains both timing discipline and melodic variety simultaneously. Recommended dose: 15-20 minutes/day, more effective than hours of unstructured jamming.
3. **Picking exercises with a fixed, uniform notes-per-string count don't prepare you for real material, which mixes counts unpredictably.** Personal anecdote given directly: arranging a Paganini caprice for guitar (from his album *Elevation*) forced him to realize pure 2-note-per-string or 3-note-per-string drilling doesn't transfer to real string-transition demands — deliberately practice **mixed-pattern** workouts (uneven notes-per-string within the same line) as a category, not just clean fixed-count drills.
4. **Sweep picking: practice loud/fast/sloppy early rather than slow-and-perfect-only.** Named mistake: only ever practicing sweep picking slow with a focus on perfect technique. Fix: deliberately push outside the comfort zone at speed early (accepting bad-sounding results at first) specifically to develop the flowing, non-down-stroke-locked wrist motion sweep picking needs — a motion that's very hard to feel or discover at slow tempos. Also: rotate through **four** arpeggio qualities (minor, major, diminished, augmented) as a "speed burst" set rather than drilling just one or two shapes repeatedly.
5. **Practice actual music theory application, not just diagrams/memorization — a concrete chord-tone arpeggiation routine.** Take the first 4-8 chords of any jazz lead sheet (doesn't require liking jazz); for each chord, play its root-3rd-5th-7th, alternating ascending on one chord and descending on the next (e.g. B♭m7 ascending root→♭7, then the next chord descending 7th→root). A genuine chord-tone-application routine, distinct from passive theory study.
6. **Speed-building myths debunked, plus a concrete drill.** False: linearly adding 2-5 BPM/day gets you to a target tempo on schedule. False: bigger/more forceful picking-hand movements produce more speed (also named as an injury risk — tendonitis). Real fix: practice **outside your comfort zone in short bursts** (single-string bursts first, tolerating sloppiness, then connect bursts together with the fretting hand) rather than a smooth linear tempo climb. **A named 7/4 speed-burst exercise** with three difficulty blocks per measure: (1) tremolo picking on one string (easy), (2) a string transition (harder), (3) one note on a higher string then back to the lower string (named as one of the hardest string-transition types to drill) — the odd 7/4 meter is deliberate, forcing timing focus alongside the technical burden.
