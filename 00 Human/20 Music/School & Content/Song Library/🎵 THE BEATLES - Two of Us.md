## Pollack's analysis

*Extracted from [[AWP — Two Of Us]] — Alan W. Pollack, "Notes On…" #167.*

**Form.** A pseudo-folk love song that would be too sweet to survive on its own terms; **uneven phrase lengths, mid-verse meter changes and one modal swerve are what keep it edible**. The verse's metric shifts are among the most extreme in the catalogue. The classic two-bridge variant with a single verse between the bridges and no instrumental solo, except that **the intro returns between the first two verses**. Intro 4 bars, all I, with a staggered entrance. Verse phrased **AB / AB, C C C′**, harmonically closed but metrically anything but — bar lengths run **4 4 2 4 4 | 3 3 3 3 | 3 2 4 4** ([[ARR-C06 — Section-length conventions, and breaking them on purpose]]). The metric bookkeeping is the good part: phrase A ends **half a bar short**, the switch to 3/4 keeps the deficit running, and the lone 2/4 bar at the end of C′ **pays back exactly the two beats that went missing**.

**Harmony.** `Verse: I (held) – IV – ii · I – V/IV | I – V/IV (3/4 bars) · I – IV – I` · `Bridge (in G minor): ♭III – v – i – ii (held 2) – V`. The verse's chord list is small and ordinary, and his point is about **function, not vocabulary**: `IV → ii` moves from one subdominant to another, so nothing progresses, and that inertia is the idyllic feel ([[HAR-C01 — The three functions: T, SD, D]]). The bridge simply **switches to the parallel minor**, flattening 3, 6 and 7 in the tune and dragging the chords with it ([[HAR-C08 — Borrowed chords and modal interchange]]) — the same device Paul used in "Here, There And Everywhere," in the same key. The difference: **here the only V in the bridge leads straight back to major**, so the minor is implied and never confirmed, and cross-relations pile up as a result.

**Notable.** The tune opens with a textbook pentatonic lick and then admits the 4th for the rest of the verse ([[SCL-C03 — Pentatonic scales]]). George is scored in tenor range and kept low, but gets one recurring scale figure that **marks each metric shift** in the verse's back half. The fade is a fabrication — the studio performance had a full ending. He rates the *Let It Be* take the definitive version, rare for that album.

**Against this page.** Agrees on G major, which is this page's as-played key; the record sounds in B♭ with the capo, and he analyses at the played pitch.
