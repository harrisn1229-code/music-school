# 5 Tips for Realistic Drums that Slap

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AKgd6cnQyTc)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AKgd6cnQyTc)

## Synopsis

Max Konyi (2022) breaks down five concrete techniques for making a programmed electronic/glitch-hop-leaning drum beat feel played rather than gridded, worked entirely on one 4-bar example beat: **form** (the beat's structural shape over time), **ghost notes**, **velocity variation**, **micro-timing humanization** (deliberately by hand, not an auto-humanize plugin), and **one distinctive special element**. The through-line across all five is restraint and intentionality — every variation technique is applied selectively (to specific hits, specific sections) rather than uniformly, because uniform "humanization" reads as random rather than played. Directly extends the existing [[Rhythm and Groove]] page's Humanization and Ghost notes sections with a fuller worked example and a sharp critique of algorithmic humanize tools.

## Techniques

- **Form: structure a drum loop as A-B-A-C (or similar), not a single bar looped flat, so the ear registers real multi-bar length.** His example beat is 4 bars — a simple kick pattern for the "A" bars, a syncopated/complex kick pattern for "B," each paired with an inverse hi-hat treatment (strong-beat hats under the busy kick section, syncopated hats under the simple kick section) so the two elements constantly trade who's "static/strong" and who's "playing around." The felt effect: the pattern doesn't feel like repetition until the full 4-bar cycle actually completes, versus a 1-bar loop (or a 1-bar loop with only tiny variations) which reads as shorter than it is. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]] (song-level version of the same "give it real length" principle, here applied at the loop/pattern level instead)
- **Ghost notes come in two flavors and both matter: a secondary quiet snare, and a secondary quiet hi-hat.** A light/soft "secondary snare" functions almost like an extra hi-hat, filling syncopated gaps the main hi-hat/kick pattern leaves open; a similar "ghost" layer exists on the hi-hat itself (a quiet in-between hit alongside the main hat and the offbeat hat). Both ghost layers exist specifically to fill gaps the main pattern doesn't hit, and their *combination* becomes a single perceived top-end texture rather than reading as separate elements. Workflow: **write the main pattern (kick, snare, basic hi-hat) completely first, then go back and find the gaps** to place very-low-velocity ghost hits into — treat ghost-note placement as a distinct second pass, not something decided while writing the main groove. → [[Rhythm and Groove]] (existing "Ghost notes and note length" section)
- **Ghost notes create a physical "push-pull" groove sensation: the ear/body leans toward a strong beat, then gets a syncopated hit instead of the expected strong hit, which is the actual pleasurable tension of a groove.** Framed explicitly as the mechanism behind why groove feels good — anticipation of the strong beat, deliberately delayed/displaced gratification via the ghost/syncopated hit.
- **Velocity should not be applied uniformly — decide per-element whether it needs variation at all.** The **main hits that hold the groove together (primary kick, primary snare) stay velocity-static/full-strength** in his genre (electronic/glitch-hop) specifically because the groove needs a dependable, unwavering anchor — softening the main kick "loses impact" and reads as weak in a genre that needs that strength. Contrast demoed directly: the same beat with ALL velocities flattened to uniform sounds like "a wall of sound" with nothing pulling the ear toward any particular hit. → [[Rhythm and Groove]] (existing velocity-humanization content, this adds the "which hits should stay static" judgment call)
- **Where velocity variation DOES belong: secondary/ghost hits, specifically weighted toward momentum into the strong beat.** A light secondary kick placed just before a strong beat is deliberately given lower velocity than the strong hit it leads into ("light stuff before the heavy beat, heavy beat is where you put the heavy stuff") — a directional velocity ramp, not just random variance. Ghost-note snares vary in velocity hit-to-hit (not static, but also not perfectly regular) based on how important each individual ghost hit is to the groove — some are near-inaudible filler, one or two are meant to be genuinely heard, and those get a velocity roll leading into the next strong hit.
- **Reject algorithmic auto-humanize for drums; hand-place (or play + hand-adjust) micro-timing instead.** A/B'd directly: running the same beat through a generic humanization algorithm ("just said go") produces timing that's randomly spread within a threshold — a few moments land well by chance, but overall it reads as "badly played," not humanized, because a real drummer's timing deviations are *informed* (by feel, mindset, groove, experience), not statistically random. His actual workflow: play the part by hand and adjust afterward, or quantize first and then manually nudge — either works, but a one-click humanize plugin does not, for drums specifically.
- **Timing-offset direction should track metric role, and stays consistent for him: strong hits land exactly on the grid; secondary/syncopated hits are shifted intentionally LATE, never early (a stated personal pet peeve against early hits, with an exception for snares in certain unspecified circumstances).** Applied per-element: kicks/snares on the beat stay exact; claps vary — sometimes late, sometimes right on, occasionally slightly early — with the *inconsistency itself* (not a fixed offset amount) being what reads as human rather than a static "always X ms late" rule; hi-hats get subtle late-shift humanization when something else (kick/snare) already owns the strong beat, but a hi-hat playing the strong beat *alone* stays exactly on-grid because it has to take over the anchoring role the kick/snare would otherwise provide. → [[Rhythm and Groove]] (extends "manual hip-hop swing… nudge only weak-beat hits late" with a more granular per-element rule set)
- **One distinctive, non-repeating special element ices the beat — used once or twice, never as a recurring layer.** A cowbell hit appears exactly once across the whole 4-bar loop, at one specific syncopated moment — its distinctiveness (a clearly pitched, tonal sound cutting through an otherwise blended kit of hats/snares/clicks) is precisely *because* it's rare; the advice is explicitly against the instinct to use every interesting sound throughout the whole beat just because it's been introduced. Find 1–3 good spots for a special/ear-catching sound and place it only there. → [[Ear Candy and Hiding Synths]] (the same "used sparingly for maximum effect" principle applied at the sound-selection level)
