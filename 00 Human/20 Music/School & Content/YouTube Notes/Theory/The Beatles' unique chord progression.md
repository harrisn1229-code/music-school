# The Beatles' unique chord progression

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KQRwViNES0c)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KQRwViNES0c)

## Synopsis

A full harmonic analysis of one song, *I Am the Walrus* — its intro's key ambiguity, a secondary dominant that deliberately fails to resolve where expected, a tritone pivot chord, and an outro built entirely from parallel major triads climbing a minor scale, likened to a Shepard tone illusion.

## What's taught

- **Intro**: performed on a Pianet (an early electric piano); the opening bar is 6 or 4 beats depending on the mix (some mixes, including the US mono, cut the first 2 beats). The intro sits in **B**, not the verse's key of A, swaying between B major and B minor — **modal interchange/modal mixture** (blending chords from the parallel major and minor of the same key) giving a bluesy ambiguity. A D major chord works as a pivot chord (it belongs to both B minor and A major), smoothing the seam into the verse's A major.
- **Verse**: mixes chords from A major and its parallel minor (A minor) for the same bluesy effect; the vocal melody repeatedly slides E→D♯, a chromatic "blue note" outside the key of A major. Structurally, the song rarely repeats a section identically — each pass through a verse/chorus varies its chords or melody.
- **Chorus — misdirected secondary dominant**: opens on B major, functioning as the **ii-chord-made-major (V/V)**, a classic secondary dominant that "should" resolve down a 5th to E. Instead the song climbs B→C→D→E — parallel major triads walking up ♭III, IV, V — deliberately subverting where a secondary dominant is expected to go. Notably, the whole song **contains no minor chords at all**, despite constantly implying minor-key color.
- **Bridge/reintro**: jumps back to B major, the key change masked by a sound-effect break (an alarm clock). Pivots step-by-step back to A major via an **F major chord** — F is in neither A major nor B major, and is in fact a **tritone from B**, the harmonically "furthest" possible chord from that key center; F moving to E pivots smoothly into an implied A minor/A major (modal interchange again) even though no A minor chord is actually played.
- **Outro**: a 7-bar loop descending the A minor scale, but with a **major triad built on every scale degree** (not the diatonically "correct" mixed major/minor qualities) — an intentional, "wonky" harmonization. George Martin's string arrangement amplifies the effect: the cello follows the descending line down to its lowest note then jumps up an octave, while the violins move in **contrary motion**, rising as the harmony falls — producing a disorienting sound often compared to a **Shepard tone** (the auditory illusion of a tone that seems to ascend or descend forever). The song fades out rather than resolving, reinforcing the effect.
