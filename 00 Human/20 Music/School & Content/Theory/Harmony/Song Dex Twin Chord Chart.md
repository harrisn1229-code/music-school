# Song Dex Twin Chord Chart

A single-sheet chord reference from 1951/53 — *"the most complete and thorough chord chart available (187 basic chords)"* — photographed into the vault 2026-08-09. Signed **Jimmy Harris** in the top corner.

![[Song Dex Twin Chord Chart.jpeg]]

## How it's laid out

A grid: **17 key spellings down, 11 chord types across**, every cell showing the chord in standard notation on a treble staff. 17 × 11 = the advertised 187.

| Group | Types |
|---|---|
| **Triads** | Major · Minor · Augmented |
| **Sixths** | Major · Minor |
| **Sevenths** | Dominant · Minor · Major · Diminished |
| **Ninths** | Dominant · Diminished |

The key column runs every practical spelling rather than every pitch class: **C · C♯ · D♭ · D · D♯ · E♭ · E · F · F♯ · G♭ · G · G♯ · A♭ · A · B♭ · B · C♭**. Enharmonic pairs get their own rows because the *notation* differs even though the sound doesn't — which is the whole point of a chart you read rather than a chart you look chords up in.

## The one editorial decision worth knowing

Printed at the top:

> *"For easier reading all possible ♭♭ or ✕ notes are substituted by their enharmonics."*

So the chart **deliberately mis-spells** chords that would require double flats or double sharps, in favour of the easier-to-read enharmonic. That is a readability choice, not a theory claim — a fully diminished seventh on C♯ properly wants B𝄫, and this chart will print A instead.

**Do not treat this chart as authoritative on spelling.** For correct spelling from the interval formula, see [[Diminished Chords]] and [[Chord Naming by Context]]. The chart is for finding the notes fast on paper, and it's honest about the trade.

## What it's good for

- Reading practice — every chord is in notation, nothing is in symbols or diagrams, so it doubles as sight-reading material for chord shapes. Pairs with [[A Modern Method for Guitar - Enrichment|Leavitt]] and [[Sight Reading]].
- Seeing all 17 spellings of one chord type at once, which makes the pattern of how accidentals accumulate across keys visible in a way a per-key table doesn't.
- A period artifact. 1951 chord vocabulary stops at ninths — no elevenths, no thirteenths, no suspended chords, no altered dominants. What a working musician was expected to know in 1951 is itself the interesting part, and it's a useful contrast with the extended vocabulary in [[Extended Chords]] and [[Altered Chords]].
