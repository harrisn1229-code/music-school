## Synopsis

Jared Borkowski's first attempt at a rapid-fire Q&A format — 10 viewer questions grabbed semi-randomly and answered back to back, most pulled from email/comments. It's a genuine grab-bag: two questions are narrow music-theory clarifications (chord-symbol enharmonic equivalence, why the circle of fourths), two are pure physical-technique fixes (fretting-hand tension, which fingers to strum with), and the remaining six all orbit the same underlying theme — practice philosophy, goal-orientation, and how to stop being overwhelmed by the sheer amount of material a guitarist could work on. Jared repeatedly circles back to one core idea across multiple answers: there is nothing objectively required to practice, only things that serve a genuinely felt goal ("vision"), and the discipline of managing overwhelm is itself a skill to practice rather than a problem to solve once and be done with. The theory/technique answers are concrete and immediately actionable (the ♭9/♭13 equivalence rule, the root-of-the-key-center drill, the arm-weight-plus-breathing fix for tension, the down-with-nails/up-with-thumbnail strum). The philosophy answers are more abstract but still land on genuinely portable, nameable ideas — Scott Young's "directness" from *Ultra Learning*, the "rotate and come back right before you forget it" interleaving logic, and "practice not getting overwhelmed" as its own trainable muscle. ## What's taught

**Q1 — Chord symbols are ambiguous by design: ♭9 and ♭13 (not ♯5) mean the same thing on a dominant chord.** A viewer flagged Jared for "playing the wrong chord" (G7♯5 instead of the written G7♭9). His answer: a dominant-7-family chord symbol represents a *scale*, not a literal fixed note collection — a dominant 7♭9 is telling you "this is the V chord of a minor key," and that minor key's harmonic-minor-derived dominant scale (1, ♭9, major 3, 4, 5, ♭13, ♭7) contains **♭13, not ♯5** — they're the same enharmonic pitch, but ♭13 is the correct name because the underlying scale is what's being represented. Practical payoff: **G7♭9 and G7♭13 as written chord symbols are interchangeable** — you can play either extension for either symbol, and which one you actually grab is a fretboard-convenience choice (easier voicing depending on which string carries the root), not a "correctness" choice. Recognizing a familiar chord *shape* is powerful, but a shape's extensions beyond 1-3-5-7 can function as more than one thing (♯5 vs. ♭13 are the clearest example), so don't assume the shape you recognize is "the" chord — check what scale/key context it's actually representing.

**Q2 — For overwhelming key-change tunes: reduce each key zone to its root and drill the switch before drilling the scales.** For a jazz standard that changes keys constantly (e.g. "All the Things You Are"), first find the key centers and mentally collapse each multi-chord zone into a single unit — "one piece of information." Then, over a backing track (or unaccompanied), **play only the root note (or a root/power chord) of each key center**, switching to the new root the instant the key changes — no scales, no licks, just the root pulse. This trains the ear/reflex to *feel* exactly when a key change happens and *hear* where the new root sits, independent of scale-shape knowledge — a prerequisite reflex-building step to run before or alongside heavier scale-shape drilling. Described as reliably unlocking the "I keep getting lost in the changes" problem.

**Q3 — There is no objectively "better" fretboard-visualization system (CAGE vs. pentatonic vs. anything else) — use whatever you already know well, and let new material attach to it.** Answering "should I always visualize the major scale instead of pentatonic shapes," Jared's position: a visualization system's only job is to help you, so there's no right/wrong choice — you default to whatever you learned first (pentatonic scales for single-note players, the CAGE open-chord shapes for chord players), and everything learned after that naturally gets related back to that first reference point ("oh, that's usually where the 13th is, and this is shifted, so that's the ♭13"). Only switch references if the current one is causing a genuine processing hiccup or extra steps — not because a "more correct" system exists. Eventually, with enough use, *everything* — every scale, every chord shape, all of theory — becomes an equally-fast reference regardless of which one you started from.

**Q4 — Disorganized practice is usually a vision/goals problem, not a discipline problem — plus the "directness" concept from *Ultra Learning*.** Responding to a self-described disorganized player ("lots of jazz chords, but I'm not making music"): the fix isn't more practice material, it's a genuinely clear end-point. Concrete framing questions: what kind of music do you actually want to play? What inspires you most? What's a specific, finished, start-to-finish piece of music you want to be able to play — and what does "done" even mean for it (comfortable performance level vs. just recording a rough take on your phone vs. just understanding the theory of it)? Once the goal is rock-solid clear, the steps toward it clarify (or you can deliberately invent them). Practicing random material recommended by outside sources, with no felt connection to *why*, is what produces the disorganized/hectic feeling described in the question. **Named concept: "directness"** (Scott Young, *Ultra Learning*) — learn something because you have a direct, immediate application for it, not because it seems generically like a good thing to know; a direct-use skill sticks because you're applying it right away, not stockpiling it.

**Q5 — Nothing is objectively mandatory to practice — everything is conditional on a specific goal ("the big if").** Extends Q4's logic to a question about whether major-triad scale patterns are "important": objectively, no single skill (chords, single notes, sight-reading, improvising) is required to have a great musical career — cites Paul McCartney (doesn't read music) as proof reading isn't mandatory even at the highest level. **The actual rule: "there's nothing you need to practice unless you have a big if"** — e.g. *if* you want to join a guitar quartet that requires reading, *then* you need to practice reading. Practice should feel like the thing you'd rather be doing over anything else in that moment (flow/zone framing); chasing that feeling is framed as more productive than grinding a checklist, because once you're in that state "everything gets taken care of on its own." The practical use of this rule: before adding anything to a practice list, name its "if" — the goal it's actually in service of — rather than practicing it because it's generically recommended.

**Q6 — Fretting-hand tension under playing intensity: use arm weight instead of grip, plus box breathing.** For a player whose fretting hand tenses up specifically when playing harder/more intensely (a rock-band-era issue Jared had himself, including a "hitchhiker thumb" habit): (1) **let the weight of the fretting arm do the work instead of squeezing** — a full barre chord can be held with essentially no thumb pressure at all by letting gravity/arm weight supply the clamping force, rather than gripping harder as intensity rises; (2) **mindful/box breathing** — while playing, take a deliberate breath, consciously scan the body for held tension, and breathe it out. *Box breathing pattern given: inhale 4 seconds, hold 4 seconds, exhale 4 seconds, hold 4 seconds, repeat.* Both together are the recommended combined fix when grip tension is a recurring problem.

**Q7 — Strumming-hand finger technique: nails down, thumbnail up.** For "which fingers should I strum with": **strum downward with the backs of the fingernails** (any of the fingers work — Jared varies between just the middle finger, the middle two, or three/four fingers depending on feel) and **strum upward with the back of the thumbnail only**. Down-up-down-up alternates fingers-down / thumb-up throughout.

**Q8 — Start a hard goal now (adapted down to size) rather than waiting to "be ready," and rotate practice material instead of finishing things in strict sequence.** Answering whether to start a big arrangement goal (a solo chord-melody version of "All the Things You Are") now or wait until finishing prerequisite course material: **go toward the exciting/inspiring goal immediately**, but adapt it down to your current skill level rather than attempting the full difficult version — play just the melody, play it much slower, learn just the roots of the chords, and work up from there; "you can manipulate anything difficult to make it easier," and getting practice at *doing that adaptation* is itself valuable, separate from the piece. Second, independent point: **rotate through multiple practice items rather than finishing one before starting the next** — could be 3 things or 100, strict order or inspiration-driven, your call — but the key discipline is **coming back to each item right before you'd have forgotten it**, which gets more retention per minute than mastering one thing fully before moving to the next (the alternative — nailing something once, then not touching it again for a long stretch — reliably loses it). Cross-references directly to spaced/interleaved-practice logic already in the vault (see Routed to).

**Q9 — Why the circle of fourths specifically (not fifths) for running material through all 12 keys: it's the most related adjacent key, changes only one scale note per step, and it's the more common real-world modulation direction.** Moving a key center by a fourth (or, equivalently, a fifth in the other direction) changes only **one note, by one half step**, out of the entire scale/key's note collection — the closest possible relationship between two different keys. Practiced in one fixed fretboard position (per Jared's system), this means each new key overlaps six notes with the previous one and differs by exactly one, which sharpens the ear specifically on *which* note changed rather than presenting a totally unfamiliar collection each time. **Circle of fourths specifically (not fifths) is used because moving up a fourth is more common in actual chord progressions/real music than moving up a fifth** — so drilling the fourths direction better matches what you'll actually encounter. Applies to running any scale, mode, arpeggio, lick, or full tune through all 12 keys.

**Q10 — "How to not get overwhelmed by everything there is to practice" is itself a skill to practice, not a problem with a one-time fix — plus a standing personal warm-up habit.** The amount of available guitar material is functionally infinite, so feeling overwhelmed is the *default* state, not a sign something's wrong — "you're never going to practice everything ever." The reframe: **treat not-getting-overwhelmed as its own muscle/skill to deliberately practice**, not a checklist problem to solve once. Practical gauge: if a practice session (even a short 20-minute one) makes time slip away and leaves you feeling nourished/excited, that's the right material — a felt, moment-to-moment check rather than a comprehensive plan. Framed close to a meditative practice: quiet the noise of "everything there is to do" and focus on the one thing in front of you; the payoff feeling described is "this is exactly where I'm supposed to be right now," contrasted with the stress-feeling of being pulled in many directions at once. **Standing personal habit named: Jared does the same specific warm-up every single time he sits down to play, without skipping it** — he offers a free PDF + video demo of it (linked in the video description / soundguitarlessons.com/warmup); the warm-up itself isn't detailed in this video beyond the fact that he never skips it.
