# Overlooked SKILLS for Learning Jazz - CREATIVITY is in your Brain

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mZJ5bn3Pyl0)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mZJ5bn3Pyl0)

## Synopsis

Jens Larsen lays out the two practice habits he says he leaned on most while learning jazz and still uses to add new material to his vocabulary: **analyzing** existing lines (breaking a phrase down into its small melodic building blocks) and **composing** (deliberately writing new lines with those blocks, slowly, with the instrument in hand, rather than only encountering new material live inside a solo). He frames this explicitly as a two-stage pipeline — analyze first to *find* the small reusable pieces, then compose to *deploy* them — worked twice at real length: once against a phrase pulled from the Charlie Parker Omnibook (a rhythm-changes A-section fragment), and once starting from a single diatonic-arpeggio idea (the arpeggio built off the 3rd of a ii chord) with no source lick at all, just Larsen composing several original 2-5-1 lines from scratch. The video's real thesis, stated directly, is about *why* to slow down: playing something once inside a fast-moving chord progression gives your ear no time to actually evaluate it, so both analyzing and composing exist to buy that evaluation time — and the byproduct of doing this repeatedly is a developed personal taste, since you're forced to judge each line ("does this work? do I like this?") rather than just reproducing what a source player did. He's explicit that the composed lines themselves are throwaway — the point isn't to end up with memorized two-bar licks to perform, it's to rehearse *the process of finding a good way to use a small fragment* so that same process runs live when improvising. This is a pure instrument/theory lesson with no gear, plugin, or production content — it slots directly alongside the vault's existing Jack Ruch "chunk, name the concept, rebuild your own solo" method as a second, independently-arrived-at version of essentially the same idea, with two differences worth keeping distinct: Larsen doesn't reduce each chunk to one named "concept" the way Ruch does (he stays closer to the literal melodic fragment and its transposed/modified variants), and Larsen adds a from-scratch composing mode with no source lick at all (start from a bare arpeggio and compose new lines directly), which Ruch's method doesn't cover.

## What's taught

**Framing — analyzing + composing as a two-stage vocabulary pipeline (~0:00-1:30):** Two habits, used together: (1) **analyze** a phrase to find its small reusable melodic building blocks, and (2) **compose** new material using those blocks — done slowly, sitting down, with the guitar in hand, deliberately *not* inside a fast-moving live progression. Stated reason for slowing down: if you try a new lick/scale/arpeggio idea live over a blues or changes and it doesn't work, the moment passes too fast to figure out why — analyzing/composing gives the ear time to actually evaluate what's happening. Also framed as the mechanism for developing personal taste: you're forced to judge "does this work, do I like this" repeatedly rather than reproducing someone else's line verbatim.

**Worked example 1 — analyzing a Charlie Parker Omnibook fragment (~1:30-9:00):** Source: a rhythm-changes A-section fragment (last part of the A section, going to the IV chord, in B♭ — so the fragment sits on E♭, with a written B♭7 back home; Larsen adds the resolving F himself since it's cut off the way he excerpted it from the Omnibook, and flags that the Omnibook itself has known transcription errors, which doesn't matter for this exercise since the goal is finding usable building blocks, not verifying Parker's exact notes). Larsen chunks the line into five distinct melodic fragments and analyzes each on its own terms:
- **Chromatic passing run** — C down through B down to B♭, played legato. Judged the least interesting/reusable piece of the line.
- **A four-note pentatonic fragment** — degrees 1-3-4-5 of a G minor pentatonic, landing over an E♭maj7 (works because G minor pentatonic sits naturally inside E♭ major). Called out as a good example of the *kind* of small, high-frequency fragment worth specifically hunting for — his stated general principle: you get more mileage as an improviser from **a small bag of small pieces** than from either a large bag of small pieces or a small bag of large pieces, because small fragments recombine into fresh material on the fly, where long memorized licks don't.
- **A plain scale run from F back to B♭** — flagged explicitly as the least useful fragment in the whole line, included only for completeness.
- **A chromatic enclosure targeting B♭** — a triplet moving chromatically A→B♭→A (i.e., stepping past the target and back) leading up into B♭ on the downbeat of the next bar; the whole preceding bar exists just to set up this arrival.
- **A descending C minor 7 arpeggio into B♭**, then a **flat-13-to-major-3rd sixth-interval leap** (C up to D♭/♭13, skip up to A/3rd) resolving down the scale (G, G♭) to F. Two named observations here worth keeping as reusable rules on their own: (1) **descending arpeggios are underpracticed** — most players default to ascending arpeggio drilling and don't build the reflex to run one downward; (2) **sixth intervals are a Parker signature** — besides plain scale steps (2nds/3rds), the 6th is the interval he reaches for most, and because a 6th is the melodic inversion of a 3rd, it's automatically consonant/melodic while still standing out as a wide leap — worth deliberately practicing as an interval class, not just encountering by accident.

**A concrete taste-vs-transcription-accuracy example (~7:00-9:00):** On that flat-13-to-3rd-then-scale-down move, Larsen states his own preference explicitly diverges from what Parker actually plays — Parker resolves by running down the plain scale (G, G♭, F), but Larsen says his own taste is to make it a fully altered line instead: flat-13 up to the sharp-9, then down through the flat-9 to the root. Framed as a deliberate, named example of the taste-development point from the framing section — noticing what a source player does, recognizing it's not what you'd choose, and consciously substituting your own preferred color rather than treating the source as gospel.

**Worked example 1 continued — composing new lines from the extracted fragments (~9:00-12:30):** Takes just the enclosure-plus-chromatic-run opening fragment (analyzed above) and transplants it onto a new 2-5-1 in B♭ major, landing it as a line over the ii chord (C minor) since the fragment starts on C minor's 5th; continues into an altered F7 resolving to B♭. Stated method in the moment: play the borrowed fragment, then ask "what's the next thing I hear?" and let the ear supply the continuation one note-choice at a time, rather than planning the whole line up front. Recommends writing lines like this down on paper in the early stages of this practice — specifically because if a composed line doesn't work, having it on paper lets you go back and diagnose *why*, the same diagnostic step used on the Parker line itself.

**Worked example 2 — composing from a bare diatonic arpeggio, no source lick (~12:30-19:30):** Starting point: the diatonic-arpeggio-practice advice every jazz student gets ("learn all the diatonic arpeggios, learn your 2-5-1s in all keys") is usually handed over as a bare instruction with no worked example of *how* to actually use it — composing is offered as the fix. Larsen takes the arpeggio built on the **3rd of the ii chord** (i.e., over a 2-5-1 in F: G minor7's 3rd is B♭, so a B♭ major arpeggio) and composes four separate original lines from it, narrating the construction of each:
1. **Straight descending B♭maj7 arpeggio → reused shape starting on a different note (reading as G minor 7) → a standard altered-C7 vocabulary line he already had internalized.** Demonstrates recombining a fresh fragment with existing vocabulary rather than inventing everything new.
2. **Same arpeggio approached with a pedal-point lead-in instead of starting cold on the descending run**, then into a C7 altered line that reuses the ♭13-up-a-diatonic-6th-to-the-3rd shape lifted directly from the Parker line's ending — an explicit example of one analyzed fragment getting reused as raw material inside a completely different composed line.
3. **Same idea, rhythm changed** — the B♭maj7 arpeggio played as a triplet instead of straight eighths, followed by G minor7 (back to straight eighths) and a C7 altered line built this time from an A♭7♯5 arpeggio (a diatonic-in-context altered-dominant substitution). Demonstrates rhythm as its own independent variable to vary once the pitch content is set — same idea, felt completely differently.
4. **Combined with the chromatic enclosure from the Parker line, retargeted.** The original enclosure targeted B♭; since this line needs to land on the arpeggio's starting note (A, not B♭), Larsen explicitly reworks the enclosure to approach A instead — chromatic run up G→A♭→A, resolving to the 7th of B♭ major, then the descending arpeggio, then a first-inversion triad line (A major triad → F♯ major triad, i.e., an implied C7-type altered sound) resolving to F major. Explicitly named as the generalizable move: **take a found device and mold/retarget it to a new context rather than using it only in its original spot** — "you've taken one idea and made it into another idea," directly growing usable vocabulary.

**Explicit meta-point — composed lines are disposable, the process is the point (~19:30-20:30):** Larsen states directly that none of the four composed 2-5-1 lines above are things he intends to actually play verbatim while soloing — the goal was never to bank memorized two-bar licks. The goal is rehearsing *the act of finding a good way to use a small fragment* so that the same searching-and-evaluating process is already trained and available live, in time, over a real tune, rather than only ever encountering a new idea for the first time mid-solo. Stated follow-up practice step: once a fragment feels solid composed at leisure, take it to a slow/medium-tempo turnaround or a well-known standard and work it in time, checking whether it actually sits well against everything else already in the playing.

**Closing — personal framing, not a universal claim (~20:30-21:15):** Larsen explicitly frames analyzing+composing as *his* approach, developed partly because he started learning jazz relatively late (in his 20s), and states a broader industry critique alongside it: jazz pedagogy tends to stop at surface-level identification ("is this a scale, is this an arpeggio") without going deeper into *how the melody is actually constructed* — he argues the deeper melodic analysis is undertaught and is where the real reusable building blocks live. Also mentions (not pulled into routing — it's a promotional pointer, not a technique) a weekly Instagram lick series where he posts unlabeled licks, discusses them in comments, then publishes his own written analysis a day or two later, covering groupings/arpeggios/enclosures in the same style as this video.
