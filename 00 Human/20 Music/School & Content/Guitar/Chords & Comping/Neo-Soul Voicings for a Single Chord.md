# Neo-Soul Voicings for a Single Chord

Part of [[Guitar Positions]]. A **catalog of 14 distinct guitar voicings for one chord** (worked on **Cm7**), the minor-chord sequel to the same channel's earlier major-chord video (7 voicings over **E♭maj7** — referenced directly in this lesson but not yet its own page in the vault). Where [[Chord Voicings]] states the theory ("chords are sounds, not spellings" — reduce any symbol to its category and raid the available substitutes), this page is a single worked instance taken to an extreme: **14 different fretboard shapes, same chord, same two fixed notes (♭3 and ♭7) holding the identity together while everything else — root, 5th — gets freely swapped for color (9, 11).** Distinct from the blues-rhythm-guitar notes on this same [[Guitar Positions]] list ([[9th and 13th Chord Voicings for Blues Rhythm]], [[Guide-Tone Diads for Blues Rhythm Guitar]]) — those build a movable I-IV-V comping *system*; this page is a single static chord explored for maximum voicing variety and ornamented for **neo-soul** color rather than moved around a progression.

## The rule that generates all 14: only ♭3 and ♭7 are fixed

Stated directly and repeatedly: **every one of these 14 shapes is a "real" voicing of Cm7 only if it includes the ♭3 and the ♭7** — those two notes are non-negotiable (matches [[Chord Voicings]]'s guide-tone priority: "the 3rd and the 7th... lose either and the chord stops being itself"). Everything else is optional and swappable:
- **Root** — droppable (rootless voicings), or usable as a placeholder to be swapped out.
- **5th** — the most commonly swapped-out note in this lesson, usually replaced by the **9th** or the **11th** for extra color, echoing [[Chord Voicings]]'s "5th is the first thing to go."
- **9th / 11th** — the two extensions used throughout as substitutes for the dropped root or 5th.

The generative move used across most of the 14 voicings: **take a plain Cm7/Cm9/Cm11 shape and ask which non-essential note (root or 5) can be swapped for 9 or 11 to make it richer**, while keeping ♭3/♭7 untouched.

## The 14 voicings

Notes listed **bottom string to top string** as demonstrated (Cm7 = C E♭ G B♭; extensions D = 9, F = 11):

1. **Cm9 (rootless), 5 on bottom:** 5 – ♭3 – ♭7 – 9. First voicing of the lesson — rootless, replaced with the 9th for color; stated as a good default when moving through voicings quickly ("really not bad at all to replace the root").
2. **Cm(9,♭3-top), ♭7 on bottom:** ♭7 – 5 – 9 – ♭3. One of Jared's all-time favorites — **half step on top between the 9 and the ♭3** (D→E♭), called "gorgeous."
3. **Cm7, classic root-position:** root – 5 – ♭7 – ♭3. "The classic stable root position, most common voicing of minor seven you might find on the guitar" (not an open-string voicing). Has an optional **non-static ornamentation** riding on top — see Embellishments below; the ornament is *not* counted as its own voicing.
4. **Cm7 inversion (reached via a Hendrix-style chromatic slide from voicing 3):** ♭3 – ♭7 – root – 5. "A standard inversion of the 1-3-5-7 in a different order."
5. **Cm11 (barred at fret 13):** ♭3 – ♭7 – root – 11. Voicing 4 with its top note (5) taken away and replaced by the 11, barred instead of fretted individually. Stated explicitly as a *separate* voicing from #4, not a variant of it.
6. **Cm11 (same shape used in the companion E♭maj7 video, reinterpreted):** root – 11 – ♭7 – ♭3. Has an optional embellishment (doubling the 11, dropping the ♭3) — see Embellishments below; also not counted as its own voicing.
7. **Cm7 (top-four-strings, all one fret; derived from a standard root-position shape by moving the low root note up an octave to the top):** ♭7 – ♭3 – 5 – root.
8. **Cm9 (rootless), derived from #7 by swapping the top root for the 9:** ♭7 – ♭3 – 5 – 9.
9. **Cm11, read as an "F7sus4" shape (root position of F dominant-7-sus-4 doubles as this Cm11 when C is treated as the root):** 11 – root – ♭3 – ♭7 (bottom to top). If the bottom note (11) is moved up a whole step instead, the same fingering becomes a plain Cm7 inversion with the 5 in that slot instead of the 11 — same relationship as #4→#5.
10. **Cm11 (all four middle strings, fret 8):** 11 – ♭7 – ♭3 – 5.
11. **Cm11 ("one of my favorite minor-11 voicings, probably the most common"):** root – ♭7 – ♭3 – 11 (top). **Whole step on top** between ♭3 and 11 (E♭→F) — the whole-step counterpart to voicing #2's half-step-on-top.
12. **Cm11, read as an "E♭6/9" shape (the standard interpretation of that shape is E♭6/9; reinterpreted here with C as the root):** ♭3 – root – 11 – ♭7.
13. **Cm-with-11, read as a major-add9 shape (the standard interpretation of that fretting is E♭maj(add9); reinterpreted with C as the root — check it against [[Add Chords]]):** ♭3 – ♭7 – 5 – 11. Very open sound, explicitly checked against the essential-note rule before counting: "does it have the ♭3 and the ♭7 in it? ... cool."
14. **Cm9, final chord — standard root-position minor-9 shape, arrived at over a composed bass line resolving ♭7→1:** root – ♭3 – ♭7 – 9. Deliberately paired with voicing #1 as its mirror: #1 is the same shape family with the 5 on the bottom and no root (rootless); #14 has the root on the bottom and no 5 — "two separate voicings" even though closely related.

## Embellishment devices (deliberately *not* counted among the 14)

Jared draws a hard line between a **static voicing** (must contain ♭3 and ♭7, everything else fixed) and an **ornament riding on top of one** — useful distinction to keep the count honest, and the ornaments themselves are the actual "neo-soul" flavor signature:

- **Non-static top-note slur (on voicing #3):** put a whole step **above** the top note on the 2nd string, then lift the fretting (middle) finger to bar that string — a movable ornament layered on a fixed chord shape rather than a new chord.
- **Doubled-11 embellishment (on voicing #6):** double the 11 (it appears twice in the shape) and drop the ♭3 out entirely for that one embellished hit — described as **"a quintessential element of the neo-soul guitar sound"** despite temporarily breaking the ♭3/♭7-required rule, because it's explicitly an ornament, not a "real" voicing.
- **General neo-soul color principle stated directly:** *"a big part of the flavor of this sound is adding in ornamentations — specifically slurs or trails on the tops of these chords."* Neo-soul, in Jared's own working definition, is "rich and extended jazz chord voicings played in a genre that's more groove/beat-based (R&B, hip-hop/lo-fi) rather than swung jazz" — the *harmony* is borrowed straight from jazz extended-chord vocabulary (this page, [[Extended Chords]]), the *ornamentation* (slurred/trailing top notes) is what marks it as neo-soul rather than straight jazz comping.

## Build order / how to use this catalog

1. Learn all 14 shapes for one chord quality (worked here on Cm7/Cm9/Cm11) — the video plays through them once slowly in a fixed order to demonstrate each one clearly.
2. Notice which note was swapped to generate each voicing from a simpler one (root→9, 5→9, 5→11, root→11) — that's the transferable skill, not the specific 14 shapes.
3. **Play them in any order and improvise** — the fixed sequence in the video is just one arrangement Jared liked, not a required order; composing/improvising through the set is the stated end goal ("if our interest is in actually composing, improvising is hugely beneficial... we find new ideas much quicker").
4. Apply the same swap-in-9/11-for-root/5 logic to other chord qualities — this is chord-quality #2 in an ongoing series (major 7 already done, minor 7 here).
