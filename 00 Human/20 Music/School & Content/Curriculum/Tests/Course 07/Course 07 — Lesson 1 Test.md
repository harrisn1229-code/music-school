> **Q1.** **A7, D7 and E7** — `A A A A / D D A A / E D A E`
> *Also accepted:* the same twelve bars written out longhand
>
> **Q2.** **I7, IV7, V7 — three four-bar phrases**
> The grouping is what makes the AAB lyric shape in Lesson 2 fit the form.
>
> **Q3.** **The turnaround, leading back to the top**
> That's Lesson 2's material — it's what makes the form circle rather than stop.
>
> **Q4.** **Because blues doesn't follow major-key functional harmony**
> The scale isn't where these chords come from, so "the major scale doesn't contain them" isn't an objection.
>
> **Q5.** **It's the home sound rather than a chord needing resolution**
> In functional harmony a dominant 7th is tension aimed at a tonic. In blues it *is* the tonic, so it sits still.
>
> **Q6.** **Colour**
> Not tension. That's the whole reallocation this lesson is making.
>
> **Q7.** **Bar 2 moves to the IV instead of staying on I**
> *Also accepted:* the IV arrives early
>
> **Q8.** **It's a separate form in its own right, not a truncated 12-bar — "Key to the Highway"**
> Treating it as a shortened 12-bar gets the phrase structure wrong.
>
> **Q9.** **i7, iv7, v7 — but the V frequently stays dominant**
> Often with a ♭VI at bar 9. The V stays dominant because the leading tone is still wanted pulling home.
>
> **Q10.** **Why the blues scale works over everything · why the ♭3 isn't wrong · why the form circles**
> *Rubric (5):* names three consequences, one point each (3) · connects at least two of them back to blues not being functional (2)
> If the dominant 7th is home rather than tension, three things follow. The blues scale works over all three chords because it isn't derived from any of them — it's imposed over the whole form. The ♭3 against a major 3rd isn't an error because there's no functional grammar being violated, only friction being used deliberately. And the form circles rather than resolving, because a tonic that's already a dominant 7th never fully settles — which is exactly why it needs a turnaround to hand it back to bar 1.
