# Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Jazz Harmony]]. Jack Ruch's practice method for turning a transcribed solo into reusable improvisation vocabulary rather than a memorized part — the applied *use* of a transcription, as opposed to [[Transcribing and Playing by Ear]]'s note on the ear-only mechanics of getting the notes off the record in the first place. Ties into [[Song Practice]] as a concrete workflow for the "learn a solo by ear" stage of practice.

## The core method
1. **Learn the solo by ear, in full, first** — chords and melody/solo line, all by ear. This alone builds vocabulary and ear, but on its own isn't enough — "it's not enough to just simply learn a solo by ear, you've got to get under the hood."
2. **Chunk it** — break the solo into small, manageable sections (a phrase, a chord or two at a time), rather than analyzing it as one continuous line.
3. **Analyze each chunk against the chord underneath it** — work out which scale, arpeggio, or harmonic device the original player is drawing from over that specific chord.
4. **Reduce each chunk to one named "concept"** — a single reusable idea (a scale choice, an arpeggio substitution, a target note) rather than the literal notes played. The concept, not the lick, is the thing worth keeping.
5. **Use the set of per-chord concepts as a template** — improvise a new solo of your own over the same changes, applying the concepts (not the original licks) at each chord. Comping distinct concepts to the same underlying chords is what turns a single transcription into a whole practice session's worth of new lines.
6. **Layer alternate concepts over the same changes** — Ruch demonstrates two (sometimes three) different valid concepts for the *same* chord across repeats of the form (e.g. blues/flat-5 phrasing vs. melodic-minor vocabulary over the same i chord — see below), reinforcing that a chord doesn't have one "correct" scale, just a menu of concepts worth having ready.
— Jack Ruch, [[How I Approach Practicing]]

## Why this works
Once fundamental vocabulary is under the fingers, what's actually needed from a great solo isn't the transcription itself but "a push, a nudge, a suggestion of where to go with something" — a seed concept that plants an idea you can then run with in your own direction, rather than a part to reproduce verbatim. — Jack Ruch, [[How I Approach Practicing]]

## A more literal alternative — verbatim transcription and stitching
Not every video in this vein reduces a transcription all the way to a named concept first. [[How to Add Bebop Jazz Vocabulary to the Blues]] teaches three real bebop saxophone licks (Cannonball Adderley, Charlie Parker, Jimmy Forrest) essentially **verbatim** — mapped to a matching structural chunk of a jazz-blues 12-bar form — then stitches all three together into one continuous chorus, seamlessly transitioning between them. Stated method: "transcribe different players, then pull the solos apart and combine them with other things you know." A useful complementary entry point before enough vocabulary exists to abstract into concepts — full breakdown on [[Bebop Vocabulary]] § *From videos*. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]], [[Blues Form and Harmony]] — Jack Ruch, [[How to Add Bebop Jazz Vocabulary to the Blues]]

**A second, even more direct instance of the same non-abstracted method.** [[How Grant Green Played Jazz Blues]] transcribes two Grant Green solo excerpts (over the same B♭ jazz-blues form) and teaches the licks essentially as-played, without reducing them to named concepts first — but states the same underlying payoff as this page's core method: "over time it starts to blend in and become a part of your playing and starts to pop out in your improvising." A recommended prerequisite is stated too — learn the arpeggios of the underlying chords first, and this style of vocabulary turns up naturally inside them (→ [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]). Full breakdowns on [[🎵 GRANT GREEN - Miss Ann's Tempo]] and [[🎵 GRANT GREEN - Number One Green Street]]. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]], [[Turnarounds]], [[Chord Substitution]], [[Blues Form and Harmony]] — Jack Ruch, [[How Grant Green Played Jazz Blues]]

**A third instance, this one making the "and make them your own" step explicit and demonstrated live rather than just implied.** [[Transcribing Licks And Making Them Your Own]] is a direct methodology companion to the entry above — same channel, same two Grant Green tunes, overlapping excerpts — but its focus flips: instead of teaching the licks as-played, Ruch names the harmonic device behind each phrase (an altered scale, a Lydian-dominant color, a diminished-7 arpeggio for a ♭9 sound, a 2-5-1-of-the-target-chord shape) and then **plays his own reworked version of it live**, closing the loop this page's core method describes in the abstract. Two concrete "make it your own" devices come out of it that aren't named elsewhere on this page: **resolving the same lick to different chord tones than the original**, and **transposing a fragment by a fixed interval (a half step or a minor third) to a related chord**, the latter identified as a genuine recurring Grant Green habit, not just a Ruch teaching trick — new device added to [[Connecting Licks Into Longer Lines]]. Also states the same "learn the chord progression first, for context" principle as [[Transcribing and Playing by Ear]]'s real-time transcription workflow. → [[Connecting Licks Into Longer Lines]], [[The ii-V-I]], [[Diminished Chords]], [[Turnarounds]] — Jack Ruch, [[Transcribing Licks And Making Them Your Own]]

## Worked example — "Delilah" (Clifford Brown / Max Roach)
Applied to a bluesy AABA jazz solo over F♯ minor — full chord-by-chord concept map on [[🎵 CLIFFORD BROWN & MAX ROACH - Delilah]]. The concepts pulled out (each usable independently of this tune): blues/flat-5 phrasing over a minor i ([[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]]), a relative-major arpeggio over a minor chord for a minor-9 color, an upper-structure diminished-7 arpeggio off the 3rd of an altered dominant, melodic-minor-family substitutions (melodic minor over the i, the altered scale over V, harmonic minor as a "major-7-over-minor" color, Lydian dominant on a half-step-above approach chord) — see [[Scale Modes Reference]] § From videos and [[Altered Chords]] § From videos for the transferable, tune-agnostic versions of these devices.

## Worked example — "D Natural Blues" (Wes Montgomery)
A second, shorter application of the same method — this time applied to a jazz icon's playing rather than Ruch's own transcription-derived variation, and restricted to just the I (D7) and IV (G7) chords of a 12-bar jazz blues rather than a full AABA form. Full passage-by-passage concept map on [[🎵 WES MONTGOMERY - D Natural Blues]]. Concepts pulled out: D7 (Mixolydian) scale into direct chord-tone targeting on the IV change, a chromatic walk-down (5th→3rd) into a D7♭9 arpeggio, the whole-half (tonic) diminished scale used as connective tissue bridging IV back to I (not a static "correct scale"), a C major 7 arpeggio over D7 for its upper-extension stack (♭7-9-11-13) followed by D minor pentatonic as a bluesy cadential move into the IV, and an A minor 7 ("v-minor") arpeggio over D7 for its own extended-color stack (5-♭7-9-11). Same step-5 payoff as the Delilah example: after naming each concept, Ruch immediately plays his own line built from it. → [[Scale Modes Reference]], [[Diminished Chords]], [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]], [[Chord-Tone Soloing]], [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]] — Jack Ruch, [[Wes Montgomery Blues Lesson D Natural Blues]]

## Worked example — "Work Song" (Nat Adderley, Wes Montgomery guitar solo)
A fourth, compact instance of the same underlying goal — reduce a short real solo to reusable devices rather than a part to memorize — this time from Nathan Borton, over a Nat Adderley original (*Work Song*, 1960, key of A♭) rather than a blues. Distinct from the Ruch and Larsen examples above: Borton doesn't run the full "chunk → analyze → name → replay as your own line" pipeline explicitly, but the shape is the same — three short excerpts (bars 2, 8, 12) each reduced to one portable device. Full passage-by-passage breakdown on [[🎵 NAT ADDERLEY - Work Song]]. Devices pulled out: a **relative-major/relative-minor arpeggio swap** (an A♭ major 7 arpeggio over F minor 7 implies a 9th; reversed, an F minor 9 arpeggio over A♭ major 7 implies a 13th — one shape, either direction, because the two chords are relative major/minor), a **passing-tone-legitimized "wrong" shape** over a V-I resolution (an F minor(major7) arpeggio that "doesn't technically work" over E♭7 until its ♭3 is reframed as a passing tone resolving to the 9th — stacking a 9th, a 13th, and a ♭9 into one short phrase), and a **movable 4-note guitar shape (C-B♭-G-F)**, shared with Grant Green, portable across any diatonic chord in the key. → [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]], [[Extended Chords]] — Nathan Borton, [[How To Use Chord Extensions In Your Solos!]]

## Worked example — "Take the Coltrane" (John McLaughlin, F blues)
A sixth instance, Jens Larsen. Full breakdown → [[John McLaughlin - This Is Not Like Other Approaches]]. Distinct focus: an improviser whose lines are built mostly from scale fragments and chromatic connectors rather than arpeggios, analyzed for HOW he stays musical without leaning on chord-tone-outlining vocabulary. Devices: **harmonic pull-ahead** — playing content belonging to the *next* chord before the bar line arrives (e.g. an F7 bar already sounding like G7 Lydian dominant), offered as an alternate reading of an apparently-outside note as an anticipated resolution instead; **triad-for-sus substitution** — an F major triad over G7, or B♭ major triad over C7, both spelling that root's 7sus4 sound without naming it that way; **motif displacement** — restating an identical short motif shifted to a different beat than its first statement, varying rhythm without varying melodic content; and a **tritone-substitution ii-V reading** (F♯7→B7 heard as the tritone-sub ii-V resolving to B♭, not named in F). → [[Pentatonic Superimposition and Side-Slipping]] — Jens Larsen, [[John McLaughlin - This Is Not Like Other Approaches]]

## Worked example — "Blackbird" (Kenny Burrell, on Jimmy Smith's *Standards*)
A fifth instance, Jens Larsen again, distinct from the others in isolating **tension sources other than harmony/note-choice**. Full breakdown → [[Kenny Burrell - How to Make the Blues Sound Outside]]. Two devices not covered by the other worked examples on this page: **rhythmic-feel contrast as an independent tension axis** — a phrase shifts from straight/medium swing eighths to a noticeably harder shuffled swing feel with the exact same notes, generating surprise purely from rhythmic character rather than pitch content ("not about altered dominants or extra chords... it's really about other aspects of music"); and **one-note, one-rhythm motif development** — a single repeated pitch (the 9th) played with a simple 16th-note rhythmic motif creates contrast against a medium-swing backdrop with zero harmonic movement at all. Also reinforces this page's general standing point about **long-arc phrasing**: ideas stretched and developed across several bars rather than resolved quickly, framed as a mark of a strong improviser independent of any specific vocabulary device. → [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]], [[Groove and Microtiming]] — Jens Larsen, [[Kenny Burrell - How to Make the Blues Sound Outside]]

## Worked example — "Billie's Bounce" (George Benson)
A third instance of the same chunk-analyze-name-the-device method, this time from a different teacher (Jens Larsen rather than Jack Ruch) applied to a 12-bar jazz blues in F. Full passage-by-passage concept map on [[🎵 GEORGE BENSON - Billie's Bounce]]. The concepts pulled out: mixing bebop-arpeggio language and blues-double-stop language inside a single unbroken phrase (no seam between vocabularies), a **half-diminished (not full dim7) arpeggio off a dominant chord's own 3rd** as a recurring signature device ([[Diminished Chords]] § From videos), treating a ii-chord as a **plain triad plus chromatic leading notes** rather than thinking in a parent scale — explicitly named as what produces bigger, less-predictable interval jumps while still always landing on a chord tone ([[Chord-Tone Soloing]] § From videos) — a bar-1 bebop cliché answered by its own melodic inverse on the next chord (call-and-response), and a well-worn F-minor blues cliché kept fresh by resequencing its notes rather than retiring it. Distinct from the Wes Montgomery and Clifford Brown/Max Roach examples above in **not reducing every passage to a single named "concept" before replaying it** — Larsen narrates the device in place on the recording rather than performing Step 5's "play it back as your own line" step explicitly, closer in that respect to the "more literal" verbatim-transcription approach described above. → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]], [[Diminished Chords]], [[Bebop Vocabulary]], [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]] — Jens Larsen, [[George Benson - This is The Best Jazz Blues Solo I know]]

## A composing-focused variant — Jens Larsen's analyze-then-compose pipeline
A second, independently-arrived-at version of this page's core method, from a different teacher (Jens Larsen). Same two-stage shape — break a source line into small reusable fragments, then deliberately reuse them — but two things differ from Ruch's method above: Larsen doesn't reduce each chunk to one named "concept," he stays closer to the literal fragment and composes transposed/modified variants of it directly; and he adds a **from-scratch composing mode with no source lick at all** — start from a bare diatonic arpeggio (no transcription to analyze first) and compose original lines with it, which Ruch's concept-extraction method doesn't cover.
1. **Analyze a source phrase into its small melodic building blocks** — worked over a Charlie Parker Omnibook rhythm-changes fragment, chunked into five pieces and judged individually rather than treated as equally useful: a chromatic passing run (judged least interesting), a four-note pentatonic fragment (flagged as exactly the *kind* of small, high-frequency fragment worth hunting for), a plain scale run (flagged as the least useful piece in the whole line), a chromatic enclosure into the next bar's downbeat, and a descending arpeggio + 6th-interval leap (see [[Bebop Vocabulary]] § *From videos* for the full breakdown of that last fragment). **Stated principle: a small bag of small pieces beats either a large bag of small pieces or a small bag of large pieces** — small, recombinable fragments are what actually pay off live, where long memorized licks don't.
2. **Compose new lines by transplanting a fragment onto a new context and asking "what's the next thing I hear?"** — rather than planning a full line up front, play the borrowed fragment into the new progression and let the ear supply the next note-choice at a time. Recommends writing early attempts down on paper specifically so a line that doesn't work can be diagnosed afterward, the same diagnostic step used when analyzing the source line itself.
3. **A from-scratch composing mode with no source lick** — starting only from a bare diatonic arpeggio (the arpeggio off the 3rd of a ii chord, over a 2-5-1), compose several original lines from it: straight descending arpeggio into existing altered vocabulary; the same arpeggio approached via a pedal point instead of starting cold; the same idea with the rhythm changed (straight eighths → a triplet); and the same idea combined with a *retargeted* enclosure (an enclosure originally built for one target note reworked to approach a different one — the generalizable move named explicitly: "you've taken one idea and made it into another idea").
4. **The composed lines are disposable — the process is the point.** Stated directly: none of the composed 2-5-1 lines are meant to be played verbatim while soloing. The goal is rehearsing the *act* of finding a good way to use a small fragment, so the same searching-and-evaluating process is already trained and available live, rather than only ever meeting a new idea for the first time mid-solo. Follow-up step once a fragment feels solid: take it to a slow/medium-tempo turnaround or a known standard and work it in time.
5. **Analyzing + composing is framed explicitly as a taste-development tool**, not just a vocabulary-acquisition one — judging "does this work, do I like this" on every composed attempt is what builds a personal sound. Worked example: on the Parker fragment's flat-13→3rd resolution, Larsen states his own taste diverges from what Parker actually plays (a plain scale-step resolution) and he'd substitute a fully altered line instead (flat-13 → sharp-9 → flat-9 → root) — noticing the source, then consciously choosing a different color rather than treating the transcription as gospel.
— Jens Larsen, [[Jens Larsen - Overlooked SKILLS for Learning Jazz - CREATIVITY is in your Brain]]

## A style-trait variant — extracting identity traits rather than per-chord concepts
Not every solo-analysis video reduces the material to a chord-by-chord concept map. [[🎵 KURT ROSENWINKEL - Nefertiti (Ballad Session)]] takes a different cut through the same underlying goal (reverse-engineer reusable vocabulary from a real solo): instead of naming one concept per chord, Jens Larsen pulls out a small set of **recurring style traits** across four excerpts of a Kurt Rosenwinkel solo — very wide range inside a short phrase, a melodic idea that survives a chord change instead of resetting, lines built from stacked arpeggios/triads rather than one scale, and tight motivic restraint (a small note pool developed without sounding obvious). The chord-by-chord concept map still applies within each excerpt, but the headline takeaway is a *player identity checklist* rather than a per-chord substitution menu — a useful complementary lens when the goal is "sound more like this player" rather than "solve this specific turnaround." Rosenwinkel's own stated practice method behind the "melody survives the chord change" trait is documented separately on [[Never-Ending Scale Exercise]]. → [[Solo Dynamics and Arc]], [[Motivic Development]], [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]], [[Harmonized Scale Triads for Soloing]] — Kurt Rosenwinkel via Jens Larsen, [[Kurt Rosenwinkel - This Is What Makes His Style Unique]]

## A collection-first variant — sort every phrase into a lick journal by device type, then plug and chug
A simpler entry point than the per-chunk concept-naming methods above: rather than analyzing each chunk against its specific underlying chord (Ruch's step 3), just **sort every extracted phrase into a "lick journal" by which device it is**, independent of which tune or chord it came from — all the 5-1 phrases together, all the CESH phrases together, all the altered phrases together, and so on. Stated reasoning: organized-by-function vocabulary is dramatically easier to digest and easier to get into the fingers than vocabulary still tangled up inside one transcribed melody, because a whole category can be **"plugged and chugged"** over any changes that call for it rather than searched for one phrase at a time. Demonstrated on a full phrase-by-phrase breakdown of Charlie Parker's "Confirmation" head melody — full breakdown → [[🎵 CHARLIE PARKER - Confirmation]], [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §*From videos*. Complementary rather than competing with the per-chord concept method above: the two-stage version (concept-name each chunk, *then* file it into a category bucket) combines both. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]], [[🎵 CHARLIE PARKER - Confirmation]] — Nathan Borton, [[Learn Charlie Parker's Phrasing Secrets... with CONFIRMATION!]]

## A practice-routine variant — turn the extracted techniques into timed drills, not just concepts
Every other variant on this page ends the extraction with a named concept or trait; this one (from a live master class, not a per-tune video breakdown) is distinct in going one step further — it's a **complete workflow for turning a listening session into an actual daily practice schedule**, extracted from Nathan Borton's own teacher, Randy Napoleon, rather than Borton's own transcribed solos.
1. **Play the model recording and have the target-technique observations be concrete and nameable, not vague.** Explicitly ruled out: "he plays fast, I want to play fast." Ruled in: specific, breakdown-able qualities — in the worked session, a room of students named **movement** (the phrasing "moved forward and back," never sitting in one rhythmic spot), **harmonic motion** (passing chords), and **melody harmonization** (chording the tune the way a piano player would).
2. **Design one concrete, systematic drill per named technique — an extreme, "do it on everything" version first, a selective/musical version second.** Two worked drills, both following the same two-stage shape: (a) **melody harmonization** — first harmonize *every single note* of the melody, deliberately unmusical ("junk-junk-junk"), to prove you can do it at all; then narrow to a specific harmonic target — only the ii–V's, only ♯IV walk-downs, only dominant-to-tonic resolutions, only the backdoor dominants — leaving the rest as a single line. (b) **passing chords** — chromatically side-slip (half-step above or below) into *every* chord change as the extreme pass, then narrow the same side-slip to just specific slots (e.g. only the ii–V's). A third drill for a technique that isn't chord-shaped at all: **movement** — alternate 2 bars of the melody played strictly to a metronome with 2 bars of free improvisation, forcing the "never sitting still" rhythmic-freedom quality to be practiced deliberately instead of left to feel.
3. **Time-box each technique against your actual daily practice budget**, rather than working all of them unstructured. Worked example: a student with 4 hours/day splits time across the extracted techniques — e.g. 2 hours on melody harmonization (1 hour harmonize-everything, 1 hour harmonize-selectively), with the remaining time going to the next technique (movement) — and reallocates hours toward the next technique as comfort with the first one increases.
4. **Write the plan down** — the explicit closing instruction, so a session is executed from a written allocation rather than improvised from scratch each day.

This differs from Ruch's/Larsen's per-chord "concept" methods above in scope: instead of reducing a solo to a chord-by-chord vocabulary menu, it extracts whole-performance-level *techniques* (harmonization, chromatic passing motion, rhythmic freedom) and answers the scheduling question ("how many hours on which technique today") that the other variants leave implicit. Full drill mechanics and the harmonization/passing-chord specifics also live on [[Chord Melody Arranging]] § *From videos*; the day-to-day spaced-practice rule this same video teaches lives on [[The Five Pillars of Jazz Guitar Practice]]. → [[Chord Melody Arranging]], [[Passing Chords]], [[The Five Pillars of Jazz Guitar Practice]], [[Song Practice]] — Nathan Borton, [Master Class: "How To Get The Most Out Of Your Practice Time"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HHzjs-wU2sw)

## A self-analysis variant — practicing by contrasting two solos over the same tune, and the "listen → predict what comes next" loop
Every other variant on this page analyzes *someone else's* recorded solo. This one, from Jens Larsen, turns the same underlying discipline (reduce playing to reusable, nameable devices rather than raw noodling) onto **your own live playing**, and answers a different problem than the other variants — not "how do I extract vocabulary from a great solo" but "how do I even practice soloing in a way that isn't just unstructured noodling with no visible progress," the way scales/exercises practice visibly is. Diagnosed failure state, named directly: if every chord gets its own disconnected idea with nothing threading them together, "that's not a solo, that's just a bunch of licks next to each other."
1. **Give soloing practice an explicit, nameable goal before you start** — Larsen's is melodicism, narrowed immediately to one drillable mechanism: [[Motivic Development|motivic development]]. A vague goal ("play more melodic") doesn't work as a practice target; a named mechanism does.
2. **Play two full solos back-to-back over the same tune and let the contrast do the teaching.** Solo 1 is the honest baseline — competently connect the harmony (arpeggios, target notes — see the [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] cross-reference below) but with no larger melodic thread linking one chord's idea to the next. Solo 2 is the fix applied: state one short motif (2–3 notes is plenty — an 8–10 note melody is "just a lot of calculating" to move through changes) and develop it continuously through a whole section, varying its **rhythm** and its **ending** rather than starting fresh on every chord, before deliberately pivoting to a new idea when it's run its course ("you don't want to stick to a motif too long"). → full bar-by-bar breakdown of both solos on [[Is Your Improvisation Practice a Waste of Time? (And How to Fix That)]].
3. **The transferable daily drill: after playing a phrase, listen back and ask "what should come next?"** — explicitly **not** a note-name or scale-name decision (Larsen rules out "now I want to hear F♯ diminished" as the wrong mental model). Think in terms of the sound/shape wanted next, then find it on the instrument. Concrete exercise version: take just the first few bars of a tune, state a simple motif, then keep changing how the phrase ends and the order of its notes — repeat until the listen-predict-play loop itself starts to feel automatic, independent of which tune it's practiced on.
4. **Have a strong harmonic overview of the tune before soloing on it, even one you've never played** — analyze it as functional devices (ii–V, turnaround, backdoor dominant) rather than a flat chord list; this is what let Larsen improvise convincingly on a jazz standard called on a gig that he'd never played before, cold, at tempo. Same "devices not isolated chords" principle as [[The Five Pillars of Jazz Guitar Practice]] Pillar 4.
5. **A concrete harmonic-connection device usable inside either solo (baseline or motif-developed): target only the notes that actually change between adjacent chords.** F major7 → F♯ diminished moves only two notes (F→F♯, E→E♭) — play exactly those two on the diminished chord to make the chord change audible without recalculating a whole new arpeggio; G minor7 → C7 moves only one note (F→E). Full worked instances → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] § *From videos*.

This differs from every other variant on this page in what it analyzes (your own real-time playing, not a transcription) and in what problem it solves (structuring practice time itself, not extracting vocabulary from a source solo) — but shares the page's core thesis that a solo should reduce to a small number of nameable, reusable devices rather than be treated as an unrepeatable, un-analyzable performance. → [[Motivic Development]], [[Chord-Tone Soloing]], [[The Five Pillars of Jazz Guitar Practice]], [[Song Practice]] — Jens Larsen, [[Is Your Improvisation Practice a Waste of Time? (And How to Fix That)]]
