# Bill Wolfer-The Man Behind MJ's "Billie Jean" CS-80

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z_AcRZcCcH0)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z_AcRZcCcH0)

## Synopsis

Primarily a history/provenance interview — Bill Wolfer, the actual creator of the CS-80 chord-stab sound already patch-documented in [[The Billie Jean Chord Stack - It's 4 Sounds!]], tells the origin story: he built the sound years earlier, on tour, then recreated it from memory when Michael Jackson specifically asked for it by name. Long-form and largely biographical (session history at Enino/Westlake, who played what on which demo, working alongside Greg Phillinganes and Rod Temperton) — honestly routed lightly, since most of the actual patch settings are already covered elsewhere; the one genuinely new, reusable piece is the sound's compositional origin.

## What's taught

- **The CS-80 chord sound's origin: recreating another artist's timbre from memory, on a different instrument, as a starting point rather than inventing from scratch.** Wolfer built the sound years before Thriller while on tour with Stevie Wonder — trying to recreate the string sound from Stevie's "Village Ghetto Land" (played on a Yamaha GX1, the CS-80's larger, non-portable sibling — "CS" reportedly stands for *Compact Synthesizer*, i.e. a GX1 in more portable form, with closely related voice architecture). He never fully nailed the recreation to his own satisfaction.
- **The specific orchestration idea behind the patch: use a synth's two independent voice paths to emulate a classic orchestral doubling move (strings + brass in unison), not to build two different chords.** The CS-80 is built as two complete, independently-voiced synthesizer "rows" (voice 1 and voice 2) stacked on one keyboard. Wolfer's approach was to build a brass-leaning voice on one row and a string-leaning voice on the other, playing the same notes together — directly modeling the traditional orchestral technique of doubling French horns with strings for a blended, "warmer than brass alone, brighter than strings alone" tutti color, translated onto a two-voice synth architecture.
- **Michael Jackson specifically remembered hearing this exact sound played live years earlier** (on the Triumph tour, during downtime/soundcheck noodling) and called Wolfer in by name specifically because of it — a case where a fairly private, half-finished, "never really happy with it" experiment ended up becoming one of the most recognizable synth textures in pop music once someone else heard it and remembered it.
