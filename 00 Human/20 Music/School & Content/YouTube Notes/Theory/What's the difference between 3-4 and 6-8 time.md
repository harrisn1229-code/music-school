# What's the difference between 3/4 and 6/8 time?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N4q2kBe82-o)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N4q2kBe82-o)

## Synopsis

A foundational clarification of why 3/4 and 6/8 aren't interchangeable despite both fitting the same six eighth-notes into a bar — the difference is entirely in HOW those notes group, not how many there are — plus the same simple/compound logic extended to 12/8 vs. two bars of 6/8, and 9/8 vs. a swung 3/4 ("jazz waltz").

## Techniques

- **The core distinction, stated precisely: 3/4 is simple TRIPLE time (3 strong beats, each dividing into 2), 6/8 is compound DUPLE time (2 strong beats, each dividing into 3) — same 6 eighth-notes, different grouping, genuinely different feel.** 3/4 reads as a waltz (clear 3-in-a-bar); 6/8 reads as a swinging, pendulum-like 2-in-a-bar. The key correction: 6/8 is NOT "six beats" — it has 2 STRONG beats, each worth 3 eighth notes. "America" (Bernstein, *West Side Story*) alternates 6/8 and 3/4 bar-to-bar specifically to create a felt speeding-up/slowing-down effect from the same notated tempo.
- **Tempo can perceptually blur the 3/4-vs-6/8 distinction, in both directions — a practical ear-training caution.** A genuinely 6/8 song played very slowly ("Gravity," John Mayer) can start to feel like 3/4, because the ear can't hold onto the 2-beat grouping across such a wide gap and defaults to counting each eighth note instead. Conversely, a genuinely 3/4 song at a fast tempo can start to feel like 6/8 (cited: genuine disagreement over "America," Simon & Garfunkel, readable either way). Takeaway: at extreme tempos, "which time signature is this" can become a real, unresolvable ambiguity rather than a factual question with one right answer.
- **A precise, previously-undocumented mechanism for a polyrhythm already in this vault**: "Daydreaming" (Radiohead) — the existing [[18 Rhythms you should know]] entry cites this as a generic "3-over-2 hemiola" example; this video specifies the ACTUAL mechanism — the piano's right hand plays in 3/4 while the left hand simultaneously plays in 6/8 (i.e., for every 3 right-hand beats, the left hand plays 2) — written on the page in one unified time signature (3/4) since polyrhythms aren't notated with two simultaneous signatures.
- **The same simple/compound logic extended one level further: 12/8 vs. two bars of 6/8, and when each notation is actually used.** 12/8 is compound QUADRUPLE time (4 strong beats, each dividing into 3) — at fast tempos it reads as a swung/shuffled 4/4 (matches the vault's existing 12/8-as-shuffle framing); at slow tempos it's functionally identical to two bars of 6/8, and which notation a transcriber picks is genuinely a matter of preference (cited: Lou Reed's "Perfect Day," transcribed as either 6/8 or 12/8 depending on the source, without a clearly "correct" answer).
- **The 3/4-vs-9/8 "jazz waltz" distinction, and why it's rarely notated the "technically correct" way.** A swung 3/4 ("Still Crazy After All These Years," "You're So Vain") is technically compound triple time — 9/8, three strong beats each dividing into 3 — but in practice these are almost always written as plain 3/4 with a "swing the rhythm" instruction instead, because it's easier to read AND gives the performer more freedom over exactly how much swing to apply, rather than locking a specific triplet subdivision into the notation. **A separate, unrelated use of 9/8** worth not confusing with the jazz-waltz case: grouping the same 9 eighth-notes UNEVENLY (e.g. 4+5 instead of 3+3+3) turns 9/8 into a genuine odd/complex meter rather than a compound-triple "clean" 9/8 — cited example: "Seven Days" (Sting), grouped 4+5.
