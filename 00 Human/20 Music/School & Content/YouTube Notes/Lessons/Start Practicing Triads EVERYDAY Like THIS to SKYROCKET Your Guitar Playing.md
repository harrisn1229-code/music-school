# Start Practicing Triads EVERYDAY Like THIS to SKYROCKET Your Guitar Playing

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ui6qUcvaKU0)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ui6qUcvaKU0)

## Synopsis
Daniel Seriff's three-stage method for building triads on the top three strings (G, B, high E) into usable fretboard vocabulary, worked entirely in the key of C major. Stage one drills the three closed-voicing shapes (root position, 1st inversion, 2nd inversion) across all three triad qualities that occur naturally in a major key (major, minor, diminished), fixed at one neck position so the ear locks onto how lowering the 3rd (and then the 5th) transforms a shape. Stage two takes each of those three shapes and walks it through the full diatonic set of a major scale — all seven triads (I ii iii IV V vi vii°) in a single inversion, one octave-plus stretch of neck per inversion type. Stage three is the payoff and, per Seriff, "the Special Sauce of the pros": apply the shapes to a real chord progression (I–V–vi–IV, i.e. C–G–Am–F) not by picking one inversion and jumping around, but by always grabbing whichever of the three shapes puts the next chord physically closest to the one just played — worked three times, starting from three different neck positions (low around fret 5, middle around fret 8–10, high around fret 12–14), to show the same voice-leading logic reproduces cleanly anywhere on the neck. This is the same channel and near-identical lesson shape as [[Copy This Triad Practice Routine, It'll 10X Your Guitar Playing]] (same three shapes, same diatonic-quality formula, same "closest shape wins" voice-leading principle) but worked in C rather than G, with a different worked progression (I–V–vi–IV rather than the full diatonic run) and three fully fingered example positions rather than one 5-fret-per-inversion layout — the two videos reinforce and cross-check each other rather than duplicating.

## What's taught
- **Stage 1 — the three triad qualities in each of three inversions, key of C, strings G-B-E (fret numbers low-to-high string, i.e. G string–B string–high E string):**
  - **Root position** (root is the lowest note): **C major = 5-5-3** (root-3rd-5th). **C minor = 5-4-3** — lower the 3rd (B string) one fret. **C diminished = 5-4-2** — also lower the 5th (E string) one fret from there.
  - **1st inversion** (3rd is the lowest note, order 3rd-5th-root): **C major = 9-8-8**. **C minor = 8-8-8** — lower the 3rd (G string) one fret. **C diminished = 8-7-8** — also lower the 5th (B string) one fret.
  - **2nd inversion** (5th is the lowest note, order 5th-root-3rd; visually "part of the D shape moved up the neck"): **C minor = 12-13-11**, **C diminished = 11-13-11** (flatted 5th, root, flatted 3rd). The major version isn't separately stated in the video — by the same "lower the 3rd for minor" logic run in reverse, it computes to **12-13-12** (raise the E string back a half step from the minor shape); flagged as computed, not a literal quote.
  - Practice suggestion: drill transitions major→minor→diminished→back within one inversion/position before moving to the next inversion, so the ear tracks exactly what each single-fret shift does to the chord's color.
- **Stage 2 — walk each of the three inversions through the full diatonic set of C major (C D E F G A B, giving chord qualities I major, ii minor, iii minor, IV major, V major, vi minor, vii° diminished — same fixed formula as [[Copy This Triad Practice Routine, It'll 10X Your Guitar Playing]]):**
  - **Root position run:** C major → D minor → E minor → F major → G major → A minor → B diminished → back to C major, climbing the neck one shape at a time on the G-B-E strings.
  - **1st inversion run:** same sequence; called out that it climbs high enough to want dropping an octave (12 frets) partway through — **A minor 1st inversion = 5-5-5** given explicitly as a mid-run reference point after the octave drop.
  - **2nd inversion run:** same sequence again, also octave-drops partway through — **F major 2nd inversion = 5-6-5** given explicitly.
  - Explicit challenge: play each inversion's full run as low on the neck as possible, then as high as possible, so the shapes get mapped across the *entire* fretboard, not just one comfortable zone.
- **Stage 3 — the "Special Sauce": voice-lead a real I–V–vi–IV progression (C–G–Am–F in C major) by always taking the closest available triad shape to the previous chord, instead of committing to one inversion for the whole progression** (explicitly contrasted with the "same inversion every chord" approach, which the video calls "not wrong" but not the best voice leading — the same closest-shape principle documented from a different worked progression on [[Chord Inversions]]). Worked three times, in three different starting positions:
  - **Position 1 (low, ~fret 3–5):** **C** root position **5-5-3** → **G** 1st inversion, located off the **high E string 3rd fret** (closest available G, versus a 2nd-fret-G-string option ruled out as too far) → **Am** 1st inversion **5-5-5** (the 2nd-fret-G-string A option was likewise ruled out as too far, so it jumps to the high-E-string-5th-fret zone instead) → **F** 2nd inversion **5-6-5**, reached by moving just the B string up a half step from the Am shape (F and Am differ by exactly one note — the 3rd, E→F). Called out directly as excellent voice leading precisely *because* it's the closest-shape choice at every change.
  - **Position 2 (middle, ~fret 7–10):** **C** 1st inversion **9-8-8** → **G**, the "little D-looking shape," **7-8-7**, found off the G on the B string's 8th fret → **Am 9-10-8** (closest A found on the B string's 10th fret) → **F 10-10-8**, again just the B string's note moved up a half step from the Am shape.
  - **Position 3 (high, ~fret 12–14):** **C** 2nd inversion (the "D-shape moved up," matching the computed **12-13-12** from Stage 1) → **G 12-12-10** → **Am**, "same inversion just up a whole step and make it minor," **14-13-12** → **F**, reached by raising the high E string's note — video says "up a step," but every other Am→F move in this same lesson is a half step (Am and F share two of three notes; the note that moves, E→F, is always a half step), so this is treated as loose phrasing rather than a literal whole step, computing to **F ≈ 14-13-13**.
  - Recommended follow-up practice: strum each position's progression once through, then arpeggiate it, then decorate it (see [[Chord Voicings]]'s decoration techniques from the companion video), and repeat the whole three-stage process in other keys and on other progressions, not just C and I–V–vi–IV.
