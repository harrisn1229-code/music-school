# Songs that use Line Cliché chord progressions

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qLHJ0mSqdmQ)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qLHJ0mSqdmQ)

## Synopsis

The general-purpose companion to Bennett's "Something" and "augmented climb" progression videos (both narrower, single-shape line clichés) — this one defines the device broadly (any chord, either voice, ascending or descending or both) and surveys it across Beatles, Motown/soul, James Bond, and jazz-standard repertoire, plus practical notes on how the device gets labeled on a chord chart and how long it can run before it stops working.

## Techniques

- **Definition**: take any chord (major or minor), pick its highest or lowest note, and move that single note by semitone steps while everything else in the chord holds still.
- **Descending examples**: "Michelle" (The Beatles) — Fm, lowest note steps down to D♭. "Butterflies and Hurricanes" (Muse) — Dm, top note steps down through six chords. "Try Some, Buy Some" (George Harrison) — doubles the device: one line cliché on the i chord (Gm), then immediately another on the iv chord (Cm).
- **Down-then-up**: "I Just Called to Say I Love You" (Stevie Wonder) — E♭m descends two steps then climbs back the same path to home.
- **Ascending, usually built off the 5th**: descending from a chord's 5th tends to collide with its other notes and sound unsatisfying, so the 5th is the degree where ascending is the natural choice instead. The James Bond theme — Em, the 5th (B) climbs to C then C♯, then falls back to C. "Hey Bulldog" (The Beatles) — Bm, top note climbs, then the same climb repeats on the IV chord (Em).
- **Jazz/American Songbook standards** ("My Funny Valentine," Rodgers & Hart; "In a Sentimental Mood," Duke Ellington — which stacks two line clichés, one on i immediately echoed by another on iv) made the device a genuine period cliché in the 1930s–50s.
- **The minor-major-7th "tell"**: a minor triad with a natural (major) 7th on top is a harshly dissonant chord rarely used on its own, but it's a very natural mid-step in a descending minor line cliché — spotting one in a song is a strong signal a line cliché is in play.
- **Chord-chart labeling**: line clichés are conventionally written as slash chords (Am, Am/G♯, Am/G, Am/F♯...) for readability, even though this is technically imprecise — the actual sounding chord at the "Am/G♯" step is a G♯ augmented triad, not literally an A-minor triad with G♯ in the bass. The imprecise shorthand survives because it visually communicates the descending-bass-line intent at a glance.
- **Practical length limit**: most line clichés run 4–6 chords before the ear runs out of satisfying new notes to track; pushing past 6–8 starts sounding aimless.
