# Clipping and Loudness

**What it is:** the modern loudness recipe — density stages → clipper → limiter, each doing the part it's transparent at, instead of one limiter doing everything and pumping.

## Hard vs soft clipping
- **Hard clip:** shears everything above the ceiling, touches nothing below. Odd harmonics, only on the shaved samples. Micro-transients (a few ms) are inaudible to remove — pure free headroom. Use for: peak control before a limiter ([[Gold Clip]], [[Gold Clip Track]]).
- **Soft clip:** a knee that rounds into the ceiling — audible warmth/saturation and rounded transients. It's a *tone*, not a headroom tool ([[kHs Clipper]] flavors, tape stages behave like this).

## The loudness stack (as used in [[Master Chain]] / [[3 Drums Bus|Drums Bus]])
1. **Density first** ([[Oxford Inflator]]): perceived loudness with zero GR — psychoacoustic, not dynamic. Effect 100% + ride input to the clip lights, *level-match the output* (the only honest way to judge it).
2. **Clip the peaks** (Gold Clip): shave 1–2 dB of micro-transient.
3. **Limit last** ([[Ozone 9 Maximizer]]): 2–3 dB GR max on the loudest section, ceiling −1.0 dBTP.
Each stage hands the next a smaller job; that division is why loud modern masters still sound open.

## Traps
- **Oversampling in clippers** can recreate overs on the way back down and silently eat the headroom you just made. Gold Clip's algorithm holds true sample peak — most others don't. Verify with a **sample-peak meter**, don't trust the label.
- Aliasing fear is overblown for transient clipping — transients mask it completely.
- Clip *earlier* too: kick/snare tracks ([[Gold Clip Track]]) so the drum bus and master barely work.

## Loudness targets (honest version)
- Streaming normalization (−14 LUFS) is **not a mixing target** — louder masters just get turned down; the only crime is smashed dynamics.
- Competitive: pop/trap ≈ −9 to −7 LUFS-I · band/organic ≈ −10 to −12 · ballads ≈ −11 to −12.
- Judge by ear at matched loudness against 2–3 reference tracks; push until it sounds *worse*, then back off.

## From videos
- **A concrete self-test for "is a loud master actually worth it": master the same source two ways — e.g. −7 LUFS via heavy limiting vs. −12 LUFS via upward compression/maximization — then normalize both to the same target (matching what streaming services do) and A/B them post-normalization.** The quieter-on-paper, upward-processed master should still sound better once both land at the same post-normalization loudness, since the detail it preserved (rather than truncated) stays audible — a practical diagnostic before deciding how hard to push a limiter, not just a claim to take on faith. → [[Master Chain]], [[Compression]] — Sage Audio, [5 Reasons to CHANGE Your Mastering Approach](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BdNItAYS1io)
- **A genre-split gloss on this note's own −14 to −8 LUFS streaming range: more dynamic genres (folk, classical, jazz) sit fine at the −14 end, louder/denser genres (rap, pop, rock) target closer to −8.** Also gives a second, close-but-not-identical lossy-encoding headroom figure: reduce true peak by **up to 2 dB** below 0 dBTP to protect against the amplitude shift (up to 2 dB) that MP3/AAC conversion can introduce — treat as corroborating this note's existing ~1 dB lossy-conversion safety margin (a different source, similar mechanism) rather than a contradiction; 1–2 dB true-peak headroom is the practical range. → [[Master Chain]], [[Limiting]] — Sage Audio, [How to Master for Streaming](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0ruBn14ftkI)
- **A clipper is the genre-right choice for an aggressive master, tape emulation is the genre-right choice for a gentle one — the same EDM-vs-jazz comparison this note's own hard/soft-clip framing above supports from the genre side.** EDM with an intentionally aggressive kick/snare/pluck → a **Clipper**, since it imparts distortion to each transient and keeps the intended aggression (tape emulation's subtle wave-shaping + high-frequency attenuation would work against that). Jazz mastering → tape emulation instead, used as a genuine alternative to limiting (gradual peak attenuation, era-appropriate shaping) rather than a clipper, which would impart unwanted aggressive transient distortion and push toward a loudness the genre doesn't need. → [[Critical Listening for Mastering]], [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[Kramer Tape]], [[kHs Clipper]] — Sage Audio, [Before You Master Music, Watch This](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tEqcGkgHgqc)
- **Mix-stage LUFS band, distinct from this note's own master-stage targets above, plus a worked example tying loudness to genre dynamic range.** A mix being prepared for handoff to mastering typically falls **~−25 to −18 LUFS integrated** (quieter than a finished master's ~−16 to −9 LUFS-I, itself a wider/more general band than this note's own tighter −9 to −7 pop/trap competitive target). No single "right" mix loudness exists because it's genre-driven: more dynamic mixes read quieter, less dynamic mixes read louder, even peaking at the same ceiling. Worked example: a pop song with ~15 dB of dynamic range (quietest content at −18 dBTP, loudest peak at −3 dBTP) reads noticeably louder overall than a classical piece peaking at the same −3 dBTP but with a quietest point around −60 dBTP — more of the pop song's signal sits close to peak level. → [[Mix Handoff]] — Sage Audio, [How to Prepare Your Mix for Mastering](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vd81zTPvnMQ)
- **Aliasing distortion, mechanically — why it "reflects down the spectrum" instead of just disappearing.** At a low-enough session sample rate (44.1 kHz cited), distortion generated by pushing a signal hard (clipping, aggressive limiting) can produce content that exceeds the frequency ceiling that sample rate is able to represent; that excess doesn't vanish, it gets **reflected back down the frequency spectrum** as aliasing distortion, which reads as harsh and unpleasant almost every time. Oversampling fixes it by giving more accurate quantization headroom before that reflection happens. *Distinct from this note's own "aliasing fear is overblown for transient clipping" trap above — that's about a specific case (short transients mask the artifact), this is the general mechanism that makes aliasing a real concern the rest of the time (sustained content, a limiter's ceiling, low session sample rates).* → [[Limiting]], [[Pro-L 2]] — Sage Audio, [How to Master Loud and Clear Without Sounding Harsh](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cy-S6YmVe2U)
- **After limiting, read LUFS and dB true peak as two separate checks, not one number.** LUFS (integrated) is the master's loudness; dB true peak is its actual ceiling risk — a true peak reading above 0 dBTP means going back to lower the limiter's output ceiling, not just trusting the LUFS figure. A general streaming-safe starting range: **−14 to −8 LUFS integrated**, **−2 to −0.5 dBTP** — wider than [[Master Chain]]'s own tighter −9 to −7 LUFS-I / −1.0 dBTP pop-trap targets, useful as a sanity-check floor rather than a replacement target. → [[Master Chain]], [[Limiting]] — Sage Audio, [How to Setup Your Mastering Chain](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y-5B4kOXUoM)
- **Clipping causes more harmonic distortion than limiting — cutting the waveform flat at the crossover point vs. attenuating it — which is exactly why some engineers deliberately run both a clipper and a limiter together rather than picking one.** Limiting exists specifically to avoid the harder clipping a digital system would otherwise force at the ceiling; clip first for transient/timbre control, then limit to catch what's left cleanly — the same clip→limit shape this note's own loudness stack (above) already runs, confirmed here as a deliberate combo rather than an accident of gear availability. → [[Limiting]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [Limiter Masterclass](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LZxglknf3xA)
- **Why clipping helps rather than hurts transient perception, mechanically: it adds high-frequency noise that functions like an exciter.** Whenever a clipper's threshold is crossed, it introduces a form of high-frequency noise/harmonic content on the transient — this brightens the master overall and, counter-intuitively, *helps retain the sense of transients* rather than blurring them, because the added high-frequency noise makes the transient easier for the ear to pick out against the rest of the mix even though the peak itself is technically being shaved. A distinct rationale from this note's existing "micro-transients are inaudible to remove — free headroom" framing above — both are true simultaneously (inaudible peak loss + audible transient-clarity gain from the added noise). → [[EQ]], [[kHs Clipper]] — Sage Audio, [Mastering Loud But Not Squashed](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YJVE4_CQ9Ts)
- **A little above −14 LUFS still reads louder than −14 even after streaming normalization — but only if dynamics survive getting there.** Mastering directly *to* −14 LUFS (matching the streaming-normalization target) is a common self-admitted mistake — pushing a master to roughly **−10 to −9 LUFS** via upward processing plus thoughtful limiting still sounds louder than a −14 LUFS master once both get normalized down to the same loudness, because the quiet detail pushed forward by the upward processing stays audible post-normalization. The caveat matters: a master that got there by *destroying* dynamics (heavy straight limiting) loses this benefit regardless of its pre-normalization LUFS number — the loudness gain has to come from upward processing, not brute-force gain reduction. Consistent with this note's own "streaming normalization is not a mixing target" framing above, adding the specific mechanism for *why* mastering a bit hot still pays off post-normalization. → [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [Mastering Loud But Not Squashed](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YJVE4_CQ9Ts)
- **The case against brick-wall limiting on a master, and three concrete alternatives that reach the same loudness without shaving every peak.** A limiter's look-ahead lets it know a peak is coming and attenuate exactly there instead of reacting like a compressor — its only real loudness benefit is that this lets you push more input gain (amplifying quiet detail, reducing masking), but the tradeoff is that a limiter *always* trims peaks, which is what reads as blurred/reduced transient impact regardless of how good the specific algorithm is. Alternatives that chase the same "amplify quiet detail" goal without shaving every peak: **parallel compression** (compress heavily, blend to ~50/50 wet/dry — [[Pro-C 2]]'s Dry Gain does this in one plugin, only half the output carries the attenuation, and Attack/Release can open up for transient retention since the blend removes the clipping risk); **gentle upward maximization** ([[Oxford Inflator]], or the unowned PSP Vintage Warmer 2 — both push quiet content up then gradually round toward the ceiling rather than squaring it like a clipper; a maximizer like [[Ozone 9 Maximizer]] is mechanically still a limiter, not automatically gentler); and **subtle multiband saturation** ([[Saturn 2]]'s Subtle Tube/Tape + Dynamic style — same upward effect as parallel compression, no attenuation needed first, adjustable per frequency band). → [[Parallel Processing]], [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[Limiting]], [[Master Chain]] — Sage Audio, [Stop Limiting Your Masters](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s_LDCzWx20g)
- **A mix-bus clipper triggered mainly by kick/bass generates harmonics that land in and amplify the low-mids — a hidden source of "muddy low-mids," not a fix for it.** High-amplitude kick and bass content crosses a mix-bus clipper's threshold first and most often; the resulting harmonics form in the frequency range directly above the source, so a bass/kick-driven clipper is, as a byproduct, a low-mid booster. Worth checking before reaching for a corrective EQ cut on low-mid mud that the clipper itself may be generating — same waveshaping mechanism as compression, just designed-in rather than an artifact to avoid (clippers square the waveform's peak on purpose). → [[BASS Slot]], [[BED Slot]], [[Saturation and Distortion]] — Sage Audio, [All Processing is Connected - Crucial Knowledge for Pro Mixes & Masters](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XV-0rdUrgHs)
- **Deliberately clip a compressed lead vocal into the red as a tone choice, not a mistake.** Pushed past a hard compression stage until visibly distorting, kept because it sounds "poppy and stabby" — a style choice, not something corrected. — Disclosure (Sol State), [How to Produce Vocals Like a PRO (Disclosure's 12 Secrets)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IwKZcTI3428)
- **LUFS is the mastering engineer's job, not the mixing engineer's — don't chase a meter number, chase how the track feels.** Push the mix loud enough that it "feels good," and only glance at a loudness meter to catch how much headroom is getting eaten (not to hit a target LUFS value); the track gets re-compressed anyway by streaming/radio/car-system playback, so obsessing over a specific LUFS reading at the mix stage doesn't make the record sound better. — Lenno (Sol State), [Use Multiband Referencing To Fix Mix Problems](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QQU2RDzoFQA)
- **Compression + saturation + limiting together lower peak level while making a mix sound louder and fuller.** With processing engaged a mix peaks lower (e.g. -9 dB) than bypassed (e.g. -6 dB) yet reads as noticeably louder and richer — reinforces the same theme from [[Disclosure - Loudness the Smart Way (Don't Smash Your Drums)|Disclosure's dedicated loudness video]] on a different song. → Disclosure (Sol State), [Make EVERY Sound Come Alive](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KS89XfqFE9A)
- **Perceived dynamics and measured dynamics are different things — a heavily limited/clipped mix can still sound dynamic even though a meter says it isn't.** Chasing measured dynamic range isn't the same goal as making a track feel dynamic; digital clipping specifically isn't inherently bad — it can function as legitimate character (a clipped kick can read as pleasantly "saturated" rather than damaged), and clipping/crushing together is a genre-identity choice for dance music, not just a loudness-maximization necessity. Two sounds at identical peak level (both 0 dBFS) can feel like completely different volumes purely from frequency balance (high-end content, low-mid character) — loudness as heard isn't just a function of level. → [[Saturation and Distortion]] — Fox Stevenson (Sol State), [You Will NEVER Use Clipping The Same Way Again](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rXbMcPeD3Mw)
- **Loudness-meter setup for a useful -14 LUFS reading: pull the color threshold down to -14 and the scale range down to -8** — on a standard LUFS meter (Youlean Loudness Meter, but the same two controls exist on most), these settings put the blue/white reading bars right at -14 LUFS-I (a solid modern streaming loudness floor) with far higher visual resolution across the practical 0-to-8-dB range than the meter's defaults give — worth setting once and reusing on every master. → Sol State, [I found the 15 BEST FREE PLUGINS on the internet (and they are soooo good)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0BTcW2PNrnA)
- **MP3/lossy export can create intersample-peak overs even on a file that reads clean on a sample-peak meter — pull the limiter's true-peak ceiling back ~1 dB as a dedicated safety margin for the conversion step, separate from the GR target itself.** The limiter's input gain gets pushed to hit a musical GR target (e.g. 2–3 dB on the loudest hits), then the output ceiling is pulled an *additional* ~1 dB below 0 dBTP purely to protect against those conversion-stage overs, not because the mix itself needs more headroom. → [[Limiting]], [[Master Chain]] — Max Konyi, [Final Mix & Master | Studio One LoFi Tutorial | Part 9](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-OKUCuyLBSg)
- **When every individual track is already heavily saturated/compressed, the master needs almost nothing** — a mastering chain built from a single hard clipper (New Fangled Saturate) rather than the expected stack of EQs/compressors/OTTs, on the reasoning that loudness and character were already built into every track during mixing — at that point the master's only job is a final clip/gain push, not another full processing pass. The even more minimal fallback: a plain Utility with Gain cranked until it's as loud as desired, clipping and all, works when the mix underneath is already dense with saturation. → [[Gain Staging]] — You Suck at Producing (Underbelly), [How To Make EDM (Emo Dance Music)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=skrcNjB4NIc)
- **"Stop avoiding the red" — clipping is a habit to embrace, not a mistake to avoid, on modern electronic drums** — the mindset framing behind this note: going into the red isn't inherently wrong, it depends on the material (don't clip a solo piano recital, but a loud electronic drum loop wants it). Demoed on a full drum loop with Saturator's Digital Clip: with it bypassed, the clip's volume meter nearly maxes out on every hit; switched on, the same loop's peaks squash down noticeably — functioning as an informal limiter/compressor that lets the overall track level get pushed up afterward without re-clipping. Same underlying mechanism as this note's hard-clip section (sine → square, new harmonics added) but framed here as a deliberate loudness lever, not just a sound-shaping side effect. → [[Saturator]] — You Suck at Producing, [5 Habits I Had To UNLEARN To Become A Better Music Producer](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wTugkLmMSCM)
- **Per-track peak-taming with Saturator's Digital Clip, then reference-track loudness+spectrum matching before touching the master** — before any mastering, run Saturator's Digital Clip curve across essentially every individual track (Drive cranked, Output pulled down to match) to squash each track's peaks toward its average level — on a drum bus this alone brought peaks down ~25 dB, freeing headroom for the eventual master. Cut low end out of every non-bass/kick element too, so the kick and sub own that space uncontested. To judge how much sub is actually needed (unreliable on weak speakers), loudness-match a reference track first via an integrated-LUFS meter, then A/B a Spectrum analyzer zoomed into the sub band against it — if the reference's sub still cuts through more at matched level, the fix is harmonic content, not volume: draw extra harmonics into the sub oscillator's wavetable (instead of a plain sine) and drive them out with a filter, rather than just turning it up. → [[Saturator]] — You Suck at Producing, [How to get INSANELY loud mixes (ASMR Tutorial)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lV_lpR2Wgv8)
- **Split mastering-limiting duty across two limiter instances instead of one** — after Saturator's Digital Clip shaves the master's peaks and Multiband Dynamics squeezes low/mid/high independently (an alternative to a single EQ move — crank one band without touching the others) and a touch of brightening EQ, run the final loudness push through two limiters in series rather than one pushed hard: a single instance driven that hard starts audibly distorting, while splitting the same gain reduction across two instances reads noticeably cleaner. → [[Pro-L 2]], [[Multiband Dynamics]] — You Suck at Producing, [How to get INSANELY loud mixes (ASMR Tutorial)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lV_lpR2Wgv8)
- **dB is logarithmic — the same size step matters more the closer you are to 0** — the perceived-loudness jump from 0 to −6 dB is bigger than the jump from −6 to −12, even though both are 6 dB steps; roughly, every 6 dB down is a *halving* of perceived loudness (every 6 dB up toward 0 is a doubling), which is why headroom management gets more sensitive the closer a signal sits to the ceiling. On Ableton's stock clip volume meter, the two thin lines are the peak reading (with an exact dB readout) and the solid bars underneath are the average/RMS loudness over time — watch both, not just one: a signal can look fine on peak and still be quiet on average, or vice versa (see [[Gain Staging]]'s VU/peak "meter sandwich" convention for the mix-wide version of this same distinction). Push a track (or the master) past 0 dB and it distorts/clips audibly — 0 dB is a hard ceiling, not a suggestion, so keep meaningful headroom between peak levels and it at every stage. — You Suck at Producing, [You Suck at Producing: Understanding Volume, dB & Clipping](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fp2o_CzG8TA)
- **Soft clipping from a compressor's pushed makeup gain — a real-world example of "soft clip = tone," not just a spec-sheet distinction** — pushing a [[Glue Compressor]]'s makeup gain hot enough (after a parallel-compression pass) to soft-clip is A/B'd directly against the same signal with the clipping bypassed; the un-clipped version reads flatly worse. Confirms this note's soft-clip framing (audible warmth/rounded transients, a tone not a headroom tool) with a concrete example that isn't a dedicated clipper device. → [[Glue Compressor]] — You Suck at Producing, [Warmth](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Yv3v9SsoDMw)
- **Hard clip visualized: Utility Gain shears a sine into a square wave, spectrum analyzer shows the new harmonics** — a beginner-friendly demo of what "hard clipping" actually means: push a [[Utility]] device's Gain knob hot on a sine-wave source and watch the oscilloscope — the peaks/troughs flatten into a square wave. The spectrum analyzer shows why it sounds "nasty": new harmonic overtones stack above the clean fundamental. Swapping a synth's oscillator to a square wave directly produces the same result at the source, no clipping device needed. → [[Utility]], [[Saturator]] — You Suck at Producing, [How to use Distortion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EolO6-evB2M)
- **A saturator/clipper as the entire mastering chain, no separate limiter** — with the clip shape switched from Soft to Hard, a meter's red line shows exactly how much is being shaved off the peaks. The Auto toggle auto-gain-matches output so Drive can be judged honestly (more Drive stops reading as "louder" when Auto's on, so you're only hearing whether it's distorting) — push Drive as hard as possible without crossing into audible distortion, then flip Auto off to hear the real loudness gained. Add dither only at the final export stage (not before) — quiet white noise that masks bit-depth-reduction artifacts. → [[EQ]] — You Suck at Producing, [You Suck at Mixing: The Ultimate Guide](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YSR6P2G5GvY)
- **Drive up + ceiling down = perceived-loudness clipping with headroom preserved, not spent** — a clipper rack's core relationship: push the input **Drive** up while pulling the output **ceiling** down, which reads as bigger/louder while actually banking headroom rather than spending it — the inverse-feeling but standard clip move; separate DC-offset-removal and "Color"/saturation toggles sit alongside it as optional extra stages. → [[Plugin Wishlist]] — dnksaus, [Dank Sauces Rack Pack - Demoing Every Rack!](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xuKc_a6xxjk)
- **Target just over -6 LUFS and don't chase the loudness wars** — barely touches the limiter and deliberately stops mastering around/just past -6 LUFS rather than pushing further, explicitly opting out of dubstep's loudness-war race in favor of dynamics and a clean top end. → [[Masking and Carving]] — Jauz (Sol State), [8 Tips for PRO Synths, PUNCHY Drums, & LOUD CLEAR Mixdowns](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pn3Izw3-Qmw)
- **Mix a lo-fi intro *deliberately quiet* to trick the listener into cranking the volume** — a low-passed gramophone/tape sample placed very low reads as "too quiet / not enough bass," so people turn it up… then the drop *slams*. Loudness as psychology, not just level. → [[Depth and Space]] — Disclosure, [Blue World](https://youtu.be/lFTEptvdBg8)
- **Ozone's genre mastering presets are too hot on input gain, not output gain — pull the INPUT down, leave the output alone.** deadmau5 found Ozone's stock genre-specific mastering presets needed roughly 8 dB of headroom (6 dB "was still too much") to sound right and avoid instantly clipping the moment a preset loads — the fix every time was pulling the preset's INPUT slider down an extra 2-4 dB before the signal even reaches the chain, never touching the final OUTPUT slider (that one just pins the ceiling and should stay put). Read: iZotope's presets ship gain-staged for a hotter incoming mix than most people's masters actually run, so the preset clips until you compensate on the way in. → [[Ozone 9 Maximizer]], [[Gain Staging]] — deadmau5 (Sol State), [Deadmau5 RANTS about Kicks](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5Bzu8xNp5X4)
