# Work Song — Nat Adderley (Wes Montgomery guitar solo)

> A Nat Adderley original from his 1960 album *Work Song*, in the key of A♭. Nathan Borton transcribes and breaks down Wes Montgomery's short guitar solo on the tune — not the whole form, just three excerpted licks (bars 2, 8, and 12) — to teach reusable devices for landing 9th/11th/13th chord extensions melodically rather than as isolated color notes. Distinct from the vault's other Wes Montgomery solo breakdown, [[🎵 WES MONTGOMERY - D Natural Blues]] (Jack Ruch's chunk-analyze-template method over Wes's own 12-bar blues) — this one is a single-teacher, three-device lesson over a Nat Adderley sideman date rather than a full chunk-by-chunk concept map, and see [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]] for how the two compare as methods.

## Chords
**Key:** A♭ (as tagged) — but the tune's actual tonal center is its relative minor, **F minor**; this is a **16-bar minor-blues variant**, not the standard 12-bar form. Commonly published changes (Real Book / widely-cited transcriptions):

`Fm7 – Fm7 – Fm7 – Fm7 / Fm7 – Fm7 – Cm7 – C7 / Fm7 – Fm7 – Fm7 – Fm7 / Fm7 – B♭7 – G7 – C7` (then back to the top)

Relative to the page's stated key of A♭ major: **Fm7 reads as vi7** (the chord the tune actually treats as "home"), **C7 as V7/vi** (the dominant of that F-minor tonic), **B♭7 as a dominantized ii7**, and **G7 as a passing secondary dominant (V7/V of vi)** that walks the turnaround down to C7 before resolving back to Fm7. Nat Adderley's own recording reportedly plays **F7 throughout** rather than a clean Fm7/F minor split — the same "every chord can be a dominant 7th, the ♭7 is a color not a tension" convention documented on [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *Why every chord is a dominant 7th*, blurring the line between a minor blues and a straight dominant blues.

Doesn't map cleanly onto this vault's `progressions:` controlled vocabulary — 16 bars rather than a 12-bar form, and the closing B♭7-G7-C7 turnaround is a descending secondary-dominant chain rather than a clean I-vi-ii-V shape — so it's tagged `progressions: []` rather than forced onto the closest-sounding label.

**What the source video actually covers** (a subset of the full form above): only three short excerpts of the solo are analyzed (bar 2, bar 8, bar 12); the full 12-bar/chorus form isn't transcribed in the video itself. Bar 2 sits over an **F minor 7** (the relative minor within A♭ major), bar 8 sits over a **V-I dominant resolution (E♭7 → A♭)**, and bar 12 is played against a stretch of **diatonic A♭-major chords**.

## Solo devices by passage (Nathan Borton's analysis)
Three short excerpts, each reduced to one reusable device — see [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]] for the general chunk-analyze-name-concept method this lesson loosely follows (without Jack Ruch's explicit "replay it as your own line" step).

- **Bar 2, over F minor 7:** an **A♭ major 7 arpeggio played over Fm7** — three shared notes plus a G that reads as the **9th of Fm9** — reversible (Fm9 arpeggio over A♭maj7 → A♭ major 13 sound), since A♭maj7 and Fm9 are relative major/minor. → [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]], [[Extended Chords]]
- **Bar 8, resolving E♭7 → A♭ (V-I):** a line that hits the **9th**, jumps to the **13th**, then uses the **♭9** resolving down to the **5th** of the A♭ tonic — read on guitar as an **F minor(major7) shape arpeggio** that only "works" over the E♭7 if its ♭3 (A♭) is treated as a **passing tone resolving to the 9th (G)** rather than a static chord tone; sounds like plain F minor without the backing chords underneath it. → [[Extended Chords]], [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]], [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]
- **Bar 12, over a diatonic A♭ stretch:** a movable **4-note guitar shape (C-B♭-G-F)** shared with Grant Green, transposable in octaves down the neck and portable across any diatonic chord in the key for an instant extension color — no single "correct" target chord prescribed. → [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]

## Steal this
- **Relative-major/relative-minor arpeggio swap** as a one-shape way to imply a 9th (major arpeggio over the relative minor chord) or a 13th (minor arpeggio over the relative major chord) — reusable any time a minor chord's relative major (or vice versa) is already comfortable under the fingers.
- **A shape that "doesn't work" over its chord in isolation can still work once one note is reframed as a passing tone resolving into a real chord tone** — always test an extension-heavy line against the actual backing progression before judging it, not a cappella.
- **A single 4-note shape (C-B♭-G-F) portable across every diatonic chord in a key**, octave-transposable — a low-overhead "extension color" tool to reach for without re-deriving an arpeggio per chord.
