# I Saw Her Standing There — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

Their first genuinely fast, hard rocker and the lead-off track on the first album — Pollack treats it less as a milestone than as a **specimen jar**, the one early song running nearly every 1963 trademark at once: boogie bass, pickup entries, tight non-parallel two-part harmony, falsetto as a structural event, blue notes against major chords.

**Form.** The fully expanded two-bridge model with **two** verses between the bridges, the second being the guitar solo. **Every section enters on a pickup**, starting with the bass note between the count-in and bar 1 — forward lean with no change to the material. The verse is 16 bars in four even phrases (establish, reinforce, climb, resolve); the bridge is **10 bars phrased 2+2+6** rather than the expected 8.

**Harmony.** Almost pure I–IV–V with slow changes, which is most of why a song that isn't a blues sounds like one. The one reharmonised bar reads at first as ♭VI but is actually the **minor iv in first inversion**, borrowed into the major key ([[HAR-C08 — Borrowed chords and modal interchange]]) — the cross-relation is timed to the falsetto whoop, and it's the verse's peak. The bridge parks on IV for six bars, reaches V, and instead of stopping at eight resolves V **deceptively to IV** before the tonic ([[HAR-C04 — Deceptive cadence]]); the delay drains tension while the phrase extension and falsetto leap push the other way ([[ARR-C06 — Section-length conventions, and breaking them on purpose]]).

**Melody.** Wide range, stepwise motion interrupted by **large leaps** — the octave jump up at the peak prepared by a nearly equal drop earlier. Stuffed with flat thirds and sevenths in a four-sharp key ([[BLU-C07 — Why the ♭3 isn't wrong]]).

**Arrangement.** The two-part vocal writing uses **open fourths and fifths, some in parallel**, deliberately unlike Everly-style thirds and sixths, and in the bridge John holds a note under Paul and then vaults an octave *above* him — so the top line switches singers mid-phrase. The solo verse quietly changes two chords back to plain ones, because both edits remove emphasis points that only work when words are landing on them.
