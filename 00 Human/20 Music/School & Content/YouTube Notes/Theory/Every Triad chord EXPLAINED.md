# Every Triad chord EXPLAINED

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=42dbRFexbXk)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=42dbRFexbXk)

## Synopsis

A complete taxonomy of every named 3-note triad type — the core four (major/minor/diminished/augmented), the sus chords, three "modal" triads named for the mode whose characteristic note they contain, and the odd-one-out major♭5 — plus a discussion of where the label "chord"/"triad" stops applying (clusters, quartal harmony).

## What's taught

- **The core four (built strictly from stacked major/minor 3rds)**: **major** (major 3rd + minor 3rd on top); **minor** (minor 3rd + major 3rd on top — a lowered 3rd from major); **diminished** (minor 3rd + minor 3rd — minor triad with the 5th also lowered); **augmented** (major 3rd + major 3rd — major triad with the 5th raised). Diminished and augmented triads are structurally symmetric (both 3rds the same size), which is why each divides the octave evenly.
- **Sus chords (broader "3-note chord" definition)**: **sus4** (3rd raised to the 4th degree) and **sus2** (3rd lowered to the 2nd degree) — both "suspend" the 3rd away from its resolved position, creating a pull to resolve back; sus2's resolution is described as less satisfying/weaker than sus4's.
- **Three "modal" triads**, each named for the mode whose defining color-tone it contains: **Lydian triad** (= sus♯4 — contains the ♯4 that defines the Lydian scale; sometimes labeled "add♯4" or "Lyd" instead); **Phrygian triad** (= sus♭2 — contains Phrygian's defining ♭2); **Locrian triad** (a diminished triad with the 3rd further lowered to a ♭2 — combines Locrian's ♭2 and ♭5). All three occur **diatonically** in a major key on the exact degree matching their mode's position in the diatonic-modes sequence: Lydian triad on the **4th** degree (Lydian = 4th mode), Phrygian triad on the **3rd** degree (Phrygian = 3rd mode), Locrian triad on the **7th** degree (Locrian = 7th mode).
- **Major♭5** — a major triad with a lowered 5th (the mirror image of building the augmented triad by raising the 5th instead). Despite being labeled by its ♭5, it's functionally the same pitch content as a "sharp 4," so it carries a Lydian-adjacent color too.
- **Ten total** under this counting: major, minor, diminished, augmented, sus4, sus2, Lydian, Phrygian, Locrian, major♭5.
- **What doesn't count, and why**: a 3-note chord missing its 3rd but keeping a 7th and 9th (e.g. a "no-3rd" dominant 7 shape) is conventionally read as an incomplete 7th chord (a "7 omitting the 5th"), not a distinct triad type. A minor triad with a raised 5th is not a new triad — it's just an inversion of a major triad already on the list (moving the ♭5-raised note to the bottom reveals a plain major triad). **Chord vs. cluster**: the naming convention for "chord" implicitly requires *some* form of 3rd-and-5th relationship (even heavily altered); a 3-note stack that's really just a 3rd + a 2nd with no 5th-like relationship is better called a **cluster**, not a chord — chords are a human labeling *construct* for describing harmony, not a natural category; harmony (notes interacting) exists regardless of whether a stack of notes has an agreed name. **Quartal harmony**: triads stacked in 4ths instead of 3rds (all the chords above are "tertiary" harmony, built from 3rds) don't fit the conventional tertiary-based Western naming system at all, and trying to force a "triad" label onto them (e.g. calling one an "11th triad") starts to break down.
