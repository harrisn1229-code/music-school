# Dominant-Altered Superimposition Over a Minor Chord

**Over a static minor i chord (a minor blues, a one-chord minor vamp), borrow the sound of that chord's own V7 as a fully altered dominant — D7alt over G minor — by superimposing the D altered scale, D half-whole diminished scale, or a whole-tone fragment, then resolving back into plain minor pentatonic/blues vocabulary.** Same underlying principle as [[Tonic Minor Soloing]] (imply a V7–i resolution inside a chord that never actually moves), but executed here as **full-scale superimposition and memorized licks** rather than single guide-tone devices — a scale-level sibling to [[Pentatonic Superimposition and Side-Slipping]]'s shape-level techniques. Worked entirely over a **G minor one-chord vamp** (D7 = V7 of Gm).

## The core move

1. **Establish the tonality first** — open every phrase with straight minor pentatonic/blues vocabulary so the ear locks onto the tonic before anything outside happens.
2. **Shift into D7-altered territory** — pick one of three scale sources for the same target sound:
   - **D altered scale** (7th mode of E♭ melodic minor) — the default choice in this material.
   - **D half-whole diminished scale** — shares the signature ♭9→♭7→root move with the altered scale (see lick 3 below).
   - **G whole-tone scale** — the most extreme option (lick 4): its major 3rd directly clashes against the Gm tonic, described in the source as "a really wild sound."
3. **Resolve back onto a chord tone of Gm** (the root, the 5th) before dropping back into pentatonic/blues phrasing to close the idea — never leave the outside material hanging unresolved.

## Four worked licks (Jack Ruch; licks 3–4 credited to Scott Henderson)

**Lick 1 — low position, arpeggio tag.**
Gm pentatonic bluesy opener → D altered scale: **♯9 up to the major 3rd** (an octave leap), back down to the **♯9** (the ♯9-to-major-3rd interval is the "nice dissonant" hook) → descend the D altered scale, **skipping the ♯4/♭5** on the way down → land back in Gm pentatonic → tag the phrase with an ascending **B♭ major 7 arpeggio** (relative major of Gm — this is *inside* harmony, not outside; it colors the line as a **G minor 9** sound).

**Lick 2 — same shape relocated to 10th position, resolves on a chord tone instead of a tag.**
Gm pentatonic (10th-fret box) → D altered scale: **♯9 → major 3rd → ♯9 → ♭9 → root → ♭7 → ♭6**, again **skipping the ♯4** → land on the **5th of Gm** (a chord tone, not a tag) → back into Gm pentatonic. Demonstrates the lick-1 vocabulary is a **relocatable shape**, not a fixed-position lick — same interval path, different fretboard position.

**Lick 3 (Scott Henderson) — bebop enclosure + the signature ♭9→♭7→root move.**
Opens with a shape that *looks* like the top of an A minor pentatonic box but is really **D7/D Mixolydian material** (♭7 down to the 5th) → a **bebop enclosure targeting the 3rd of D7**: approach from above the target, add two chromatic notes below it, then slide up into the 3rd (this opening section works over a straight D7/D Mixolydian on its own, no outside content yet) → the outside hook: **♭9 → ♭7 → root** (relative to D) — flagged explicitly as "a common sound," present in **both** the half-whole diminished scale and the altered scale, and named as "a signature thing of these outside sounds" → resolve into Gm pentatonic.

**Lick 4 (Scott Henderson) — the most outside of the four; D altered into G whole tone, bookended by chord tones.**
**Root of D → ♭9 → ♯4/♭5** (straight D altered scale) → shift into a **G whole-tone** fragment for the tag (the whole-tone scale's major 3rd against the Gm tonic is the "wild" clash) → the whole line starts on the **5th of Gm** and ends on the **root of Gm** — both chord tones — with the outside content sandwiched deliberately between two anchors rather than left to float. Resolve into Gm blues/pentatonic phrasing.

## Practice / drilling this into other keys and progressions

- **Transpose the whole system, not just the licks.** The generalizable recipe is: over any static minor i, find its own **V7**, play that V7 as **altered** (or half-whole diminished, or a whole-tone fragment), then resolve to a chord tone of i. Drill it in at least 2–3 other keys (e.g. Am → E7alt, Dm → A7alt, Cm → G7alt) — moving the same fingerings up/down the neck (licks 1–2 are explicitly shown as movable shapes, not fixed to G).
- **Isolate the two recurring degree-paths before drilling full licks:**
  - **♯9 → major 3rd → ♯9** (the octave-leap dissonant interval from licks 1–2) — loop it alone, in time, over a static minor-i drone/vamp until it's not a "reach."
  - **♭9 → ♭7 → root** (the licks 3 signature move, shared by altered and half-whole diminished) — same drill, loop it alone first.
- **Skip-note discipline:** licks 1–2 both deliberately skip the **♯4/♭5** descending through the altered scale — practice the full scale first, then the "skip that one note" pattern as its own exercise so the skip becomes a habit, not a memory slip.
- **Bookend discipline (lick 4's rule, generalizable to all of them):** always know which chord tone of the tonic minor a line starts and ends on before adding outside content in the middle — practice picking a start chord-tone and end chord-tone first, then improvise only the "outside" middle between them.
- **Apply over a real minor blues form**, not just a one-chord vamp: the same D7alt-over-Gm vocabulary works over the V-chord bar(s) of a 12-bar minor blues in G (see [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *Minor blues* — the V frequently stays a dominant even in a minor blues, which is exactly the harmonic gap this whole technique exploits).
- **Ear-first sequencing, per the source's own stated method:** learn the lick verbatim first, then investigate the underlying scale/theory afterward — not the reverse. Sing/hum the ♯9→3rd and ♭9→♭7→root intervals before drilling them on the neck.
- **Reference listening for the vocabulary:** Robben Ford, Scott Henderson, John Scofield — named directly as players who use this outside-over-minor sound "to great effect."
