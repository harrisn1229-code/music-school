## Synopsis

BassBuzz's "blunder buster" exercise: rather than five separate drills for five separate technique problems (which the video argues bloats a practice routine into a boring, hours-long mess that people quit within a week or two), it's **one single riff that progresses through five levels**, each level specifically dialed to expose and fix one of five named beginner bass-technique failure modes — flying fingers, fretting too hard, uneven alternate plucking, "duck wrist" on octaves, and technique breakdown at speed. Each blunder gets a short before/after demo (including footage of the presenter's own younger, worse technique) and a bare-minimum prep drill to do before attempting that level of the exercise, rather than a full separate practice routine per problem — the whole point being to keep the daily fix down to 10 minutes. ## What's taught

**Blunder 1 — Flying fingers (fretting hand).** Unused fretting fingers pop up high off the fretboard instead of staying low, which looks sloppy and audibly clunks/muddies the sound, worse at speed — demonstrated on the presenter's own current-vs-early-career playing, and confirmed against a deliberately exaggerated "flying fingers" impression that turned out to be an accurate mirror of his actual old technique. **Bare-minimum prep before attempting level one:** the "one on, one off" fretting game — hover all four fingers over easy-to-reach frets, then press down **one finger at a time** (varying the order, increasing speed) for "one on"; for "one off" (the harder direction), press all four fingers down, then **lift exactly one finger at a time**. Doesn't mimic real playing motion, but builds the awareness/coordination the exercise itself then applies. Starting goal: gradually bring fingers closer to the fretboard; more advanced players can rest fingers on the neck between notes. → routed to [[Fretting-Hand Strength, Stretch, Stamina, and Finger Control]] (its Exercise 4 is a different, more developed flying-fingers fix from a separate BassBuzz video — this video's one-on/one-off game is a lighter, faster prep drill for the same failure mode, now cross-linked there).

**Blunder 2 — Fretting too hard (invisible bad habit).** Root cause given: beginners consciously press hard to avoid buzz while fingers are still weak; fingers get stronger within the first month or two, but the habit of high perceived effort persists, so players end up pressing far harder than needed just to get a clean note — described as "a vice grip without realizing it." Costs: fatigue, reduced top speed, and literal pain on longer pieces. **The fix: minimum viable pressure (MVP).** Rest a finger (worked example: index on the 7th fret, A string) on the string **without pressing**; pluck it — no note sounds, confirming there's zero pressure. Add pressure gradually until the string starts to **rattle** against the fret; add just a **tiny bit more** until the note goes clean; **stop there and hold that exact pressure** — no harder. Practice level two of the exercise one note at a time, deliberately aiming for MVP on each note, to internalize the feeling rather than the numbers. Named as something that has to be **actively re-checked** even by experienced players, since pressure habits creep back up the longer a session runs. → fills a gap explicitly flagged as undetailed in [[Goal-Tiered Practice and the Self-Taught Bass Commandments]]'s Commandment 5 pitfall table (now filled in with this drill) and cross-links [[Fretting-Hand Strength, Stretch, Stamina, and Finger Control]]'s Exercise 1, which fixes the opposite pressure failure (not pressing hard/close enough) — the two notes now bracket correct fretting pressure from both directions.

**Blunder 3 — Uneven alternate plucking, most audible on a "chugging" driving-note pattern.** Alternating index/middle plucking fingers don't yet deliver equal volume when a player is new to alternate plucking, which is most obvious on repetitive chugging basslines. **Prep drill:** intentionally play **uneven** first (loud-soft-loud-soft on alternating fingers) to build awareness that uneven isn't inherently "wrong," it's just not what you want right now; then flip to **soft-loud-soft-loud**; then push further to **loud-loud** and **soft-soft** on consecutive plucks to find each finger's individual weak spot; switch between the patterns to build conscious volume control per finger before attempting level three of the exercise. → routed to [[String Crossing and Plucking Patterns]] as a new "dynamics control" section — that note already owns rake-vs-alternate finger-choice and N-notes-per-string drilling, but had no existing content on **volume evenness** specifically, which is the actual skill this blunder targets.

**Blunder 4 — "Duck wrist" on octaves.** Named failure: the fretting wrist bends sharply ("quack, quack, quack") when jumping octave shapes, which hurts and causes the plucking hand to scramble trying to keep pace. **Ergonomic fix (works even with small hands):** raise the strap so the neck isn't hanging too low, tilt the neck up further, and add a bit more curl/bend in the fingers so the wrist itself can stay straighter — demonstrated on a BassBuzz forum student ("Sarah") whose wrist bend visibly reduced after weeks of practice. **Technical fix — split-finger technique:** normally index and middle **fretting** fingers travel together as a unit when crossing strings; for a fast low-string/high-string octave jump, **split them** instead — index finger stays anchored near the low string, middle finger stays anchored near the high string, so the hand doesn't have to relocate the whole shape for every jump. Applied per note-pattern in the exercise: low-high-low-low → split for the first two notes, un-split (both fingers together) for the low-low pair; low-high-low-high → split throughout; low-low-high-high → no split needed at all, since the notes are doubled up in pairs. → new note created: [[Octave Technique — Split-Finger Fretting and Duck-Wrist Prevention]] (no existing home for octave-specific fretting-hand mechanics in the vault).

**Blunder 5 — "Death by speed" (technique backslides under tempo pressure).** Even solid slow technique tends to fall apart once tempo goes up, and the video is explicit this matters even for players with no ambition to shred, since plenty of well-known basslines still demand real speed. Baseline advice (repeated, self-aware about repeating it): **always start slower than target and build up gradually.** **Hand-isolation prep drill for level five specifically:** rest the plucking hand on the strings to mute them and run through the fretting motions alone, **humming the bassline** to internalize the pitches without needing audible confirmation from actually fretting hard — trains flying-fingers control and MVP pressure while isolated from the plucking side; then flip it — mute with the fretting hand and drill **plucking alone**, again humming along, watching the tab for which string each note falls on and noting which plucking transitions feel hardest; recombine only once both hands are independently solid. → routed to [[Interleaved Practice, Chunking, and Deliberate-Practice Drills]]'s existing "Hands-separate practice" section as a bass-specific worked example (that section already documents the same isolate-then-recombine method from a guitar/classical-guitar source — this is an independent convergence on the identical mechanism from a different instrument and channel).

**Format note (not itself a technique):** the video closes with a genuine two-week field test — BassBuzz forum students ran the five-level exercise daily and their before/after clips are shown, functioning as social proof rather than a marketing claim alone. This is a stronger accountability move than most BassBuzz routine videos in the vault, which typically claim "30-day" outcomes without showing the actual before/after footage.
