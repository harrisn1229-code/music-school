# Pitch and Formant

General best practice for tuning, pitch-shifting, formant moves, and varispeed vocal character. Specific tools: [[Auto-Tune Pro]], [[Melodyne]]. See also [[De-essing]].

## What it is
Correcting, tuning, and re-charactering the voice — from transparent tuning to formant tricks and tape-style varispeed color.

## Owned (first-call)
[[Auto-Tune Pro]] · [[Melodyne]]

## From videos
- **Half-speed piano tracking as a two-in-one solution: it makes an otherwise-unplayable part physically playable AND produces a harpsichord-like timbre as a byproduct.** The famous baroque piano solo on "In My Life" (The Beatles), confirmed directly by George Martin's son Giles: George Martin scored a counterpoint part too fast/complex to play live at normal tempo, had the tape machine **slowed to half speed (an octave down)**, played the passage comfortably at that slower tempo, then sped the tape back to normal for playback — pitch and tempo both double back up together, producing both the "impossible" playing speed and the bright, harpsichord-like tone. Giles notes his father already knew this half-speed-piano-reads-as-harpsichord trick from his own pre-Beatles novelty-record work (comedy records, *Sparky's Magic Piano*-style recordings) — the technique predates this song by years, it wasn't invented for the session. Reusable recipe: when a part is either too fast to play cleanly OR you want a harpsichord-adjacent character from a piano without an actual harpsichord, track it with the session slowed (tempo down, matching pitch down an octave), then speed the whole take back to normal afterward. Owned lane for the varispeed mechanism: [[Varispeed 3.0]]. → [[🎵 THE BEATLES - In My Life]] — Tape Notes (Giles Martin), [[TN 190 The Beatles (Giles Martin) - Full Video Episode]]
- **Pre-digital vari-speed as a two-in-one pitch/tempo tool: on tape, they're physically locked together, so any splice-compatibility fix has only one free variable.** "Strawberry Fields Forever" (Beatles): two takes recorded in genuinely different keys AND tempos needed splicing into one continuous track. Since no single vari-speed setting could match both simultaneously, the engineers prioritized matching KEY over tempo — vari-speeding each take toward a shared middle pitch, accepting a small residual tempo mismatch, then hiding that mismatch by placing the splice at a natural musical pause rather than trying to eliminate it. Result: a genuinely non-standard, in-between pitch center across the finished track — a byproduct of solving a technical constraint, not a stylistic choice. Companion device, same song: fade-out immediately followed by fade-in to skip an unwanted passage (timing-flawed percussion from a vari-speeded take) without a hard edit. → [[The music theory of "Strawberry Fields Forever"]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Why is Strawberry Fields Forever in A half-sharp major]]
- **Vocal formants F1/F2/F3 all live in the mid-frequency range; F3 (roughly 2–5 kHz) is the single most important one for perceived clarity, and lines up with the ear canal's own resonance.** Formants are clusters of frequencies encoding which vowel is being sung/spoken; typically three per voice (F1 lowest, F3 highest), varying by singer and by vowel — worked example: a vocalist with a ~17 cm vocal tract singing "oo" produces formants around roughly 400 Hz, 1.1 kHz, and ~2 kHz (sung vowels skew toward frequencies matching the intended note). F3 sits in the 2–5 kHz range the ear is most sensitive to: boost there if a vocal/instrument reads unclear, attenuate there if it reads too harsh/upfront. Isolate a given voice's F3 carefully — once found accurately, very small EQ moves produce a large clarity change. → [[EQ]], [[LEAD Slot]] (2.5–6 kHz) — Sage Audio, [Mids - the Most Important Frequency Range](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=23pezFNXW_4)
- **Lower a lead vocal's formant to build a distinct low-vocal "double" that's really a thickening layer, not a genuine second take.** Stelios: "at the time that was done using iZotope VocalSynth, and you just lower the formant down a little bit — that's just the common strategy to take the lead vocal and change the formant to help it beef up the actual lead." Reads afterward as a deliberate high-vocal/low-vocal pair rather than a doubled unison. Unowned plugin — see [[Plugin Wishlist]]. → [[Plugin Wishlist]], [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]] — Mix with the Masters (Stelios), [[Vocal and synth production with Stelios]]
- **A second, more harmonically deliberate case of the pitched-duplicate-stack idea below: copy a clip and pitch each copy to a SPECIFIC interval — an octave, a third, a fifth — to build an actual chord voicing out of one source, not just a textural spread.** Emile Haynie, inside Pro Tools Elastic Audio: "maybe I'll do one down, to one down, one, one up one" (three copies, pitched to different octaves), then generalized past octaves — "you could tune it, you could change the key, you could add a third, a fifth, you could start making chords out of like a simple thing... just all Elastic Audio, copying it and changing each one." Where the Shawn Everett entry below spreads copies by rough/arbitrary amounts for evolving texture, this is the same mechanic aimed at real harmony — each copy's pitch is a chosen scale degree, not a texture variance. Owned-lane: duplicate the clip in Ableton, Warp mode on each copy, transpose independently. → [[Layering and Stacking]], [[Sampling and Sound Selection]] — Emile Haynie (Mix with the Masters), [[Arrangement & Processing with Emile Haynie]]
- **Pitch a bank of duplicated layers by a DIFFERENT rough amount each, not uniformly, then crossfade and arrange the results as one evolving texture.** Building a multi-layer metallic texture, five-plus duplicated copies of the same source are each pitch-shifted a different amount (talked through roughly: a slight bump up, one flagged to go down, then −4, −2, unchanged, +2, +4, +6 across the remaining copies), crossfaded at the edit points, then muted/arranged across the timeline to follow the track's dynamic rather than all playing at once — the varied pitch spread (paired with the reverse/invert/splice pass on the same duplicates, see [[kHs Reverser]]) is what keeps a duplicated-source stack from reading as one static layer repeated. *(qualitative — semitone values are best-effort reads off thin, demonstrative captions, not confirmed exact settings; general starting point)* → [[Layering and Stacking]], [[kHs Reverser]] — Shawn Everett (Mix with the Masters), [[Digital Audio Manipulation with Shawn Everett]]
- **Tuning discipline: tune vocals "all the time" because it sounds better, but the transparency bar is total — zero audible artifacts, ever.** Greg Wells: "same with tuning on a vocal — I never want to hear tuning on a vocal. I tune vocals all the time because it sounds better, but I don't want to hear any artifacts or any unnatural thing of tuning." Same "invisible as a technique, load-bearing if removed" standard he applies to doubling and to reverb/delay tails elsewhere in the same breakdown — pitch correction judged by whether it's audible AS correction, not by how much correction is applied. *(qualitative — the clip cuts off before naming a specific plugin technique for achieving that transparency)* → [[Vocal Tuning]], [[Artificial Double Tracking (ADT)]], [[Auto-Tune Pro]] — Greg Wells (Mix with the Masters), [[Bringing vocals out of the speakers with Greg Wells]]
- **Pitch a mono synth note up roughly an octave specifically so it can cut over the drums — a cut-through/arrangement decision made before any tone shaping.** Listening back to a raw keyboard part (mono note, subtle stereo delays sitting low/background), the original register "was going to be hard to get over those drums," so the pitch gets raised about an octave first — register decided before EQ or effects touch the sound. Immediately followed by a mid-range EQ balance pass since the source now spans two registers at once (see [[EQ]]). *(qualitative — no confirmed semitone count or plugin named, auto-caption transcript)* → [[EQ]], [[Width and Stereo]] — Mix with the Masters (Tchad Blake), [Shaping synth sounds with Tchad Blake](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mf8iWnF0JXQ)
- **Named aesthetic philosophy: chop, re-pitch, and stitch together audio in ways that are "impossible to play" — deliberately, because the ear recognizing "not the real thing" is the exciting part, not a flaw to hide.** "You take music or sound... that you record with an instrument, and you start chopping it up and doing things to it that the ear knows is not the real thing... when you take vocals and chop them up, when you take synthesizers or pianos and re-pitch them and stitch them together in a way that is impossible to play — as listeners, we respond to that in a different way. We can hear that things are chopping instantly, because it sounds unnatural — it's exciting. That's the holy grail: finding a sweet spot between something that's real and expressive, and then fucking with it in a way that's kind of alien or robot." Justice pair this with Melodyne's Resynthesize mode used for texture rather than correction. *(qualitative — no specific pitch-shift amounts or chop lengths given)* → [[Melodyne]], [[Sampling and Sound Selection]] — Justice x Kevin Parker (Mix with the Masters), [Behind the Track｜'Neverender' by Justice & Tame Impala](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=emP9EWcqss4)

- **The "discordant edge": push a detune well past your normal tolerance, deliberately, and chop the automation so it reads as unsettled rather than broken.** Two-stage recipe: (1) a **normal/baseline detune** — transparent compression + a cassette/tape-emulation plugin, kept inside a stated **~30-cents-max rule of thumb** *(general starting point, not a hard spec)*; (2) for the actual "discordant edge," set the detune range to a full **semitone** and draw an automation clip with points going **well over 50 cents**, then chop that curve into short jagged segments rather than a smooth ramp — attributed to a James Blake move on "Radio Silence." The chopped-automation step is what separates "discordant" from just "out of tune": a smooth 50-cent drift reads as a mistake, a chopped one reads as a chosen texture. → [[Automation]], [[BED Slot]] — loner, [James Blake's production tricks are GAMECHANGING!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9FX7p23hSlc)

- **Tape varispeed's core mechanism, stated plainly: tempo and pitch move together, in either direction, and the harmonics shift with them** — slow down = softer transients, sped up = tighter groove; even a subtle move (a real cited example: Tears for Fears' "Everybody Wants to Rule the World," sped up on tape by ~4%) is enough to shift how a track feels emotionally without touching the arrangement. Real-world guidance: keep it to a few percent, not a speed-halving (a full 15→7.5 ips change drops a source a full octave — much further than the actual creative move usually goes). - **Varispeed as a mix-fatigue reset, not just a tone effect** — bounce a fatigued mix into a new session and speed it up or down just to hear it with fresh ears; slowing down in particular tends to surface buried melodic ideas that get lost after working a track too long, worth noting and folding back into the real mix. Also usable at the mastering stage: a small speed/pitch shift right before finishing sometimes just sits better than the original. — Zach Scholz, [This One Knob Completely Changed My Music](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OeHzepmklOo)

- **Auto-tune as texture, not correction.** On a super-low setting the phasey/flangy artifact adds character even when the take is in tune — often more tracks are left *untuned* than tuned — Disclosure, [Waterfall](https://youtu.be/sL5CG444qwQ)
- **Tune the lead, leave harmonies/adlibs untuned** to keep human character in a stack — Disclosure, [You've Got To Let Go](https://youtu.be/1iRPvK1gaOI)
- **Varispeed for a different vocal frame:** vary the project speed +9% (and tempo +9 BPM), record, then set it back — you sing in your natural range but the played-back *tone* is shifted, like recording to tape at a different speed (the Beatles slowed the whole "Strawberry Fields" tape in mastering). Gives a melancholic character — Disclosure, [Looking For Love](https://youtu.be/NlJt0pCptGk)
- **Sing in falsetto, then pitch down** so you can deliver a melody the way you mean it rather than straining in chest voice — Disclosure, [Looking For Love](https://youtu.be/NlJt0pCptGk)
- **Automate formant to follow the lyric's meaning** — format down on sad/"going down" lines, up on hopeful lines, so production tracks the emotion — Disclosure, [Looking For Love](https://youtu.be/NlJt0pCptGk)
- **Formant-down a doubled BV** (AlterBoy-style) for an octave-feel layer under the lead — Disclosure, [NO CAP](https://youtu.be/kz5dmb3QdI8)
- **Pitch a word up a few semitones for energy** — nudging a line up ~3 semitones lifts the entry into a verse; small chromatic pitches on single words add hooks — Disclosure, [NO CAP](https://youtu.be/kz5dmb3QdI8)
- **Vocoder for harmony instead of sung harmony** — carry the harmonic movement on a vocoder (iZotope VocalSynth; owned equivalent [[Vocoder]]) rather than stacked sung parts — Disclosure, [Looking For Love](https://youtu.be/NlJt0pCptGk)
