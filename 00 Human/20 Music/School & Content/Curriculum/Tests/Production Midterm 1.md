> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **Bypass the chain and audition other samples — avoid adding more processing**
> Selection is the fix; processing is not.
>
> **Q2.** **Body · pitch envelope · click · saturation**
> *Also accepted:* sub or tone for body · transient or noise for click
>
> **Q3.** **Up 12 to 36 semitones, falling to zero over 15 to 40 ms**
> That fast drop is the "pew" the ear reads as punch.
>
> **Q4.** **To hold the body before the decay starts — roughly 30 to 50 ms**
> With Hold in place the decay curve only ever shapes the tail instead of eating the body.
>
> **Q5.** **F — and a tuned kick reinforces the key rather than fighting it**
> A kick landing on the root every bar can establish a tonality the rest of the track barely states.
>
> **Q6.** **Tune it to the fifth above and let it bend**
> *Also accepted:* tune up an octave · let a click layer carry the audibility · use a knock kick around 100 Hz and leave the sub to the 808
>
> **Q7.** **Because real deviation is informed and random deviation isn't**
> A drummer's timing tracks feel, groove and the role of each hit. Random scatter has none of that structure, so a few hits landing well by luck doesn't rescue the rest.
>
> **Q8.** **Anchors on-grid, secondaries take the variation — the rule follows metric role, not instrument**
> A hi-hat marking a strong beat alone has inherited the anchoring job and stays on-grid; the same hat on offbeats is a secondary. Secondaries go late, never early, by a different amount each time.
>
> **Q9.** **As a second pass after the main pattern is done — they displace the listener's anticipation**
> The ear leans toward an expected strong beat and gets a syncopated ghost instead. That displaced anticipation is the push-pull, which makes ghost notes structural rather than decorative.
>
> **Q10.** **A range, not a value — too wide reads as inconsistent rather than natural**
> Each hit plays back at a random velocity inside the band, so no two loop passes are identical.
>
> **Q11.** **A-B-A-C, with the hats inverted against the kick on each bar**
> Syncopated hats under the simple kick on the A bars; strong-beat hats under the busy kick on B. Whatever the kick is doing, the hats do the opposite, so the two elements keep trading who anchors and who plays around.
>
> **Q12.** **Sparsest Intro, duplicate for A, exactly three changes — an element made more active, or a new one added**
> Each change must be immediately obvious and present throughout. A swap counts; crashes at the start and tom fills at the end don't.
>
> **Q13.** **Finish the groove then write around it · land the roots by ear before decoration**
> The trap in the first is following the kick map instead of the pocket. The trap in the second is treating an inability to hear the roots as a writing problem when it's an ear-training gap.
>
> **Q14.** **Its register — the same line an octave too low goes from fine to mud**
> Move it up rather than processing it.
>
> **Q15.** **Octave up and back, occasionally through a passing tone — fills steal the melody's rhythm**
> The 5th above is the classic passing tone. Copying the rhythm rather than the notes keeps the busyness cohesive.
>
> **Q16.** **Imagine a bassist physically playing it · never two genuinely different basslines at once**
> Doubling the same line on two instruments is a different thing and is fine. The band test is the four-limb constraint applied to one player.
>
> **Q17.** **Showcase lines work over static held chords · busy harmony above means roots only**
> With a vocal or a synth hook on top, the bass supports rather than competes.
>
> **Q18.** **The riffs are the line**
> Static harmony moves all the interest into the fills between roots, and one perfectly placed fill can be the entire event.
>
> **Q19.** **What story the bass is telling · what the keys are telling · whether they talk to each other**
> Keep the harmony dead simple — two chords is enough — and let the arrangement carry it as call and response.
>
> **Q20.** **A 16th grid with notes moved off the kick's strong beats · a chromatic walk between phrases**
> The walk steps through out-of-scale passing tones toward the next section's starting note, so it works whatever the scale is.
>
> **Q21.** **Analyse both, pick one to own the sub, high-pass the other until the clash stops**
> If high-passing the kick guts it, swap in a kick with more inherent high-end character and cut that one instead.
>
> **Q22.** **Pitch it up an octave or more, tune it there, drop it back — sub pitch is inaudible as pitch**
> The ear can't do the job at 40 Hz on most systems, so tuning in place is guessing.
>
> **Q23.** **Synth patch · different synth · real or sampled bass · DI plus amp sim — then limit, saturate, sidechain**
> A limiter rather than a compressor: bass wants a ceiling with its dynamics intact, not gain-riding that pumps.
>
> **Q24.** **The idea was never bad — it was alone. It's asking for a B section, not a new idea**
> Loop-itis is a misdiagnosis rather than a skill gap.
>
> **Q25.** **No amount of subtraction sustains a song — expect most second-loop attempts to be discardable**
> The discards are the cost of the method, not evidence it's going wrong.
>
> **Q26.** **Duplicate the drums and strip everything · write a complementary part · test the return**
> Complementary means different chords, opposite density and a different mood. Coming out of B back into A should feel like arriving home.
>
> **Q27.** **The relative key guarantees the notes fit · the withheld chord guarantees the arrival is unfamiliar**
> Both solve "new but not random" from opposite ends. The relative major of A minor is C — identical scale tones, different centre.
>
> **Q28.** **Arrangement interest is scheduled absence, not added layers**
> The instinct it inverts is that a flat section needs something added; the move is to decide what leaves and when it comes back.
>
> **Q29.** **Start low, build to exactly one apex, pull back after it — holding the peak reads as exhausting**
> A track that reaches maximum and stays there has a plateau rather than a peak.
>
> **Q30.** **Three to four elements simultaneously, not gradually — punctuated with a crash**
> Gradual change reads as drift rather than as a transition.
>
> **Q31.** **House is flat 16 bars · pop is proportional, and the chorus gets longer each return**
> Roughly a 4-bar intro with verse, pre-chorus and chorus each about double it. The chorus is teased first and longest at the very end.
>
> **Q32.** **Most cohesive transitions weren't using risers — the technique is automation across the section**
> Filters, detune, sends and wet/dry automated through both sections, which relocates the transition from an inserted object into a property of the sections.
>
> **Q33.** **One recognisable element carried unchanged straight through it**
> A motif or a signature sound gives the listener something to hold while everything else changes.
>
> **Q34.** **Cut every added layer so only the essential element carries through**
> The emptiness gives the payoff something to land against, and the sudden reduction reads as tension release rather than as a dropout.
>
> **Q35.** **A weak kick wants a different sample · a wrong bass tone wants a different source**
> *Rubric (5):* names Course 10's version — bypass the chain and audition other samples rather than processing a weak kick (2) · names Course 11's version, the sound ladder, or swapping a kick that can't survive a high-pass (2) · states the shared principle that a chain refines a source that's already close and can't transform a wrong one (1)
> In Course 10 it's the opening claim: a kick that isn't hitting is almost always the wrong sample, so bypass everything and audition replacements before touching a device. In Course 11 it shows up twice. The sound ladder is the same instruction with more rungs — synth patch, different synth, real or sampled bass, DI plus amp sim — because a bass patch has more ways to be replaced than a one-shot does. And the sub-ownership decision carries it too: a kick that falls apart when you high-pass it is telling you it had nothing above the sub to begin with, so the answer is a different kick rather than a more careful filter. The shared principle is that the fix is upstream. Processing refines something already close and cannot convert a wrong choice into a right one, and the hours spent trying are expensive precisely because they feel productive the whole time.
>
> **Q36.** **Because bass locks to the pocket, and there's no pocket until the groove exists**
> *Rubric (5):* says step 1 is to finish the drum groove and write the bass around it (1) · says the bass locks to the pocket rather than to the kick map, so it needs a real groove to lock to (2) · says rhythmic choices made with no drums playing are provisional and a finished loop will overrule them (2)
> The dependency is real because the thing the bass is being written against doesn't exist yet. A bassline's rhythm is not a property of the bassline — it's a relationship to where the kick and snare actually land and how the hats sit around them, which is to say it's a relationship to the pocket. Write it first and you're writing against an imagined groove, so every rhythmic decision is provisional and the finished drums will overrule most of them. The specific failure is worse than just wasted work: a line written in silence tends to lock to an even grid, and when the drums arrive it either fights them or has to be rewritten from scratch. Note that the dependency isn't "follow the kick" — that's the trap inside step 1. The bass locks to the pocket, which sometimes means landing with the kick and often means not.
>
> **Q37.** **Choke groups inside a loop · stripping before a drop · scheduled absence across the arrangement**
> *Rubric (5):* names subtraction inside a loop — choke groups, thinning rather than stacking past three changes, or the five-to-seven-sound referee (2) · names stripping every added layer immediately before a payoff (1) · names scheduled absence and staggered entrances across the whole arrangement (1) · says all three work because contrast rather than absolute density is what a listener perceives (1)
> At loop scale it's choke groups cutting an overlapping tail, and the tier method's instruction to thin out rather than stack once three changes are in — delete the closed hats sitting under a new open hat instead of adding a fourth element. At section scale it's the bars before a drop, where everything the build stacked comes out at once so the payoff has somewhere to arrive. At song scale it's the whole arrangement: entrances staggered so one idea holds attention for minutes, and an energy curve that starts deliberately low and comes back down after its apex. What they share is that none of them is about how much is playing at any moment. Each is about the size of the difference between one moment and the next, because that difference is what the listener actually perceives — which is why the reflex to add is wrong at all three scales.
>
> **Q38.** **Both build from a deliberately sparse starting point in a fixed number of obvious steps**
> *Rubric (5):* maps the tier method's sparsest-Intro-plus-three-changes onto the energy curve's start-low-and-build (2) · maps the tier method's "each change obvious and present throughout" onto the curve's three-to-four elements changing simultaneously at a boundary (1) · maps B onto the apex and the Outro onto the pull-back, then names the real difference — the energy curve mandates exactly *one* apex across a whole song, where the tiers are states with no one-peak rule (2)
> The correspondence is close enough that the second is the first scaled up. Both start deliberately below where instinct would start, because starting low is what buys room to build. Both change in discrete, obvious steps rather than gradually — three changes per tier at loop scale, three or four elements at once at a boundary — for the same reason: a change the listener doesn't notice isn't a change. Both cap the number, and both say to thin rather than stack past the cap. The shape maps too, further than it first looks: B is the tier method's apex, and the Outro is its pull-back — strip aggressively, dropping the busiest and most recently added elements first, so it reads as an ebb rather than a reset to the Intro. What the song scale adds is a constraint rather than a stage. The energy curve mandates exactly *one* apex across the whole track, because a peak is only a peak relative to everything around it. The tiers carry no such rule — they're states you can move between, and nothing stops a loop having two equally strong ones. So the discipline that has to be imported when you scale up is the refusal to let a second peak match the first.
>
> **Q39.** **They share a fundamental, so they'll sum unpredictably — sidechain, or separate them in register**
> *Also accepted:* check polarity first · decide who owns the sub and high-pass the other · tune the kick to the fifth and leave the root to the 808
>
> **Q40.** **The joins — carry one element unchanged across each boundary and strip the layers before each payoff**
> *Rubric (5):* says the sections exist but the transitions between them don't, which is a separate job (1) · names carrying one recognisable element unchanged across the boundary (2) · names stripping the added layers immediately before the payoff so it arrives against space (2)
> Three finished things and no connections between them is the ordinary state of a project that's about to stall. Sections are a contrast problem and joins are a continuity problem, and having solved the first well can make the second worse — the more the B section differs from A, and the more the bass and drums change with it, the more the boundary reads as an edit rather than a transition. The two devices split the job. Carry one recognisable element — the motif, the signature sound, whatever the section is identifiable by — straight through unchanged, so there's a thread the listener holds while everything else moves. Then strip every added layer in the last bar or two before each payoff, so the new section lands against space rather than against a wall. Automation across the boundary is the third piece and the one that does the most work; a riser, if it's still wanted, goes on last and only once the join already works.
