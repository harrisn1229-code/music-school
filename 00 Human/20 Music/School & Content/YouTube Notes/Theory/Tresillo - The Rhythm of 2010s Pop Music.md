# Tresillo - The Rhythm of 2010s Pop Music

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DZ_yT_ukkKA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DZ_yT_ukkKA)

## Synopsis

A traced history of the tresillo (3-3-2) rhythm — already documented structurally in [[18 Rhythms you should know]] — from its sub-Saharan African origin through the Atlantic slave trade into Cuba, then outward through European classical music, American ragtime/jazz, and Jamaican dancehall into 2010s mainstream pop (the "Despacito" rhythm).

## Techniques

- **The full lineage, as a chain of small concrete transformations rather than one static pattern**: sub-Saharan Africa → brought to Cuba via the slave trade, becoming the rhythmic foundation of Cuban **contradanza** → reaches Europe as the **habanera** (contradanza + an added backbeat — the one concrete change habanera makes to bare tresillo, matching [[7 Rhythms you should know]]'s habanera entry), heard in Bizet's *Carmen* → carried from New Orleans to Havana by ferry-traveling musicians and absorbed into ragtime/early jazz, audible in the left-hand bassline of Jelly Roll Morton's "New Orleans Blues" → taught to stride pianist James P. Johnson by South Carolina dock workers, who altered it into the **Charleston rhythm** by removing tresillo's last beat (this matches [[18 Rhythms you should know]]'s Charleston entry — "the first two notes of a tresillo" — from the other direction: Charleston = tresillo minus one note) → resurfaces via Jamaican **dancehall**, becoming so associated with the style it's often just called the **Dembow rhythm** (after Shabba Ranks' "Dembow") → spreads to **reggaetón** (Puerto Rico) → mainstream pop breakthrough via Caribbean/Latin artists (Sean Paul, Beenie Man, Daddy Yankee, Shakira) → by 2015–2016 a dominant rhythmic influence on US/European pop production generally (the "Despacito" era).
- **Practical takeaway for production**: three of the vault's already-documented rhythms (tresillo, habanera, Charleston) are not independent patterns but successive edits of the same 3-3-2 cell — habanera = tresillo + backbeat, Charleston = tresillo − last beat. Naming a groove as "tresillo-derived" is a faster way to communicate a rhythmic idea than describing the hit pattern from scratch.
