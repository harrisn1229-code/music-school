# Interleaved Practice, Chunking, and Deliberate-Practice Drills

Part of [[Song Practice]]'s practice-methodology material — Daniel Seriff's general-purpose deliberate-practice toolkit for *how* to structure any practice session, instrument-agnostic in application even though demonstrated on guitar. Where [[The Five Pillars of Jazz Guitar Practice]] answers **what** a complete jazz-guitar practice system needs to cover (ear skills, vocabulary, technique, theory, repertoire) and [[Three-Stage Warm-Up (Physical, Mental, Resonance)]] (same channel, Daniel Seriff) answers how to warm up before a session, this note answers **how to drill any single piece of material** once you've decided to work on it — the mechanics of interleaving, chunking, hands-separate isolation, trouble-spot targeting, edge-of-ability loading, and mental rehearsal. Several pieces here overlap in spirit with [[The Five Pillars of Jazz Guitar Practice]]'s "long-term mindset" (spaced short reps beat cramming) and [[Backward Chaining Practice]]'s clean-reps-before-expanding logic — cross-linked below rather than duplicated.

## Interleaved practice, not massed practice

**Massed practice — repeating the same thing over and over trying to perfect it — is named as the single biggest mistake guitar players make.** It feels productive (fingers moving, time being logged) but the brain checks out and you stop actually learning; you're going through the motions, not learning.

**The fix: interleaved practice — rotate between several different things within one session** instead of drilling one thing for 20 minutes straight. *(Worked example: 2–3 minutes each on five different things, then cycle back through all five again next session.)* Why it works: the act of cycling back to something forces **recall/retrieval practice** — every time you return to an item you're quizzing yourself on it, and that retrieval attempt is what builds deeper learning than uninterrupted repetition. It will feel harder and less immediately satisfying than massed drilling ("feeds your ego a little less") — that discomfort is explicitly framed as the signal you're in the zone where the learning is actually happening, not a reason to retreat to something comfortable. Sourced to *Make It Stick* and *Peak* (Anders Ericsson). — Daniel Seriff, [[How to get SO GOOD at guitar it feels ILLEGAL (Using Neuroscience)]]

**Cited research numbers for exactly how much this costs/pays off (a second, independent source converging on the same mechanism):** interleaved practice **roughly tripled subsequent test performance vs. blocked practice — 63% vs. 20%** in one study cited — but at a real short-term cost: **accuracy *during* interleaved practice itself dropped to ~60% vs. ~89% for blocked practice.** In other words interleaving makes the practice session visibly messier and more mistake-prone while it's happening, and that's precisely what makes the later test/performance better — this is the concrete number behind "feeds your ego a little less" above. Researchers also checked whether interleaving's benefit was just spaced repetition in disguise and found it isn't — **interleaving (mixing the order) carries a separate, additional benefit on top of spacing (widening the gap between touches).** Much of the underlying research is on motor skills specifically (directly relevant to an instrument) — cited example: interleaved batting practice (mixed pitch types) outperformed blocked batting practice (all fastballs, then all curveballs) on later hitting performance. — Jared, [Spaced Repetition Method & Interleaving Practice: Boost Your Music Retention 2-3x!](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v-kaN9ViRCw)

**Interleaving the *order itself*, not just which items rotate:** with three items (Song/exercise A, B, C), don't settle into a fixed cycle A→B→C→A→B→C — change the permutation each pass (A-B-C, then B-C-A, then C-A-B…), which scales to real variety once you have a real batch of material (12 songs → many possible orderings). The opposite, **block practice** (AAA, then BBB, then CCC, even within one sitting), is the same failure mode as massed practice above, just applied across multiple items instead of one.

**A remembered (unverified by the source, flagged as such) fighter-pilot training anecdote** illustrates the same practice-vs-performance tradeoff vividly: pilots trained with heavy context-switching/chaos/surprise did far worse *in training* than pilots trained on a clean linear curriculum — but dominated when the two groups then competed against each other, because the linear group had over-optimized for one fixed context and hadn't built the skill of handling the unfamiliar. — Jared, [Spaced Repetition Method & Interleaving Practice: Boost Your Music Retention 2-3x!](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v-kaN9ViRCw)

**Interleaving degrades when it silently turns into a fixed autopilot path** — a named failure mode from teaching improv specifically: a student settles on one arpeggio-through-the-changes approach that "works," runs it identically every time, and stops actually improvising — the path becomes a drilled lick rather than real-time decision-making, and flexibility to jump to a different note or recall *why* it's the right chord tone is lost. Fix: **interleave the *approach*, not just the material** — vary which technique/lens is applied to the same changes, not only which song is being played. — Jared, [Spaced Repetition Method & Interleaving Practice: Boost Your Music Retention 2-3x!](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v-kaN9ViRCw)

## Spaced repetition — the forgetting-curve schedule

**Definition: "gradually increase the time between review sessions, reinforcing the material just as you're about to forget it."** Also called spaced learning, distributed practice, spaced practice — same mechanism, several names — and it's the mechanism underneath this note's [[The Practice Priority Pyramid|Rotation principle]] and this note's own 15–20 minute session-chunking rule above.

**The forgetting-curve shape (illustrative, not a strict prescription — the source is explicit he doesn't personally chase exact intervals):**
- Learn something new → ~100% retention immediately, then it drops off fast.
- Review again quickly (before it's gone) → back to ~100%, and the next safe gap is a little longer (illustrative: ~2 days).
- Each successful review **widens the gap needed before the next one** — illustratively 2 days → ~7 days → longer again — the curve flattens more each cycle.
- **After roughly the 5th review cycle, a piece can go ~2 months between touches and still hold** — treated as the point it's genuinely in long-term memory. *(General starting shape, not a fixed schedule — adapt freely.)*
- **Real-world use counts as review** — if a piece gets played in a gig, taught, or referenced in a jam, that's a review cycle without a dedicated practice slot, and it extends how long the piece can go untouched afterward.

— Jared, [Spaced Repetition Method & Interleaving Practice: Boost Your Music Retention 2-3x!](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v-kaN9ViRCw)

**Independent convergence from a bass-side channel, same core spacing claim, plus a motor-learning/sleep mechanism not detailed above:** frequent distributed practice sessions beat rare "mega" sessions — cited mechanisms are the rest between sessions helping consolidate memories, and sleep specifically helping the brain finish learning new motor skills *(qualitative claim from the source, not a cited study — treat as a general spaced-practice/motor-learning finding rather than a sourced statistic)*. The source's own comparison: his bass practice stayed frequent ("rare that I don't pick it up at least a few times a week") for years, while his guitar practice — tied to an unrealistic "mega session or nothing" standard — went weeks or months untouched. **The practical rule stated directly: "put in the time you have, not the time you wish you had, and just show up as often as you can"** — the same "consistency over intensity" conclusion as this note's own spacing/chunking material, arrived at from a bass-practice angle rather than guitar. — BassBuzz, [[10 Years of Bass Advice in 10 Minutes]]

**Case study for spacing at the extreme — biweekly-only, zero practice between sessions, ~1 year:** a private student who never practiced between lessons, seen only every two weeks, picked apart a technically demanding Steely Dan solo ("Bodhisattva") in ~30-minute chunks purely during lesson time, with **neither teacher nor student touching the material in between.** After ~6 months to a year of this, **both** could play the entire solo — despite each session opening with near-total forgetting of the last one. **The mechanism: every cold re-encounter forced a genuine re-learn, and each of those forced re-learns reinforced the material more deeply than the same total time spent unbroken would have.** — Jared, [[Spaced Repetition Method & Interleaving Practice: Boost Your Music Retention 2-3x!]]

## The batch/collection scheduling method (concrete "how" for spacing + interleaving together)

**Don't work one piece to completion before starting the next — turn a single target song into a batch of ~10–12 items** (6 is fine too) and treat the whole batch as one project (a "concept album," not "a single"). Work a little on item 1, move to item 2, work a little, move on, and so on; when rotation brings you back to item 1, expect to have mostly forgotten it and treat that as the intended review moment, not a setback, before adding anything new to it.

**Worked scheduling contrast — "learn 12 songs, one per month":** the wrong version is literally finishing song 1 in month 1, then starting song 2 in month 2, etc. The right version under this method: **treat all 12 as one rotating batch from day one**, spacing and interleaving the touches across all of them from the start — claimed to converge on *all 12 solid* in **less total time** than the sequential version, because each piece gets ongoing spaced review instead of being learned once and abandoned. Progress feels slower moment-to-moment than "finishing" one thing fast (a "candy" satisfaction) — that's the expected, correct-feeling tradeoff, the same shape as the practice-vs-test accuracy tradeoff above. Generalizes past repertoire to techniques, exercises, and multi-course curricula — rotate between courses rather than finishing one before starting the next (same guidance given independently in [[How I Organize My Guitar Practice (So I Never Want to Stop)]]).

**Choose material worth reviewing forever.** Since anything not actively used eventually needs a cold-start review regardless, the compounding payoff of this method only accrues on pieces you actually want to keep returning to — material picked purely to "check off a list" doesn't get the same benefit because there's no pull to revisit it. Worked examples of pieces the source has cold-started and relearned dozens of times over years, each time baking deeper into long-term memory: Bach's Cello Suite No. 1 Prelude, Tárrega's "Lágrima" and "Recuerdos de la Alhambra," Jerry Reed's "The Claw," "Autumn Leaves," "Georgia on My Mind."

— Jared, [[Spaced Repetition Method & Interleaving Practice: Boost Your Music Retention 2-3x!]]

## The "principle of added stress" (context-variation beyond just item order)

Credited to a classical piano professor's lecture: **deliberately add controlled stress/distraction into practice sessions so real performance conditions (nerves, an unfamiliar room, an audience) feel less foreign when they happen for real** — concrete examples the professor used on himself: friends vacuuming around him or physically getting in his way while he practiced, background noise, changed lighting. Framed as the same underlying principle as interleaving applied more broadly than item order: **a fixed, always-identical practice context creates an efficient but brittle "autopilot"** — the same reason you recognize your dentist at the office but might not on the street. *(Flagged by the source himself as a tangent — concrete but not deeply developed; a starting point for varying practice environment, not a worked system.)* — Jared, [[Spaced Repetition Method & Interleaving Practice: Boost Your Music Retention 2-3x!]]

## The practice journal — plan forward, don't log backward

A plain notebook or a document is enough; the discipline that matters is **writing down what you're going to work on *before* the session, not logging what you did after.** You'll typically be revisiting the same items tomorrow and the day after to bring them to an adequate level, so the journal is a rolling forward plan, not a diary.

- Track concrete parameters in it: **tempos, keys, tracks/material played over.**
- **Interleaved practice only works if you actually know what you're rotating between** — without a journal you sit down and "pick around," which defaults you back to whatever's already comfortable (the material you've played for years).
- Two payoffs of writing it down in advance: (1) **you show up with a plan** instead of improvising your session on the spot; (2) **you start to notice patterns** — which things keep coming up as challenging, which you've been avoiding, which you thought you knew but keep getting wrong when you come back to them.
- **Rotate across roughly five categories** per session: skills and technique, theory and ears, creative application, and tunes/tracks/licks/lines (the source names four cleanly and references "five different categories" without fully separating the fifth in the transcript — treat the fifth as likely a split between "tunes/tracks" and "licks/lines," and adjust the category list to fit your own instrument/style rather than trying to force an exact five-way split).

— Daniel Seriff, [[How to get SO GOOD at guitar it feels ILLEGAL (Using Neuroscience)]]

## Session length: 15–20 minute chunks, enforced with a hard timer

**You should not practice in long unbroken stretches — 15 to 20 minutes per chunk is about the maximum** before focus drops off completely and you're "just spinning your wheels." Multiple shorter sessions spread across the day will always beat one long marathon.

- **Use a physical timer, hard cutoff, not a soft "when it feels done" stopping point.** Seriff uses a cheap kitchen timer and moves on the instant it goes off, no exceptions — block sizes named: 30 seconds, 1 minute, 2 minutes, 3 minutes, 5 minutes (pick a size per item, not one fixed size for everything).
- Traced to *The Musician's Way* by **Gerald Klickstein** (the transcript's auto-captions garble the name as "Jeroen Klijsen," but Seriff separately credits Gerald Klickstein by name later in the same video for the hands-separate method below, and both references describe the same teacher, so this is very likely one person the captions rendered two different ways — treat "Jeroen Klijsen" as a caption artifact for Gerald Klickstein).
- **This is the same underlying rule as [[The Five Pillars of Jazz Guitar Practice]]'s day-to-day spaced-vs-massed guidance** ("spaced daily reps beat one long cram session" — 30 min/day beats one 2-hour block, with the same hard-timer mechanic) — two different teachers converging on the identical mechanism and even a similar timer-based enforcement method.

— Daniel Seriff, [[How to get SO GOOD at guitar it feels ILLEGAL (Using Neuroscience)]]

## The additive approach (chunking)

Anders Ericsson's *Peak* calls this **chunking**; Seriff's practical name for it is the **additive approach**, and frames it as one of the most practical applications of deliberate practice there is. Generalizes to any material — a chord progression, a lick, anything being learned — regardless of difficulty.

**Method, worked on a 4-chord progression (G, Em, D, C):**
1. **Don't try to play the whole thing first.** Start with just the first piece alone — here, the G chord. Don't play through it, just play the one chord, 3–5 times, with a small pause between reps, until it's solid.
2. **Add the next piece and drill the pair, still without playing through the rest.** G then D — not the full progression, just the two-piece unit, pausing between reps.
3. **Add the third piece the same way.** G, Em (as the third addition in the demonstrated order) — stop, repeat, pause between reps.
4. **Add the final piece, arriving at the complete progression only after each smaller piece was already solid on its own.**

*(general starting point: 3–5 clean reps per addition before adding the next piece, with a deliberate pause between each rep — the source doesn't give a stricter numeric bar than this)*

— Daniel Seriff, [[How to get SO GOOD at guitar it feels ILLEGAL (Using Neuroscience)]]

**Independent convergence, bass side — chunking applied to fixing a fretting-hand fingering rather than a chord progression.** BassBuzz's "chunk it" tip for de-overwhelming a flying-fingers fix on a one-finger-per-fret bass exercise: work a small handful of notes (e.g. just the first five of the riff) in slow motion, as much as needed, until it feels smooth and comfortable, then move on to the next chunk, then the next, then start piecing them together — same core shape as Seriff's method above (small solid pieces first, whole thing last), applied to a fretting-hand coordination fix rather than a chord-progression memorization task. Full exercise: [[One-Finger-Per-Fret 5-Level Daily Drill]]. — BassBuzz, [[Practice THIS Daily (10 Mins)]]

## Hands-separate practice

For anything technically demanding, credited to studying classical guitar with **Gerald Klickstein**:

1. **Work the fretting-hand shape alone first**, with no picking/strumming at all, until it feels solid.
2. **Mute the strings and drill the picking/strum pattern alone**, isolated from the fretting hand entirely. *(Worked example strum pattern: down-down-up-up-down-up.)*
3. **Combine without sounding it yet:** fret the chord shapes with the left hand while **miming the strum pattern in the air** with the right hand — full coordination rehearsal before the two hands are actually allowed to make sound together.
4. Only after that does the actual playing happen.

**Aim for 3–5 clean repetitions at each stage before moving to the next one.** Seriff still uses this himself, not just with students, whenever something is proving genuinely challenging.

— Daniel Seriff, [[How to get SO GOOD at guitar it feels ILLEGAL (Using Neuroscience)]]

**Independent convergence, different instrument and channel — bass-specific worked example, applied to speed rather than a new coordination skill:** BassBuzz recommends the same hands-separate isolation specifically for recovering technique that backslides under tempo ("death by speed") — solid slow-tempo technique commonly falls apart once a line speeds up, and working each hand alone first is offered as the fix rather than just "practice slower." Mechanics: **mute the strings with the plucking hand and drill fretting alone**, humming the bassline's pitches rather than relying on audible confirmation from actually sounding the notes — this isolates and trains flying-fingers control and fretting pressure without the plucking hand's timing demands competing for attention; then **flip it — mute with the fretting hand and drill plucking alone**, again humming along, reading the tab to confirm which string each note falls on and noting which plucking transitions feel hardest; only recombine once each hand is independently solid. Named payoff: with both hands "not sucking by themselves," ramping up to full speed afterward is a much smoother process than going in cold on both hands at once. — BassBuzz, [[Practice THIS Daily (10 Min Technique Fix)]]

## Target the trouble spot directly — don't restart from the top

**The average player, on a mistake mid-passage, either restarts from the beginning or glazes over the error** — which means the opening of a piece/progression/song gets drilled far more than the actual problem material, and the real trouble spot never gets fixed. Result: players get very good at the first section of a piece specifically *because* they always restart there.

**Instead: the instant something breaks down, go directly to that specific spot** — "every time you hit the C chord, you botch it → go straight to the C" — and isolate and drill just that trouble spot rather than replaying the whole passage around it hoping it smooths out.

— Daniel Seriff, [[How to get SO GOOD at guitar it feels ILLEGAL (Using Neuroscience)]]

**Independent convergence, different channel, same source material:** Jared (Sound Guitar Lessons) arrives at the identical principle citing the same Ericsson/*Peak* material directly — "push yourself well outside your comfort zone and see what breaks down first, then design a practice technique aimed at improving that particular weakness" — plus a case study (research subject "Steve," extending digit-span memory) showing an apparent hard ceiling was really just one or two specific problem components, not the whole skill maxing out; also cites a doctor-performance study showing skill can *decline*, not just plateau, once a task stops being challenging. Full extraction on [[Repetition with Manipulation (Getting Past a Plateau)]]. — Jared, [[How to Get Better at Guitar - The science of getting past a plateau]]

## Practice at the edge of your ability ("deep practice")

Credited to Daniel Coyle's *The Talent Code*: **if material feels fully comfortable and familiar, you are not practicing — you're just playing, and you're not growing.** Real practice means pushing tempos and material that are just slightly out of reach; sitting down and gravitating to songs you've played for years is explicitly called out as *not* practice.

- **Discomfort at the edge of ability is a signal, not a problem** — that's specifically where improvement lives.
- Neuroscience framing given (qualitative, not verifiable from a transcript — treat as narrative color rather than a cited claim): practice at the edge of capability is what causes neural pathways to be wrapped in **myelin**, speeding brain-to-finger signal transmission — offered as the reason advanced players "look effortless." Rejects "muscle memory" as a literal mechanism — the memory/skill lives in the brain's neural pathways, not "in the hands."

— Daniel Seriff, [[How to get SO GOOD at guitar it feels ILLEGAL (Using Neuroscience)]]

## The illusion of knowing

**Recognizing a concept intellectually is not the same as being able to play it.** A student hears something familiar, thinks "I know that," and moves on — but conceptual recognition ≠ mastery. **Real mastery = playing it on demand, cold, without hesitation**, including outside comfortable/familiar contexts; if a skill only comes out under familiar conditions, it isn't mastered yet.

Corollary called out directly against the format this very video is in: **watching lesson videos alone will not make you better** — you have to put your hands on the instrument, sound bad, diagnose why, and fix it. — Daniel Seriff, [[How to get SO GOOD at guitar it feels ILLEGAL (Using Neuroscience)]]

## Mental models — audiation and internal rehearsal away from the instrument

**The ability to hear music clearly in your head before playing it** — visualizing the fretboard, saying note names, seeing triads/CAGED shapes — without touching the guitar. Framed as the most underused form of practice available, and usable anywhere, any time, entirely away from the instrument. The best players can rehearse whole passages internally because their mental models/internal representations are strong.

**Two concrete drills:**
- **Hum or sing a phrase before playing it** — "if you can't hum it or sing it, you shouldn't be playing it."
- **Close your eyes and visualize the fingering before executing it.**

Cross-referenced by Seriff to *Peak*, *The Art of Learning*, and *The Inner Game of Music* as a recurring theme across the deliberate-practice literature.

— Daniel Seriff, [[How to get SO GOOD at guitar it feels ILLEGAL (Using Neuroscience)]]
