# 5 songs with unusual chord progressions

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=URO0EfmxYw0)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=URO0EfmxYw0)

## Synopsis

Five progressions distinctive enough that Bennett couldn't find other well-known songs using them, each unusual for a different reason: a tritone-related pair, a deliberately harsh voicing choice, a circle-of-fifths chain that doubles as a key-change setup, a tritone leap saved by a common tone, and a chromatic mediant used as both harmonic color and a modulation pivot.

## Techniques

- **"Subterranean Homesick Alien" (Radiohead)** — Dm → A♭ (2nd inversion, E♭ in the bass) → Dm, a tritone-related pair with no shared diatonic key, deliberately unnatural-sounding to underscore the song's alien-abduction theme. (Already documented in full on [[Circle of Fifths]].)
- **"Michelle" (The Beatles)** — F → B♭7♯9 (the "Hendrix chord" — combines the major 3rd D and the ♯9/D♭ simultaneously for deliberate dissonance; frequently mislabeled B♭m7 in chord charts, a smoother voicing the Beatles specifically avoided) → E♭6 → Ddim7 → Bdim7 → C → Bdim7 → C. Diminished-7th chord inversions are enharmonically OTHER diminished-7 chords — Ddim7 and Bdim7 share the exact same four notes; only which note sits in the bass differs.
- **"Mrs. Robinson" intro (Simon & Garfunkel)** — F♯7 (5 bars) → B7 → E → A → D (1st inversion) → Bm → F♯7 → E7. Nearly the entire progression is a circle-of-fifths chain (F♯→B→E→A→D, each a descending 5th), which also functions as a key-change setup: the closing E7 pivots from the intro's key of F♯ into the verse's key of A.
- **"Sir Duke" chorus (Stevie Wonder)** — B → Fm7 → Emaj7. B→Fm7 is a tritone leap (the same maximally-unrelated relationship as the Radiohead example), but it works because the two chords share a common tone (D♯/E♭, present in both) that glues them together across the harsh root movement.
- **"Light My Fire" verse (The Doors)** — Am7–F♯m7, a chromatic mediant (roots a major 3rd apart, no shared diatonic key) that still works because the chords share 50% of their notes (A and E — root and 5th). Also readable as modal interchange, since F♯m7 is the diatonic vi of A major even though the verse sits in A minor. The same F♯m7 then serves as a pivot chord into the chorus's unrelated key of D major (diatonic there too), smoothing an otherwise-abrupt key change.
