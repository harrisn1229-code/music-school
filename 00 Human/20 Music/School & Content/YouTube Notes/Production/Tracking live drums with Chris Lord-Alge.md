# Tracking live drums with Chris Lord-Alge

## Synopsis

A short, in-the-room Mix with the Masters clip of Chris Lord-Alge (CLA) getting live drum sounds up from scratch — not mixing a finished multitrack, but the earlier "getting sounds" stage: a drummer plays the song's groove while CLA works mic by mic (kick, snare top/bottom, overheads, toms one at a time, room, hi-hat), listening in solo and making calls in real time. It's a compact but genuinely dense demonstration of a tracking-stage philosophy that's the flip side of everything this vault's mixing-stage notes cover: **decide as much as possible with mics and a good performance, and spend the mix-stage EQ/dynamics budget only on the two mics that need it (kick and snare).** Concrete moves: opening up a snare that was tracked too dark ("never be shy with EQ"), catching a too-loud bottom-snare mic and a bus limiter check before levels get printed, treating the overhead as the single most important mic in the room ("that's the whole picture of the record"), checking toms one at a time with consistent EQ moves across the set but different pan positions, and — the standout single fix — solving a hi-hat mic's excess proximity-effect low end by physically moving the mic back a foot instead of reaching for an EQ cut. On the room mic he states a clean philosophy: no reverb (the room already gives you the room), just a little bus compression from a hardware Fairchild he has on hand. Every number given is qualitative (no dB/Hz/ratio stated) but every move is concrete and actionable — this is a "where do I spend my attention" lesson more than a settings tutorial, and it's the clearest source in the vault so far for how a live-tracking session actually gets decided at the mic, before a DAW plugin gets touched.

## Techniques

- **"Never be shy with EQ" — stated the moment the snare's top mic sounded "a little too deep" on first listen.** CLA had a "big fat snare" preset-style setting loaded, un-committed, and opened it up to hear it more raw first; hearing it read dark, his stated rule is to reach for EQ decisively rather than tentatively second-guess a correction. → [[EQ]], [[Drum Mic Technique]], [[1 Drums Snare-Clap Track|Drums Snare-Clap Track]]
- **Direct mics get the EQ heat; ambient mics (overhead, room) mostly don't get touched at all.** Explicit stated philosophy: "the important thing about cutting drums is the direct mics — the kick and snare — that's where you're gonna put some heat and EQ'ing. The room tracks... they're so good you're not gonna EQ those, so it's easy — left for later." A clean two-tier mental model for where to spend mix-stage processing effort on a live kit. → [[EQ]], [[Drum Mic Technique]], [[2 Drums Group|Drums Group]]
- **Snare bottom mic caught too loud in the blend during the initial level pass** — flagged and pulled down relative to the top mic before moving on, part of getting basic levels right before any real EQ/dynamics decisions. → [[Drum Mic Technique]], [[Gain Staging]]
- **A bus limiter checked on the drum bus during the tracking pass itself, not deferred to mixing** — "take our bus limiter, make sure — gotta make sure you're doing right," confirming levels are safe before printing. Treated as part of getting sounds, alongside mic checks, not a later mix-stage add. → [[Limiting]], [[Drum Mic Technique]], [[3 Drums Bus|Drums Bus]]
- **Overhead mic named as the single most important mic on the kit** — "overhead is real important, that's the whole picture of the record." Framed as capturing the overall balance/character of the whole kit at once, distinct from any single close mic. → [[Drum Mic Technique]], [[2 Drums Group|Drums Group]]
- **Tom-by-tom check: single hits per drum, hard-panned differently per tom, but EQ kept consistent across the set.** Toms checked one at a time with individual hits to confirm basic levels are right, panned to distinct positions (one hard right, the next center — working across the kit), while deliberately trying "to keep your EQ changes from tom to tom" minimal — cohesion across the set matters more than tuning each tom individually. *(qualitative — no pan/Hz values given)* → [[Drum Mic Technique]], [[EQ]]
- **Room track soloed and auditioned on its own before any decision is made about it** — "check out the room track now, let's see what this thing does... that's a room sound... and solo," reacting positively to what it captures on its own before folding it back into the mix. → [[Drum Mic Technique]], [[Depth and Space]]
- **Room mic processing kept to exactly two moves: no reverb, and light bus compression from a hardware Fairchild.** "I got a Fairchild and it kicks ass, so why not use it. So this is no reverb, just a little bus compression." The room mic already supplies real room ambience, so no artificial reverb is added on top of it — compression only, to glue/control it. → [[Limiting]], [[Compression]], [[Drum Mic Technique]], [[Vintage Gear Worklist]]
- **Overall stated philosophy: keep the miking simple, get a good drummer, and the real work is in the BLEND, not the plugins.** "The secret here is, like, look, if you keep the miking simple and you have a good drummer, it's really easy... it's all about how you blend it." A direct statement that level relationships between mics do more work than processing choices once the mics/performance are right. → [[Drum Mic Technique]], [[Gain Staging]]
- **Hi-hat mic proximity-effect fix solved by physically repositioning the mic, not by EQ.** "I think my hi-hat mic is just a little too — proximity effect on this... all I want you to do is just pull the hi-hat back a foot." The symptom was excess low end from the hi-hat mic sitting too close (a directional mic's bass response rising with proximity) — fixed at the source by increasing mic distance rather than cutting the buildup afterward with an EQ. → [[Drum Mic Technique]], [[Vocal Mic Technique]], [[1 Drums Hat Track|Drums Hat Track]]
