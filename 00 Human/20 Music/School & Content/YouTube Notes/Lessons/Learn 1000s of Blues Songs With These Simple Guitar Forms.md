# Learn 1000s of Blues Songs With These Simple Guitar Forms

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kA2u0PETrP8)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kA2u0PETrP8)

## Synopsis

A Daniel Seriff lesson (same channel/key/forms as the vault's existing [[Essential 12 Bar Blues Guitar Lesson]]) teaching the two most common 12-bar blues progressions — the **dominant blues** (I7-IV7-V7) and the **minor blues** (i7-iv7 + ♭VI7-V7 turnaround) — but this time the actual teaching payload is a **fretboard navigation trick**, not just the chord sequence: a small "L-shaped" root map that lets you find the IV and V chord roots purely by *moving the same chord shape*, once you know where the I chord's root sits. Worked from two different starting points (root on the low E string, root on the A string) so the same L-shape works no matter which string the tonic happens to fall on. This is the concrete, fretboard-mechanical layer the vault's existing blues notes ([[Blues Form and Harmony]], [[Essential 12 Bar Blues Guitar Lesson]]) don't yet have — those document the *harmony*, this one documents *where your hand goes*. Also makes a useful terminology correction: the common I-IV-V dominant blues gets casually called "minor blues" by ear (because minor pentatonic solos over it), but harmonically it's a **dominant blues** — every chord is a major/dominant chord, not a minor one — and the *actually*-minor form (i7-iv7) is a separate progression with its own turnaround.

## What's taught

- **Terminology clarification (new to the vault in this exact framing):** people often call the plain I7-IV7-V7 blues form a "minor blues" because the minor pentatonic scale solos so well over it — but it's **not** minor; all three chords (A7, D7, E7) are dominant 7ths, which are major-quality chords (major 3rd). The better name is **dominant blues**. The form Seriff *does* call minor blues is the separate i7-iv7 progression below. Reinforces [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *Why every chord is a dominant 7th* from the student-confusion angle rather than the theory angle. *(~5:40–6:20)*
- **The dominant blues in A, 12 bars, three chords:** A7 (I), D7 (IV), E7 (V), all dominant 7ths, four beats/bar. Reference songs named: **Sweet Home Chicago, Mustang Sally, I'm a King Bee** (plus "lots of others nearly identical with a slight variation"). Matches [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *The 12-bar form* and the quick-change layout already logged from the sibling video. *(~0:40–1:40)*
- **The "L-shape" root map, version 1 — I chord rooted on the low E string.** Concrete, movable, three-chord shape system:
  - **I chord (A7):** root on the low E string. Play any dominant-7 shape you know off that root — a full **E-shaped bar chord**, or (Seriff's preference, and what he demonstrates) a **shell voicing** (root-3rd-♭7, smaller/more portable, easier in a band mix, avoids requiring a full bar-chord grip). Matches the shell-voicing shapes already documented on [[Chord Voicings]] § *From videos* (Seriff's own E-string-root shell: dominant 7 = `8 x 8 9 x x` off a fret-8 root, i.e. the general shape — flat the 7th off a maj7 shell).
  - **IV chord (D7):** move down **one string** (toward the floor, i.e. E string → A string) at the **same fret** — that's the D root. Play the same chord-shape family there (bar chord = classic A-shaped bar chord; shell = same shell shape relocated).
  - **V chord (E7):** from the IV chord's shape, move **up two frets** (toward the body) at the **same string**. That's the E7.
  - **The resulting root-note layout (I → down a string, same fret → IV; IV → same string, up two frets → V) traces a small "L"** — the mnemonic Seriff gives for memorizing where the IV and V roots sit relative to the I, once you know the I root's string and fret. Explicitly stated to work in **any key**, not just A, since it's a relative/movable shape. *(~1:40–3:10)*
- **The L-shape root map, version 2 — I chord rooted on the A string.** Same mnemonic, mirrored for when the tonic falls on the A string instead (worked from **A at the 12th fret of the A string**):
  - **I chord:** shell voicing = root, 3rd, ♭7 (or the full bar-chord version).
  - **IV chord:** move to the **low E string**, then **down two frets** (toward the headstock) — gives the D root.
  - **V chord:** from the IV chord's shape, **up two frets** (back toward the body), same string — gives the E7.
  - Root layout again traces an L, mirrored from version 1's — confirms the mnemonic is really "one string over + a fret offset," not tied to a specific starting string. *(~3:20–4:40)*
- **The minor blues in A, 12 bars, two chord qualities:** Am7 (i) and Dm7 (iv) — **minor 7, not dominant** — described as a flexible, moody-but-mellow chord ("bluesy, funky, jazzy"). No quick change (unlike the dominant form): **Am7 ×4 / Dm7 ×2 / Am7 ×2 / turnaround**. Root/home position given explicitly: **A on the 5th fret of the low E string.** Reference songs: **BB King's "Thrill Is Gone," Mr. PC (John Coltrane), Fleetwood Mac, Albert King** — matches [[Blues Form and Harmony]] § *Minor blues* and the A-key instance already logged from the sibling video. *(~6:20–7:20)*
- **Minor 7 voicing preference stated directly: the small minor-7 shell/partial shape over the full bar chord** — drops the two unnecessary extra strings, easier to fret, can be played 4-finger, 2-finger-plus-bar, or full-bar fingerstyle (careful not to let the open low string ring if fingerstyle). *(~7:20–7:50)*
- **The minor blues turnaround — the part people "frequently mess up," named and corrected:** guitarists commonly assume a **5-4-1** turnaround (Em7-Dm7-Am7) but that's actually rare; the much more common turnaround is **♭6 → 1** (technically ♭VI7 → V7 → i7 → I7 in full): in A, that's **F7 (or F9) → E7 (or E7♯9, i.e. the "Hendrix chord," already documented on [[Altered Chords]]) → Am7 → A7**. This is the *third* independently-sourced worked instance of the ♭VI7-V7 minor turnaround in the vault (C minor and a second A-minor instance already on [[Blues Form and Harmony]]/[[Turnarounds]]) — new detail here is the specific **F9** and **E7♯9 (Hendrix chord)** voicing options for the two turnaround chords. *(~8:00–8:50)*
- **Root map for the minor turnaround (not a clean L, but the same relative-shape logic):** i chord (Am7) root on the E string → **down one string, same fret** for iv (Dm7, also minor 7) → **up two frets** for V (E7) → **up one more fret** for ♭VI (F7/F9). Same "same shape, count frets/strings" navigation principle as the dominant blues L-shape, just a 4-root chain instead of a 3-root L. *(~8:50–9:20)*
