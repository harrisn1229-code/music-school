## What's taught

**The two-fret rule (the whole "no theory" trick).** Find your target/root note — the note you need to hit to follow the current chord — then either **walk up**, starting two frets *below* the target and stepping up chromatically one fret at a time (example given: target D on the 5th fret of the A string → start on the 3rd fret, walk 3rd → 4th → 5th), or **walk down**, starting two frets *above* the target and stepping down into it (example: target C on the 3rd fret of the A string → start on the 5th fret, walk 5th → 4th → 3rd). That's the entire rule — "just count two frets and walk this way," no scale/key awareness required to execute it, even though there's a real theory reason it works (deliberately withheld from this video, framed as content for BassBuzz's paid Beginner to Badass course instead).

**Rhythm placement — when the walk starts, not just which notes.** Two named default lead-in placements, both landing the walk's last note right before the target's downbeat: (1) **walkup rhythm** = two eighth notes before the target, counted "...and 4 and 1," demonstrated on the Pixies example; (2) **walkdown rhythm** = two sixteenth notes before the target (a tighter, more syncopated lead-in), counted "3 4 e and a 1," demonstrated on both the Fleetwood Mac and Earth, Wind & Fire examples. The placement detail matters as much as the fret choice — a walk placed too early in the bar doesn't read as a lead-in.

**Not every chord change gets a walk.** Confirmed directly in the "Gold Dust Woman" breakdown: the bassline goes straight from D to G with no walk at all on that particular change, then uses walkdowns on the other changes — the device is a deliberate placement choice per chord change, not a rule applied uniformly every time the harmony moves.

**Worked example 1 — Pixies, "Hey" (bassist Kim Deal).** Target notes B, D, G, F#, cycling back to B. Walksup into D and into F# using the two-fret/two-eighth-note formula (the F# walk specifically: open string → 1st fret → 2nd fret); the G target uses a different pattern not broken down in the lesson; return to B (2nd fret, A string) repeats the walkup pattern. Stated payoff: "those extra notes add some suspense that make you want to hear the next bar" — framed as a forward-propulsion device for the listener's attention, not decoration for its own sake.

**Worked example 2 — Fleetwood Mac, "Gold Dust Woman" (bassist John McVie).** Target notes D, G, C, back to G, back to D. D→G has no walk. Walkdown into C (5th → 4th → 3rd fret, two-sixteenth-note lead-in). A different, non-walkdown pattern into G (not detailed). Walkdown back into D (7th → 6th → 5th fret). Stated payoff, backed by an explicit before/after A/B comparison in the video: "those walk patterns give this bass line all of its character" — played without them, the line noticeably flattens.

**Worked example 3 — Earth, Wind & Fire, "Can't Hide Love" (bassist Verdine White).** Target notes F, Bb, G, C, F. Walks *down* into Bb, walks *up* into G, goes straight to C (no walk), walks *down* into F — the clearest single example of walkups and walkdowns combining freely within one bassline (not a per-song either/or choice), using the same two-sixteenth-note rhythm as "Gold Dust Woman."

**Applying it to write an original bassline — demonstrated twice, from a "boring" root-only starting point.** Progression 1: G–C–F–G (returning to a low G after a high-G detour). For each target, both a walkup and a walkdown option exist; the video shows two different valid full-bassline solutions over the identical progression (walk up→C / down→F / down→G / up→low-G, vs. down→C / down→F / up→G / down→low-G) explicitly to demonstrate there's no single "correct" answer — direction choice per chord is a creative decision. Rhythm used throughout: the two-eighth-note walkup placement. Progression 2: A–F–D–E, a different key/feel, walked through the same way, first draft: walkup→F, down→D, down→E, up→A.

**The fix-it hack — when a walk sounds wrong, add a gap.** On progression 2's first draft, two of the walks read as audibly "weird" in context — the walkup into F and the walkdown into E — specifically because the *first* note of each walk jumps out oddly. Fix: **move the walk's starting note one fret further away from the target**, opening a one-fret gap before the walk resumes chromatically in — for a walkup, drop the first note back an extra fret (instead of two consecutive frets below); for a walkdown, push the first note an extra fret further above. Retested, both patterns "sound a lot better." The video states there's a real music-theory explanation for *why* the flush version clashes (a scale/key-membership issue) but deliberately doesn't teach it here, framing "does it sound better with a gap?" as a usable-without-theory diagnostic test rather than teaching the underlying rule.
