# How to Play Blues Bass (in 5 Levels)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KZqqMQvOMIc)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KZqqMQvOMIc)

## Synopsis

A BassBuzz "levels" lesson (a different one from [[The Five-Tier Bass Theory Curriculum]]'s theory-by-goal video — same channel gimmick, unrelated content) that packages blues bass into a strict five-level vocabulary ladder: two movable fretboard shapes that locate the 1-4-5 of any 12-bar blues without theory, then four escalating layers of melodic material (shuffled root notes → a pentatonic "Blues Box" → triad-derived scale runs → chromatic walkup patterns), and — the video's headline claim — a named three-step method for actually fusing all four layers into free improvisation, which the host frames as the piece almost no one teaches explicitly ("us teachers usually just throw you a bunch of s**t, and then you have to put it all together yourself"). Every level is anchored to a real recording rather than an abstract example: Tommy Shannon (Stevie Ray Vaughan's Double Trouble, by way of Johnny Winter) is the through-line reference, cited first for shuffled-root-notes fundamentals ("Blues Makes Me Feel So Good," Albert Cummings) and again as the capstone target for full off-the-cuff mastery ("Pride and Joy," SRV); other songs cited for individual patterns include "Green Onions" (Booker T & the MGs), "I'm Tore Down" (Eric Clapton), "Hound Dog" (Elvis), "Crazy Little Thing Called Love" (Queen, John Deacon), "Ice Cream Man" (Van Halen), "Blue Suede Shoes" (Elvis), a Gary Hoey blues instrumental (captioned "Boot Mill Blues," likely a mishearing of "Boot Hill Blues"), "Killing Floor" (Howlin' Wolf) and its uncredited lift into "The Lemon Song" (Led Zeppelin), and "Tush" (ZZ Top, Dusty Hill), which alone carries both named chromatic walkup patterns. Two of the video's own fretboard shapes are physically verified against standard bass tuning in this vault's extraction (not just taken on faith from the transcript) — see the new technique note below. Beginner-facing, pure instrument/technique content — no plugins, effects, or production anywhere in the video.

## What's taught

- **[~0:00–0:45] Framing: five levels from "essential fundamentals" to "freely improvising and jamming," via a method the host says he's never seen taught elsewhere.** Sets up the whole video as building toward off-the-cuff blues improvisation, not just a lick collection.
- **[~0:45–2:15] Level 1a — The 12-Bar Blues Form.** Twelve bars, three chords (1, 4, 5), standard layout: 4 bars on I, 2 on IV, 2 back on I, then a closing 4-bar chunk of V→IV→I→I. Framed as the reason a blues tune is learnable so fast — whatever's in the 12 bars just repeats. Explicitly named as also underlying a lot of non-blues material, from James Brown to AC/DC. Full harmonic theory (why every chord is dominant, minor-blues variant, etc.) deliberately deferred to a companion PDF — this video stays theory-light on purpose.
- **[~2:15–4:00] Level 1b — Blues Survival Shapes.** Two movable "triangle" fretboard shapes that locate the 4 and 5 relative to any chosen root (the song's key), with zero interval/theory knowledge required. **High shape**, worked in full on camera: root note (example: A♭, 4th fret of the E string) → 4 = same fret, one string up (D♭, 4th fret A string) → 5 = two frets further up that same string (E♭, 6th fret A string). **Low shape**: described as "the flipped-around" version of the high shape — root high, 4 down a string and back two frets, 5 up two frets from there on that same lower string; not fully fretted on camera, but its "flip" is mechanically consistent (verified separately in the routed technique note) with the high shape's "1 and 4 share a fret" becoming "1 and 5 share a fret" instead. Both shapes transpose to any key by relocating the starting fret.
- **[~4:00–5:15] Level 1c — The shuffle feel.** Most (not all) blues uses a **shuffle**: an uneven, "long-short" triplet-based subdivision rather than a straight, evenly-divided 4/4 groove — described as feeling "lumpy," like marching with a broken foot. Beginner-level detection method given: listen for "the limp" in the guitar, drums, hi-hat, or ride cymbal, and match it, rather than assuming every blues shuffles.
- **[~5:15–6:15] Level 1 worked example: Tommy Shannon's part on "Blues Makes Me Feel So Good" (Albert Cummings).** Shuffled root notes only, no embellishment — explicitly praised as "super legit" *because* it's played by Tommy Shannon (early career with Johnny Winter, later Stevie Ray Vaughan's Double Trouble), not despite its simplicity. Framed as the floor, not the ceiling — the video is explicit that root-note shuffling alone gets boring fast and the rest of the video exists to add flavor on top of it.
- **[~6:15–7:15] Level 2 — Common patterns: riff-style blues.** The bass just doubles the guitar's riff, relocated to whichever chord (I/IV/V) it's currently under. Worked example: "Green Onions" (Booker T & the MGs) — a four-note riff played on I, then repeated transposed on IV and V.
- **[~7:15–8:15] Level 2 — The Blues Box.** A single movable pentatonic/blues-scale shape (visual demo only — no exact fretting given in captions) that generates idiomatic blues ideas when restarted fresh from each of the Level 1 survival-shape roots (1, 4, 5) as the 12-bar form moves through its chord changes. Genre-general — the same shape gets renamed "the rock rectangle" or "the funk fa cube" depending on style/teacher, but it's the identical box. Attributed as a real, taught technique: Tommy Shannon himself teaches this shape in his own "Hot Licks" instructional DVD. A second worked Blues Box pattern, over Eric Clapton's "I'm Tore Down," is referenced as living in the video's companion PDF rather than demonstrated on camera.
- **[~8:15–9:45] Level 3 — Triad-derived scale patterns.** Requires light scale/triad theory (or straight shape-memorization as a fallback). Three named, escalating patterns, each pinned to a real recording: **1-3-5** (Elvis's "Hound Dog"); **1-3-5-6**, credited directly to John Deacon on Queen's "Crazy Little Thing Called Love"; and the fuller **1-3-5-6-7-6-5-3**, cited in "Ice Cream Man" (Van Halen), "Blue Suede Shoes" (Elvis), and a Gary Hoey blues tune captioned as "Boot Mill Blues" (near-certain mishearing of "Boot Hill Blues" — flagged, not corrected outright).
- **[~9:45–11:00] Level 4 — Chromaticism, two named walkup patterns.** General move: insert a note outside the current scale on purpose, for tension, then resolve it into the target. **Pattern 1, the three-fret walkup** — climb chromatically over three frets to a target note (usually the root or 5th of the next chord), name-checked as a bass-wide device (Chameleon, Smoke on the Water) beyond just blues, with two worked blues instances: walkup to the 5th in "Killing Floor" (Howlin' Wolf), directly noted as the same line Led Zeppelin lifted uncredited into "The Lemon Song"; and walkup to the root/octave in "Tush" (ZZ Top, Dusty Hill), used there to return from IV to I. **Pattern 2, the two-fret walkup** — a turnaround-specific device: in the last four bars of the 12-bar form, a quick chromatic lead-in to the closing V chord. Same song/player as Pattern 1's ZZ Top example — Dusty Hill's "Tush" carries both walkup patterns in one tune.
- **[~11:00–12:20] Level 5 — Off-the-Cuff Blues Mastery: the three-step fusion method.** The video's headline claim, framed as the missing piece most blues/bass teaching skips. **Step 1:** drill Levels 1–4 individually, in order, over a real 12-bar backing track (the video's own play-along, key of E) — multiple full passes per level before advancing; roughly a month or two of consistent practice to work through all four. **Step 2 — "transition on the transitions":** commit to one main approach for most of the bassline (e.g., the Blues Box), then swap in a different approach specifically on the **transition bar** (the bar right before a chord change) as a lead-in fill — worked both directions: Blues Box for 3 bars + a chromatic 3-fret walkup on bar 4 leading into IV, or a 1-3-5-3 triad foundation with the Blues Box swapped in on the transition bar instead. Recommended at least a month of practice here too. **Step 3 — free improvisation:** mix all four vocabulary layers bar-to-bar with no planned pattern, starting at slow tempos and building up, explicitly built on top of (not skipping) Steps 1–2. Capstone reference: Tommy Shannon's playing on "Pride and Joy" (SRV) — shuffled root notes, chromaticism, and scale patterns combined, "and a couple other cool tricks."
- **[~12:20–end] Closing plug.** Points to the host's separate "How I'd Learn Bass if I Could Start Over" video for the psychological/mindset side of self-taught practice (comparison, mistakes, beginner frustration) rather than more notes/technique.
