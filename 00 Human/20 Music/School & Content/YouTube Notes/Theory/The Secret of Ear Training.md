# The Secret of Ear Training

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u0P7gh789RI)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u0P7gh789RI)

## Synopsis
The earliest video in this batch (2022) and the origin point of Max Konyi's whole feeling-state ear-training philosophy — the seed that [[A Guided Tour of Musical Feeling]], [[Essential Ear Training - Feeling the Minor Scale]], and everything downstream of them build on. The central claim, stated as plainly as it ever gets across the channel: ear training isn't learning to recognize a note, chord, or scale as an abstract concept — it's learning to recognize the **subjective feeling state** that comes attached to it, and attaching a label (a number, a chord name) to that feeling after the fact. This video narrows to just one feeling state — the **tonic** — and lays out three escalating levels of practice for recognizing it, ending with two real-music drills using Konyi's own copyright-free production-stream tracks.

## Techniques
- **The core claim: ear training targets the feeling, not the note.** What's being learned when you "recognize" a chord, scale, or note isn't the pitch or pattern itself — it's the felt, subjective quality experienced alongside it. The conceptual label (a chord name, a scale-degree number) is just the tag applied to that felt quality after the fact, not the thing itself being trained.
- **Why the tonic specifically, and why first.** The tonic — the home note, key center, resting point of a scale/mode/key/song — carries a feeling of restfulness, completion, and stability. Konyi frames it as the single most foundational feeling state to learn first because virtually all of modern tonal music organizes itself around this one tonal center, which is what gives every other note and chord its experiential meaning relative to "home."
- **The same physical pitch feels completely different depending on its tonal context — demonstrated directly, not just asserted.** The same note (C) is played first as the tonic of C major (restful, complete), then as a non-tonic scale degree inside F major, then inside F♯ minor — in each case it no longer carries the "home" feeling, even though the physical pitch never changed. This is the direct experiential proof behind [[Scale Degree Ear Training]]'s claim that degrees are relationships, not pitches.
- **Level 1 — scale-based tonic recognition.** Play a scale (any key, any mode — doesn't have to be a major scale; works for minor, Dorian, harmonic minor, anything with a clear tonal center) up or down, and stop one note short of the tonic. Let the feeling of near-completion build, then **sing** the resolving note before playing it — producing the feeling, not just perceiving it passively, is explicitly what locks it in.
- **Level 2 — chord-progression-based tonic recognition.** Take a simple chord progression that stays in one key (start with something familiar and easy to repeat, e.g. I–IV–I–V), stop on a non-tonic chord, and sing the note of resolution before playing the actual tonic chord. Escalate over time to more complex progressions still within one key, then to progressions you improvise on the spot and have to resolve without knowing in advance where they're going.
- **Level 3 — tonic recognition in real, streaming music, two variants.**
  - **Variant A — with a song whose chords you already know:** every time the tonic chord recurs, consciously emphasize/flag it internally ("there it is, there it is, home") — including when it shows up mid-phrase rather than only at obvious structural start/end points (worked example: in a I–vi–IV–V-type loop, the tonic can be buried as, say, the second chord rather than the first). Once that's solid, move to a song you *don't* know the chords for and try to feel for the same tonic arrival "live," then check your guess by looking up or working out the actual chords.
  - **Variant B — the hardest, most diagnostic version:** let a full, unfamiliar arrangement play until you've heard "enough" of it, then **stop playback at a genuinely random moment** and try to resolve everything you've heard so far into a single note within yourself — asking "if this song ended right now, what note would it resolve to?" — without knowing what chord or melodic note you're actually stopped on. Successfully doing this on music you've never heard before is Konyi's stated benchmark for having actually internalized tonic recognition, not just learned about it conceptually.
- **The "narrowing" experience as new notes arrive is a real, noticeable phenomenon worth paying attention to** — demonstrated on a slowly-building original track: as each new instrument/note enters, the mind is described as actively factoring toward "the lowest common denominator," the single note that makes sense of everything heard so far, narrowing the candidate tonic down note by note rather than guessing once at the end. Framed explicitly as a subtle-but-noticeable internal experience to watch for while practicing, not just an abstract metaphor.
- **Practice examples pulled from the creator's own copyright-free production-stream material** — used specifically so the real-music drill (Level 3B) could be demonstrated without a copyright-restricted commercial track, and to model that the drill works equally well on original/DIY material as on released songs.
