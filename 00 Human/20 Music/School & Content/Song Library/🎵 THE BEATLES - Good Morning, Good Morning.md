# Good Morning, Good Morning — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

Frugal materials, radical surface. **Four chords total and a tune built almost entirely out of one interval shape**, wrapped around the most aggressive metric irregularity John had attempted to that point. Pollack's insistence is that for all the revolutionary gesturing the song sits on a completely classic pop/rock skeleton — the economy is the point, and he reads it as impatience.

**Form.** The 4-bar intro is square on paper, but its first and last chord changes land on a **strongly syncopated offbeat**, hinting at what's coming. The verse has four phrases — a traditional count at anti-traditional lengths of **10, 12, 9 and 14 beats**. Draw the barlines however you like; the beat totals come out the same ([[ARR-C06 — Section-length conventions, and breaking them on purpose]]). The bridge runs **5 measures**, and both meter and chord rhythm regularise there, which the song badly needs by that point — the odd section length is the only irregularity left standing.

**Harmony.** The whole song uses **I, IV, V and ♭VII**. The recurring **cross-relation between V and ♭VII** is what makes so small a set sting ([[POP-C09 — Mixolydian ♭VII]]) — four chords is plenty when two of them disagree about the same scale degree.

**Melody.** An uncanny number of phrases **span a fourth subdivided into a third plus a second, in either order**. One interval shape, permuted, is what holds a rhythmically chaotic verse together ([[MEL-C03 — Motifs and how to develop them]]).

**Arrangement.** The surviving backing track with a single-tracked lead exposes the build method with textbook clarity: backing vocals, brass and animal effects were each applied **modularly** on top of a plain outline. Set against "She Said She Said", the other extreme meter experiment: there the trouble is quarantined in the contrasting sections and comes off as passively wobbly, while here it lives in the main verse, gets far more airtime, and reads as active agitation.
