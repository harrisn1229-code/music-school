# It's Only Love — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

A short, soft-focus John song whose whole harmonic character comes from **chromatic voice leading**, with cross-relations, a transient augmented triad and an odd root succession all falling out of it as side effects. The organising trick: the same ♭VII chord is used twice with two different meanings, changed only by what approaches it.

**Form.** Verse and refrain twice, plus intro and outro. The boundary between them is deliberately soft — the verse ends so open that the two arguably function as one long combined unit. The verse is **12 bars in AAB**, matching the blues phrase pattern with neither blues harmony nor blues style; the refrain is 8 bars in two roughly parallel phrases, each launched by the same route.

**Harmony.** The verse opening is **generated by a descending chromatic bass**, which forces an odd root succession from I through iii to ♭VII. Putting that iii chord in second inversion and letting the bassline's logic carry it is what keeps it from sounding arbitrary — in root position the same progression is much stranger, and some analysts would decline to call it a chord at all. The ♭VII is borrowed both times ([[HAR-C08 — Borrowed chords and modal interchange]], [[POP-C09 — Mixolydian ♭VII]]) but functions differently: in the verse it lands as **IV-of-IV**, arrived at chromatically from above; in the refrain it's approached from vi, where you'd expect IV or ii, and acts as a **subdominant leading directly to V**, setting up a cross-relation. Low-grade dissonance runs continuously ([[HAR-C09 — Non-harmonic tones]]) — some resolving properly, some left as pure colour. Both verse phrases open out to an **augmented V** which then declines to resolve and backs off to IV instead; the refusal is the emotional content.

**Melody.** The hooks are all built on a sighing **6–5 appoggiatura** — the guitar lick descending through intro and outro, and the vocal's peak moments in both verse and refrain.

**Arrangement.** Guitar-dominated with almost no percussion, which should read as Byrds-style folk rock except the hazy final mix pulls against it. John is single-tracked in the verses and doubled in the refrains, and the double drifts far enough out of sync to raise the question of whether one pass is a surviving guide vocal.
