# Why Grant Green's Minor Blues Soloing Is PERFECT (Jazz 101)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KwCPedoBbGY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KwCPedoBbGY)

## Synopsis

Nathan Borton pulls four concrete, transferable concepts out of a single Grant Green solo — the minor blues chorus over Green's own tune "Trace and Tracy" (B♭ minor) — arguing that Green's minor-blues vocabulary is under-discussed compared to his major/jazz-blues playing and deserves the same close-reading treatment. The video is explicitly framed as a blueprint that generalizes to *any* minor jazz situation, not just this one tune. Four devices, in the order taught: (1) **tonic minor soloing** — implying a momentary V7-to-i resolution inside a static minor chord by borrowing from the minor-6/minor-major-7 "tonic minor" sound, first as a single outside note resolving down a half step, then as a full 5-1 line played straight over the unmoving chord; (2) **color tones** — deliberately landing on the 9th, 11th, and natural 13th of the minor chord as a melodic feature, not incidental color; (3) **the "dilla rhythm"** — a named syncopated embellishment cell Green uses to break up straight eighth-note lines while moving through scale or arpeggio tones, demonstrated over both a scalar walk-down and a minor-6 arpeggio; and (4) **blues language** — small one-octave blues boxes moved around the neck, plus the blues scale played vertically up and down the neck rather than boxed horizontally. The most valuable material is the tonic-minor device (a genuinely underdocumented bebop-over-static-minor trick) and the naming of the dilla rhythm as a reusable rhythmic vocabulary item, since most minor-blues instruction stops at "use the minor pentatonic or blues scale."

## What's taught

- **Tonic minor sound, defined.** A static minor (i) chord can be heard/voiced as a **minor 6** or **minor-major 7** chord, both of which contain the i chord's own V7 chord's guide tones — so lines built around that sound imply a momentary V7-to-i resolution without the harmony actually moving. Framed directly against the more common default of running plain minor pentatonic over a minor tune. → [[Tonic Minor Soloing]]
- **Device 1 — single outside note implying a quick 5-1.** Over E♭ minor: **D natural → E♭** — D is foreign to the E♭ minor scale but is the **major 3rd of B♭7** (V7 of E♭ minor), so resolving it down a half step compresses a V7→i cadence into two notes. Over B♭ minor: **A natural → B♭**, identical logic — A is the major 3rd of **F7** (V7 of B♭ minor). Both phrases are drawn from the same line in Green's solo. → [[Tonic Minor Soloing]]
- **Device 2 — a full 5-1 line over a chord that never moves.** A complete phrase outlining **F7 moving to B♭ minor**, played in full over a static B♭m vamp (no V chord is actually sounding in the rhythm section), with a plain minor pentatonic/blues phrase tagged onto the end to resolve the ear back into "home" minor language. Stated explicitly as mixable — start in tonic-minor/5-1 vocabulary, then drop into pentatonic/blues to close a phrase, rather than treating the two as separate toolkits. → [[Tonic Minor Soloing]]
- **Color tones, named and targeted.** Over B♭ minor: the **9th (C natural)**, the **natural 13th (G)**, and (named as a further option, not worked into a specific line) the **11th (E♭)**. Framed as "consciously landing on or emphasizing" these extensions as a feature of tonic-minor or blues lines, layered directly on top of one of the earlier tonic-minor phrases (the same line replayed with an added 13th emphasis). → [[Extended Chords]]
- **The "dilla rhythm."** A named syncopated rhythmic cell Green uses to interject rhythmic variety (he's cited as constantly shifting between eighth notes, quarter notes, and triplets across the solo) — described as a rhythm that "fluidly moves between either scales or arpeggio tones," contrasted directly against playing a plain, evenly-spaced eighth-note line. Demonstrated twice: embellishing a straight walk down the **tonic minor scale**, and embellishing a **B♭ minor 6 arpeggio**. Presented as directly, immediately applicable vocabulary rather than an abstract concept. → [[Dilla Rhythm Ornamentation]]
- **Blues language, chorus 1 close.** A phrase from the end of Green's first chorus built from **small, one-octave blues boxes** scattered across the neck — the point being that useful blues vocabulary doesn't require one big scale-length box, just small movable shapes. → [[Blues Lead Phrasing and Touch]]
- **Blues language, chorus 2 open.** A phrase from the start of Green's second chorus that plays the **blues scale vertically, up and down the neck**, deliberately working the high register rather than staying horizontal in one box — and lands again on the **minor 11th** extension (which is coincidentally already part of the blues scale), tying the color-tone device back into pure blues-scale vocabulary. → [[Blues Lead Phrasing and Touch]], [[Extended Chords]]
- **Closing framing.** All four devices are stated as transferable to *any* minor jazz situation, not tune-specific — tonic minor for harmonic sophistication over static minor chords, the dilla rhythm for rhythmic variety, color tones for melodic interest, and the blues-box/vertical-scale material as the connective "classic blues language" underneath all of it. No settings/gear/production content — pure improvisation vocabulary and theory.
