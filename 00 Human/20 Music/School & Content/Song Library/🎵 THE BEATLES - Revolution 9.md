# Revolution 9 — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

**Form:** Pollack's own answer is Beginning · Middle · End, with a fade. This is the one track in the series that **defeats his method outright** — no sections, no progressions, no phrases, no tune to measure — so he abandons bar-by-bar analysis and writes about the listening experience instead. His framing: the piece is radical in what it withholds and surprisingly conservative in what it is actually made of.

Everything in it is a **found object**. Nothing is synthesised; the raw material is pre-existing musical and spoken recordings plus real-world noise, with the musical clips weighted heavily toward classical sources. Post-processing is deliberately limited to three operations — **speed changes, reversed tape, and heavy crosscutting**. Pollack rates that low-tech restraint as the piece's saving grace, and sets it against Stockhausen's *Gesang der Jünglinge*, far more technically arcane in how it handles vocal material.

Despite the anti-narrative surface, several sources **recur as motifs** without persisting across the whole piece: the title phrase, a soft slow piano passage in a Chopin manner, orchestral and choral snippets, spoken recitation. They surface the way a familiar face does in a crowd. His guess is that the assembly was stream of consciousness rather than genuine aleatoric procedure.

The claim worth keeping is about listening rather than composition: sustained ambiguity of content pushes the listener to **project a continuity that is not in the material**, the way a sleeping mind absorbs an outside noise into a dream. And context is part of the work — the track is inseparable from the album it sits on.
