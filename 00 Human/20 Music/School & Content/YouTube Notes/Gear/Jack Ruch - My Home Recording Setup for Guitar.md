# Jack Ruch — My Home Recording Setup for Guitar

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KYTGFneEJ2A)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KYTGFneEJ2A)

## Synopsis

Jack Ruch (blues/jazz guitar teacher, previously only sourced in this vault for lesson content — 27 notes under [[YouTube Notes Index|Jack Ruch]]) walks his actual studio signal chain end-to-end for the first time: guitar → pedalboard → amp, mic'd with two mics into a pair of API mic preamps → Universal Audio Apollo → computer (recording software: Luna — Universal Audio's own DAW, skipped here since Nate runs Ableton; monitors: Yamaha). The video is a plain gear-and-technique walkthrough rather than a breakdown of a finished track, and it lands remarkably close to Nate's own rig: **same interface family** ([[Universal Audio Apollo Twin MkII]]), **same close mic** ([[Shure SM57]]), and the same "small clean amp + pedals + two blended mics" philosophy already documented on [[1 Electric Guitar Amp Track|Electric Guitar Amp Track]] from Kenny Beats' [[Kenny Beats - Guitar Recording Process (Pedals, Amps)|Guitar Recording Process]] video. The most valuable, reusable material: the two-mic blend technique (bright/cutting dynamic + dark/warm ribbon, positioned by ear rather than formula), the "gain the preamp as hot as possible without clipping" tracking discipline, the "process almost nothing on the way in except via the amp+pedals, save all polish for the master bus" philosophy, and concrete Boss Delay Machine / pedal-stacking settings that map cleanly onto Nate's own board ([[Line 6 DL4]], [[Big Muff Pi]], [[Boss DS-2]]).

## Techniques

- **Signal chain overview (0:00–1:00):** guitar → pedalboard → amp (mic'd with two mics) → pair of API mic preamps → Universal Audio Apollo → computer. DAW is Luna (skipped — Nate uses Ableton); monitoring is a pair of Yamaha monitors ([[Yamaha HS8]] is the owned equivalent). "The guitars change from video to video... but everything else here stays set up pretty much the same" — a fixed, repeatable capture chain reused across every session, character baked in at the amp/pedal stage rather than rebuilt each time.
- **Guitar for this session: a Danocaster Tele with "throwback" pickups** — not owned; general lesson (single-coil neck/bridge/middle "quacky" tones demoed) applies to Nate's [[Fender Stratocaster]].
- **Set the amp clean with a touch of reverb as the platform, before any pedals** — "that's how I like to set an amp generally... it gives you a really nice platform to start adding pedals and effects, and it kind of works equally well with every guitar." Confirms the exact philosophy already on [[Fender Blues Junior]] / [[1 Electric Guitar Amp Track|Electric Guitar Amp Track]] (clean-platform-first, dirt from pedals not the amp).
- **Amp used: a mid-'60s Fender Princeton Reverb with an Eminence speaker** — small vintage blackface-era Fender combo, same lane as the owned [[Fender Blues Junior]] (and the same lane Kenny Beats' Vibro Champ occupies) — a small clean Fender platform is a repeated pattern across every guitar-recording source in this vault now.
- **Boss Delay Machine settings — the reusable recipe:** three, maybe four repeats that "die out and get a little bit dark as they go," with a slight bit of modulation on the tail; tap tempo set to the song for a faster slapback or a longer ambient wash depending on mood. → [[Line 6 DL4]] (owned delay pedal, same "last in the pedal chain" slot), [[Delay]]
- **Roger Mayer Voodoo 1 — used as a boost, not a dirt pedal.** "I set it kind of like a boost. It adds a little bit of hair... it honestly in a track still kind of reads as clean... but it's nice for certain types of clean sounds where you want a little bit more character and grit." Used for bluesier rhythm parts needing subtle compression/character without full overdrive. Not owned — general lesson (a clean-boost stage ahead of the real dirt pedal) maps to running a low-gain stage before [[Boss DS-2]]/[[Big Muff Pi]] rather than jumping straight to full drive.
- **Analog Man King of Tone (red side) — the "more obvious" overdrive**, used after the Voodoo 1 in the signal path. Stacking the two: "you get more of that low-end body from the Voodoo One and... a little bit of extra push of that boost going into the King of Tone" — a boost-into-overdrive stack for extra push and low-end body, not just "more gain." → general pedal-order lesson for [[Boss DS-2]] (mid-forward dirt, same "cuts where the Muff scoops" role as King of Tone here).
- **Yard Box fuzz — rarely used, but described as "kind of like a fuzz distortion," "a great pedal," hard to find.** Habit worth noting: "oftentimes I'll stack it just in front of my overdrive sound" — fuzz-before-overdrive stacking, the same family of move as [[Big Muff Pi]] stacked with [[Boss DS-2]] already documented on that gear note, just reversed order (fuzz first here vs. Muff-then-DS-2 there) — worth A/B'ing pedal order rather than assuming one fixed sequence.
- **Arion chorus — rarely used, but has a second voice: set faster for a "faux Leslie" rotating-speaker effect** rather than its default slow chorus sweep. → [[Chorus-Ensemble]] (owned; faster Rate + higher Amount is the owned-plugin equivalent of this move).
- **Two-mic amp technique — a Shure SM57 (dynamic) + Royer 121 (ribbon) blend:** "the warmer, fuller sound of the Royer ribbon mic combined with the more focused mid-rangey harsher sound of the 57, which really cuts through the mix." Exactly the same two-voice-blend logic already documented for [[Shure SM57]] + [[Neumann TLM 102]] on [[1 Electric Guitar Amp Track|Electric Guitar Amp Track]] §1, with a ribbon standing in for the condenser's "air" role — reinforces the pattern rather than replacing it. → [[Royer 121]] (not owned; comparison note added)
- **Mic positioning is by ear, not formula:** "I just kind of positioned those a little bit off the center of the cone of the speaker, back an inch or two off the grill cloth. Basically, just move it around until it sounds good. I don't really think about it too much." A looser, more intuitive framing of the same "an inch of movement changes the tone" principle already on [[Shure SM57]]'s Key Parameters table.
- **Mic preamp gain staging: set the API preamps as hot as possible without clipping.** "I just set the preamps right until they're... not clipping. So, as loud as they can get without hitting the red... you want to hit your recording software, the computer, with as much signal as possible. That's going to give you a nice loud full sound that's really going to sit in the track the best." A plain restatement of the hot-but-not-clipping tracking discipline — re-tune per guitar/amp-volume combination, not a set-once value. → [[Gain Staging]]
- **Almost no processing on individual guitar tracks** — no EQ, no compression, no plugins on the guitar tracks themselves; all the character is printed at the amp/pedal stage. Reinforces the existing house rule already on [[1 Electric Guitar Amp Track|Electric Guitar Amp Track]] ("most amp takes want nothing — the character was printed at the amp/pedals").
- **Master-bus glue, applied specifically because the audience hears it small:** subtle mastering-style compression/glue on the whole track, not because it needs to be "loud," but because computer speakers and phone speakers lose nuance and detail — printing loud/glued/mastered audio keeps it legible on small playback systems. Chain used: an **API summing plugin** + an **API bus [compressor]** ("just kind of use some general default settings and set it in a subtle way to sound nice"), a **Waves SSL bus compressor**, and a **Waves 1073 plugin in mid-side** ("that just kind of widens everything out and gives everything a little bit of space"). → [[Master Chain]], [[API-2500]] (the "API bus" plugin is very likely the same Waves API 2500 already owned), [[Solid Bus Comp]] (owned SSL-style bus-comp equivalent), [[Width and Stereo]]
- **Overall philosophy, stated directly at the close:** "essentially it's a pretty simple [chain] — good guitar, a couple of good pedals, a great old amp, mic'd with that classic combination of mics... using a couple of good mics and really using good mic preamps does make a huge difference. I've done it without the mic preamps and there's a noticeable difference... and then just a good interface... and then just using your ear to match the volume, set the compression, and get everything sitting nicely in the mix." Good outboard preamps are called out explicitly as a real, audible upgrade over interface-only gain — general validation for anyone considering an outboard mic-pre purchase.
- **Backing-track workflow (video-page-only, not a reusable technique):** most backing tracks for his lesson videos are tracked live with a real band (keyboards, bass, drums) at a friend's (Wes Little's) studio; the files get dragged into his own session afterward and he records/films his own guitar on top. Channel-specific production logistics, not a transferable recording technique — kept here for reference only.
