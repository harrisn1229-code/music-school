# Ultimate Beginner's Guide To Intervals On The Guitar!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6ftJebnX-MI)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6ftJebnX-MI)

## Synopsis

A full interval-by-interval walkthrough (unison through major 7th), all played from a single root (A, 5th fret low E), pairing each interval's characteristic sound/color with a concrete practical use in riff-writing or chord voicing rather than treating them as abstract distances.

## What's taught

Per-interval sound/use notes (root A throughout):
- **Minor 2nd** — the most dissonant interval playable as two simultaneous pitches (only microtonal bends go smaller); used for dark/dissonant riffs or tension-and-release moves.
- **Major 2nd** — noticeably less dissonant than the minor 2nd; frequently used in higher octaves stacked on a perfect 5th, or as the extension on top of minor/major 7th voicings — where it's the **same pitch as a 9th**, just heard as a chord tension rather than a plain interval once placed in a higher octave. Also the substitute tone in a **sus2** chord.
- **Minor 3rd / Major 3rd** — the interval directly responsible for a chord reading as minor (sad) vs. major (happy); root + minor 3rd + 5th = minor triad, root + major 3rd + 5th = major triad.
- **Perfect 4th** — "underrated"; it's the top interval of a power chord without the root doubled, but its more interesting function is substituting for the 3rd in a major/minor voicing to build a **sus4** chord (deliberately neither major nor minor).
- **Augmented 4th / diminished 5th (tritone)** — the maximally dissonant, "evil"-sounding interval, common in metal/rock riffs specifically for the tension it creates; resolves by stepping up to the 5th or down to the 4th.
- **Perfect 5th** — the power-chord interval, "the backbone of any iconic riff," commonly extended with an octave on top via the pinky.
- **Minor 6th / Major 6th** — memorization hook: minor 6th shares the minor 3rd's sad color, major 6th shares the major 3rd's happy color. Minor 6th commonly resolves back down into the perfect 5th in rock/metal riffs. **Major 6th over a minor triad is the defining Dorian color tone** — natural minor + major 6th = Dorian, so adding this interval directly onto a minor chord's root produces an instant Dorian character shift.
- **Minor 7th / Major 7th** — mainly heard stacked onto minor/major triads to form minor-7 and major-7 ("jazz") chords, giving a noticeably more sophisticated sound than the plain triads.
