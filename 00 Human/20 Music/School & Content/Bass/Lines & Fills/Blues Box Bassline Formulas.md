# Blues Box Bassline Formulas

BassBuzz's direct sequel to [[Blues Bass Survival Shapes and Vocabulary Ladder]] — that note's Level 2 section named "the Blues Box" but couldn't give it exact fretting (visual-only demo in that source video); this video is the payoff, giving the box a precise four-note shape **and** seven named, song-sourced rhythm/note-order "formulas" for turning that one shape into idiomatic blues (and funk) basslines in any key. Where the survival-shapes note's Level 2 box reads as "a pentatonic/blues-scale shape," this video's actual Blues Box is a **smaller, more specific four-note subset** — root, 5, ♭7, octave — not the full five-or-more-note pentatonic/blues-scale run fretted on [[Bass Scale Shapes — Major, Minor, Pentatonic & Blues]]. Same "shape once, move it to each chord" teaching model as [[Bass Pentatonic Riff-Writing Recipe]] and [[Root-Fifth Bass Lines and the 3-Step Style Rhythm Formula]] — same channel, same shape-first pedagogy, this time built around a dominant-7-flavored four-note box instead of a full scale or a two-note interval.

## The Blues Box shape

Four notes, playable with just two fingers (index, pinky), tracing a zigzag across three adjacent strings. Worked example starting on **D** (5th fret, A string):

| Finger | String | Fret (relative to root fret) | Note (D example) | Scale degree |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Index | String N (A) | root fret (X) | D | Root (1) |
| Pinky | String N+1 (D) | X + 2 | A | 5 |
| Index | String N+2 (G) | X | C | ♭7 |
| Pinky | String N+2 (G) | X + 2 | D | Octave (8) |

So the shape is: **root → up a string + up 2 frets (the 5) → up another string, back to the root's fret (the ♭7) → same string, up 2 frets (the octave).** Verified against standard EADG bass tuning: A string 5th fret = D, D string 7th fret = A (D string open is D, +7 semitones = A, a perfect fifth up), G string 5th fret = C, G string 7th fret = D. Matches the video's own theory callout — "root, five, flat seven, and octave" — for players who want to know what they're playing; otherwise just memorize the shape (index-pinky-index-pinky) and skip the note names entirely.

**This is a genuinely different, smaller shape than the full minor pentatonic/blues-scale box** fretted on [[Bass Scale Shapes — Major, Minor, Pentatonic & Blues]] (that shape uses 5–7 notes and up to four fingers per position). The two-finger four-note box here trades vocabulary size for near-zero technical difficulty — it's the "you can literally only produce cool-sounding notes" starter box, not the full scale.

**Genre-general, not blues-exclusive.** The video explicitly calls out that this same four-note shape is also "the Funk Box" (from a separate BassBuzz "Booty's Funk Formula" lesson — not yet in this vault) and "the Jazz Box" depending on which lesson/genre it's taught under — same notes, same shape, the genre label is just framing. Cf. [[Blues Bass Survival Shapes and Vocabulary Ladder]]'s own note that the pentatonic-style box gets renamed "the rock rectangle" / "the funk fa cube" by other teachers — same phenomenon, two different specific box shapes.

## Moving the box — applying it over a chord progression

The box is **movable**: find the root of whichever chord you're currently on (via the [[Blues Bass Survival Shapes and Vocabulary Ladder|survival shapes]] or the 12-bar blues's own 1-4-5), restart the same four-finger shape from that fret, using the E or A string as the starting string so there's enough neck above it to fit the whole box. Worked example: guitarist comping D → G → D → G (two bars each) — playing root notes only under each chord change is called out as **the boring baseline option**; relocating the full box to D, then to G, then back, is the actual payoff of learning the shape rather than just playing roots.

## The seven formulas

Each formula = **the same box shape** + **a specific rhythm** + **a specific note order**, each one lifted from a real recording. Every formula plays over a 12-bar blues in the stated key (even when the source song isn't itself a 12-bar blues) and — the one rule that holds across every single formula — **starts on the root, the lowest note of the box, to keep the low end anchored** on the downbeat of each chord.

| # | Formula | Source song / artist | Key | 1 / 4 / 5 frets | Rhythm | Note order |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The Basic Box** | (formula demo, no song cited) | D | 1 = D (A str, fret 5) · 4 = G (E str, fret 3) · 5 = A (E str, fret 5) | Straight quarter notes | Box notes in order: root, 5, ♭7, octave |
| 2 | **The Box Shuffle** | "Little by Little" — Junior Wells (Susan Tedeschi version) | A | 1 = A (E str, fret 5) · 4 = D (A str, fret 5) · 5 = E (A str, fret 7) | Shuffle eighth notes ("lumpy" — see [[#Shuffle feel primer]]) | Box notes in order |
| 3 | **The Reverse Box Shuffle** | "I'm Tore Down" — Eric Clapton | C | 1 = C (A str, fret 3) · 4 = F (E str, fret 1) · 5 = G (E str, fret 3) | Same shuffle rhythm as Formula 2 | Reversed: root, octave, ♭7, 5 |
| 4 | **The Funky Formula** | Otis Redding (source song title dropped mid-sentence in the captions — not independently identified) | C | Not given on camera (12-bar blues in C) | Straight eighth notes (no shuffle) | Not specified in captions beyond "funky" — source song itself isn't a 12-bar blues, formula adapted to fit one |
| 5 | **The Finger Pop Formula** | "Shake and Finger Pop" — Jr. Walker & the All Stars | F | 1 = F (E str, fret 1) · 4 = B♭ (A str, fret 1) · 5 = C (A str, fret 3) | Same straight-eighths rhythm as Formula 4 | Reordered box notes vs. Formula 4 (exact order not itemized in captions) — source song also not originally a 12-bar blues |
| 6 | **The Funky Trunk Formula** | "Leavin' Trunk" — Taj Mahal | B♭ | 1 = B♭ (E str, fret 6) · 4 = E♭ (A str, fret 6 — captioned "A♭," almost certainly a mishearing: B♭'s 4 is E♭, and the A string's 6th fret is E♭, not A♭) · 5 = F (A str, fret 8) | **Sixteenth notes** — the rhythmic subdivision step up from Formulas 4–5, flagged as genuinely harder for players new to 16ths | Funky note ordering, same family as Formulas 4–5 |
| 7 | **The Tasty Triplet Shuffle** | "The Blues Is Alright" — Gary Moore | Not given in captioned portion | Not given in captioned portion | Shuffle-family rhythm with a **triplet** at the end of the phrase (harder to play — why it's saved for last) | Similar to Formula 2's order; **at the end of the 12-bar form, break the pattern to just "chug" (repeated hits) on the V chord for a bar** — a rhythmic hit done in unison with the rest of the band, common at the close of a blues form |

**Reading the table:** formulas 1–3 are the same straightforward shuffle/straight family in ascending difficulty (basic → shuffle → reversed-note shuffle); formulas 4–6 are the "funky" family, escalating from straight eighths (4) through a reordered straight-eighths variant (5) to sixteenth notes (6); formula 7 returns to the shuffle family but adds a triplet and a scripted end-of-form band hit.

## The 4(+1)-step process — building your own bassline with the box

The video's own generalized method for applying the box live, e.g. jamming with a guitarist and drummer on a 12-bar blues:

1. **Find the key.** Ask the guitarist rather than guess — "it's better than playing the wrong notes."
2. **Find your 1-4-5** using the [[Blues Bass Survival Shapes and Vocabulary Ladder|survival shapes]] (high or low shape, either works).
3. **Pick a rhythm that fits the drums/groove** — straight or shuffle, matched to what the band is actually playing (see [[Blues Bass Survival Shapes and Vocabulary Ladder]] § *The shuffle feel* for the "listen for the limp" ear check). Gets easier with more experience in a given style.
4. **Pick an order of box notes** — reuse one of the seven formulas above, or invent your own ordering. **The one hard rule: always start on the root** (the box's lowest note) so the bassline stays anchored to the low end on each chord's downbeat — every single one of the seven formulas follows this rule.
5. **Optional — combine formulas within a song.** Small rhythmic swaps (e.g. Box Shuffle ↔ Tasty Triplet Shuffle) can happen "whenever you feel like it" since the difference is subtle. **Big changes in how busy the bassline is should land at the top of the 12-bar form** (e.g. a new solo section building excitement) rather than mid-phrase — breaking that rule "breaks the flow of the groove" and, per the video, "upsets your bandleader." *(general starting point for ensemble discipline — same underlying principle as [[Locking In With the Drummer]]'s "serve the groove before serving your own ideas.")*

## Shuffle feel primer

Same "even vs. lumpy" ear-training framing already logged on [[Blues Bass Survival Shapes and Vocabulary Ladder]] § *The shuffle feel*, restated here with the video's own count: straight eighths ("even") land exactly halfway between beats — *one-and-two-and*; shuffle eighths ("lumpy") sit closer to the outer notes of an eighth-note triplet — *one-uh-two-uh*. Formulas 2, 3, and 7 above use the shuffle feel; Formulas 1, 4, 5, and 6 use straight subdivisions (quarters, straight eighths, or sixteenths).
