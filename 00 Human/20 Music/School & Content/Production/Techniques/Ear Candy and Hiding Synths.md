# Ear Candy and Hiding Synths

Small, often-subliminal details that **reward attention and accentuate the vocal without competing with it** — Ian Kirkpatrick's signature obsession. The rule that governs all of it: candy serves the song and the story, and it lives *just at the point of perception* — present, but never enough to distract. Companion to [[Arrangement and Song Form]], [[Depth and Space]], [[Ian Kirkpatrick - The Production Secret Behind Huge Pop Hits|rate of information]], and [[Automation]].

## Hiding synths in / behind vocals
- **A synth playing the note, buried behind a vocal word, to make that word "pop."** Instead of a reverb throw on a standout word, put a synth on the same note *underneath* it — loud enough to feel, low enough that "you don't even know." His current obsession. → [[Ian Kirkpatrick - Hiding Synths In Vocals]], [[Ian Kirkpatrick - Hiding Synths In Vocals (pt 2)]]
- **Build the synth patch *from the vocal itself.*** Drag a word (e.g. "move," "me") into **PadShop** and make a patch from it, so the pad sounds like the vowel — the "ooh" of *move*, the "ee" of *me*. The synth and the word are the same timbre, so it fuses. → [[PadShop]], [[Pitch and Formant]]
- **Sidechain mode (iZotope VocalSynth 2):** route a synth loop into VocalSynth's sidechain so the *synth* modulates the *vocal* — combinations "that don't exist anywhere." → [[Ian Kirkpatrick - Creating Vocal FX (VocalSynth 2)]]

## Vocal candy
- **Tiny vocal fragments on their own track** — chop little pieces of the lead to a dedicated track for scoop/echo candy (one of Ian's "favorite echoes ever"). → [[Delay]], [[EchoBoy Jr]]
- **Pitched "altar-boy" vocal** — pitch a vocal snippet up (Little AlterBoy / Output Exhale) as a pre-drop excitement source. → [[Little AlterBoy]], [[Exhale]], [[Pitch and Formant]]
- **A chop that stops before the snare** — cut a vocal so it ends *before* the downbeat instead of clipping it on the beat, so you hear it stop ("a little *oh what the—*"). Automate the LFO Tool duck to switch on exactly when the chorus drops. → [[LFO Tool]], [[Automation]]
- **Lower/upper octave doubles** — add an octave under a vocal (VocalSynth Compuvox / Polyvox / Biovox in auto mode) to fatten it or reach notes the singer can't. → [[Ian Kirkpatrick - Pitch Shifted Vocals (VocalSynth 2)]]

## Found-sound & texture candy
- **Keep the room talk in.** Tapping a guitar/desk for percussion with **friends chatting left in the recording** ("the more talking the better, it sounds natural"), gated. → [[Ambient Texture and Field Recording]], [[TEXTURE Slot]]
- **Reverse-reverb swell into the "one"** — resolves attention onto the downbeat. → [[Reverb]]
- **A texture "just at the point of perception"** — a low chorus layer kept barely audible on purpose; felt, not heard. → [[Depth and Space]]

## Discipline
- **Candy must survive the "does it distract from the vocal?" test.** If a rhythmic or melodic detail pulls the ear off the story, it's over-produced — cut it ([[Ian Kirkpatrick - The Production Secret Behind Huge Pop Hits|rate of information]]).
- **The *reason* is the candy.** New Rules' "everything stops" moment came from an idea, not a plugin — chase the inspiration, then build the detail to serve it.

## From videos

- **A whole episode's worth of "happy accident" textures treated as load-bearing, not decorative — Arlo Parks & Baird, across three songs of *Ambiguous Desire*.** Four distinct examples, all unrepeatable one-offs: (1) an old Crumar keyboard's binary chorus circuit cranked with no notes played, generating a crackly/swirly noise-texture bed then run through delay — "pretty thin" alone but the mix "would fall apart" without it; (2) an Ibanez electro-acoustic nylon guitar played very loud through pedals, its own pickup effectively acting as a room mic and picking up drum bleed — no dry DI exists, "that's how it is"; (3) a guitar-headstock string-pluck used as a cold-open hook, deliberately chosen for sounding non-guitar-like; (4) a homemade baritone guitar's badly-intonated original take salvaged by replaying it note-by-note rather than tuning-correcting it, with some notes deliberately left to swell. A fifth item, an out-of-tune studio piano, was preserved by sampling it note-by-note into a permanent custom instrument rather than replaced — "it's magic, forever." A sixth: a reversed, chopped scratch-vocal take buried purely as a buzzing "wasp"-like texture, sitting alongside an admittedly-unexplained randomly-labeled "Jingle Jangle" chopped/delayed track neither writer can fully account for. → [[Bass Design]], [[Piano Recording]], [[Arlo Parks Vocal Chain]] — Arlo Parks & Baird (Tape Notes), [[TN 183 Arlo Parks & Baird - Full Video Episode]]
- **Use Ableton's transpose tool on existing vocal material to generate quick ad-lib/ear-candy flourishes, rather than recording new takes for each one.** Stelios (producer for Lolo), scattering ad-libs through a verse: "some of those were created by using Ableton transpose." Paired with a stated instinctive, fast placement habit — "it's good to just kind of think really fast, or not think at all, and just see what happens" — rather than a planned map of where flourishes land. Also uses plain background "ahs" under a bare verse purely for a hypnotic, loneliness-breaking texture, distinct from the pitch-shifted ad-libs. → [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]] — Mix with the Masters (Stelios), [[Vocal and synth production with Stelios]]
- **A buried, genre-incongruous instrumental lick, discovered only because the whole track was played through live rather than looped.** A "kind of country"-flavored acoustic guitar part sits quietly in the background of Lola Young's "Messy" — a texture the artist "would never really put in her song" on purpose, but the band loves it precisely because it's a little buried, mixed low rather than brought forward. Because the take was a full live band performance rather than one part looped throughout, small unplanned licks and riffs "poke out" at different moments from different players rather than the same phrase repeating identically — full-take live tracking as an ear-candy *discovery* mechanism, not just a recording philosophy. → [[Vocal Comping and Takes]] — Mix with the Masters (Lola Young, Jared Solomon), [Behind the Track｜'Messy' by Lola Young](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=02QKFlkbmYY)
- **Build muted alternate "passes," then unmute them briefly whenever the mix needs a hit of ear candy — an explicit fix for listener ear-fatigue.** "One of the things that unifies us is we'll do several passes of something — often weird passes, like a slightly distorted synth, a slightly distorted bass, vocal throws. You do a full pass of the song, then you mute it, and you just listen to the song, and every time your brain wants a little bit of candy, you unmute a little moment of it — because if something's always present in a production, you forget about it a minute in." The mechanism: build the candy as a full-length muted layer first, then bring in only brief unmuted moments by ear during playback, rather than pre-planning exact automation points — a workflow variant of "constant presence gets tuned out" applied at the arrangement-decision stage, not just to a finished mix. → [[Automation]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]] — Mix with the Masters (Benny Blanco & Selena Gomez), [Behind the Track｜'Younger And Hotter Than Me' by Selena Gomez & benny blanco](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rkJ-LaoArUY)
- **Land accent one-shots right ON the downbeat (not scattered) for instant impact, and place small extra textures at specific structural points, not evenly throughout.** Recreating Dez Wright's "Flocky Flocky": two accent one-shots placed together right on beat 1 give a flip its immediate punch, and a couple more synth textures added specifically at the MIDDLE and END of the loop (rather than a constant texture bed) are described directly as "the ear candy." Same discipline shows up on "7 a.m. on Bridal Path," where textures/accents change every 4 bars against a repeating chord loop to keep it from feeling static. → [[Layering and Stacking]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]] — loner, [dez wright's synth secrets will elevate your melodies!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_8vOz54DTiY)
- **Texture redefined as "moments that may only appear once," not a continuous background layer — a concrete fix for a vague word.** Mk.gee describes his own album *Two Star and the Dream Police* as "a Sonic story book," and its textures as the quirky, standout moments that stick with you — one-off events, not a constant wash sitting under the mix. Useful as a working definition whenever "add texture" starts meaning nothing specific. → [[Sound Selection Guide]] — loner, [the secrets behind Mk.gee's HYPNOTIC production!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=969p5Q4JSZU)
- **Deliberately pick the "wrong"/skip-past preset for a texture layer, not the pleasant one.** Mk.gee's synth-texture layers are ones that "nine times out of 10, if I clicked on it, I would skip straight past it" while browsing — the glitchy, weird, imperfect sound is the point, sourced from his actual modular-synth setup where those moments happen naturally. Recreated by clicking through Analog Lab sound-effects preset banks until an off/wrong-feeling one lands, rather than auditioning for a "good" sound. → [[Plugin Wishlist]] — loner, [the secrets behind Mk.gee's HYPNOTIC production!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=969p5Q4JSZU)
- **Glitched-out lossy vocal chop + a pitched-up texture loop, layered as two separate texture events on the same background moment.** Record a vocal chop with a melody you like → basic pitch/reverb + compression → a lossy-codec plugin (Goodhertz Lossy) for an "old MP3/nostalgic phone" character → layer a short segment of a separate loop pitched up two full octaves on top. Inspired by the background vocal chops on "How Many Miles." A lighter counterpart on the same page: a plain vocal phrase + simple room-reverb processing, sat low/filtered in the background (from "New Low") — not every texture layer needs the heavier chain. → [[Ghz Lossy 3]], [[Reverb]], [[Layering and Stacking]] — loner, [the secrets behind Mk.gee's HYPNOTIC production!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=969p5Q4JSZU)
- **Bounce an existing programmed part to audio and distort it, rather than sound-designing a separate ear-candy fill from scratch.** A snare roll that already exists in the drum loop gets bounced/printed to audio and run through distortion for a bit-crushed-sounding fill — reusing material already in the arrangement as the ear-candy source instead of building something new. Inspired by drum fills on "Alesis." → [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[Arrangement and Song Form]] — loner, [the secrets behind Mk.gee's HYPNOTIC production!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=969p5Q4JSZU)
- **A simple core groove + a sparing ear-candy layer + one register-contrast element = the "Weeknd formula"** — the underlying observation on a Weeknd/Metro Boomin-style beat: the drums/harmony stay rhythmically and harmonically simple at their core, but there are *always* lots of ear-candy elements on top for flavor. Two worked examples from the same beat: (1) a single sustained "air candy" note added purely as a texture layer, not a functional musical part; (2) a **high-pitched organ deliberately contrasted against the lower synth chords** (echoing the high organ on "Til I Bleed Out"'s outro) — register contrast used as an ear-candy device even without adding harmonic complexity. → [[AIR Slot]], [[Layering and Stacking]] — loner, [the production secrets behind the weeknd's evolving sound!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GMeZtNImaT0)
- **Two textural synth layers as pure ear candy once the main beat is locked, each with a different job** — a gritty, aggressive texture that fills otherwise-empty mid-range frequencies, layered under a second, more ambient/atmospheric texture — the Mike Dean *Rodeo* move for finishing off a track once the core elements (drums, bass, main synth/guitar) are already done. → [[MIDS Slot]], [[TEXTURE Slot]] — loner, [the secrets behind mike dean's 'rodeo' production!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V9FhndW8t74)
- **A dedicated outro-only synth section, justified explicitly by "no vocal to leave room for" — push density further once the artist is done.** Mike Dean's recurring move to close out Travis Scott album tracks: once the last vocal is out, add a whole new synth stack that would've been too busy under a vocal — a long, winding pad progression (down the scale then back up, for suspense), an aggressive synth-bass patch, a synth brass part that tracks the top note of each chord, a soft synth bell, a wide synth arp, and (panned to balance it) a plucky synth with heavy delay to help it sit. The general principle: sections with no vocal are where a producer can add the MOST ear candy, not the least — density budget freed up specifically by the absence of a vocal to protect. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]] — loner, [the secrets behind mike dean's 'rodeo' production!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V9FhndW8t74)
- **Deliberately break a plugin or DAW parameter outside its intended range as a discovery method** — Laxcity's approach: tweak a control not designed to be touched that way (his example: a master-bus pitch parameter) and record whatever comes out, treating misuse/edge-cases as a sound-design source rather than staying inside intended parameter ranges. Because everything in a DAW can be recorded, an accidental discovery is always capturable — the "mistake" only has value if you're rolling. → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]] — Sol State (ft. Laxcity), [Wish I knew this before spending $3k on plugins](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s7cx6n8HqRQ)
- **Chases imperfection and character over a polished mix, deliberately.** With age, prefers old, crackly, character-heavy mixes over clean ones — polish is easy to achieve now, but natural character is the harder skill: "I'm never looking for a super clean professional mix now, I'm looking for character, I'm looking for vibe, I'm looking for a surprise, I'm looking for something in there I don't quite understand how it was done." — Disclosure (Sol State), [Uncommon Advice from Benny Blanco, Disclosure, Kenny Beats, Chris Lake, Jon Sine](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j4aDSXwm-QI)
- **One distinctive, non-repeating element on a drum loop ices the beat — used once or twice across the whole loop, never as a recurring layer.** A tonal, clearly-pitched sound (his example: a cowbell) dropped in at exactly one syncopated moment across a multi-bar loop stands out precisely *because* it's rare against an otherwise blended kit of hats/snares/clicks. Resist the instinct to use an interesting sound throughout just because it's been introduced — find 1–3 good spots and place it only there. → [[Rhythm and Groove]] — Max Konyi, [5 Tips for Realistic Drums that Slap](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AKgd6cnQyTc)
- **Automate a tape-stop's start/stop instead of leaving it static, or swap in a fast pitch-up** — let a sound ring one time through and tape-stop the next for variation rather than repeating the same wind-down every time (automate the tape-stop plugin's own start/stop clip); a fast octave-up pitch effect gets a similarly attention-grabbing result without needing a tape-stop plugin at all, and works on full chords too. → [[Reverb]] (reverse-tape-stop riser, adjacent trick) — Synthet, [every trick to EAR CANDY](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MqqKtwdHAGw)
- **Automate a pad/arp's Decay knob live for a "door opening" swell** — ride the instrument's own Decay parameter up *while it plays* instead of leaving it static; reads as a swell/bloom rather than a static texture, and works well sped up for a faster build. Blend a pad in with light delay/reverb, and try automating the *mix* of those sends so they swell in and out rather than sitting static. → [[Automation]] — Synthet, [every trick to EAR CANDY](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MqqKtwdHAGw)
- **A short-decay high-pitch hit on beat 1, used sparingly, is an underused flavor** — a bright, short hit (crash-adjacent, some delay, stereo width) placed deliberately on the first beat of a bar reads as a memorable accent precisely because it's rare — landing on every bar kills the surprise. Vocal chops work in the same slot. For sustained high content, strings/drones as a repeating top-layer note bind a section together. → [[AIR Slot]] — Synthet, [every trick to EAR CANDY](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MqqKtwdHAGw)
- **Reverse-tape-stop riser for a transition or vocal entrance** — a slow tape-stop, reversed, plays its wind-down character backward into a rising riser; on a vocal it reads as a whoosh-in entrance. → [[Reverb]] — Synthet, [crucial tip for SOME music producers](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PTnRnI10vDM)
- **Play the reverse of the *upcoming* chord just before it lands** — chop each chord into its own clip, reverse a duplicate, and place it right before the chord it previews — primes the listener for the change rather than just decorating the current one. A section-transition variant: isolate just the FIRST chord of the next section, reverse it, and stretch/drag it to fade into the downbeat as a pickup lead-in — temporarily disable any sidechain ducking and open the filter's high end for that one moment so the reversed swell reads clearly. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]] — Synthet, [reverse ANYTHING](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tvErmRA0jAk) · You Suck at Producing, [How to write instrumentals for vocalists with Splice!](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NnIgCEn3f08)
- **Put a synth on the note behind a standout vocal word instead of a reverb throw** — loud enough to feel, hidden enough that the listener doesn't clock it. — Ian Kirkpatrick, [Hiding Synths In Vocals](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/9Eza4jngazI) · [pt 2](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/U6R0cfwdwqU)
- **Make a synth patch out of the vocal word in PadShop** — the "ooh"/"ee" of the word becomes the pad, so it fuses with the vocal. — Ian Kirkpatrick, [Hiding Synths pt 2](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/U6R0cfwdwqU)
- **Chop a vocal so it stops *before* the snare, and automate the duck on at the drop** — the stop itself is the hook detail. — Ian Kirkpatrick, [HIT Vocal Chain](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LMjkfMIhXdA)
