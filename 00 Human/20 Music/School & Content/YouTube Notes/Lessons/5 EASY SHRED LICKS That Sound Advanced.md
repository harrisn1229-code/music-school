# 5 EASY SHRED LICKS That Sound Advanced!

## Synopsis

Five licks framed as sounding harder than they actually are, each with a specific "why it's easier than it looks" mechanical explanation — a stretch-pentatonic shape with a note deliberately omitted, a whole-tone legato pattern, and a diminished-arpeggio slide sequence exploiting symmetry, among others.

## What's taught

- **Stretch pentatonic (3-note-per-string) with the middle note deliberately omitted, plus odd (5-note) grouping.** E minor pentatonic played in a stretch 3-note-per-string shape, but skipping the middle note of the 3-note set on each string — the resulting shape is nearly identical across every string except the G string (ring finger needed there). Combined with playing the lick in groups of 5 instead of a straight run, and economy picking (down-up-down-up-down-down, a small built-in sweep) rather than strict alternation — the picking pattern repeats mechanically once learned, which is why the lick reads as harder than it plays.
- **Whole-tone scale legato lick — one repeated fingering, relocated, not picked.** The A whole-tone scale (all whole-step intervals, easy to memorize once seen as a single-string pattern) fingered with index-middle-pinky, then the *exact same fingering* is simply relocated to different string locations rather than learning new shapes per position. Because it's played almost entirely with hammer-ons/pull-offs (legato) rather than picked, hand synchronization and string-transition picking accuracy are removed from the difficulty entirely — only fretting-hand timing matters.
- **Diminished arpeggio symmetry — one shape, moved in minor 3rds.** E diminished arpeggios are symmetrical (stacked minor 3rds), so the *same* arpeggio shape used for one diminished chord is reused for all of them just by sliding it up or down exactly 3 frets (a minor 3rd) — connected across positions with slides rather than separate memorized shapes per diminished chord.
- **A sweep-picking dead-zone trick:** using upstrokes specifically to "escape" a string you don't want to sound (avoiding accidental noise between target strings) rather than picking every note in strict alternation.
