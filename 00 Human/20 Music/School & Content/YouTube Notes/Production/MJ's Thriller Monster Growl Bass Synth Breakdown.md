# MJ's "Thriller" Monster Growl Bass | Synth Breakdown

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aHwu_seA6tY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aHwu_seA6tY)

## Synopsis

A one-off transitional sound effect from Thriller's bridge — three saw oscillators an octave apart, swept open by filter envelope while pitch glides down from a high starting note, used exactly once to mark the bridge's high point before the drop into the next section.

## Techniques

- **"Reverse glide" entry trick**: turn glide OFF, play (or silently set) a HIGH note first so the pitch anchor sits up top, then turn glide ON and play the actual target LOW note — the pitch audibly slides DOWN from the high starting point to the played note, rather than the more common up-glide. Combined with a slow filter-envelope sweep opening at the same time, this is what produces the "opening up from below" growl effect.
- **Three saw oscillators stacked an octave apart (not detuned/unison), equal volume, no modulation on any of them** — the pitch movement in the sound comes entirely from the glide/portamento, not from any oscillator-level modulation; simple octave-stacking plus one shared filter sweep is the whole "size" of the patch.
- **Bass-register sweeps read as richer/more satisfying specifically because low notes keep more of the swept harmonics inside audible range** — a filter sweep on a high note pushes its harmonics further above hearing range as it opens, so a comparatively smaller portion of the sweep stays audible; the same filter-envelope sweep on a low note keeps ripping through audible harmonics for its whole travel. Worth choosing the register of a filter-sweep effect deliberately for this reason, not just for pitch.
- **Keyboard filter tracking amount trades off directly against initial cutoff frequency, and matters most when a synth only offers partial/fractional tracking options (1/3, 2/3, full 1V/octave) rather than a continuous knob.** With tracking OFF, the filter's starting cutoff has to sit higher to get the same brightness a tracked filter would reach at a lower starting cutoff; the two settings compensate for each other, so "should tracking be on" isn't answerable without also knowing where cutoff itself is set. Useful when moving a patch between instruments with different tracking implementations (some are all-or-nothing, some offer fractional amounts, some are fully continuous).
- **Resonance on a low, dense bass note clears low-frequency mud rather than just adding emphasis** — pushing resonance up on this patch measurably thins the raw low end while sharpening the swept cutoff frequency, which is desirable here specifically because the full track is already dense down low; the resonant peak "carves a path" for the sound to cut through rather than fighting the rest of the low end for space.
- **Use a signature sound exactly ONCE, at the song's structural high point, rather than repeating it** — the arranging principle behind the whole patch: it appears only at the single bridge-to-final-section transition, deliberately not copy-pasted elsewhere, specifically because its rarity is what makes it register as a dramatic marker. A general orchestration reminder: identify the one moment in a pop arrangement that deserves a unique transitional sound, rather than reusing a good effect wherever it would technically fit.
