## What's taught

**The core challenge/game.** Keep a steady drone bass note going with the thumb, non-stop, while the fingers improvise melody over it — framed explicitly as a game ("don't step on the crack," "Lava Monster"): **miss the bass note and you start the whole thing over from the top.** The rule forces three things simultaneously: a locked groove/steady tempo, real thumb/finger coordination (the "one-person piano duet" feel — left hand vs. right hand independence on a single instrument), and the specific sound of a **drone bass** under a moving upper voice. Jared explicitly frames this as more valuable than undirected noodling — the constraint turns idle scale-noodling into "real music," something you could sit and play for hours. *(~0:10–2:15)*

**Setup used in the demo.** Guitar tuned down a whole step (so the "low E" drone is actually sounding D), but he thinks of it as E throughout; playing in **E minor** — E minor pentatonic, E natural minor, and E blues all get used as the scale over the same held bass note. **Prerequisite: map the scale on the fretboard first** — know where the notes of your chosen scale live before starting the drone drill; the exercise assumes fretboard fluency, it doesn't build it. *(~9:45–10:05)*

**Discipline rule: if you're going to slip, slip on the melody, never the bass.** The drone is the non-negotiable part of the exercise; treat the top line as expendable and the bass as sacred — this is the whole point of the drill, so protect it over everything else. *(~3:40)*

**The 9-step rhythmic-duration ladder** — the systematic "break it down from the ground up" method he says he actually used ~10 years ago to build this coordination, and recommends over just trying to improvise cold (which he says will just be frustrating since the hands aren't ready yet). Work through each duration on the melody voice, over the same steady drone bass, until it's solid before moving to the next — same idea as leveling up in a method book (define your own pass bar per level: "can I do this for 30 seconds straight," "can I play up and down a scale form without messing up," etc.):
1. **Whole notes** — bass notes felt as quarter notes 2-3-4 under one long melody note; deliberately slow down and let it "sink in," described as sounding "haunting," like a movie soundtrack.
2. **Dotted half notes** — every 3 beats (1-2-3, 1-2-3…).
3. **Half notes** — every 2 beats; any notes, not necessarily in the scale — pure separation/coordination training at this stage, "like what a drummer has to do," rubbing-your-tummy-patting-your-head territory.
4. **Dotted quarter notes with a rest** — a dotted quarter (quarter + eighth = 1.5 beats) then a rest, staying in 4/4; syncopated, "on the beat, off the beat" (the classic Bo Diddley-style rhythm — "one-and, one-two-and").
5. **Quarter notes straight** — comparatively easy once the syncopated version is down; flagged as the duration hands tend to default to by accident when attempting the others.
6. **Dotted eighth-note rhythm** — same on-off syncopation idea as step 4 but faster/eighth-note-based; hard to even talk over while playing it.
7. **Eighth notes straight** — constant, even eighths, no swing required; called out as directly useful for real improvising later — the skill of tying phrases together continuously "without having to stop because you ran out of places to play."
8. **Quarter-note triplets** — 3 melody notes per beat; he notes he used this duration for the harmonics figure in this very video's intro.
9. **Sixteenth notes** — 4 melody notes per bass note, the fastest and final rung; slow the tempo down as needed while staying on one note before trying to move around at speed.

Once you can do a little of *each* rung, the recommendation is to move to free improvisation over the drone — by that point "your hands are ready," so the remaining difficulty is musical (making a good line), not mechanical (the coordination itself). *(~4:55–13:10, the bulk of the video)*

**Musical variations, once the base coordination holds (for after the ladder, or to try immediately for flavor):**
- **String-position color.** Instead of picking anywhere in the scale, deliberately play the melody voice along one string group for a distinct color: top note articulated on the **2nd string (B)** for "a cool bluesy sound," the same idea moved to the **3rd string**, or to the **4th string** (playing strings 4-3-2 as a set). A simple way to get several different tone-colors out of the same scale/drone setup without changing anything else.
- **Octaves.** Play the melody voice in octaves over the drone bass for a fuller top-line sound (he flags a dedicated "getting started with octaves" video as forthcoming on his channel — not yet released at time of this video).
- **Move chords/roots over a static or shifting bass.** Once the base coordination is solid, harmony can move while the drone concept stays: keep the same root note in the bass and shift the color above it (E drone under an A minor chord shape, i.e. holding the bass constant while the harmony above it changes), or actually move the root itself (drop to an A-string bass note under an A minor chord, then move back to E) — a minimal, two-voice chord progression built directly out of the drone exercise rather than a separate skill.
- **Swap the scale color while keeping the same drone.** Same E bass note, but change what's played over it — from E minor/E minor pentatonic/E blues to **E phrygian dominant (the 5th mode of harmonic minor)** for an exotic, different-feeling color over the identical drone note. General technique: pick any scale that shares the root with your drone note and drop it in — the drone stays fixed, the color on top changes completely.
- **Self-timed endurance challenge.** Time yourself trying to keep the "don't miss the bass" rule going continuously — he notes even **one minute straight** is genuinely hard, and a full **five minutes** is "really really challenging."
