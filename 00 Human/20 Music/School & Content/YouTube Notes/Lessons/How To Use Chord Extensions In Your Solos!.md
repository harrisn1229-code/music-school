# How To Use Chord Extensions In Your Solos!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=e9tZi8NuPRs)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=e9tZi8NuPRs)

## Synopsis
A short, dense jazz-guitar lesson from Nathan Borton built entirely around a single short Wes Montgomery guitar solo from Nat Adderley's 1960 album *Work Song* (in the key of A♭). Rather than teaching scales, Borton reduces the solo to three reusable devices for getting "buttery," melodic chord extensions (9ths, 11ths, 13ths) into a solo: (1) **playing a whole chord's arpeggio on top of a different chord** — an A♭ major 7 arpeggio over an F minor 7 reads as an F minor 9 sound (and the relationship reverses too — an F minor 9 arpeggio over A♭ major 7 gives a major 13 sound), because A♭ is F minor's relative major and shares nearly all the same notes; (2) the same "play a chord on top of a chord" idea applied to a **dominant resolution**, where a shape that "shouldn't" work over the V chord functions anyway once one note is treated as a passing tone rather than a chord tone; (3) a **movable four-note guitar shape** (C-B♭-G-F) that Wes shares with Grant Green, playable in octaves up and down the neck and reusable over any diatonic chord in a key for an instant extension flavor. It's a compact, single-idea-per-example lesson — closer to a "steal this shape" video than a full harmonic-analysis breakdown — and closes by pointing the viewer at the parts of the solo it deliberately didn't cover (Wes's rhythm/articulation and his use of octaves) as further study.

## What's taught

- **Chord-on-chord arpeggio device (bar 2 of the transcription).** Wes plays an **A♭ major 7 arpeggio over an F minor 7** chord — not an F minor arpeggio, a *different* chord's arpeggio laid on top. The A♭ major 7 arpeggio (A♭-C-E♭-G) shares three notes with Fm7 (F-A♭-C-E♭) and adds the **G**, which functions as the **9th of F minor 9** — so the borrowed arpeggio reads as an extended version of the underlying chord rather than an outside sound. Most of the solo is described as simply "melodic ideas in the key of A♭" rather than a series of separately-chosen scale/arpeggio choices per chord — the A♭-major-key material naturally implies extensions over the diatonic minor chords underneath it.
- **The same relationship works in reverse.** Playing an **F minor 9 arpeggio over an A♭ major 7 chord** gives an **A♭ major 13 sound** — the relative-minor/relative-major pair is reusable in either direction depending on which chord is actually sounding. Framed as a single shape to think of as covering *both* chords: A♭maj7 ↔ Fm9 (rooted a minor 3rd apart, the standard relative-major/relative-minor relationship).
- **Extensions into a dominant resolution (bar 8 of the transcription).** Over the turnaround's dominant chord, Wes's line hits the **9th**, jumps to the **13th**, then uses the **♭9** to resolve down to the **5th of the tonic chord** — three extensions stacked into one short phrase rather than one at a time.
- **A shape that "shouldn't" work, made to work via a passing tone.** Borton reads that same bar-8 line on guitar as an **F minor(major7) shape arpeggio** — and flags directly that this shape "doesn't technically work" over the underlying **E♭7** chord *unless* the shape's flat 3rd (**A♭**) is treated as a **passing tone that resolves to the 9th (G)** rather than as a static chord tone. Played alone (no backing), the line just sounds like plain F minor; only once a real **V-I (E♭7 → A♭) progression** is put underneath it do the extensions and the resolution actually read as intended — both the **13th** and the **♭9** come out of the same line. Practical takeaway: **always test an extension-heavy shape against the actual backing chords**, not in isolation, before judging whether it "works."
- **A shared Wes Montgomery / Grant Green movable 4-note shape (bar 12 of the transcription).** Four notes — **C, B♭, G, F** — form a single movable shape that both guitarists lean on. It **transposes in octaves** up and down the neck (same four-note relationship, different register) and, more importantly, is **portable across every diatonic chord in the key** (A♭ major, in this solo) — not locked to one specific chord the way the bar-2/bar-8 devices are tied to their specific harmony. Borton explicitly leaves the choice of *which* diatonic chords to use it over as a personal-taste exercise for the viewer ("you'll have to find which chords you personally prefer to use this shape over"), rather than prescribing a fixed target chord — but states that **no matter which diatonic chord it's played against, it produces good extension color**.
- **Practice/further-study pointers (stated, not demonstrated in this video).** Learn the whole short solo by ear rather than just the three excerpted licks. Two specific things Borton flags as worth digging into on your own that this lesson doesn't cover: Wes's **rhythmic feel/articulation** (quarter-note swing rhythms mixed with triplet lines, called out early as "definitely something to be noted") and his **use of octaves** — both left as open threads for further transcription work on the same short solo.
