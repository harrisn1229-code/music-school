## Synopsis

BERNTH compares three fretboard-navigation systems for soloing — CAGED, 3-notes-per-string, and pure interval-based visualization — walking the pros/cons of each rather than pushing one as "correct." Land: learn CAGED *and* 3nps (they solve different problems), and consider interval-based thinking once fast, complex chord changes make note-by-note/shape-by-shape tracking too slow.

## What's taught

- **CAGED system, full derivation (~0:00–5:30).** Map the notes of a chord (worked example: C major = C, E, G) across all five neck positions; each position happens to fall under a familiar open-chord shape (C, A, G, E, D), which is where the name comes from. Same shapes repeat in every key — for A major, walk A-shape → G-shape → E-shape → D-shape → C-shape instead. Add the pentatonic's two extra notes (D, A for C major pentatonic) to each of the five chord shapes to get five pentatonic-scale positions; add two more (F, B) to get five full major-scale positions. **Pros:** natural bridge from chord shapes to scales/arpeggios (more musical-feeling than memorizing dot diagrams); positions are easy to remember relative to each other since they're always in the same C-A-G-E-D order regardless of key; strong side-effect theory education (you're forced to think in actual notes, not shapes); results in more musical improvising because you're tracking real notes. **Cons:** CAGED shapes mix 3-notes-per-string and 2-notes-per-string fingerings within one shape (slower for pure speed/shredding); resulting arpeggio shapes can be awkward for sweep picking; only 5 patterns total vs. 7 needed to map all 7 modes cleanly, so CAGED alone under-serves mode study.
- **His CAGED practice routine:** at each of the five CAGED positions, drill in this order — chord shape → basic arpeggio → major pentatonic → full major scale — for a complete top-to-bottom understanding of that position before moving to the next.
- **3-notes-per-string (3nps) system (~5:30–8:40).** Build symmetrical 3-note-per-string shapes for a 7-note (heptatonic) scale, yielding 7 positions across the neck instead of CAGED's 5 — and those 7 positions are simultaneously the 7 modes of the scale (e.g. for C major: the D-rooted 3nps shape = D Dorian, E-rooted = E Phrygian, F-rooted = F Lydian, G-rooted = G Mixolydian, A-rooted = A Aeolian, B-rooted = B Locrian). **Pros:** symmetry makes fast/shredding lines easier to execute; arpeggio shapes derived from 3nps positions suit sweep picking better than CAGED's; best available tool for internalizing modes since it's a complete 7-shape set. **Cons:** shredders using 3nps commonly skip the underlying theory and just memorize/run the shapes, which limits creative note choice; without deliberate study the connection between chords/scales/arpeggios is less obvious than with CAGED; some 3nps fingerings repeat a note within one string-set, which can sound "awkward" if used carelessly at first — prefer non-repeating fingerings while still learning.
- **Interval-based visualization (~8:40–end), presented as a third, more advanced option.** Instead of memorizing either shape system, apply the scale's **interval formula** directly to any root: major scale = 1-2-3-4-5-6-7 (root, M2, M3, P4, P5, M6, M7); natural minor = 1-2-♭3-4-5-♭6-♭7. Knowing the formula lets you find "the notes of G major" (or any key) on the fly without needing to recall a shape or look it up. Framed as the go-to approach for players (cites modern non-rock/metal styles) soloing over fast-moving, complex chord changes where there isn't time to reference a shape or think through accidentals — apply the interval structure to whichever root is currently sounding and bypass shape-lookup entirely. Full treatment referenced as living in BERNTH's Patreon-exclusive Fretboard Mastery course (not vault-owned — noted here as an unowned resource, not queued).
- **Overall recommendation:** learn both CAGED and 3nps — "no wrong or right," each solves a different problem (musicality/theory-connection vs. speed/mode-mapping) — and treat interval-based thinking as a later-stage upgrade once shape-based systems start feeling too slow for real improvising.
