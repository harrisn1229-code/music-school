## Techniques

- **Session build order: bass first, top of the session, before anything else.** Mosty's stated reason is dual — the bass is "an easier instrument for me to find" so it's a creative on-ramp into the mix, and in reggaeton specifically the bass is what "moves the song" for the club/dance-floor context the genre is written for; a song with insufficient or badly-placed bass risks the dance floor not moving to it. → [[Bass Design]], [[SUB Slot]]
- **Heavy compression on a sine-wave sub bass specifically to correct note-to-note loudness inconsistency, not for glue or character.** His stated reasoning: a pure sine-wave bass has no distortion/harmonic content to smooth out its dynamics, so "each moment at each frequency generates a different volume level" — heavy compression evens that out and, as a side effect, adds a bit of perceived definition to a bass that otherwise "lacks definition." *(no ratio/threshold survives the garbled transcript — described qualitatively)* → [[Bass Design]], [[Compression Staging]], [[SUB Slot]]
- **A small frequency boost on the bass "to raise the frequency a little in that area," on top of the compression** — a secondary definition move layered after the compression stage rather than a primary EQ pass. *(exact Hz not recoverable — general definition-boost move, mark as qualitative)* → [[Bass Design]], [[EQ]]
- **No sidechain on the bass against the kick on this particular song, by deliberate choice — the bass was left to "cover" the kick's energy rather than duck under it.** Mosty explicitly says he doesn't remember exactly why he skipped it here, just that he felt it wasn't needed — a data point for the "sidechain isn't mandatory, especially when the song is arranged simply" school already logged on [[Sidechaining]] and the [[4 Bass/1 Digital Bass Track|Digital Bass Track]] chain's hip-hop counter-school entry, now with a reggaeton example. → [[Sidechaining]], [[4 Bass/1 Digital Bass Track|Digital Bass Track]]
- **The dembow "click" layer's individuality comes from the COMBINATION of common samples, not the samples themselves — the samples are explicitly generic and expected to sound like everyone else's.** "The clicks... are out there in online libraries and they tend to sound the same... they were put together in a unique way." A direct, genre-specific confirmation of the "arrangement of ordinary parts creates a distinctive whole" idea — see full layer breakdown below. → [[Layering and Stacking]], [[Sampling and Sound Selection]], [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **The dembow click stack, layer by layer (at least four distinct percussion layers combined into one texture):** (1) a "12-beat" click layer pitched/EQ'd for extra top-end air; (2) the "classic" dembow pattern on four beats, phase-inverted to resolve a clash with other layers in the stack and low-cut to make room for the other clicks; (3) a layer with more overt reggaeton character (carrying some distortion) that supports and pushes the kick's punch forward in the mix; (4) a layer emphasizing the kick's own fundamental note, pitched lower, reinforcing the low end underneath the click texture. Mosty's own framing: "if you listen to them separately, they don't have the same [effect], but you're creating patterns" — the composite texture only reads as the genre's signature dembow sound once all four layers sum together. → [[Layering and Stacking]], [[Phase Alignment]], [[Genre Drum Patterns]], [[TRANSIENT Slot]]
- **Parallel kick boost: duplicate the kick channel, drive it through one aggressive plugin printed hot (no dry/wet blend retained), then double THAT processed layer again for extra body — a two-stage parallel stack, not a single duplicate.** Described as a technique "learned a long, long time ago" from another video. Build order: (1) duplicate the kick channel; (2) run the duplicate through an unnamed but "quite aggressive" plugin, committed without saving a bypass/dry blend, which alone gave "more of a click... an impressive transition compared to the original"; (3) boost the duplicate around **~60 Hz** *(qualitative — the only number that survives translation intact)*; (4) duplicate that processed layer a SECOND time ("I did that twice... double the sound") purely for extra body/attack, not to push the effect further — described as "more different bodies... more attack" rather than more aggression. → [[Kick Design]], [[Parallel Processing]], [[SUB Slot]], [[TRANSIENT Slot]]
- **Fix the phase clash the doubled parallel-kick layers create by flipping polarity on one of them before finishing the blend** — a direct, expected consequence of stage 4 above (two processed duplicates of the same hit rarely stay in phase with each other), resolved with a straightforward polarity flip rather than manual time-nudging. *(the specific plugin/method used past the flip is unrecoverable in translation)* → [[Phase Alignment]], [[Parallel Processing]]
- **Tune the parallel kick's emphasized frequency to the song's tonal center, not an arbitrary "kick frequency."** Mosty frames the boosted-frequency choice on the parallel layer as tied to "the fundamental of the tonality of the song" rather than a fixed go-to number — the same "tune the kick to the key" principle already logged from Disclosure on [[Kick Design]], now confirmed independently from a reggaeton mixer. *(exact frequency/key not recoverable)* → [[Kick Design]], [[SUB Slot]]
- **Loosen a programmed dembow pattern off its strict grid using the DAW's swing function, credited to a collaborator ("Sky") rather than Mosty himself.** The dembow pattern as typically programmed reads as fairly grid-locked/mechanical; the collaborator applied FL Studio's swing parameter to the pattern specifically to soften that grid-locked feel. *(no swing percentage or subdivision given)* → [[Rhythm and Groove]], [[Genre Drum Patterns]]
