# Digital Patterns

Part of [[Jazz Harmony]]. A **numeric, scale-degree-based melodic cell** — a short (typically four- or
eight-note) phrase written as scale-degree numbers rather than as absolute notes, so the exact same
shape transfers instantly to any scale, any chord, any key. The concept is Jerry Coker's, from his book
*Elements of Jazz Improvisation* (traced back to David Baker), taught here by Steve Kabulk. It solves a
specific problem the video opens with: **"what do you play in between your licks and language?"** — the
connective tissue that keeps a solo from sounding "noodly and unfocused" between the phrases you already
know.

## The core idea

Number every note of a scale 1 through 8 (C major, ascending: C=1, D=2, E=3, F=4, G=5, A=6, B=7, C=8).
A digital pattern is that same sequence of *numbers*, not notes — e.g. **1-2-3-5** or the eight-note
**1-2-3-4-5-3-2-1** — played forward or backward. Because the pattern is stored as scale degrees, not
pitches, it is instantly:
- **Portable across keys** — the same 1-2-3-5 shape works over any chord once you know that chord's
  scale.
- **Portable across scale quality** — apply the identical numeric shape to C Dorian instead of C major
  and the pattern automatically becomes minor; no new fingering to learn, just a new scale underneath
  the same numbers.
- **Stackable** — described directly as "musical Legos": short, neutral cells that combine over any
  chord or reproduce the sound of any scale, useful standalone before they're ever combined with
  anything else.

Two named patterns from the lesson: **1-2-3-5** (four notes) and **1-2-3-4-5-3-2-1** (eight notes,
ascending then descending) and **1-2-3-4-5-7-6-5** (eight notes, used as a connector — see below).
*(general note: the book itself catalogs many more numeric cells than the handful demonstrated in this
video — treat these three as a starting vocabulary, not the whole system.)*

## Use 1 — generate ideas from scratch

The most basic application: play a digital pattern directly over its matching chord/scale as a
melodic idea in its own right, no other vocabulary required. This is the "just by themselves, they are
game changers" use case — a fast way to have *something* concrete to play that isn't scale-noodling.

## Use 2 — connect existing licks and language

The video's worked example: a phrase from **"Honeysuckle Rose"** played over an F7 chord, set inside a
2-5-1 (Gm7 – C7 – F). To lead *into* the F7 language, play a digital pattern over the two chord (Gm7)
first — **1-2-3-4-5-7-6-5** is named as "the perfect melodic bridge" into the language that follows.
Reversing the order works too: play the F7 language first, then use a digital pattern to *exit* the F7
chord — the worked example closes the whole phrase with a **1-2-3-5** pattern over the B♭maj7 (the I
chord). This is the direct answer to the "what fills the gap between licks" question the lesson opens
with — a digital pattern is deliberately neutral connective material, not another lick to memorize.

🎬 *From video:* mix in [[Bebop Vocabulary|enclosures]] leading into the first note of a digital pattern,
and use the underlying chord's **arpeggio** as another connective element in the same phrase — the
worked example chains all three: a C minor arpeggio → a digital pattern → existing language → an
enclosure into the first note of the next digital pattern (B♭) → the eight-note pattern ascending → a
closing arpeggio of the chord. Same "learn it, then combine it" logic as
[[Chord-Tone Soloing]]'s ladder — digital patterns, enclosures, and arpeggios are three separate,
individually-drillable building blocks meant to be strung together, not one monolithic device.

## Use 3 — access advanced sounds via chord-scale substitution

This is where digital patterns stop being just a connective filler and start functioning as a
**substitution/reharmonization tool**, because the pattern is numeric and scale-agnostic — swap the
underlying scale and the same numbers instantly outline a different (and more advanced) sound.

**Minor 9 sound over a minor chord — upper-structure minor a 5th up.** It's common to play a minor
chord/scale built off the **5th degree** over a minor chord to imply a minor 9 (extended) sound: over
Cm, play G minor (G-B♭-D-F) instead of C minor — G minor 7's notes are C minor's 5th, ♭7, 9th, and 11th,
so it colors the plain triad without restating the root. Apply a digital pattern to the G minor scale
while the underlying chord is still Cm, or **alternate** C-minor-based and G-minor-based digital
patterns in the same phrase to mix the plain and extended colors. **The same device also works over a
major chord**, off the same 5th-degree relationship — "we're gonna play major over major": over a
B♭maj7, play digital patterns built on an F-major scale/arpeggio (F is B♭'s 5th) rather than off B♭maj7
directly, worked as the closing move of the video's 2-5-1 example. → [[Upper Structure Chords]],
[[Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords]] (related "chord built above the root, played over the
same root" devices, worked out in more formal detail) · relevant slot: this is a harmony-outlining move
over the [[BED Slot]]/[[LEAD Slot]] register where a solo line sits.

**Altered-dominant sound — minor a half step above the root, from melodic minor.** Over a dominant 7
chord, play digital patterns off the **minor scale/arpeggio a half step above the root**, and
specifically off that **key's melodic minor** for the true altered-dominant color: for F7, that's
**G♭ minor** → **G♭ melodic minor** for the digital patterns. (G♭ melodic minor is the same pitch
collection as the F altered scale — the 7th mode of melodic minor built a half step above the dominant
root — so this is the same altered-scale-over-a-dominant logic as [[Altered Chords]] and
[[Scale Modes Reference]], reached here through a minor-scale digital pattern rather than through
naming the altered scale directly.) **Full worked 2-5-1 pathway** combining both substitution ideas,
stated directly in the video as "G minor for the two chord, G♭ going down a half step for the five
chord": over the `Gm7 – F7 – B♭(maj7)` 2-5-1, play **G minor** digital patterns on the ii (Gm7 — already
minor, no substitution needed), then **G♭ minor / G♭ melodic minor** digital patterns on the V (F7, the
altered-dominant substitution above), resolving into the I (B♭maj7, closed with the 5th-degree-major
substitution — an F-major-based digital pattern — from the previous section). → [[Altered Chords]],
[[Scale Modes Reference]], [[Chord Substitution]].

**Tritone substitution pathway.** The tritone sub replaces a dominant 7 chord with the dominant 7 a
tritone (three whole steps) away — same rule as [[Chord Substitution]] §2. Applied to the same 2-5-1:
F7's tritone sub is B7, so the digital-pattern pathway simplifies to **[G] minor → B[7] → B♭** (ii,
tritone-sub V, I) — a cleaner three-stop path than the altered-scale route above, landing on the same
destination chord. *(Caption confidence flag: the transcript names the first stop "C minor" here,
which conflicts with "G minor for the two chord" stated moments earlier for the same Gm7–F7–B♭maj7
2-5-1 — captured as the more internally-consistent [G minor] reading rather than guessed, but treat the
first-chord label as unconfirmed until re-checked against the video audio.)* → [[Chord Substitution]] §2.

**Navigating hard changes — the Coltrane reference.** The lesson names **John Coltrane's use of digital
patterns on "Giant Steps"** as the classic advanced example: because the pattern is stored as portable
scale-degree shapes rather than memorized fingerings, the same handful of numeric cells can be
re-applied at speed across Giant Steps' fast-moving key centers — a direct, practical link to
[[Chord Substitution]] §5's Coltrane changes / three-tonic-system material. Also usable *within* one
static sound to link bebop ideas together, not just across changing harmony.

## Practice progression (four phases, from the video)

1. **Foundation.** Play a couple of digital patterns through an entire tune, forward and backward, over
   whatever harmony is passing — just getting comfortable with the shapes before combining them with
   anything else.
2. **Connection.** Mix in simple arpeggios, using them to connect into the digital patterns drilled in
   phase 1.
3. **Integration.** Add in language/licks you're currently working on (e.g. the Honeysuckle Rose lick
   from this lesson) around the patterns.
4. **Substitution.** Use digital patterns to outline substitute harmonies (upper-structure minor/major,
   altered-dominant minor, tritone sub) and connect them with the vocabulary already internalized in
   phases 1–3.

Stated directly: work on only a couple of patterns at a time and expect internalization to take a
while — the same "over-learn one device before adding the next" method as
[[Chord-Tone Soloing]]'s ladder and [[Bebop Vocabulary]]'s enclosures.
