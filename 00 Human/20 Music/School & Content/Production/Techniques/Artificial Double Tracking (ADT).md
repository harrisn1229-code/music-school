# Artificial Double Tracking (ADT)

**What it is:** a studio process that **fakes a second take from a single performance** — the signature Beatles/Lennon vocal-doubling sound. One vocal reads as two slightly-different voices without singing it twice.

## The origin (log this)
- Invented by Abbey Road engineer **Ken Townsend in April 1966**, expressly because **John Lennon hated the tedium of manually double-tracking his vocals** and asked for a shortcut. First used across *Revolver*, then on nearly every Lennon vocal (and many instruments) from 1966 on.
- **How the original worked:** the recorded signal was tapped from one tape machine and fed to a **second tape machine to delay it**; the delay time was **wavered by driving that machine's capstan with a variable-frequency (varispeed) oscillator**, then the delayed copy was recombined with the original. Two machines → one wandering, slightly-detuned copy.
- Lennon called it **"Ken's flanger"**; McCartney famously fobbed him off with a nonsense explanation about a "double-bifurcated sploshing flange" — which is where the word **[[Modulation|flanging]]** comes from. ADT and flanging are cousins (both = signal + short *modulated* delay); flanging uses shorter delays + feedback for the jet-swoosh, ADT uses a slightly longer, subtler wander.

## Why it works
- A **static** short delay is just a slap/comb. The **modulation** (the varispeed wobble) is the whole trick — it makes the copy drift in **time and pitch** like a real second performance would, so the ear hears "two takes," not "one take + echo."
- Typical feel: **~7–25 ms delay** + a slow, shallow **pitch/time wander** (a few cents). Pan the copy opposite the dry for width, or keep it centered for thickness.

## How to do it in the box (owned tools)
The modern equivalent is a **micro-pitch/modulated-delay doubler** — pick by flavor:
- **[[MicroShift]]** / **[[Little MicroShift]]** — the cleanest modern ADT (Eventide H3000 micro-pitch): detuned + delayed L/R copies. **Style I, Detune/Delay ~100%, Focus ~120 Hz, Mix 100% on a [[Width Send|width return]].** Best for a wide, invisible double.
- **[[Doubler2]] / [[Doubler4]]** — literally a modeled ADT: set **Detune ~8–15 cents, Delay ~10–20 ms, Modulation (Rate/Depth) ON** so the voices wander, Dry/Wet ~20–30%. This is the closest to the tape behavior.
- **[[Chorus-Ensemble]] / [[kHs Chorus]]** — more audible modulation (a "wider" wander) when you want vintage motion, not an invisible double.
- **[[EchoBoy]] / [[kHs Delay]]** — a single ~15 ms delay with **wow/flutter or a modulated time** for a mono ADT-thickening in one channel.

## When to reach for it
- **Thickening a single lead** without a real double (the Lennon move) — or **doubling ad-libs/harmonies** fast. See the width hierarchy in [[Width and Stereo]]: a **real** double still beats ADT for width; ADT is for a bigger-from-one-take sound or the specific vintage wander.
- Its natural home is [[1 Double Vocal Track|Double Vocal Track]] (as the *fake-double fallback*) and the [[Width Send]].
- **Beatles/vintage lane:** the authentic ADT sound is slightly detuned, wandering, and often panned to one side (Lennon's vocal frequently sat ADT'd and offset).

## From videos

- **A real-world case where an unintended discrepancy between two manually double-tracked takes became a permanent, audible fingerprint on a released recording.** "Imagine"'s piano riff carries a faint "ghostly" D note in some published transcriptions but not others — session footage confirms Lennon doesn't play it on his primary take, and the likely source is the double-tracked overdub layered on top (they'd briefly tried a second live keyboardist, Nicky Hopkins, before scrapping that for Lennon overdubbing himself). **Diagnostic tip**: listen for where a part suddenly widens from mono/narrow into full stereo image — that's often exactly where a second independently-performed take has been layered in, and any note-level inconsistency between the two takes shows up as an audible artifact right there. → David Bennett Music Theory, [[John Lennons imaginary note]]
- **The origin mechanism, from the Abbey Road engineer who built it**: Ken Townsend routed the same signal down two paths — one dry, one delayed by roughly 100ms (achieved via tape speed offset, 15 vs. ~14 in/sec) — then recombined them, producing the same thickened effect as a manually re-sung double take without requiring a second vocal performance. Built specifically because John Lennon had grown tired of the tedium of real double-tracking (re-singing a take to match the original's exact timing/phrasing); Lennon "fell in love with it straight away" on first hearing it. Audible on "I'm Only Sleeping" — the first line ("Lift my head") is a dry take, the next line is passed through ADT and noticeably thickens. → [[Vocal Tuning]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[8 Things The Beatles Pioneered]]
- **De-ess a double MUCH harder than the lead — sibilant/consonant timing mismatches are what give away "two takes" faster than any other cue.** Doubling works because small timing/pitch/amplitude/frequency variations between takes blend into one bigger, more complex sound — but sibilants and hard consonants are short and percussive, so if they land even slightly out of time between the lead and the double, the ear catches the mismatch and hears two distinct performances instead of one thick one. Fix: de-ess the double/BGV much harder than the lead so it blends rather than announces its own timing; alternatively attenuate highs with EQ/dynamic EQ, or boost the mids slightly (accentuating the pitch differential between takes produces a subtle chorus effect that helps fusion). Directly reinforces this note's own five-cue grouping mechanism above — onset mismatch (cue 2) is exactly what a mistimed S/T/K exposes. → [[De-essing]], [[Vocal Stacks and Ad-libs]] — Sage Audio, [The 5 MOST IMPORTANT Vocal Mixing Tips](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rXk1lvT6AhU)
- **Why doubling reads as "one voice" instead of two — auditory scene analysis (the "cocktail party effect") names the exact five cues a doubled take must share.** The brain groups sound sources as one when they share: harmonic content, onset (when the sound begins), frequency co-modulation, amplitude co-modulation, and location. A double is different enough not to be phase-identical, but similar enough across all five to read as one thickened voice rather than two distinct ones — this is the mechanism, not just a description, behind the doubling effect. → [[Width and Stereo]]
- **Deliberately GROUP two dissimilar sources (different singers, a lead + counter-melody) into reading as one, by matching them across all five auditory-scene-analysis cues — a five-move recipe.** Worked example, two vocal takes from different singers tracked in different rooms: (1) **compress both similarly** → matches amplitude co-modulation; (2) **de-ess both** → attenuates high-frequency transients similarly, reducing onset differences (see [[De-essing]]); (3) **EQ both similarly** → makes frequency responses read alike; (4) **saturate both with identical settings** → generates overlapping harmonic content even with different underlying melodies, more effective the more aggressive the saturation (see [[Saturation and Distortion]]); (5) **send both to the same reverb/delay and pan both to the same location** → matches the location cue. Won't read as identical as a true double, but meaningfully increases the odds the two voices are perceived as belonging together — usable for genuine grouping (a duet) or, run in reverse (keep the cues distinct), for deliberate separation of two similar-sounding layers. → [[De-essing]], [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[Compression]], [[Width and Stereo]] — Sage Audio, [Psychoacoustics: The Least Talked About, Most Important Part of Music Production Pt.1](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SZ_2yIPp4g4)
- **The doubler audibility test: never chorus/phasey, and the proof it's doing its job is that pulling it out is audibly worse — not that you can hear it while it's in.** Greg Wells: "a little bit of kind of a doubler effect is nice if it doesn't sound phasey, if it doesn't sound like a chorus thing." On a "raging rock track" specifically, a little doubling avoids having to turn the vocal fader up to compete — the target is you never consciously register the doubler while it's on, but "if you take the doubler out, it's like — what happened, why doesn't it sound as good." Same "invisible until removed" standard Wells applies to reverb, delay, and tuning elsewhere in the same breakdown. *(qualitative — no detune/delay/mix values given)* → [[Doubler2]], [[Depth and Space]], [[1 Main Vocal Track]] — Greg Wells (Mix with the Masters), [[Bringing vocals out of the speakers with Greg Wells]]
- **A "basic" doubler used sparingly — light width, not a wide effect — as the closing stage of a fixed, always-the-same tracking-vocal chain.** After a fixed EQ recipe, light compression, RVox (push forward), and reverb/delay sends, the chain closes with "the simple, the basic doubler, just to kind of spread his voice out just a little bit." Reached for identically on every session, built live into the artist's headphone monitor while they're tracking. Owned equivalent: [[Doubler2]] (2-voice matches "basic" better than the 4-voice [[Doubler4]]). *(qualitative — no detune/delay/mix values given)* → [[Doubler2]], [[Width and Stereo]], [[1 Main Vocal Track]] — Kuk Harrell, [[Radio-ready vocal effects with Kuk Harrell]]
- **ADT applied to a GUITAR for width, not a vocal — a use case outside ADT's usual doubling role.** On a from-scratch 60s/70s psych-rock guitar recreation, Waves Real ADT (unowned, see [[Plugin Wishlist]]) was inserted after the amp+pedal stage specifically "to widen the guitar even more," ahead of reverb/delay. Owned equivalent: [[Doubler2]] or [[MicroShift]] at the settings above. Distinct on the same build from a REAL double on the vocal (record the vocal twice, matched as closely as possible) — the two techniques side-by-side confirm the width hierarchy: a real double for the vocal, an ADT-style fake for the guitar where a second take wasn't practical. → [[Beatles Psych-Rock Sample Chain]], [[Fender Stratocaster]] — loner, [these 1960's production secrets will change the way you make samples!?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ngz_fZraTP4)
