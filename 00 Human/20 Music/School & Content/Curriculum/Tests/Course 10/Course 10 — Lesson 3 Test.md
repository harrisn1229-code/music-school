> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **A-B-A-C inside the single loop.**
> Four bars that aren't four copies: a simple bar, a varied bar, back to simple, then a different variation.
>
> **Q2.** **The inverse of the kick — strong-beat hats on B, syncopated hats on A.**
> Whatever the kick is doing, the hats do the opposite. That way something is always anchoring and something is always playing around, and the two keep swapping which is which.
>
> **Q3.** **Because the cycle doesn't resolve until all four bars have passed.**
> If bar 2 varied the kick *and* the hats in the same direction, both elements would be busy at once and then both plain at once — one change the ear reads in a single bar. Inverting them means each bar has an anchor and a decoration, but a *different* one each time. The listener has to hold all four bars to hear the cycle, which is the difference between a four-bar loop and a one-bar loop played four times.
> *Rubric (5):* says the inversion keeps an anchor present in every bar (2) · says the roles swap rather than both changing together (2) · says the cycle only resolves across the full four bars (1)
>
> **Q4.** **Sparsest Intro that works, duplicate for A, make exactly three changes. Three more for B.**
> Each tier is the previous tier plus three, so you get Intro / A / B as finished clips before any arranging happens.
>
> **Q5.** **An existing element made more active, or a new element added.**
> Each must be immediately obvious and present throughout the section. Subtle or intermittent changes don't count — the point is that a listener registers the tier change without being told.
>
> **Q6.** **The swap counts. The crash doesn't.**
> A swap — jingle bell to ride, toms pitched up into a new voice — is a real change to what's present. Crashes and tom fills are transitional and don't count toward the three.
>
> **Q7.** **Four jumping-off points instead of one clip you keep overfilling.**
> Building one loop and thinning it later means every decision is a subtraction from something you've already fallen in love with, and the natural pull is to keep adding rather than to strip. Starting from the sparsest version and adding in fixed increments means the quiet tiers actually exist as finished material, so an arrangement has somewhere to go early on rather than starting at full density and having nowhere to build to.
> *Rubric (5):* says you get multiple finished density levels up front (2) · says the alternative overfills a single clip (2) · notes it leaves room to build, or that subtraction later is harder than addition (1)
>
> **Q8.** **A group where one sample cuts another off — enable *cut itself* on the group.**
> *Also accepted:* choke / cut self / mono group
>
> **Q9.** **A real kit can't ring an open hat through a closed one. Same thinking: overlapping bass tails.**
> One pair of hats, one state at a time. Bass tails are the worse case, and the four-limb ceiling is the same constraint applied to writing rather than to tails.
> *Also accepted:* 808/bass note overlap · a drummer only has four limbs
>
> **Q10.** **Thin out rather than stack.** Don't add a fourth change.
> Past a point more elements stop reading as more energy. The method's own answer to a flat section is subtraction, not another layer.
