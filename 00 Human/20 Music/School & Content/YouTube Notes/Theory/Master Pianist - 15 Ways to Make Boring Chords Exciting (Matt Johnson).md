# Master Pianist: 15 Ways To Make BORING Chords EXCITING (Matt Johnson from Jamiroquai) [Chord Hacks]

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E_-wCr48RJM)

## Synopsis

Jamiroquai's keyboardist Matt Johnson on what actually makes a chord sequence feel interesting. His core claim: no chord is boring in isolation — it's context (what comes before/after, what plays over it) that does the work. Covers circle-of-fifths motion, slash-chord bass tricks, borrowed major/minor substitution, rhythmic placement of chords (not just their pitches), left-hand bass reversal, and — the most transferable idea for a producer — fitting harmony around the vocal rather than the other way around, plus the "one curveball" theory of what makes an otherwise-predictable progression memorable.

## Techniques

- **A chord isn't boring on its own — context (before, after, and what's layered over it) is what makes it feel exciting or flat.** The framing behind every other technique in this video: changing perceived interest without changing the chord itself is usually possible.
- **Circle-of-fifths root motion as an instantly-familiar "round robin" progression** — root movement by descending fifths (equivalently, ascending fourths) strings chords together in a way that sounds recognizable even to listeners with no theory vocabulary for it. Worked example: Cm9 → Fm9 → B♭m9 → E♭m9 → A♭maj7 → D♭maj7 → back to Cm9. → [[Chord Progressions]]
- **Change the bass note under an unchanged chord (a slash-chord move) to create movement without changing the harmony itself.** Example: an F minor 7 chord with its bass note raised a fourth to B♭ — same chord, different bass, "feels completely different." A small, cheap way to add motion to a static-feeling loop. → [[Chord Inversions]]
- **Swap one expected minor chord for major (or vice versa) as a simple, reliable "spice" move** — when a sequence feels predictable, changing the quality of just one chord (not adding new chords) is often enough to make the whole thing feel fresh. → [[Borrowed Chords]]
- **End a chord loop on a V (a fifth) to resolve smoothly back to the top of the progression** — a slash-chord V (in C, an F over a G bass) reliably returns a loop to its start; cited example: Anita Baker's "You're the Best Thing." → [[Chord Progressions]]
- **Push a chord in early (e.g., by a 16th note) instead of hitting it square on the beat — rhythmic placement, not new harmony, makes a sequence feel like it's flowing.** Distinct from adding chords: the same two chords played with syncopated timing feel far more alive than the identical chords played squarely on the downbeats. → [[Rhythm and Groove]]
- **Insert a passing chord purely for rhythmic push, even when it adds little real harmonic weight** — approaching the target chord from a semitone or whole tone below (or via its own V) right before it lands gives a little forward "push" into the next chord. Cited as a habit throughout Jamiroquai's own catalog (e.g., "Don't Give It Up") — the passing chord's job is momentum, not harmonic color. → [[Chord Progressions]]
- **Reverse which chord's bass note plays first in a two-chord vamp, without changing the chords themselves, for a completely different mood.** Worked example: Brian Wilson's "Surf's Up" verse cycles G minor 7 and D minor 7, but the bass plays the SECOND chord's note under the FIRST chord (and vice versa) rather than following the chords in lockstep — same two chords, reversed bass order, different feeling entirely. A left-hand-bass technique distinct from always playing the root. → [[Chord Inversions]], [[Bassline Writing]]
- **Fit harmonic movement into the gaps BETWEEN vocal phrases, not underneath the vocal itself.** The single most producer-relevant piece of advice in the video: let the singer's phrase finish, then move the harmony in the space that opens up — rather than harmonic movement and vocal movement competing for attention simultaneously. When producing a singer, actively resist the instinct to have them sing constantly; a simpler, sparser vocal line supported by strong harmonic movement underneath it usually beats a busy vocal over static chords — most hit records are built this way. Cites Jay Kay's riff-and-repeat vocal style specifically as an example: the chords carry the "flavor" and movement while the vocal repeats a simple line on top. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]], [[Melody Writing]]
- **The "one curveball" theory of a memorable progression: a genuinely good progression is mostly predictable, with exactly one unexpected moment that catches the ear precisely because it violates the pattern.** Worked example: substituting a major 7 into a spot the ear expects a minor chord. Too many surprises and nothing stands out as special; the skill is picking the one moment that gets to be unexpected and leaving the rest familiar. → [[Borrowed Chords]], [[Chord Progressions]]
