# Jack Ruch — TRICKS and TIPS for Soloing Over the Most Common BLUES Progression!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7dgl0Q4gOlw)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7dgl0Q4gOlw)

## Synopsis

Jack Ruch (blues/jazz guitar teacher — see also [[Jack Ruch - Beyond Pentatonics - Add Dominant 7 Arpeggios to Your Blues]], [[Blues Lead Phrasing and Touch]]) breaks down two full solos over a **12-bar blues shuffle in E** (E7-A7-B7, the standard blues-gig/jam-session progression), teaching two concepts he says are the actual point of the lesson: (1) **building a short melodic motif and repeating it** to give a solo structure, and (2) **using chord tones to outline the changes** — specifically, reading chord tones straight out of a visualized chord grip rather than a memorized scale/arpeggio system. Both solos are played entirely in **open position**, so a recurring, distinctive thread runs through the whole video: exploiting E's open strings (high E, open B, open low E) as ringing pedal tones and pivot points underneath fretted lines, including a hybrid pick-and-middle-finger technique to sustain one while picking the other. The most concrete, drillable material is the two-bar-motif structure (build it, repeat it verbatim, then vary the repeat on a second pass so it sets up the next chord change), the specific IV/V chord-tone targets he reads out of the A7 and B7 grips, and the recurring turnaround shape (double-stop into a flat-5 passing move into a chromatic walk-up). The video ends with the two solos strung together into one longer performance take (no new technique content in that closing performance beyond what's already broken down).

## What's taught

**Form and setup:** 12-bar blues shuffle in the key of **E** (I7 = E7, IV7 = A7, V7 = B7) — called out directly as "one of the most common types of tunes that gets called on blues gigs and jam sessions." Both solos are played in **open position** (not a moveable CAGED shape), and both are organized the same way: **divide the 12-bar form into 2-bar chunks** and treat each chunk as a unit to build phrasing around.

**Concept 1 — motif building and repetition (Solo 1, bars 1–4):**
- Motif built from **E minor pentatonic**: B (4th fret, G string) hammered up to D (3rd fret, B string) then E (5th fret, B string), back down to B; slide down to A; a half-step slide up from A to **B♭ (the ♭5 blue note)**, pull off to open G; hammer on from G up to **G♯ (the major 3rd)** — the classic blues ♭3→3 blend, "breaking out of the minor pentatonic a little bit."
- While playing that shape, the **high E string rings out** underneath — grabbed and held with the picking-hand **middle finger** while the pick handles the fretted notes (see Touch/hybrid-picking below).
- **Structure: play the motif over bars 1–2, then repeat it over bars 3–4** before the IV chord arrives — the repetition itself is the phrasing device, giving the solo a recognizable 2-bar unit before the harmony moves.
- On chord tones: this motif isn't chord-tone-derived — it's the "stay in one scale, add a signature blues inflection" half of the lesson, contrasted directly against Concept 2 below.

**Concept 2 — chord-tone targeting off a visualized chord shape (Solo 1, bars 5–8 and 9–end):**
- **On the IV chord (A7, bars 5–6):** instead of continuing the pentatonic motif, Ruch looks at the **A7 chord shape** as "a visual guide to see where my chord tones are" — slides up to **C♯ (the 3rd)**, then grabs **G (the ♭7)** right out of that same chord grip, using the open E string as a connector. Stated directly: "really all that is is the third, fifth, and flat 7 of an A7 chord" — read straight off the shape, not derived from a scale run.
- **Bars 7–8:** back to the original motif (repeated again — a third structural use of the same 2-bar idea).
- **On the V chord (B7, bars 9–10):** a named "go-to move" — use the **open B string as a pivot point** (it's the root of B7), playing a line built loosely out of B minor pentatonic with one **chromatic passing note**: B up to D, open E, up to F♯, then pull off F back to E.
- **Back on the IV chord (A7) again:** repeats the earlier 3rd/5th/♭7 targeting idea, this time framed as a descending E-blues-scale-flavored line using the same flat-5 slide (A→B♭) and minor-to-major-3rd hammer (G→G♯) devices from the opening motif — chord-tone targeting and motif vocabulary deliberately blended together, not kept as separate toolkits.
- **Turnaround:** a "go-to lick" built on a **double stop — B (5th) with D (♭7) on top of an E7** — with the open high E string ringing underneath; then a repeat of the flat-5-passing move (slide A→B♭, hammer G→G♯); then a **chromatic walk-up from A to B** to land squarely on the incoming B7 (V) at the top of the next chorus.

**Solo 2 — blended pentatonics, motif variation, and a full arpeggio run:**
- Opens by **blending E major pentatonic and E minor pentatonic in the same phrase**: starts on notes from E major pentatonic (C♯, E, F♯), shifts into minor-pentatonic territory with a pull-off from D down to B (♭7 down to 5th), hits A, then another pull-off from G down to F♯ (minor 3rd down to the 2nd) down to the root, then up to the major 6th — explicitly described as combining the minor pentatonic's ♭7/♭3 with the major pentatonic's 2nd/3rd/6th in one line.
- **Same 2-bar-chunk structure as Solo 1, but with the second repetition deliberately varied rather than identical.** The first motif pass (bars 1–2) is repeated with a twist the second time (bars 3–4): slides up to a high C♯ on the high E string, grabs the G below it, descends blending minor/major pentatonic again, but this time **ends on C♯ — the 3rd of the upcoming A7 chord** — turning the repeat's ending into a chord-tone target that sets up the IV chord rather than just echoing the opening. This is the clearest concrete instance in the video of "vary the repeat so it pivots into what's coming next."
- **On the IV chord (A7):** from that C♯ landing note, plays a full **A7 arpeggio run** (root-3rd-5th-♭7, back down through root-3rd-5th-♭7) — the same shape as Solo 1's single-note targeting, now extended into a complete arpeggio pass — followed by a line blending the arpeggio with bluesy chromatic vocabulary, using the open E string as a pivot (mirroring the open-B-string pivot used over B7 in Solo 1) and the same ♭5 chromatic passing-tone device.
- **Back to the I chord (E):** reuses earlier motif material (♭7-5th-minor 3rd-9th-root descending line).
- **On the V chord (B7):** chord-shape targeting again, this time explicitly reading off **two stacked chord grips** — a B bar chord and its first inversion (3rd in the bass) — playing straight out of both: root, major 3rd, 5th, root, ♭7.
- **Back on the IV chord (A7):** the same A7-bar-chord-shape targeting (root-3rd-5th-♭7, root), then a deliberate switch back to **bluesy E minor pentatonic with the ♭5 blended in**, closing the solo on a straight E minor pentatonic phrase.

**Closing performance:** the two solos are strung together — open-position Solo 1 (with the ringing high E), then a position shift up the neck into Solo 2, then several more choruses continuing to combine the phrasing-motif and chord-tone-targeting ideas freely. No new technique introduced in this section; framed explicitly as a listening exercise ("pay attention to how I'm using some phrasing motifs to structure the solo and... ways that I'm using chord tones to outline the different changes").
