## Synopsis

Video #17 in Jared Borkowski's (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared) top-25-jazz-standards series, covering "Someday My Prince Will Come" — Frank Churchill and Larry Morey's 1937 waltz from Disney's "Snow White," canonized as a jazz standard largely through Miles Davis's 1961 recording. Two things make this entry distinctive within the series: it's a 3/4 waltz (an unusual, valuable time-feel against a catalog otherwise dominated by 4/4), and its 2nd chord is a dominant 7♯5 — described as the tune's signature sound and reason enough on its own to learn it. The lesson's two standout technical moments are a superimposition trick (any inversion of a half-diminished shape doubles as a rootless dominant-7♭9♭13 voicing a major third above its root — meaning fretboard work on half-diminished shapes pays double duty) and a concrete, no-nonsense jazz-waltz comping backdrop (bass-note-then-two-chord-stabs on "1, and-of-2, 3") that solves the specific problem of 3/4 feeling unfamiliar after a career mostly spent in 4/4. The melody analysis also introduces a genuine classical-theory term — the appoggiatura — precisely and with a worked example, rather than the looser "passing tone" language used elsewhere in the series.

## What's taught

- **Song background.** Written 1937 by Frank Churchill (music) and Larry Morey (lyrics) for "Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs." Solidified as a jazz standard via Miles Davis's 1961 recording (with John Coltrane, Hank Mobley, Wynton Kelly, Paul Chambers, Jimmy Cobb). A 3/4 waltz, 32-bar ABAC form, harmonically rich (2-5-1s, modulations, chromatic chords, inversions) — flagged as a great tune specifically for building comfort in 3/4, a time feel many jazz players under-practice relative to 4/4.
- **Appoggiatura, precisely defined and demonstrated twice in the same phrase.** A non-chord tone that is *leapt to* (not stepped to) and *resolves down by step*, and which must land on a strong beat — distinguished on the page from the looser "passing tone"/"approach tone" language. Both matching A/B theme phrases in the melody use one: a leap up to the chord's ♯11 (a non-chord tone), landing on a strong beat, resolving down by step onto the chord's 3rd. The video also separately points out **thematic repetition** as a compositional lesson worth internalizing on its own — the two phrases share both rhythm and actual pitches, not just a rhythmic shape, and "it's always so much simpler than we think" when a song is well constructed. → [[Non-Harmonic Tones]] § *From videos*
- **Chords: key of B♭ major.** Tonic B♭6/B♭maj7 — major 6 and major 7 fully interchangeable off any root (same practice habit documented from [[BODY AND SOUL Guitar Lesson (chords, tab, scales, melody) No. 18 of Top 25 Jazz Standards for Guitar|Body and Soul]]). **A generalizable rule about the IV chord of any major key:** the major IV chord always has an available ♯11, because the 4th degree of a major scale, treated as its own root, produces the Lydian mode (♯4/♯11) — demonstrated on the tune's E♭maj7 (IV of B♭) even where the melody doesn't strictly require the ♯11.
- **The tune's signature sound: a dominant 7♯5 (as the iii chord, in second position) — flagged as worth learning this tune for on its own.** A flat-13 voicing is preferred in practice over a literal sharp-5 shape (same pitch, different practical spelling/fingering — flat-13 keeps the 5th natural in the grip) — the flat-13 reading is itself an interpretation signaling the chord comes from the C harmonic minor scale (V of the relevant minor key).
- **Slash chords for deliberate chromatic bass motion, same device as in [[BODY AND SOUL Guitar Lesson (chords, tab, scales, melody) No. 18 of Top 25 Jazz Standards for Guitar|Body and Soul]]'s bridge** — always voiced explicitly, even without a bassist, because the bass line itself is melodic.
- **A diminished chord's practical identity can be overridden by the melody note sitting on top of it.** A C♯dim7 with the melody note A functions, in practice, as an A7♭9 rather than a "pure" C♯dim7 (adding A to a C♯dim7 makes it read as a rootless A7♭9) — cited as one of the concrete payoffs of chord-melody study (playing chord and melody together reveals functional readings that chord-symbol reading alone misses, echoing [[THE GIRL FROM IPANEMA Guitar Lesson (chords, tab, scales) No. 19 of Top 25 Jazz Standards for Guitar|Girl from Ipanema]]'s internalization tip).
- **Voicing superimposition: any inversion of a half-diminished shape built on a dominant chord's ♭7 also voices that dominant's ♭9/♭13.** F half-diminished (F–A♭–C♭–E♭) reduces exactly to ♭7-♭9-♭13-3 of G7 — every note in the half-diminished shape is a legitimate G7♭9♭13 tone. Practical payoff: fretboard work invested in half-diminished shapes and their inversions doubles as a rootless voicing vocabulary for the dominant chord built a major 3rd below (any of several F half-diminished inversions can be played directly over a G7♭9♭13 without change). Same "one physical shape serves two chord identities" theme documented from the other two videos in this batch. → [[Chord Voicings]] § *From videos*, [[Diminished Chords]] § *From videos*
- **Comping tip: a reliable 3/4 jazz-waltz backdrop rhythm.** Bass note alone on beat 1, chord stabs on the "and" of beat 2 and on beat 3 ("1, and-of-2, 3") — a fallback solid enough on its own ("if you only do this the whole time, it's still going to sound great") to build from before adding variation. One named variant: play the bass note and chord together on beat 1, then the chord alone twice more. Framed around comping's actual job — accompaniment, not soloing — "something solid, something cohesive, something rhythmic," not the flashiest thing you can play. → [[Jazz Comping]] (new technique note)
- **Scales: two valid options for a dominant 7♯5, chosen by ear rather than a fixed rule.** The whole-tone scale (contains all the chord's tones, sounds more open/ungrounded, no ♭9/♯9) versus the altered scale (7th mode of melodic minor, "super Locrian" — same chord tones plus a ♭9 and ♯9 the whole-tone scale lacks) — both fully valid, chosen here by ear/listening/transcription study rather than by rule. → [[Altered Chords]] § *From videos*
- **Chord-tone soloing drill: arpeggiate a chord's tones starting from EVERY note in a given fretboard position, not just from the root.** The stated reason: real improvisation lands on notes mid-shape, not always the root, so root-anchored-only arpeggio practice leaves gaps where the chord is "known" abstractly but gets lost live; drilling every starting point closes that gap. → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]] § *From videos*
- **Internalization tip: melodic sequences/interval patterns (3rds, 6ths, 4ths, 7ths) through the tune's key center, independent of the moving chords underneath.** Claimed mechanism: intervallic momentum perceptually "overpowers" harmonic motion, so the ear follows the pattern rather than registering a passing chord as unaddressed — offered as part of *why* the simplified-scales approach (deliberately ignoring some passing harmony) still sounds convincing rather than sloppy.
- **Listening recommendation:** Emily Remler, "Cookin' at the Queens" (plus a nod to the Miles Davis original).
