# Analog vs Digital Workflow Philosophy

Not a signal-chain technique — a set of working-philosophy positions on the analog/digital question, drawn from a conversation between Anthony Marinelli and Mark Barton (a working analog synth designer — co-designer of the Synthesis Technology Zeroscillator, the first commercially successful through-zero-FM Eurorack module, and formerly a speech-synthesis researcher at Apple — now a third-party developer for Cherry Audio). Barton's central technical claim and Marinelli's pushback on what that claim does and doesn't settle are both worth keeping, since they land on a genuinely useful working standard rather than a "team analog / team digital" argument.

## The technical claim

Modern digital synthesis, run at 64-bit floating point (53 bits of accuracy), exceeds analog electronics' signal-to-noise ceiling (roughly 100 dB on a very good analog circuit) by hundreds of dB. The common "digital is stepped, analog is continuous" objection misses that a DAC's reconstruction filter mathematically reconnects the digital steps into a genuinely continuous waveform on the way out — so the "smoothness" argument for analog doesn't actually hold once the full signal chain (including reconstruction) is considered. Analog's continuity is real but noisy in transition; digital's reconstructed continuity is smoother.

## The blind-test standard

Cherry Audio's actual development bar for a plugin emulation: A/B it blind (someone else switches between the original and the emulation without saying which is which, on single notes or short phrases) — if a listener genuinely can't tell them apart, ship it. Real anecdote proving how strong the sighted-bias effect is: users who believe they prefer the sound of the original vintage Synclavier over the "Regen" (the modern hardware reissue) turn out to be picking at chance (50/50) in a genuine blind test — knowing in advance which one is playing is itself the deciding factor, not the sound.

## What the technical argument does NOT settle

- **A hard limit on available voices/tracks forced an "arrange as you build" workflow in the pre-DAW era — and it produced records without ever doing a mute-mix pass.** On scoring 1983's *War Games*, a Synclavier's limited polyphony (32 voices for a long stretch) meant the arrangement had to be more or less decided WHILE building/sequencing the sounds — sounds were bounced to multitrack tape in stages (bass first, then the next figure, etc.), with EQ/effects and even reverb printed at bounce time rather than deferred, because there was only one hardware reverb unit in the room. Contrasted directly with a modern DAW's ability to defer every decision to a later mute-mix pass: "we weren't really arranging after the fact like today... we kind of knew the arrangement and developed it inside here." The claim isn't that infinite recall is bad — it's that having *some* real constraint pushes toward committing to an arrangement early, which is a genuine creative discipline worth deliberately reintroducing even when the tool no longer forces it. → [[Committing and Printing]] — Anthony Marinelli, [[War Games - The Sounds I Made For The Film]]
- **The listener doesn't care whether a knob is real or a mouse-driven virtual one — but the performer's experience still matters, and current interfaces (digital or analog) haven't caught up to a truly expressive instrument.** The comparison drawn: a fine violin or a well-built piano lets a skilled player not have to "fight" the instrument to get expression out of it; no synthesizer interface, analog or digital, is there yet — MPE and polyphonic aftertouch are real steps, but the field is still fundamentally keyboard-shaped. A performer fighting an instrument's limitations (cited example: a real vintage Moog Modular's genuine intonation drift) reads through to the listener even when the listener can't articulate why.
- **A synthesizer being DEFINED BY ITS LIMITATIONS is a real creative virtue, not a deficiency to engineer away.** Counter-framed against "digital lets you have unlimited filters/modules/anything you can imagine": more available options isn't automatically better music, any more than an orchestrator needing 8 French horns when 4 (or fewer) would say the same thing — "give me one good filter and one good knob, I don't need five... I can only think about one at a time." A tool with hard boundaries forces a decision instead of deferring one.
- **Deliberately tapping an analog system's natural unpredictability is a real, ongoing creative source — not nostalgia.** Genuine analog quirks (drift, a mixer that grinds and overdrives just from being turned up too far, subtle differences between two "identical" knob positions) produce accidents a player can notice and lean into; digital, by contrast, has to have unpredictability deliberately programmed in rather than it emerging from imperfect components. Both are legitimate creative resources, but they're not interchangeable — a digital emulation that perfectly nails the *sound* doesn't automatically reproduce this.
- **Excess choice is a real creative cost, independent of the analog/digital question.** With effectively unlimited plugin/preset/module options now available, composers/producers still have to actively narrow their own choices to finish a piece of music — "you get liberated by narrowing the field down." More available tools doesn't remove that discipline; if anything it makes it more necessary.

## Even "digital" instruments are analog at both ends

A "digital synthesizer" is never digital start to finish — a Haken engineer, asked about the Continuum specifically (technically a digital instrument), pointed out that the hall-effect sensor data reading a player's finger position on the ribbon is fundamentally an ANALOG signal, which has to be very carefully converted to digital without degrading it, processed digitally, and then just as carefully converted back to analog for audio output. The genuinely hard engineering work in a "digital" instrument often lives at exactly those two conversion boundaries, not in the digital processing itself — worth remembering before treating "digital" and "analog" as if they were two cleanly separated categories rather than a signal passing through both domains on its way from a player's hand to a speaker.

**An informal survey at Superbooth '24** (Anthony asking random attendees "what do you think of when I say digital / analog") mostly surfaced personal free-association rather than technical content — digital: computers, fingers, glitch, resolution, perfection, clipping-at-0dB, single-processing; analog: organic, warm, history, physicality, Moog, vinyl, windage, "terribly detuned in certain temperatures," round, raw. One consistent, more substantive theme across several actual synth/audio-hardware designers interviewed: several described analog circuit design specifically as genuinely difficult to do well, and described their own work as being about carefully managing where and how much analog is used in an otherwise digital signal path, rather than treating "more analog" or "more digital" as an automatic goal in either direction.

## A useful thought experiment

If every circuit board inside a beloved piece of hardware (the example used: an ARP 2600) were secretly replaced with a digital emulation behind the identical physical panel — same knobs, same layout, A-to-D converters on every pot — and you found out two weeks later, would it change how you felt about the instrument? If the honest answer is "not really, especially if it also gave me better reliability, repeatability, recall, and noise floor," that's a genuine signal the sound itself was never actually the sticking point — something else (familiarity, ritual, the physical act of turning a real knob) was doing the emotional work, and it's worth being honest with yourself about which one it actually is when deciding what to keep, sell, or reach for on a given session.
