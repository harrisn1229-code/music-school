# How using the TELEPORT can IMPROVE your PRACTICE routine!

## Synopsis
A hybrid gear-review-plus-practice-routine video. Jack Ruch demos the **Teleport**, a new looper pedal from his friend Michael Palmazano that records tracks to jam along with and shares them via a companion app, then uses it as a springboard to walk through how he actually uses a looper in his own daily practice — not a deep feature-by-feature tutorial (he defers that to Michael's own channel), but a highlight of what he likes about this looper specifically and, more valuably, a full worked demonstration of his loop-based woodshedding routine. He picks an eighth-note-rock drum groove at 86 BPM, records a short part with the built-in drum machine and one-measure count-in for reference, then switches to a slower 128-ballad-feel groove and works through a longer, more harmonically involved progression — a Ray Charles-style gospel changes sequence — breaking it into small chunks (starting with E♭maj→G7→Cm, then a secondary ii–V into the ♭IV, B♭m7→E♭9→A♭7), looping each chunk, working out arpeggio-based lines against it out of time first and then in time, and gradually tying the chunks together into a full solo over the whole progression. The most valuable material isn't the pedal review itself but the transferable practice methodology: loop small, harmonically tricky chunks with a drum groove under them so phrasing gets tested against a real rhythmic/harmonic bed instead of played free, then expand outward once each piece is comfortable. He closes by naming what he likes best about the Teleport specifically (dedicated, no-latency record button; built-in drum machine; count-in) while noting he personally now mostly practices against DAW backing tracks day to day rather than a pedal looper.

## What's taught
- **The Teleport looper pedal** — records multiple tracks (four shown, all "empty" until recorded) to jam along with, has a **built-in drum machine** with selectable grooves (eighth-note rock at 86 BPM, a 128-ballad feel used later) and a drums on/off toggle, and connects to a companion app for sharing recorded loops/performances with other players. → [[Woodshedding Changes with a Looper]]
- **Setup is fast and beginner-friendly** — Ruch, self-described as "not particularly tech-savvy," was able to get it running quickly; the interface is laid out clearly enough that no deep tutorial is needed to start looping.
- **A one-measure count-in before recording** locks the first take in rhythmically — described as "super helpful" specifically for practice use, since it removes the guesswork of where beat 1 lands. → [[Woodshedding Changes with a Looper]]
- **Dedicated, single-function record button with virtually no latency** — the standout feature versus other loopers he's used, where record often shares a footswitch with secondary functions, introducing lag that makes syncing multiple layered rhythm parts (or live looping) a challenge. → [[Line 6 DL4]], [[Woodshedding Changes with a Looper]]
- **Core practice philosophy: practice within the context of a song or chord progression, not in isolation** — ties to his and Brett Papa's new course on "using songs as a vehicle for practice" (playing over changes, triads, arpeggios, scales, chord substitutions, modes of melodic minor). → [[Song Practice]]
- **Loop small chord chunks, not the whole progression, when the progression is long or harmonically tricky.** Demonstrated on a Ray Charles-style gospel progression: the opening three chords (**E♭maj → G7 → Cm**) are looped on their own first because the chord tones there "need specific addressing" — not a simple diatonic run, so it's worth isolating before moving on. → [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]] § *Chunk-then-connect*, [[Woodshedding Changes with a Looper]]
- **Two-stage working method per chunk: stop the loop and plan chord-tone targets/arpeggio shapes in silence first, then start the loop and test the idea against the groove.** He explicitly frames free (unlooped) playing as risky — "you can come up with nice lines, but you don't really know if they're going to necessarily work and fit into the pocket of the groove" — the loop is what actually validates a line's timing and fit. → [[Woodshedding Changes with a Looper]]
- **A secondary ii–V used to resolve to the ♭IV rather than the I**, looped as its own chunk: **B♭m7 → E♭7/E♭9 → A♭7** ("a 251 going to the four chord"). Worked the same way — stop, plan arpeggio shapes and chord tones per chord, start the loop, test/refine phrasing, accept early "flubs," iterate. → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]
- **Chunks are progressively combined** — after each 2–3-chord unit is comfortable on its own, adjacent chunks get tied together into longer phrases, and eventually the entire progression is looped as one long backing track to play a full solo over from start to finish (including a ♯IV chord and a turnaround later in the form, mentioned but not chord-named in the transcript).
- **Match the drum groove/tempo to the target song's actual feel** before looping a chunk — switched from an 86 BPM eighth-note rock groove (first demo) to a slowed-down 128-ballad-feel groove for the gospel progression, rather than practicing everything against one generic click.
- **Historical practice note:** Ruch describes years of practicing this exact way with just a small looper and his amp, cycling chord progressions repeatedly to work out ways of navigating changes — framed as especially valuable for longer, more twist-filled progressions where the backing track keeps you "accountable rhythmically and harmonically." He's since largely moved this workflow into his DAW, using backing tracks he produces for his Patreon lessons, but affirms the value of having *some* way to record yourself looping changes, pedal or otherwise.
- **Endorsement framing, not a paid feature walkthrough** — Ruch is friends with the Teleport's creator (Michael Palmazano) and says so plainly; he frames this as returning a favor for someone who's been helpful to his channel, and directs viewers to Michael's own channel for an in-depth feature tutorial rather than attempting one himself here.
