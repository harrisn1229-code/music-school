# From Ear Training Apps to Real Music

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QejyY6SBICU)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QejyY6SBICU)

## Synopsis

The missing half of every ear-training method: **why app skill does not transfer to real music, and what to do about it.** Max is blunt about his own product — *"being good at an ear training app is not really worth anything… you can get all the gold gems and everybody thinks you're the man. But if you can't do it in real music, does it really matter?"* His answer is that the premise of a *path* from app to real music is itself the bug: real music belongs in the routine **from day one**, running alongside the app, not waiting at the end of it. Split cleanly into a melody half and a chords half, and the two halves end on **opposite** instructions — which is the most useful thing in the video.

## What's taught

### Melody

- **Contextual over intervallic.** Apps teach melody one of two ways: absolute distances between notes (*"it went up a fifth, then down a second"*) or scale degrees against a key centre. He is not neutral — *"real music is contextual and your training should be contextual too. Melodic intervals are a support to that. They're not the main event."*
- **Three reasons app skill fails in the wild.** (1) **Changing harmony** — chords move underneath a held melody note and change every relationship it has; he flags this as worth a video of its own. (2) **Arbitrary instrumentation** — an app gives you a piano or one drone timbre; real music gives you a voice inside a full arrangement. (3) **A mindset gap** — in the app you are *training*; with music you are just enjoying it, and those are different mental postures that have to be deliberately bridged.
- **Get the pure feeling states perfect in the app first.** The app is *"training wheels"* — a simplified ground where a degree can be learned unobscured, so that in real music you know what you are even listening for.
- **Exercise 1 — listen with the answers already in hand.** Transcribe (or look up) a melody, know the key, then listen to the actual record **with the degrees consciously in mind**: *"you know the melody goes 3 4 3 2 3 1… you keep that in mind and you repeat, you pause, you sing, you listen again with the knowledge in mind."* He calls what this feeds the brain an **"information soup"** — sound, label, and feeling arriving together until the brain synthesises the link itself.
- **Exercise 2 — produce melodies while naming their degrees.** Improvise or write over a drone, backing track or band, staying conscious that you are playing *1 5 6 5 1 7 1 5 ♯4 5 2 3 2 1*. **Degrees of the key, not of the chord.** Aimed straight at a habit guitarists have: running scale shapes with no idea what any note *is*.
- **Exercise 3 — one degree, held under everything.** Pick a single scale degree, and over music whose key you know, sing or play **that degree continuously** while the harmony moves under it. His analogy is the whole point: you know an object well, then see it *"in broad daylight… in shadow… under a strobe light… under blue light"* — confusing at first, then you learn to see through the lighting to the object. The "twoness" of 2 survives every chord it sits over.
- **Three closing tips.** Push the rest of the music to the background and keep the melody in front — *"parting the waters."* Aim for **immediate recognition, not calculation** — no melodic resolutions, interval math or theory derivation unless you genuinely have to. And **ignore the pitch**: be with the *feeling* of each note, since that is what stays constant across octave and key.

### Chords

- **The same three problems, sharpened.** **Arbitrary instrumentation** — the chord is *"distributed across the whole arrangement,"* not played by one piano. **Arbitrary voicings** — C-E-G, E-G-C and G-C-E are all C major but land differently. **Distractions** — drums, bass, melody, second harmony instrument, none of it holding still.
- **Learn qualities and degrees in isolation, then take them out.** Chord qualities as *vertical entities* (major, minor, suspended, minor 9, diminished 7…) and chord degrees (the 4 chord, the 5 chord, ♭2, ♭3) drilled in the app or on the instrument — then fed the same information soup across **a wide range of genres and arrangement styles**, so the mind can work out what the common thread is and what to discard.
- **Exercise — guess before you check.** Transcribing chords starts as soon as you can stand it. For each chord going past, call it **without touching an instrument** — major? minor? something else? sus? diminished? — *then* do whatever it takes to verify: listen for the bass note, fumble at the instrument, use what theory you have. *"Get dirty and make mistakes… you kind of need to fall in every hole."*
- **Exercise — narrate your own playing.** Whenever you play, stay conscious of key, chord number and quality: *"I'm on a one chord, it's major. I'm on a six chord, it's minor."* Even in trivial songs. Varied input plus correct labels, repeatedly.
- **What he actually did:** no method at all — brute-force transcribed *"hundreds and hundreds of songs,"* whole playlists of pop and folk, chorus by chorus, **one to two hours every day.** He is openly unsure whether to recommend it, which is why the exercises above exist.

### The one contrast worth remembering

**Melody and chords ask for opposite listening.** For a melody you *block out* the arrangement and keep one line in the foreground. For chords you do the reverse — a chord's identity **is** *"the sum total of all tonal harmonic interaction between all the instruments,"* so you stay open and let everything **fuse into one quality**. Trying to isolate an instrument while chasing chords is working against the thing you are trying to hear. (The exception: chasing one player's specific voicing, which is a different job.)
