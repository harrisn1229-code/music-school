# The 7 Must Know Licks To Solo Over Any Jazz Standard

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Lb2FMVWjuBs)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Lb2FMVWjuBs)

## Synopsis

Nathan Borton lays out a compact, seven-lick "core vocabulary" for soloing over any jazz standard — each lick keyed to a different harmonic situation rather than to one tune — then, in the video's payoff move, combines all seven into a single worked solo over "Stella By Starlight," picked specifically because that standard "almost has every type of progression in it." The seven: (1) a **major** sound (Charlie Parker) — a decorated major arpeggio, reusable over any diatonic chord in the key; (2) a **minor** sound (Pat Martino) — the mirror-image line, distinguished by a natural 7th rather than the expected ♭7; (3) a **tonic dominant** sound (Grant Green) — the bebop dominant scale landing on the 13th, for a dominant chord that sits still rather than resolving (a blues, or the B section of Rhythm Changes); (4) a standalone **5-to-1 resolution line** used both as a closer for the other licks and on its own; (5) a **major ii-V-I** lick that outlines the ii chord with a motif, then splices into lick 4 to resolve to the 3rd of I; (6) a **minor ii-V-i** lick built from the half-diminished ii chord's own arpeggio plus the Locrian mode, again closing with lick 4's resolution device; and (7a) a **backdoor dominant** lick (♭VII7 → I) built from the melodic minor sound, plus (7b) two named **diminished passing-chord** progressions (iii–♭iii°–ii, IV–♯IV°–I) offered as memorized "small language bits" rather than derived live. Borton is explicit throughout that most of these devices generalize past their literal chord type — major/minor/tonic-dominant language works over any diatonic chord of the matching quality, not just a literal I, ii, or V. The video is a Patreon funnel (the full etude, in PDF and Guitar Pro, is gated there), so the free captions carry the concept-naming and harmonic framing for all seven licks plus the closing solo-assembly method (enclosures, shortening licks, or leaving space to combine them) — exact fretboard positions and full note-for-note transcription of the licks themselves aren't in the free captions. This is the fourth Nathan Borton video now in the vault to use this exact "N licks/sounds you need" teaching format (alongside [[Unlock Jazz Improv with ONE Grant Green Solo?! Honestly, Yes.|Unlock Jazz Improv with ONE Grant Green Solo?! ## What's taught

- **Lick 1 — Major sound (Charlie Parker): a major arpeggio decorated with ornaments/enclosures around each arpeggiated tone.** Generalization stated directly: usable "not just over major chords but any diatonic chord" in the key being played. → [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]], [[Bebop Vocabulary]] § 2 (enclosures)
- **Lick 2 — Minor sound (Pat Martino): the mirror-image minor line, distinguished by the natural 7th rather than the expected ♭7 over a minor chord.** Same reusability claim as lick 1, over any diatonic chord in the minor key being played. Distinct from Borton's separate [[Pat Martino Minor Conversion Method]] video/system — this is a single decorated arpeggio lick, not the fretboard-shape-mapping reduce-everything-to-minor method. → [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]], [[Pat Martino Minor Conversion Method]], [[Seventh Chords]] (the mM7-family natural-7-over-minor sound)
- **Lick 3 — Tonic dominant sound (Grant Green): the bebop dominant scale, landing specifically on the 13th, over a dominant chord that isn't resolving.** Named "tonic dominant" directly — the chord "stays on the 5 [chord] and doesn't want to resolve to the one." Stated use case: a dominant that "lasts for a really long time," e.g. a blues or the B section of [[Rhythm Changes]]. Generalizes over any diatonic chord in the key, "you just want to make sure to resolve it correctly." This is the third vault video to independently land on this exact bebop-dominant/13th-targeting device — see [[Bebop Vocabulary]] § *From videos* for the other two, both also crediting Grant Green. → [[Bebop Vocabulary]], [[Blues Form and Harmony]]
- **Lick 4 — A standalone 5-to-1 resolution line: single notes outlining the V chord's voice leading straight into the I.** Framed as reusable connective tissue rather than a one-off lick: "this type of language can be used at the end of major, minor, and [tonic-dominant] language to resolve the line" — as well as standalone. This is the device licks 5 and 6 both splice into to close. → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Guide Tones]]
- **Lick 5 — Major ii-V-I: a minor-sounding motif outlining the ii chord, then a walk-down straight into lick 4's 5-to-1 line, resolving to the 3rd of the I.** Explicitly framed as one option among several for soloing a 2-5-1 (major/minor/tonic-dominant language all work too) — outlining each chord of the progression individually is offered as a distinct, deliberate choice. → [[The ii-V-I]]
- **Lick 6 — Minor ii-V-i: targets the D minor 7♭5 (half-diminished ii chord) arpeggio, filling the gaps with the Locrian mode, then closes with lick 4's 5-to-1 device again, resolving to the 3rd of the i.** → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Diminished Chords]], [[Modal Jazz]] (Locrian as the ii chord's own mode)
- **Lick 7a — Backdoor dominant: ♭VII7 → I ("the flat seven of any key resolving to the one chord"), an alternate resolution to plain 5-to-1.** Stated directly: "this line uses the sound of melodic minor, which is the sound that best fits the back door" — no hedging between derivation options, melodic minor stated as the default fit. Fourth vault source landing on the melodic-minor/backdoor pairing — see [[Turnarounds]] § *From videos* for the others. → [[Turnarounds]] § *Backdoor*, [[The ii-V-I]] § *Guitar shortcut: the power-chord finder*
- **Lick 7b — Two named diminished passing-chord progressions, offered as memorized "small language bits" rather than derived live: iii → ♭iii° → ii, and IV → ♯IV° → I.** Stated practice advice: "it's always a good idea to have little small language bits to deal with these type of diminished chords." → [[Diminished Chords]]
- **Assembling the solo: three named ways to combine licks into a continuous line, demonstrated by stitching all seven into one solo over "Stella By Starlight."** (1) **Enclosures** — glue two licks together using an enclosure shape rather than a scale walk-up. (2) **Shortening a lick** — play only part of a memorized phrase, trimmed to fit the available space before the next chord change. (3) **Leaving space** — don't connect two licks with a device at all, just rest and re-enter on the next one. Tune choice stated directly: "Stella By Starlight" was picked because it "almost has every type of progression in it," making it a stress-test for the whole seven-lick vocabulary rather than a showcase for one device. This video uses "Stella By Starlight" purely as an etude/practice vehicle, not as taught repertoire (chords, melody, arrangement), so at the time this page was written it wasn't routed to a song reference. **Update 2026-08-11:** a dedicated chord-by-chord Stella lesson has since landed → [[🎵 VICTOR YOUNG - Stella By Starlight]], [[How To MASTER Stella By Starlight...
