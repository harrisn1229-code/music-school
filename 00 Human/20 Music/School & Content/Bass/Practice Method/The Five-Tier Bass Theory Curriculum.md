# The Five-Tier Bass Theory Curriculum

BassBuzz's answer to "how much music theory do I actually need to learn bass?" — a five-level goal ladder that maps *how much and which* theory to learn directly onto *what you actually want to do with the instrument*, rather than a fixed syllabus everyone is supposed to work through in order. **Core thesis, stated as the video's real complaint about most bass/theory teaching:** teachers routinely dump theoretical jargon on beginners without ever asking where the student is trying to get to — the video's own analogy is a gym coach putting a client who "just wants to lose eight pounds and not have back pain" on an Olympic-weightlifting program (300g of protein a day, TRT, max squat). The fix isn't "skip theory" (the video is explicit that's *also* wrong) — it's **matching the theory dose to the goal tier**, and each tier is defined by what it unlocks, not by difficulty for its own sake. **The universal filter, given directly as the closing takeaway and the thing to apply to every item in every tier below:** *"will learning this make me play closer to my goals? If you or your teacher can't answer that question, don't learn that thing."* This is a companion framework to [[Goal-Tiered Practice and the Self-Taught Bass Commandments]] — that note's long/medium/short-term goal ladder is the general self-teaching structure; this note is the theory-specific instance of the same idea, answering "which theory is the short-term goal material" rather than practice scheduling generally. The two are meant to be read together.

## The five levels

### Level 1 — The Bedroom Player
**Goal:** play favorite songs for yourself, low-pressure, no ambition to perform or jam with others. Framed as a fully legitimate, common goal on its own — "very common... and totally legit" — not a lesser stopping point on the way to something else.

- **Minimum theory required: effectively zero.** "You can mostly say f**k theory and learn intuitively" — theory answers *why*, and a bedroom player doesn't need the why to enjoy playing. Explicitly: "even with zero theory you can get all the way from your bedroom to Wembley Stadium."
- **What actually unblocks a bedroom player instead of theory: tab, not sheet music.** Sheet music takes months to read fluently; tab ("Tabanese") is learnable in minutes and tells you exactly which fret to play. The one real gap tab leaves: it shows *what* notes, not *when* — so pair tab with **basic rhythm theory** (see [[Time Signatures & Note Values]]) once a riff's timing won't click by ear alone.
- **Technique outranks theory at this level.** Good basic fretting/plucking technique is named as the thing that actually turns a "home run riff" into a playable one; a bedroom player with strong technique and zero theory "can get pretty far."
- **No home team needed** — level one is explicitly where most working teachers are least useful ("you don't really need us unless your favorite songs are crazy jazz fusion, in which case stick around for level five").

### Level 2 — The Casual Jammer
**Goal:** play music with friends, hold your own showing up to an informal jam without embarrassing yourself.

- **Note names on the neck** — the first real theory requirement. Communicating by fret number alone ("three, five?") breaks down the moment other musicians are involved; knowing note names lets you follow a chord/note called out verbally. → [[Bass Fretboard Note Memorization System]] is the vault's existing drill for exactly this.
- **Basic drum/rhythm-section awareness** — "the first thing I ever learned about bass was that when the bass drum hits a note, you play with it... the rhythm section is the master of the universe." Framed as more urgent than melodic theory once a real drummer is in the room — losing the pocket, not playing a wrong note, is the failure mode named.
- **Common bass line shapes, used without necessarily knowing the underlying theory.** Named explicitly as the "minimum theory way" to stop playing boring root notes: "you can fully fake it as a level two casual jammer by using shapes... watch any pro bassist play, you'll see them using these common bass line shapes, whether they know the theory or not." Scales/triads/major-minor theory *would* help build these shapes from first principles, but the video's point is the shapes are learnable and usable as pattern-recognition alone, no formal theory required — the practical version of this is BassBuzz's own movable scale-shape teaching, see [[Bass Scale Shapes — Major, Minor, Pentatonic & Blues]] and [[Scales]] § *From videos*.

### Level 3 — The Bar Band Bassist
**Goal:** learn a real setlist fast (the video's framing device: a Saturday jam goes well, Sunday the band calls wanting a gig booked "maybe in a week" — 10 songs, one week, an audience).

- **Intervals and ear training** — named as what turns "learn a bar's worth of songs in a week" from panic into a repeatable skill: "you'd learn those same songs with less scrambling and sweating if you learned intervals and ear training." → [[Intervals]], [[Harmonic Ear Training]], [[Scale Degree Ear Training]].
- **Chord progressions — called "the biggest key to understanding the *why* of modern music."** Positioned as the fix for a specific failure mode: memorizing a song by rote (which fret, in what order) without understanding *why* the notes go in that order, which is what makes memory fail under gig pressure. → [[Chord Progressions]].
- **Major/minor chords, arpeggios, diatonic chord progressions, Nashville numbers** — bundled together as "the kind of theory that makes people quit," explicitly flagged as needing to be taught piece-by-piece with real musical examples rather than absorbed as classroom abstraction. → [[Chord Naming by Context]], [[Chords by Key]].
- **The minimum-theory shortcut for this tier: three named scales cover 90%+ of common riffs**, each tied to a concrete listening example — **major pentatonic** (cited: Queen), **minor pentatonic** (cited: Audioslave), and the **blues scale** (cited: "Sunshine of Your Love," already the internalization riff BassBuzz uses for the blues scale elsewhere in the vault — see [[Blues Scales and Blue Notes]] § *From videos*). → [[Scales]] § *From videos*.
- **Explicit, load-bearing caveat — read this before treating level three as mandatory:** "I know many gigging bass players who probably don't know half the theory I mentioned in level three, or at least they don't know that they know it... just like you can speak English without thinking 'ah yes, prepositional phrases.' So once again, the minimum theory you need to play bars is actually still zero." Level three theory's actual job is **speed** — learning the *why* makes memorizing a 10-song setlist in a week faster and steadier under pressure, not gatekeeping whether you can gig at all.

### Level 4 — The Working Pro
**Goal:** take *any* gig, with *any* band, without the specific-repertoire safety net of "the same songs every time" — the threshold where theory stops being optional because the material itself keeps changing out from under you.

- **Major and minor scales (not just pentatonics)** — named as the foundation the pentatonics sit on top of, needed once you're generalizing across arbitrary songs rather than working from a fixed vocabulary of shapes. → [[Scales]].
- **Reading music — called "the most hotly contested topic in all of music theory."** The stated payoff is narrow and concrete, not general musicianship: reading opens specific gig categories that are otherwise closed (musicals, cruise ships, session work) rather than making you a better player in the abstract. **Named counter-caveat:** famous musicians who can't read "usually got famous playing with one band" — reading's value is specifically about *gig flexibility across many different projects*, which is exactly the level-four definition above. **Quoted authority, with the specific reading skill named:** session legend **Lee Sklar**, in a *Music Radar* interview — *"You have to be able to read on many levels, Nashville number charts, chord charts, notated music and so on."* → [[Reading Bass Clef]] § *From videos*, [[Sight Reading]].
- **The circle of fifths — deliberately downgraded from its usual billing.** Explicitly contrasted against "theory book authors" who call it "the Rosetta Stone of music theory": *"for the modern musician, the circle of fifths is like, kind of useful for memorizing key signatures, but it's not the frigging Rosetta Stone. It's just a map of how different keys are related."* Direct prioritization advice for this tier, credentialed by the speaker's own background (undergrad theory + 15 years teaching): **"if you want to be a working pro, skip the circle and work on your groove"** — i.e. at this tier, marginal practice time is better spent on rhythm-section feel than on a reference tool. → [[Circle of Fifths]] § *From videos*.

### Level 5 — The Extreme Jazz-Fusion Shredder
**Goal:** play anything, with anyone, at any shred level — named as only worth pursuing if you specifically "love going ooh at abstract theory," crave "the completionist dopamine rush of knowing every theory fact ever," and want to walk in the footsteps of **Jaco Pastorius** and **Stanley Clarke**. Framed as the level most teachers push on students who never asked for it — the video's explicit reason for including this tier's full list even for a casual jammer is so the viewer can *recognize* over-scoped teaching and opt out of it.

The list, given rapid-fire ("60 seconds on the clock"):
- **All seven major modes** (Ionian through Locrian) — see [[Diatonic Modes]].
- **Odd time signatures** (5, 7, 9) — named use case: playing along with Snarky Puppy and Rush. See [[Time Signatures & Note Values]], [[Syncopation and Metric Displacement]].
- **The whole-tone scale** — named use case: sounding like Thelonious Monk.
- **Harmonic minor and its modes; melodic minor and its modes** — see [[Scale Modes Reference]].
- **Upper chord extensions** — 9ths, 11ths, 13ths and their alterations, plus sus/diminished/augmented chords — see [[Extended Chords]], [[Suspended Chords]], [[Diminished Chords]], [[Augmented Chords]].
- **Common polyrhythms** — named, only half-jokingly, as necessary "or Dennis Chambers will eat you for breakfast."
- **Harmonics** — named use case: sounding like Jaco Pastorius.
- **Non-diatonic chord changes** — tritone substitutions, secondary dominants, borrowed chords ("modal interchange"). See [[Chord Substitution]], [[Secondary Dominants]], [[Borrowed Chords]].

**Not detailed here on purpose** — this level is presented as a checklist of *topics to go find dedicated instruction on*, not something the video itself teaches; the vault's existing theory library above already covers every item at real depth. The point of listing it in full is tier-recognition (see below), not curriculum content.

## A sixth data point: the ear-driven exception (Billy Sheehan)

A "rules of bass" breakdown states the standard version of this note's core thesis directly, then names its own counter-example: teachers want you to learn theory so you understand *why* things sound good — but Billy Sheehan is offered as a pro who "seems to do just fine without it." **Sheehan quoted directly:** "I have patterns and notes and melodies and moves that I do — I don't know what scale they are, I don't know what notes they are, I flunked rudiments of music in high school." **The verdict lands exactly on this note's own universal filter, applied to the theory-or-not question itself rather than to a specific topic within theory:** yes, you can skip theory if you have an amazing ear (Sheehan's case) — but without one, theory is the more reliable path to *build* a better ear, on top of the practical payoffs of being able to talk about music, understand what's happening in a song, and analyze other players' parts. Sheehan's case maps closest to **Level 1 — The Bedroom Player's** "minimum theory required: effectively zero" territory above, but by a different route than that tier describes: not a deliberately low-theory *goal*, but an exceptional, unusually well-developed *ear* substituting for formal theory entirely — the video's closing point is that this substitution is real but rare, not a general permission slip to skip theory. → BassBuzz, [[The 10 Rules of Bass Playing (That Pros Break)]]

## The load-bearing move: tier-recognition, not tier-completion

The video's real payoff, stated directly, isn't "here's level five" — it's teaching the viewer to **recognize which tier a given piece of theory actually belongs to**, so they can tell when a teacher (or a book, or a YouTube algorithm) is teaching them level-four or level-five material for a level-one or level-two goal. Applied test for any theory topic, from any source: *would learning this make me play closer to my actual goal?* If the answer is no, or if nobody can answer the question, it's the wrong material for right now — not wrong material, period. This is the same underlying diagnostic as [[Goal-Tiered Practice and the Self-Taught Bass Commandments]]'s Commandment 1 (set a goal before judging any exercise/topic as useful), applied specifically to theory topic selection rather than practice material generally.
