# 4 levels of the Circle of Fifths

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=K0_nf1YGXQI)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=K0_nf1YGXQI)

## Synopsis

David Bennett walks through four progressively more advanced applications of the circle of fifths, each building on the idea that distance around the circle = harmonic distance: reading key signatures, judging how smooth or bold a modulation will feel, judging how smooth or jarring a chord progression's root motion will feel, and ordering the modes by brightness.

## Techniques

- **Key signatures** — count the sharps/flats in a key signature, step that many places around the circle from C (clockwise for sharps, counterclockwise for flats) to find the major key. The circle also gives the specific sharps/flats in the order they're added to the staff (e.g. A major's three sharps are F♯, C♯, G♯, in that order).
- **Modulation smoothness** — keys adjacent on the circle share all but one note, so modulating between them sounds subtle: "I Want to Hold Your Hand" (The Beatles) moves G major → C major, differing only in F♯ vs. F natural. Keys far apart on the circle share few notes, so modulating between them sounds bold and disconnected: the bridge of "Summer of '69" (Bryan Adams) moves D major → F major — deliberately used there to inject energy and grab attention by standing out from the rest of the song.
- **Chord-progression smoothness via circle-of-fifths root motion** — a progression whose roots step around the circle (a 5th at a time) sounds cohesive even when the chords don't all belong to one key: "Hey Joe" strings together major chords each a 5th apart. A progression whose roots JUMP across the circle (a tritone — the maximum possible distance) sounds jarring/acrobatic: Radiohead's "Subterranean Homesick Alien" intro moves Dm → A♭ → Dm, deliberately unnatural-sounding to underscore the song's alien-abduction theme (not a "bad" chord change — an intentional effect).
- **Mode brightness ordering** — each step clockwise around the circle adds a sharp and produces the next-brighter mode; each step counterclockwise adds a flat and produces the next-darker mode. C major → add F♯ (one step clockwise, to G major's signature) → C Lydian, one degree brighter. C major → add B♭ (one step counterclockwise, to F major's signature) → C Mixolydian, one degree darker. C major → add the four flats of A♭ major (B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭, four steps counterclockwise) → C Phrygian, four degrees darker.
- **Why the fifth is the organizing interval at all**: beyond the octave, the fifth is the simplest, most consonant interval (a 3:2 frequency ratio) — the reason harmony built from stacked fifths reads as maximally cohesive to the ear.
