# Practice Mindset — Hard Truths and Realistic Expectations

Eight practice-relevant observations from Brandon D'Eon's "25 Hard Truths About Guitar," extracted from a much longer, mostly-comedic list of mindset/industry commentary. Grouped here as realistic-expectation-setting rather than technique instruction.

- **Your rhythm playing is probably worse than you think.** "Rhythm" here means literally playing in time with a metronome, not comping chords. Diagnostic: play a simple strum pattern against a metronome — many experienced players fail this cleanly regardless of years played.
- **Most practice isn't practice — it's just playing.** Intentional, goal-directed work on a specific weakness is a different activity from noodling on material you already know, not a lesser version of the same thing.
- **You aren't as good as you sound to yourself.** Self-perceived skill runs reliably inflated (the "most drivers rate themselves above average" bias, applied to playing). Fix: record yourself playing your best material and listen back. See [[Recording Yourself for Self-Diagnosis]] for the fuller method.
- **Performance conditions cost real, measurable skill.** Playing for someone else in real time can cost roughly half your practiced skill level (one reported student estimate: closer to 80%) — a function of the changed environment/judgment context, not a loss of underlying ability. Practice performance conditions deliberately (playing for others, recording live) rather than assuming solo fluency transfers automatically.
- **Fixing an ingrained bad habit feels like temporary regression — expect it, don't read it as failure.** A real technique correction (e.g. changing pick grip) typically dips performance for weeks to months before recovering past the prior level.
- **"Finding your own voice" too early is usually avoidance of studying established players.** The actual sequence: study the language first (transcription, vocabulary from players you admire), and an original voice follows from having material to draw on — not from deliberately avoiding influence.
- **"I just play by ear" as an exclusive claim is often theory-avoidance dressed as a virtue.** Playing by ear is a legitimate skill on its own; treating it as the *only* legitimate approach is usually a coping frame for skipped or abandoned theory study, not a real methodological stance.
- **Splitting practice across many instruments at once dilutes skill on all of them.** Build real depth on one instrument before adding the next, rather than developing several simultaneously.

- **Metronome-tool specifics, a habit-formation tracking tip, cultivating personal strengths over conformity, and find-a-mentor — four new points from a companion "6 practice tricks" video (daily-routine/early-morning content from the same video duplicates the bullet below and isn't re-logged).** Metronome practice is framed as the most neglected fundamental — it's how you find technique weak spots at specific tempos (his own example: discovering alternate-picking breakdowns at tempos he wasn't drilling) and track speed progress objectively; tool pick was metronomeonline.com for a tolerable long-session click tone, no forced accent (works for any note grouping), and a built-in practice-time tracker that persists across tempo changes. **Habit-formation tip:** write down the four topics worked each day for the first few weeks to lock in a new daily routine. **Cultivate personal strengths rather than conforming**: a conservatory anecdote — years spent trying to "fit in" playing standard jazz repertoire produced mediocre results; the turning point was choosing fast, technical fusion pieces for exams instead, leaning into an actual strength rather than matching the room's expectations. **Find a mentor**: a real teacher (in person where possible) both corrects mistakes and supplies motivation a solo learner can't generate alone. — BERNTH, [[6 Awesome Practice Tricks - Boost Your Guitar Progress TODAY!]]
- **Five non-exercise habits credited for overall progress, not a technique list.** (1) **Practice-first scheduling**: make practice the very first daily task (after breakfast) — most energy/focus is available early, and a session after 8-10 hours of work is measurably worse; structure it as a 1-hour routine split into four 15-minute blocks with rotating content. Separately: the bigger unlock was **splitting free playing from goal-directed exercise practice** — mixing the two (his own younger-self habit) blunted the progress curve versus doing exercise practice as its own dedicated block. (2) **Record and share videos, made deliberately challenging** (not just demonstrating what's already solid) — camera pressure raises the bar over private practice, and a video archive lets you compare against footage from a year or two ago on a low-motivation day. (3) **Take gigs/jobs outside your comfort zone** (cited: acoustic sideman work, slide/bottleneck studio work despite feeling like a non-expert) — an unfamiliar, exposed setting (e.g. an in-ear-monitored acoustic gig on stage) surfaces small technical gaps a comfortable setting hides. (4) **Cut alcohol** — framed as a productivity cost (lost practice/recording days after touring/shows), not a moral stance. (5) **Teach/answer student questions** — working through other players' problems surfaces the practitioner's own blind spots and the highest-value, most-common fixes across many students, feeding back into personal practice. — BERNTH, [[5 Habits That RADICALLY Improved My Guitar Playing!]]
- **A decade-long cautionary data point: technique-only practice with no parallel theory study has a real ceiling that speed and distortion can only hide, not remove.** Years spent learning isolated licks/patterns and moving them chromatically up and down the neck without understanding the underlying scale or key — a lick would land right by luck when it happened to match the song's key, and fail unpredictably the moment it was transposed or the harmonic context changed. Fast, heavily-distorted playing disguised the gap for a while, but it collapsed immediately against a real request (e.g. "solo over this in F♯ minor"). Named as the single biggest regret of a 10-year retrospective — bigger than either of two technique issues covered in the same video. — BERNTH, [[10+ Year Guitar Progress - 10,000 Hours Later (DON'T Make These MISTAKES...)]]
- **Perceived speed depends more on clean execution and timing lock than raw BPM.** Demoed with a composition that sounds far faster than its actual tempo (160 BPM 16th notes) suggests: every note connects fluidly and stays audible (clean execution), and the take was recorded against a drum track specifically to nail the rhythm without dragging or rushing (timing lock) — see [[Recording Yourself for Self-Diagnosis]] for the DAW-grid checking method used to verify it. A well-executed lick at a lower tempo reliably reads as faster/more impressive to a listener than a rushed, sloppy lick at a genuinely higher one — prioritize these two factors before chasing a higher BPM number. → [[How To Sound Faster WITHOUT Playing Faster! - Guitar Lesson]] — BERNTH, [How To Sound Faster WITHOUT Playing Faster! | Guitar Lesson](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8iIUGDLIQfI)
- **Isolate-the-failure-moment methodology for building your own exercises.** When a specific lick or transition is especially hard, isolate exactly that moment and turn it into its own targeted drill rather than only drilling the whole passage — BERNTH's own worked example: a "flying pinky"/finger-independence problem traced to precisely where fingers failed to stay close to the fretboard during a string change became its own isolated exercise. A generalizable exercise-design principle: analyze what's actually happening at the exact point of failure, then build a drill from that isolated moment. — BERNTH, [[The 7 Guitar Exercises That Will Change Your Life]]

Direct claim: given real, sustained work, anybody can get good at guitar. Paired advice: get a teacher when stuck and frustrated rather than concluding the frustration means a lack of talent — being stuck alone is normal, not a sign to quit. - **Five self-diagnosed beginner regrets from a companion BERNTH video — new content only; daily-routine/practice-plan overlap with other bullets on this page is skipped.** (1) **Theory-avoidance has a real cost, told as a failure story**: years of "I just play by ear" as a stated creative-freedom stance collapsed at a conservatory jazz-guitar entrance exam — asked to improvise over a plain blues in F, with zero theory grounding it "sounded absolutely terrible" despite years of advanced metal technique; learning basic theory (intervals, cadences, chord construction) afterward didn't constrain his playing, it enabled it. (2) **Fix clean/efficient technique fundamentals early, not just "technique" as in named tricks** (tapping, sweeps): specifically flags wrist angle, whether picking motion comes from the wrist vs. the whole arm, and thumb position (behind the neck vs. sticking out) — his own uncorrected picking angle and "flying pinky" became a ceiling on advanced material years later, requiring a painful technique rebuild. Explicitly acknowledges the counter-argument (some great players' quirky technique IS their sound) but recommends clean fundamentals as the safer default. (3) **Set a specific goal, ideally with a "no-pressure deadline"** — a student who set a concrete target (learn a specific song by a target date) progressed faster than the author's own goal-less beginner years, even after missing the deadline; goals also help filter which technique/theory topics are actually worth drilling for the music you want to play. (4) **Find a mentor sooner, specifically framed as a genuine regret**: years of self-teaching advanced shred technique alone (no local teacher covered sweep picking/tapping) before finally getting structured conservatory feedback — named directly as "probably one of the absolute biggest mistakes" costing multiple years of progress. — BERNTH, [[5 WORST BEGINNER GUITAR MISTAKES - Tips For Faster Progress!]]
- **The guitar progress curve naturally flattens after the beginner phase — that plateau is expected, not a sign of doing something wrong, and the fix at that stage is habit consistency, not a smarter exercise.** A second, independent source for this page's "most practice isn't practice — it's just playing" point: practicing time excludes gigs, studio sessions, and rehearsals even though they're hours on the instrument — practicing means focused, deliberate work on a specific exercise or concept. Common failure mode named: starting with an unsustainable 3-4 hour daily plan, which erodes in frequency over weeks until abandoned; fix is a routine sized to actually repeat daily (BERNTH's own: 1 hour in four 15-minute rotating blocks, or 30 min/two blocks to start smaller). — BERNTH, [Why You Make NO Guitar Progress - Change That Today!](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hUtuZgCZ0w8)

Explicit warning against front-loading the fun material "to convince yourself to start" — ending happy is argued to matter more for long-run consistency than starting easy. — BERNTH, [The 4 Biggest Guitar Practice MISTAKES! - **A larger, mostly-new set of 19 mindset/practice tips from a dedicated "tips I'd give my younger self" video — one point ("most practice isn't practice") duplicates this page's existing bullet, the rest are new.** Stay physically relaxed while practicing (zero tension anywhere except fingers and a small part of the wrist — forearm, shoulders, neck, and stomach specifically flagged as common unconscious tension spots) since "the way you practice is the way you perform." Get fluent with a metronome specifically — framed as *the* defining trait separating amateur from pro, since virtually all recorded music is tracked to a click. Prioritize learning the fretboard's note names early, since hunting for notes is what stalls improvisation. Develop a legato ("smooth and connected") default tone rather than an unconsciously staccato one — staccato has its place but shouldn't be the accidental default. Set a specific, pinpointed goal rather than a vague one ("get better" isn't a goal). Consistency beats intensity for rate of improvement. Transcribe/study players you want to sound like directly — a universal skill-acquisition method, not genre-specific — and let originality emerge from synthesizing multiple influences rather than from deliberately avoiding influence. **Accuracy before speed**: nail rhythms correctly at a slow tempo first; speed follows once the rhythm is genuinely internalized (echoes [[10 Hidden Soloing Mistakes and Constraint-Based Fixes]]'s mistake 6 from the other direction — building up to a tempo ceiling rather than starting there). Push past the "80–90% good enough" point most players stop at to get the last 10–20% of real polish on a piece. There's no shortcut around raw practice time for most mechanical problems (an F barre chord has no trick — just reps). Deliberately practice performing in front of others early to build tolerance for stage-fright discomfort before it's high-stakes. Learn entire songs, not just the parts you like, for attention-span and completionist skill reasons. Playing songs alone builds you into an entertainer, not necessarily a musician — genuine musicianship (deep instrument/theory knowledge) is a separate, additional pursuit. Jamming with others exposes real gaps that solo practice hides. Expect skill growth to take longer than intuition predicts early on, but note growth compounds — each subsequent skill acquisition tends to go faster than the last. Theory is less intimidating in practice than its reputation suggests, and knowing the rules is what lets you break them meaningfully. **Track progress with concrete numbers, not songs** — a song is binary (can/can't play it), but something like "which keys can I play major 7 arpeggios in, and at what BPM" is a trackable, specific metric worth logging in a practice notebook. Think critically and attempt genuine problem-solving before asking for help or quitting — most sticking points just need more deliberate practice, not a shortcut. — Brandon D'Eon, [19 Unique Guitar tips I would give my younger self](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6Ykzhpupwao)
