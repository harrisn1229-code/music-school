# Reharmonization - Making Songs Fresh & Personal by Reharmonizing

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lVKoGq3cFRA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lVKoGq3cFRA)

## Synopsis

Jens Larsen steps back from his usual "how far can you go" reharmonization videos to talk instead about how he actually uses reharmonization in real arrangements — subtler, more restrained, not chasing shock value. Rather than presenting reharmonization techniques in the abstract, he works through the **A section of "Body and Soul"** (in D♭, the standard's usual key) bar by bar, offering 2–4 alternative harmonizations at each stage, always checked against the melody, not just against the chords. This melody-first framing is the video's real thesis and the thing that separates it from a typical "here are some cool reharm tricks" video: every substitute is explicitly tested against whether the melody note is still a usable tone of the new chord, and several times Larsen explicitly rejects an otherwise-good-sounding chord because it doesn't fit the tune's actual melody. He also spends real time on *when* to deploy a given reharmonization within a song's form (first time through plain, later passes get the surprise) and closes with a discussion of influences (Bill Evans, Herbie Hancock, Brad Mehldau for harmonic reinterpretation; Jim Hall for meter/key-change moves; John Scofield for groove-based reinterpretation) and a practical bandstand reason to build these arrangements at all: giving a band an arrangement of a tune they already mostly know is easier to sight-read than handing them all-new original material.

## What's taught

### The baseline changes (Body and Soul, A section, key of D♭)

*(Caption confidence flag: several chord names are garbled or ambiguous in the auto-transcript — e.g. "this BL is sounding all fashion style" almost certainly means an "old-fashioned"-style altered dominant, and a few chord-quality words drop out entirely. Captured as the most internally-consistent reading rather than guessed further.)* As stated: **E♭m7 – B♭7 – E♭m7 – A♭7 | D♭maj7 – G♭7 – D♭/F – Edim7 | E♭m7 – E♭m7/D♭ – Cø7 – F7 | B♭m7 – (turnaround back to D♭ or to E♭m for another A)**. Functionally: bars 1–2 are a two-bar ii–V (E♭m7 is the ii, A♭7 the V, with B♭7 as a passing/auxiliary dominant on the way); bars 3–4 use G♭7 as an auxiliary/secondary dominant off the IV; the D♭/F–Edim7 pair is heard as either an F-minor-flavored passing move or a D♭-with-bass-note reading resolving to a **♯4 diminished, functioning as a ii-chord-in-second-inversion with the 7th in the bass**; bars 9–12 are a long cadence to the vi degree (B♭m) via Cø7–F7; the closing bars carry an "old-fashioned"-style altered A♭7 (♭9/♯13-ish reading) before the turnaround. **The whole progression is "not really complicated" but dense — roughly two chords per bar throughout** — which is Larsen's stated reason for the reduce-then-refill strategy below rather than trying to wedge new chords into an already-full bar.

### 1. Reduce first, then refill

**Core workflow move, stated as a general strategy, not specific to this tune:** when a progression already has two chords per bar, don't try to cram substitutions into what's already there — first **reduce the passage down to its harmonic essence** (what is this bar *actually* doing, functionally?), then **refill the reduced skeleton with new chords** of your own choosing. Worked twice in the video: the opening E♭m7–B♭7–E♭m7–A♭7 (four chords) reduces to "just a two-five that's two bars long"; the D♭maj7–G♭7–D♭/F–Edim7 stretch (four chords) reduces to "just tonic" (i.e. all four bars could be played as a single D♭ chord, or D♭maj7 → B♭m or similar, before refilling). → [[Reharmonization]], [[Chord Progressions]] § *Harmonic function*

### 2. Line-cliché substitutes over a static two-bar ii–V

Over the two-bar E♭m7–A♭7 (functionally a slow ii–V), three interchangeable **line-cliché** options, all keeping the E♭m7 start (needed to fit the melody) and adding descending or held inner-voice motion before landing on A♭7 → D♭: (a) **E♭m7 → E♭m(maj7) → E♭m6 → A♭7 → D♭** — a chromatically descending 7th (the classic "my funny valentine"-style minor line cliché, moving ♭7 → maj7 → 6 inside a static E♭m chord); (b) the same shape played as its common alternate voicing (referred to by ear as "the other one," same line-cliché family, e.g. the **"Stairway to Heaven"**-style minor descending line); (c) an **A♭ pedal point** under either version — Larsen notes the whole two-bar unit, or even the *entire* opening passage, can be pedaled on A♭ underneath the moving upper chords. **Explicit criteria for when to reach for these:** they're the safe default any time a static ii–V needs "some movement but you don't want to go far away" — usable even while comping in real time, and audible enough that alert bandmates will hear and react to it. → [[Reharmonization]] § *7. Reharmonize the rhythm* (adjacent device), [[Guide Tones]] (the moving inner voice is a guide-tone-adjacent line), pedal point technique general case not yet a standalone note — candidate, see Routed to.

### 3. Function-change substitute for a deliberate "surprise" — nonfunctional major 7 chords

A further-out option over the same E♭m7–B♭7 opening: keep the first two chords **identical to the plain version** (so the ear has the real progression established), then on the *second* pass through the same two bars, swap the expected motion for **A major 7 → C7 → D♭** — described as **"a sharp-4-diminished type sound resolving"** into the D♭ tonic. **The mechanism stated directly: the substitute changes the chord's *function*, not just its color** — a major 7 chord "rests in itself" (i.e. sounds fully consonant on its own) while still being outside the key, so it reads as *consonant but surprising* rather than merely tense. Larsen names this device — leaning on nonfunctional major-7 chords for reharmonization surprise — as the throughline he hears in Herbie Hancock and Bill Evans reharmonizations generally, not a one-off trick. **Explicit placement rule: don't use this the first time through the form** — establish the plain version first (e.g. coming out of the bridge), then deploy the surprise chord on a later pass, ideally into the third A section. → [[Reharmonization]] § *1. Change the chord's function under a held note*, [[Diminished Chords]] (the ♯4-diminished-type resolving sound), [[Chord Substitution]]

### 4. Free-association outside-key targeting via a shared common tone

A "further away" option over the same opening two bars, described as **almost a wrong-key Giant Steps move**: starting from E♭m7, Larsen looks only at the *upcoming* target notes needed in the melody/voice-leading (here, an **F and an E♭**), then picks an out-of-key chord that contains both — landing on **B7♭5**, which resolves up a half step to **E major 7(♭5/♯11)**, which then moves to A♭. **Stated method, generalizable:** identify the note(s) you need to land on next, find *any* chord (in or out of key) that contains them, and use that as the pivot — "no real logic," deliberately outside-key scale/chord sounds, freely associated rather than derived from a rule. Framed as the furthest-out end of the reharmonization spectrum covered in this video — a "how far can you go" move saved for a single pass, not a default. → [[Reharmonization]], [[Chord Substitution]] § *5. Coltrane changes*

### 5. Old-fashioned altered dominant + bass-note-movement substitution, with a chromatic-ascent mirror of the tritone sub

Over the D♭ → (two bars later) E♭ stretch that's really just an embellished tonic, two bass-driven options: (a) an **"old-fashioned"-sounding altered dominant, D♭ → C7alt(♭9/♭13)** — noted as functionally identical to G♭7 with a different bass note (i.e. **G♭7 respelled/heard as C7 altered**, purely a bass-note-movement reharmonization, not a new chord family), and (b) substituting **F minor7 with B♭7** at a spot where the melody carries the 3rd or the F-minor tone — flagged as a recurring, reusable move ("you'll see there's one other place in the song where you can do that") whenever a iii-type chord's 3rd sits in the melody, since that's exactly the condition that licenses turning it into its **tritone dominant**. Larsen then generalizes a **mirror-image bass-motion principle**: whenever a chromatically **descending** bass line under a major-scale progression works (tonic → chromatic-down → dominant), you can very often also make it work **ascending** instead — because "if you're going from C down to B♭, and B♭ is the dominant resolving to the next chord, then going from C *up* to E works too, because E is the tritone substitution of B♭." Result: **both a chromatically descending and a chromatically ascending bassline are available under the identical melody**, worked concretely as D♭maj7 → D7 in place of the plain descending option. → [[Chord Substitution]] § *2. Tritone substitution*, [[Chromatic Bass Lines]]

### 6. Static two-bar minor chord: parallel-minor "step out," or tritone-sub the resolving dominant

Over a stretch that's essentially two bars of the same E♭m chord (or, alternately, read as a long cadence to the vi degree via a false-degree bass move), Larsen names but explicitly **rejects for this tune** the common solo-context move of stepping into parallel minor chords and back with a dominant, because it doesn't fit this melody. What *does* fit: on the **Cø7 → F7** pair leading to B♭m, tritone-sub the dominant — **F7 → B7** (a straight application of tritone substitution) — or go further and play a full **B major7(♭5)** in that slot instead of a dominant at all, resolving directly without the F7 step. Direct statement of the constraint that rules moves in or out here: "there's not really a lot you can do with it, even though you only have the [7th] in the melody" — i.e. a melody note that's *only* a 7th (not a 3rd) of the target chord narrows the safe substitute list much further than a melody sitting on a 3rd would. → [[Chord Substitution]] § *2. Tritone substitution*, [[Guide Tones]]

### 7. Melody-altering substitute borrowed from a Dave Holland record

At the C7–B♭m spot (normally B♭m7, the vi), a deliberate melody-changing substitute Larsen flags as one of the few times he'll actually alter the tune's melody and keep it anyway: instead of B♭m7, play **A major7(♯11)** — which requires changing the melody note from the tune's actual C–D♭–F to include an E natural. Stated reasoning (tentative, in his own words): the A major7 sound in this context functions as a **♭6 relationship to D♭ minor**, which is "kind of related" to the tune's home key, and he isn't fully sure why it works or where he originally got it, but attributes the source to **a Dave Holland record**. Framed candidly as an exception to the melody-must-fit rule elsewhere in the video — included because it "sounds really nice," not because it passes the usual test. → [[Reharmonization]] § *The one constraint*, [[Borrowed Chords]]

### 8. Turnaround: a direct, out-of-key dominant back to the top

For the A-section turnaround (heading either back to another A or to the bridge), beyond the standard dominant/diminished-at-the-end option and a direct move to the iii degree, Larsen highlights going **directly to B** (a dominant a half step above the target, out of key) as a favorite: "this is nice because it's so definitely not sounding like a one chord, and it's really a dominant — it's not really in the key, so it wants to move on" — deliberately maximizing pull back into the top of the form rather than resolving comfortably. → [[Turnarounds]], [[Chord Substitution]] § *2. Tritone substitution*

### 9. Form awareness governs how much reharmonization to use, and where

Threaded through every technique above, stated as a repeated, deliberate constraint rather than a footnote: **how far you push a reharmonization depends on where it sits in the form** — is this the ending, the last A, or a transition between the first and second A section? Larsen explicitly times his more "out" substitutes (the function-change major-7 surprise, the free-association Giant-Steps-flavored move) to land on a *later* pass through the form, after the listener has heard the plain version once, framing reharmonization-as-development rather than reharmonization-as-confusion. → [[Reharmonization]] § *When not to*

### 10. Comping style should match the reharmonization

A performance-practice note, general rather than tune-specific: heavier reharmonizations (line clichés, sustained pedal points) suit a **more sustained, held-note comping style** rather than a busy, chord-per-beat approach — Larsen states directly that how he plans to *play* the tune (sustained vs. dense) is part of deciding which reharmonization option to reach for, not a separate decision made afterward.

### 11. Influences and adjacent, non-harmonic reinterpretation approaches

Named directly as the sources of this melody-first reharmonization approach: **Bill Evans**, **Herbie Hancock** (specifically his reharmonizations while playing with Miles Davis), and **Brad Mehldau**, all described as coming "mostly out of the piano players." Two adjacent, non-chordal reinterpretation approaches also named: **Jim Hall**, credited with changing a tune's **meter** and modulating **keys within the form** as an alternative to chordal reharmonization (Larsen states he's borrowed the key-change-within-form idea directly, less so Hall's specific harmonic reharmonizations); and **John Scofield**, credited with reinterpreting a tune's **groove/feel** rather than its harmony at all — cited example: playing "Secret Love" as a **bossa nova groove** instead of an up-tempo swing. Framed as three different axes of the same goal (harmony, meter/key, groove) for making a standard "a personal statement." → [[Modulation (Key Change)]] (Jim Hall's within-form key changes), [[Loop-Based Harmony]] (adjacent groove-reinterpretation framing)

### 12. Practical bandstand payoff of building a reharmonized arrangement

A rehearsal-efficiency reason to write reharmonized arrangements of tunes the band already knows, rather than all-original material: when musicians read a chart of a standard they already know **except for the reharmonized spots**, they only have to absorb the *changes* rather than sight-reading an entire new piece cold — described as a genuinely practical reason this approach is worth the arranging effort, separate from the artistic payoff. Mentioned alongside a concrete example: Larsen's own Patreon arrangement of "You Stepped Out of a Dream," where he altered both meter and harmony for a trio concert.
