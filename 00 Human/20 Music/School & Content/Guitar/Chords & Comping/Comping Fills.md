# Comping Fills

Part of [[Jazz Harmony]] — the melodic-fill counterpart to [[Jazz Comping — Rhythmic Motif and Walking Bassline]] (which covers *when* to strike a chord and what the bass is doing) and [[Chord Voicings]] (which covers *which notes* to voice a chord with). This note is about the third comping element: the short melodic phrases played between or on top of the chords, and specifically *when* and *why* to reach for one — sourced from a breakdown of Joe Pass comping behind Ella Fitzgerald.

## The core tip: fills only happen when the soloist rests
Joe Pass never fills over the top of a vocalist or soloist who's still singing/playing — a fill only enters the texture when there's genuine space, i.e. call-and-response spacing rather than decoration layered underneath an active melody. Before reaching for a fill, check: is anyone else actually playing right now? If yes, hold the comp; if the space is genuinely open, that's the cue.

## A fill's job is to connect, not decorate
Quoted directly from Pass himself: a fill has "gotta go... with the melody... to the next bar." A fill that just sits on top of the current chord and doesn't lead anywhere is doing it wrong, by Pass's own framing — the fill's function is to **move the music forward to the next chord or the next section of the form**, the same forward-motion logic that drives a bebop line. *(This reframes a fill as a harmonic/structural device, not a purely melodic flourish — treat "where does this fill lead" as the first design question, before "what notes sound good here.")*

## Short vs. long: let the form decide
Don't default to one fill length. Read how much open space the form actually gives you at that moment:
- **Short fill** — a few notes, e.g. filling the tail end of a bar while the vocal phrase is between lines. Functions almost like a passing tone at the phrase level.
- **Long fill** — used when there's genuinely more room (a longer instrumental gap, a turnaround, a full bar or more open). A long fill can stand on its own as a real melody, or be woven directly into the chords rather than staying purely single-note.

## Build the fill bottom-up: simple line first, color added after
Same "staying practical" logic documented on [[Chord Voicings]] for building a chord bottom-up: start a fill from a simple, recognizable base — e.g. a plain major blues melody — and only then layer color on top (a major 7th slid into, a ii-V-ish passing move, a run of dominant chords down to a rootless target). This keeps the fill playable and musical in real time instead of trying to compose something harmonically dense from the first note. *(General starting point: sketch the fill as a simple diatonic or blues line first; add alterations/passing chords as a second pass, not simultaneously.)*

## A fill can carry real harmonic motion, not just melody
A fill isn't restricted to single notes over a static chord — it can itself move the harmony, e.g. inserting a passing chord mid-fill to walk from one chord to the next (C6 → [G7 passing chord] → C7), or landing on a rootless voicing of the destination chord to keep the texture consistent with the rest of the comp. Treat a fill as a compressed version of the full "chord + bass + melody" comping toolkit, deployed in miniature during the open space.

## Worked example
Over "Gee Baby, Ain't I Good to You" (blues-inflected standard, bigger/more colorful chords than a typical shell-voicing comp): a short fill closes bar two of the A section — a simple melodic fragment on the A7 (13 → ♭13 → 9th on the D7) — after which the vocal returns to finish the phrase. A longer fill later in the form mixes blues and jazz vocabulary, doubles as a turnaround back toward the A7 that opens the next A section, and — going into the bridge — extends into a full chord-solo-style passage: simple major blues melody first, then a major 7th (slid into), a D♭7 → C7 move, and a run of C7 chords down to a rootless F7 to keep the phrase moving into the next section. → [[The Secret Jazz Chord System of Joe Pass]]

## Approaching a chord shape like it's a single note in a line
A different device from the fills above — this decorates the **chord voicing itself**, not the space between chords. Treat a comping voicing as if it were one note in a melodic line, then apply the same approach-note/enclosure vocabulary ([[Bebop Vocabulary]] §2–3) directly to the shape:
1. Start with basic, fundamental voicings over the progression (worked over a plain 2-5-1).
2. Add approach notes/enclosures **into** the shape the same way you'd chromatically approach a single target tone — e.g. the half-step-per-arpeggio-tone device reused from soloing, applied to comping shapes instead.
3. Once that's comfortable, swap in richer voicing color for the same targets — a **minor 9**, a **major 6**, a **major 6/9** shape — each one just another approachable target.
4. **Know the inversions of each shape**, not just the root-position grip — inversions are what make the approach-into-a-voicing device usable across a full progression rather than jumping around the neck.
Stated payoff: once chord shapes are treated as single-note-line targets, comping opens up the same expressive range melodic soloing gets from the same vocabulary. Full device (plus the pivot-tone and arpeggio-approach devices it's paired with in the source video) → [[Pivot Tone Lines]].
