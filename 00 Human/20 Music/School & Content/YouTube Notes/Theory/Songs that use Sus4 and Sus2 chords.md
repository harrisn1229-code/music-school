# Songs that use Sus4 and Sus2 chords

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=h3fj8fX9k04)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=h3fj8fX9k04)

## Synopsis

Sus4 vs sus2 contrasted directly: sus4 almost always resolves back to its parent major/minor triad (strong tension-release), while sus2 is much weaker/muted and often just sits unresolved as an open-sounding color chord in its own right. Plus a bass-note trick that can literally convert a sus4 into a sus2 depending what's underneath, and a closing pivot into quartal harmony (chords built from stacked 4ths rather than 3rds).

## Techniques

- **Definitions**: sus4 = major/minor triad with the 3rd suspended UP to the 4th. Sus2 = the 3rd suspended DOWN to the 2nd. Sus4 is the more common of the two, sometimes labeled simply "sus" (4 assumed).
- **Sus4 on the dominant (V), resolving to V, dragging out a V-I perfect cadence**: "For No One" (The Beatles, end of chorus, Gsus4→G→resolves to I). "Crazy" (Gnarls Barkley) — identical device, same scale degree.
- **Sus4 on the tonic**: "Pinball Wizard" (The Who, iconic riff rocks Bsus4↔B). "Crazy Little Thing Called Love" (Queen, quick D↔Dsus4 riff). "Cold as Ice" (Foreigner, piano intro, E♭sus4↔E♭m).
- **Sus2's weaker pull means it often does NOT resolve, unlike sus4** — used instead as a standalone open/ambiguous color chord: "Love Song" (Sara Bareilles) — a B♭sus2 that never resolves to B♭ major or minor within the progression. "Don't Dream It's Over" (Crowded House) — opens by pivoting between two E♭sus2 voicings; because it's the very first chord heard (before any wider harmonic context establishes major or minor), the sus2's inherent ambiguity is especially effective, keeping the listener genuinely unsure whether the song will land major or minor.
- **A partial/reinterpreted sus2 resolution**: "The Scientist" (Coldplay, opening riff) — Fsus2 moves to Dm7, and since 3 of Dm7's 4 notes form an F major triad, it functions as a loose, disguised resolution back to F even though the literal next chord isn't F major.
- **Sus2 as the entire harmonic basis of a song, just re-voiced with different bass notes**: "Champagne Supernova" (Oasis) — every step of the progression is the SAME Asus2 chord, with only the bass note underneath changing.
- **Both sus4 and sus2 used together for extra character**: "Exit Music (For a Film)" (Radiohead) — a Bm vamp punctuated every other bar by a quick Bsus2→Bsus4 move, the sus4 acting as a turnaround back into Bm.
- **Full riffs built from cycling sus2→triad→sus4→triad**: "Summer of '69" (Bryan Adams) — an arpeggiated riff on both the tonic (D) and dominant (A) chords. "Other Side of the World" (KT Tunstall) — a similar riff.
- **A concrete bass-note trick that literally converts a sus4 into a sus2 (or vice versa)**: "Free Fallin'" (Tom Petty) — the progression's Fsus4 chord (F root, C 5th, B♭ suspended-4th) gets a B♭ bass note underneath once the bassline enters in verse 2 — the SAME three notes, but B♭ in the bass forces the ear to hear B♭ as the new tonic, which makes F read as the 5th and C read as the suspended 2nd instead: the chord is now functionally a B♭sus2, despite containing identical pitches to the original Fsus4. A clean, concrete demonstration that a suspended chord's identity (sus2 vs sus4) is partly a function of context/bass note, not just the notes themselves.
- **Quartal harmony** — chords built by stacking 4ths instead of the usual stacked-3rds (tertiary harmony); because of how standard chord-naming works, quartal chords frequently get labeled as sus4 chords even though they're conceptually a different construction. "Tarkus" (Emerson, Lake & Palmer) builds its chords this way. "Maiden Voyage" (Herbie Hancock) — a classic quartal-harmony example: the upper chord tones are stacked in 3rds as usual, but they sit on top of 4ths-based lower structures, giving the whole chord an unusually open, suspended quality.
