# Creative Process and Idea Capture

How to deliberately set up conditions for inspiration to arrive, and what to do the moment it does — distinct from arrangement/mixing technique, this is about the writing process itself.

## Set the parameter, then let it go

Michael Jackson's own described method (recounted by his engineer, on writing "Billie Jean" and "Beat It"): consciously state the specific creative goal to yourself — "I have to write a song with a driving, hooky bassline," or "this one needs a rock edge" — then deliberately **stop thinking about it** and let it sit in the back of the mind rather than actively working the problem. Over-rehearsing a goal in conscious thought is described as producing tension that blocks the spontaneous result ("you get locked... it doesn't flow"); the parameter-setting step is what points the subconscious at the right target, but the actual idea has to arrive on its own.

## Capture the idea the instant it arrives

Quincy Jones's rule for a spontaneous idea, regardless of when it strikes: write it down or record it immediately, because the window closes fast — "if I don't, it's going next door to Hank" (Henry Mancini, his neighbor). The moment of inspiration is treated as fragile and non-negotiable to act on right away, not something to trust yourself to remember later.

## Don't discard "gibberish" placeholder lyrics too quickly

When writing a melody by singing nonsense syllables or half-formed words before real lyrics exist, specific words that surface during that process aren't always random — they can be a buried signal of what the song is actually about. "Annie, are you OK?" (Michael Jackson's "Smooth Criminal") reportedly surfaced this way, sourced from a CPR-class catchphrase lodged in memory, and became the hook a full narrative was then built around. Treat an unexpectedly specific phrase in a scratch vocal as worth investigating before rewriting it away.

## A reference sets the ENERGY, not the content

When a creative brief points at another song ("we need something with that *My Sharona* energy"), the brief is naming a feeling/spirit to hit, not a template to copy — Michael Jackson's "Beat It" was written in response to exactly that kind of brief and shares none of the referenced song's actual content, only its crossover pop-rock intensity. See also [[Steve Porcaro In-Studio - Writing Human Nature and Unreleased MJ Songs Part 1]] for the same discipline applied to a sonic (not songwriting) reference: a reference sound is a starting point that still has to be tailored to the actual song.

## From videos

- **Freestyle wordless melody first, mine lyrics from a journaled real event second — plus "springboarding," a named technique for building a song out of order.** Arlo Parks: every song starts as a mumbled/freestyled vocal over the instrumental with no lyrics, just an emotional-center phrase that recurs (e.g. "dulled my senses"); real lyrics arrive afterward, sourced from journaling a specific lived night out in sensory detail the same night (a friend's shoes, a drink, a club's architecture) and building the song from those documented details the next day, rather than from abstraction. Once one 4–8 bar section feels exciting, it gets looped on repeat and sung over freely before the rest of the song exists — "springboarding," throwing into the next section "into the abyss" rather than building chronologically from the top. Baird's framing of the whole album's ethos: "limitations are a strength — you can do a lot with a little," restated as a closing rule of thumb — when a section feels too safe vs. too weird, always choose too-weird, because it's easy to simplify a good weird idea afterward and hard to add "sauce" to a boring one after the fact. → [[Vocal Comping and Takes]], [[Arlo Parks Vocal Chain]] — Arlo Parks & Baird (Tape Notes), [[TN 183 Arlo Parks & Baird - Full Video Episode]]
- **A bare-bones, deliberately low-pressure mobile rig, mixed live all the way through instead of left for a separate mix pass.** Myles Smith / Peter Fenn write almost the entire album on the road (desk, small [[Universal Audio Apollo Twin MkII|Apollo]], one vocal mic, guitars, a MIDI controller) specifically to keep sessions feeling like a living room rather than a studio — "90% of our songs are written in Airbnbs." Enabled by [[UAD Spark]] (unowned), a plugin suite needing only an internet connection so the exact same chains travel anywhere. Remote co-writes lean on FaceTime plus [[Audiomovers LISTENTO]] (unowned) for live-streamed studio audio between LA and London. Mixing happens continuously during writing (a standing master-bus chain is kept on throughout, not applied at the end) and the final mix engineer works from a frozen session rather than printed stems — "the mix is such a part of it as we go along... we don't need someone to recreate it." Reference checks happen by day-bouncing to a car stereo. → [[Master Chain]] — Myles Smith / Peter Fenn (Tape Notes), [[TN 184 Myles Smith & Peter Fenn - Full Video Episode]]
- **A fixed, deliberately-not-expanded gear palette across a whole album, used as the constraint that forces new textures.** Charli XCX / A.G. Cook / Finn Keane's *Music, Fashion, Film* came almost entirely from what was physically present in one studio room (Minimoog, PPG Wave, Roland TR-606, Vermona DRM, a handful of guitar pedals) — on a return session weeks later, the trio deliberately did not add new gear, treating the limitation itself as the tool for finding new textures within a fixed set rather than expanding options. → [[Sound Selection Guide]] — Charli XCX / A.G. Cook / Finn Keane (Tape Notes), [[TN 185 Charli XCX, A. G. Cook, Finn Keane - Full Video Episode]]
- **Record a long-form spoken-word interview as raw sampling material, marking usable moments live during the take rather than on a post-hoc listen-through.** Charli XCX flew to record a ~90-minute conversation with David Cronenberg about mortality, texting the engineer to mark specific moments as they happened ("mark this, mark this"), ending up with ~100 marked candidates. One short phrase was found — by chance — to land rhythmically on the beat and became the finished song's looped hook. → [[Sampling and Sound Selection]] — Charli XCX / A.G. Cook / Finn Keane (Tape Notes), [[TN 185 Charli XCX, A. G. Cook, Finn Keane - Full Video Episode]]
- **Two producers working in parallel DAW sessions, routing one session's live output directly into the other's as a sound source, rather than bouncing/exporting stems back and forth.** Finn Keane: "I was plugged into the desk... using my session as an instrument" — a real-time inter-session routing workflow for two people building the same track from separate project files. → [[Session View Workflow]] — Charli XCX / A.G. Cook / Finn Keane (Tape Notes), [[TN 185 Charli XCX, A. G. Cook, Finn Keane - Full Video Episode]]
- **Build a private sample bank from your own unstructured jams instead of sampling other people's records, and release the same song at multiple finish levels (raw "source," polished "main," club/extended edits) as planned content, not remix afterthought.** Joy Anonymous: week-long writing retreats produce 100+ no-intention loop-station jams (one mic, no goal), later mined over months/years into full songs — explicitly reasoned as sidestepping sample-clearance cost/hassle while keeping "our own initial essence" in the DNA of every track. Each finished song then ships as up to four versions from the same source pool (raw/source, main single, 7-inch, club) — sometimes the raw version is recorded first, sometimes reverse-engineered afterward from the polished one. → [[Layering and Stacking]] — Joy Anonymous (Tape Notes), [[TN 186 Joy Anonymous - Full Video Episode]]
- **A blind A/B test for whether a rough idea is actually good: slip it into a Bluetooth speaker playlist alongside already-released music from other artists, unannounced, and watch whether anyone reacts.** Removes the bias of people knowing it's yours or unfinished — a genuine reaction (or none) reads as a faster, more honest signal than a studio-room "sounds great." Paired with their broader advice: get a rough idea in front of a real room (a friend, an open mic, a small DJ set) as fast as possible rather than polishing alone — a real reaction beats a polished but unheard idea every time. → Joy Anonymous (Tape Notes), [[TN 186 Joy Anonymous - Full Video Episode]]
- **Use an AI music generator (Suno) to turn a sung voice-note into a disposable placeholder reference track for session players to play along to — never as material that reaches the final record.** Sang a rough melody idea into a phone, ran it through Suno to get a synth "AI horn" stand-in, then had a real horn section play their actual parts along to that AI reference in the studio instead of reading sheet music — approached with visible unease about the tool itself, used strictly as a scratch-track shortcut for briefing musicians, not as a compositional source. → Joy Anonymous (Tape Notes), [[TN 186 Joy Anonymous - Full Video Episode]]
- **Capture a spontaneous live moment and ship the finished track within 48 hours, as a deliberate scarcity/authenticity move.** A collaborator's improvised spoken intro, captured live at a festival show by their own sound engineer, was licensed and mixed in the next day and the finished single was out two days after the performance — reasoned explicitly as a way to give a release "the value of having been there," countering how disposable music can feel when there's constant new output. → Joy Anonymous (Tape Notes), [[TN 186 Joy Anonymous - Full Video Episode]]
