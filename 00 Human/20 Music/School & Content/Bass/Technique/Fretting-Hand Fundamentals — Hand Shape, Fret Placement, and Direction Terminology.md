# Fretting-Hand Fundamentals — Hand Shape, Fret Placement, and Direction Terminology

BassBuzz's foundational "here's the lowdown on left-hand technique" lesson — the beginner-basics companion to [[Beginner Bass Lesson No. 1 (Your Very First Lesson)]]'s own fretting-hand section and the "day one" starting shape [[Fretting-Hand Strength, Stretch, Stamina, and Finger Control]]'s four exercises all assume already exists. Where those notes cover *exercises* (rattle elimination, stretch, stamina, flying fingers) and Beginner Bass Lesson No. 1 covers the very first thumb/hand-shape myth-bust, this video is the fullest single statement of the **static hand-shape rules** themselves — four-finger use, thumb placement, finger angle, fret placement, and the specific **no-palm-contact** rule not yet documented elsewhere in the vault — plus a genuinely new topic: the vault's first explicit teaching of bass **direction terminology** (up/down string, up/down fret/neck), which matters because every other bass note in this vault uses that terminology assuming it's already understood.

## Use all four fingers, pinky included, from day one

Index, middle, ring, and pinky — all four fretting fingers get used, not just index/middle/ring. Named directly: the pinky is going to feel weak at first, but it gets used to playing bass with time and becomes just as capable as any other finger. Sets up the video's own practice groove below, which deliberately walks through all four fingers in order (index→middle→ring→pinky) on every string. This is the same "don't skip the pinky" premise behind [[Fretting-Hand Strength, Stretch, Stamina, and Finger Control#Why the pinky matters: reach and energy savings (the Chris Squire counter-example)|Fretting-Hand Strength's pinky section]] and the whole point of One Finger Per Fret in [[Simandl vs. One Finger Per Fret — Choosing a Bass Fretting Technique]].

## Thumb placement — relaxed, mid-back-of-neck, touching but not pressing

Thumb rests **relaxed on the middle of the back of the neck** — not up near the top edge, and not floating off in space away from the neck. It should be **touching the neck but not pressing into it** — contact without grip, "flowing around the neck" rather than anchored with pressure. This sharpens (doesn't contradict) [[Beginner Bass Lesson No. 1 (Your Very First Lesson)|Beginner Bass Lesson No. 1's own thumb myth-bust]] — that note debunks forcing the thumb pinned behind the middle finger and says let it rest wherever it naturally wants; this video adds the missing detail on *how much contact*: touching, not gripping, not pressing.

## Finger angle — perpendicular to the strings

Fingers should be **perpendicular to the strings** (equivalently, parallel to the fret wires) rather than slanted across them — "six of one, half a dozen of the other" phrasing for the two ways of stating the same target angle. Some slant is unavoidable and fine the farther the fretting hand sits from the body (naturally more slanted up the neck), but the aim throughout is to stay **relatively perpendicular**. Reason given: a number of downstream mechanical benefits ride on it — this is also the geometry that keeps the pinky reachable (see palm-contact section below, where breaking perpendicular is the direct cause of a stranded pinky).

## Fret placement — middle-to-end of the fret, never past the wire

Fret **toward the middle-to-end of the fret space**, close to the fret wire itself, rather than in the middle or back of the space. **Why:** the closer the fingertip sits to the fret wire, the stronger the angle of contact on the wire, which is what produces a clean, non-buzzing note — you can fret almost right up against the wire as long as you don't cross over it (crossing the wire changes which note sounds). This is the identical fix already documented as **Exercise 1** on [[Fretting-Hand Strength, Stretch, Stamina, and Finger Control#Exercise 1 — Strength builder (rattle elimination)|Fretting-Hand Strength's rattle-elimination exercise]] and **Level 1** of [[Fretting-Hand Coordination 5-Level Daily Drill#Level 1 — Basic pattern and fret placement|the Fretting-Hand Coordination drill]] — a third independent BassBuzz source teaching the same fret-wire-proximity rule, reinforcing it as a bedrock fundamental rather than a one-off tip.

## Overall relaxed hand shape

The stated overall goal of left-hand technique is **maximum relaxation** — mimic what the hand naturally does at rest, not a tensed shape. Reference point: let the arms hang at the sides — loose shoulder, loose elbow, relaxed wrist, fingers gently curled. That gently-curled resting shape takes **less** muscular effort to hold than a flattened or backward-angled hand, which is a common beginner over-correction. Practical implication: avoid backward finger angles and unnecessary straightness — they're a waste of effort when the relaxed curl does the job. A little wrist curl is fine and will vary with fretboard position and the specific shape a passage demands. This is the same target shape [[Beginner Bass Lesson No. 1 (Your Very First Lesson)]] describes as "find your hand's natural relaxed shape first, then bring the neck to it" — this video restates it as the standing goal for *all* left-hand playing, not just the first-contact setup step.

## No palm contact — especially on the index-finger side

**Don't let the palm touch the neck**, especially on the index-finger side — named as a very common beginner habit. There should be visible **negative space** between the neck and the hand; only the fretting fingertips and the thumb make contact, nothing else. **Why it matters (the causal chain, stated directly):** palm-on-neck contact forces the fingers into **backward angles**, twists them **toward the body**, and makes the **pinky hard to use** because it gets pulled away from the neck by the twist. Avoiding palm contact is what makes the other three fundamentals above possible **simultaneously** — perpendicular fingers, comfortable use of all four fingers (pinky included), and the relaxed forward curve all depend on keeping the palm off the neck; losing this one costs the other three. Also cuts friction, which matters for moving fluidly up and down the neck — paired with **not gripping with the thumb** as the other named friction source.

## Direction terminology — "up" and "down" mean pitch, not gravity

Bass terminology for direction is based on **pitch, not physical/gravitational direction** — worth knowing explicitly so instructions ("go up a string," "shift down the neck") aren't misread literally.

- **Up/down a *string*:** strings are ranked low to high by pitch — **E < A < D < G**. "Up a string" from the E string means moving to the A string (a higher-pitched string), even though on a standard bass orientation that string sits physically *below* the E string on the fretboard. "Down a string" is the reverse (A→E, etc.).
- **Up/down a *fret* / "up the neck" vs. "down the neck":** moving toward higher-numbered frets (toward the body, higher pitch) is **up a fret** / **up the neck**; moving toward lower-numbered frets (toward the headstock, lower pitch) is **down a fret** / **down the neck**. This is the reverse of the string convention's physical orientation but consistent in the one way that actually matters: **both are always about the direction of pitch, never about gravity or hand position.**
- **Practical payoff:** every fret/position instruction elsewhere in this vault (e.g. [[Look-Ahead Position Shifting]], the Simandl/OFPF shifting language in [[Simandl vs. One Finger Per Fret — Choosing a Bass Fretting Technique]]) uses "up the neck"/"down the neck" assuming this pitch-based convention — this note is the explicit definition those other notes were built on top of without stating.

## Practice groove — 9-10-11-12 across the E and A strings (all four fingers, half notes)

The video's worked example, designed to drill all four fundamentals above in musical context (BassBuzz calls it "as though this was written by a bass teacher" — it's a pure technical exercise disguised as a groove).

- **The line:** on the **E string**, fret **9th fret with index, 10th with middle, 11th with ring, 12th with pinky** — same finger-per-fret assignment [[Simandl vs. One Finger Per Fret — Choosing a Bass Fretting Technique|OFPF uses]], covering a clean 4-fret span at the 9th position. Then repeat the identical index-middle-ring-pinky/9-10-11-12 pattern on the **A string**. The whole groove is just that 8-note phrase (E string then A string) repeating.
- **Rhythm:** **half notes** — pluck on beats **1 and 3** only, each note held for two full counts (the first note rings through 1–2, the second through 3–4), then the pattern repeats.
- **Right-hand pattern:** at this slow a tempo, index-finger-only plucking is a legitimate fallback if alternating is too much — but the target to build toward is strict **index-middle alternation** (works cleanly here because there are exactly four notes per string, i.e. an even split: index-middle-index-middle). The lesson is explicitly left-hand-focused; the right hand is secondary here (contrast [[String Crossing and Plucking Patterns]], where alternation vs. raking is the whole subject).
- **Progression:** rehearsed a cappella first, then against a backing track at **three successive speeds — slow, medium, then full tempo** — each pass reinforcing the same checklist: perpendicular fingers, no palm contact, relaxed thumb, gentle forward curl.
- **Advanced aside, explicitly optional for beginners:** keeping the fretting fingers **down** on their frets as later fingers land (rather than lifting each finger back up before the next one presses) is named as an efficiency technique worth knowing about but not something a beginner needs to force yet.
- **Companion drill, different string/source:** [[Fretting-Hand Coordination 5-Level Daily Drill]]'s Level 1 runs the *identical* index-middle-ring-pinky, frets-9-through-12 shape on the **G string** instead, ascending then descending, at a flat 120 BPM tempo ladder rather than a 3-speed backing track — two independent BassBuzz sources landing on the same 9-12 four-finger shape as the go-to first fretting-hand pattern, on different strings.
