# 3 Levels to Mixing Major & Minor Pentatonic Scales

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1jb7fVA6RfA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1jb7fVA6RfA)

## Synopsis

A short, tightly-structured lesson on combining the major and minor pentatonic scales **of the same root** (not a relative-major/minor pair — both scales are built on E throughout), taught as three escalating levels, each demonstrated with a short solo over a backing track. Level 1 is the loosest: alternate freely between major-pentatonic licks and minor-pentatonic licks over a static one-chord vamp, useful specifically for harmonically ambiguous material (power-chord rock/funk, a lot of blues) where there's no third in the chord to commit the harmony to major or minor. Level 2 tightens the rule by tying scale choice to the chord itself over a 12-bar blues: major pentatonic on the I chord, minor pentatonic on the IV and V chords. Level 3 is the payoff — instead of switching whole scales, blend the two scales' notes together by swapping a single note in each: over the I7 chord, drop the major pentatonic's 6th to the ♭7 (borrowed from minor pentatonic) so the scale contains every chord tone of the dominant-7 chord; over the IV7 chord, raise the minor pentatonic's ♭7 to the major 6 (borrowed from major pentatonic). This is the same "swap one note to unlock all the chord tones" mechanic already documented on this channel's [[Minor 6 Pentatonic Scale]] and [[Diagonal Pentatonic Blues Soloing]] pages, here framed explicitly as the third rung of a practice progression that starts from "just alternate scales" and ends at "edit one note per chord." The whole lesson is a clean, reusable teaching ladder — good as a practice-order template, not just a lick source. Tabs/backing tracks are Patreon-gated (mentioned but not included in the free video).

## What's taught

### Fundamentals (key of E)
- **E major pentatonic: E – F♯ – G♯ – B – C♯**, playable in five positions up and down the neck.
- **E minor pentatonic: E – G – A – B – D**, same five-position system, same root.
- Both scales share the same root (E) throughout the lesson — this is a **parallel** major/minor pentatonic relationship (same tonic, different quality), not the relative-major/relative-minor pairing (e.g. E major pentatonic / C♯ minor pentatonic, which share identical pitch content) documented elsewhere in this vault.
- **Warm-up drill named directly:** to build comfort combining the two scales, alternate between playing the E major pentatonic and E minor pentatonic scale runs within the same fretboard position, position by position through all five.

### Level 1 — Free alternation over a static vamp (no harmonic commitment)
- **The core move: play a licked phrase in major pentatonic, then move the identical fingering/lick up the fretboard by 3 frets to get a minor-pentatonic-flavored version of the same phrase.** Demonstrated live in the video — a major pentatonic phrase, then the same shape shifted up 3 frets, landing in minor pentatonic territory. This works because of how the two scales' movable box shapes interlock across the neck; the practical takeaway is you don't need to consciously re-finger a new scale, just relocate the same physical shape 3 frets up (major → minor) or down (minor → major).
- **When to use this level:** tunes/sections with **no clearly defined harmony** — power-chord-based rock and funk riffs (no 3rd in the chord, so no major/minor commitment implied by the harmony) and a lot of blues. Since the harmony doesn't force a choice, freely alternating major and minor pentatonic phrases both "work" and adds contrast/color.
- **Worked demo:** soloing over a static **one-chord vamp in E**, alternating major-pentatonic and minor-pentatonic phrases by moving similar-shaped licks up and down the neck 3 frets, covering both the "major" and "minor" flavors within the same solo.
- **Drill to practice this level:** take a short pentatonic lick you already know, play it as-is (major), then transpose the identical shape up 3 frets and play it again (minor) — repeat back and forth until the 3-fret relocation is automatic, then apply it over a one-chord vamp of your own.

### Level 2 — Tie scale choice to the chord (12-bar blues in E)
- **Rule: major pentatonic on the I chord, minor pentatonic on the IV and V chords.** Worked over a simple 12-bar blues in E: E (I) → major pentatonic; A (IV) → switch to minor pentatonic; back to E (I) → back to major pentatonic; B (V) → minor pentatonic again.
- **Why this is a step up from Level 1:** it's still whole-scale switching (not blending notes), but now the switch is **triggered by the chord itself** rather than chosen freely — an explicit stepping stone toward chord-tone targeting, which Level 3 completes.
- **Drill to practice this level:** loop a 12-bar blues shuffle backing track in any key, and drill switching pentatonic scale (major on I, minor on IV and V) exactly on the downbeat of each chord change — first with simple scale runs, then with real phrases, until the switch lands cleanly in time.

### Level 3 — Blend the scales by swapping one note per chord (the payoff level)
Instead of switching whole scales, **edit a single note in each scale so the resulting 5-note set contains every chord tone of the dominant-7 chord it's played over** — demonstrated over the same 12-bar blues in E:
- **Over the I7 chord (E7):** start from **E major pentatonic (E F♯ G♯ B C♯)** and **drop the 6th (C♯) to the ♭7 (D)** — borrowed from the minor pentatonic. Resulting scale: **E – F♯ – G♯ – B – D**. This scale now contains every chord tone of E7 (root E, major 3rd G♯, 5th B, ♭7 D) plus the 2nd/9th (F♯) as the one extra color note — stated directly in the video as covering "all of the notes" of the E7 chord.
- **Over the IV7 chord (A7):** start from **A minor pentatonic (A C D E G)** and **raise the ♭7 (G) to the major 6 (F♯)** — borrowed from the major pentatonic. Resulting scale: **A – C – D – E – F♯**. *(Confidence flag: the transcript's spoken recitation of A7's exact chord-tone list is garbled in the auto-captions and doesn't cleanly match the note-swap math above — the note-swap itself, minor pentatonic with ♭7 raised to major 6, is stated clearly and unambiguously and matches the identical mechanic on [[Minor 6 Pentatonic Scale]]; treat the specific "which notes are chord tones" recitation for A7 as unconfirmed against the video rather than the swap mechanic itself, which is solid.)* The root (A) and 5th (E) are direct A7 chord tones either way; the minor 3rd (C) reads as a blue note against A7's major 3rd (standard blues bend-toward-major-3rd practice, same logic as [[Blues Lead Phrasing and Touch]]); the added major 6 (F♯) is the borrowed color note driving the "blend" sound.
- **The general method, stated directly:** "just by making subtle little adjustments like that you can start to play things that really live inside of the chord changes instead of just playing one scale over all of the changes" — swap exactly one note per chord (major pentatonic's 6th ↔ minor pentatonic's ♭7, in whichever direction covers the dominant chord's tones) rather than committing to a whole scale.
- **Same underlying trick as [[Minor 6 Pentatonic Scale]]** (drop ♭7 to major 6 within a minor pentatonic box) and the IV7/V7 note-edits on [[Diagonal Pentatonic Blues Soloing]] (drop major pentatonic's 3rd to get the ♭7-carrying altered shape) — this video is the first source in the vault to name **both directions of the same swap** (major pentatonic losing its 6th to gain a ♭7, and minor pentatonic losing its ♭7 to gain a 6th) as two ends of one blending technique, explicitly framed as the "combine the scales" endpoint of a practice ladder rather than an isolated trick.
- **Drill to practice this level:** over a static dominant-7 vamp (I7), play E major pentatonic normally, then replay identical phrases with every 6th swapped to the ♭7; over the IV7 vamp, play minor pentatonic normally, then replay with every ♭7 swapped to the major 6 — the same "paired lick" ear-training method as [[Minor 6 Pentatonic Scale]]'s practice drill, applied here to both scale directions instead of just one.

### Practice ladder (ties the three levels together)
- **The levels are a deliberate teaching progression, not three unrelated tricks:** Level 1 (free alternation, no harmonic rule) → Level 2 (whole-scale switching tied to the chord) → Level 3 (blend the two scales via a single note swap per chord, so the scale itself tracks the chord tones). Practicing in this order builds the ear and the fretboard vocabulary needed for the final, most useful level.
- **Concrete session structure to lift from this video:** (1) drill the 3-fret major↔minor relocation shape in isolation; (2) loop a static one-chord vamp and freely alternate major/minor licks (Level 1); (3) loop a 12-bar blues and switch scale by chord — major on I, minor on IV/V (Level 2); (4) loop the same 12-bar blues and play the note-swapped hybrid scales instead of switching whole scales (Level 3) — same chord loop reused across stages 2–4 so only the vocabulary changes, not the backing track.
