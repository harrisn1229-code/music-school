# All The Things You Are - Harmonic Analysis

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pAnT_wi2WJY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pAnT_wi2WJY)

## Synopsis

A full chord-by-chord harmonic analysis of the Jerome Kern jazz standard "All The Things You Are," walked through in Jens Larsen's usual method: figure out the **form** from the melody first (before touching the chords), then work out the **key centers and modulations**, then the **chord-scale/soloing choices**, closing with a short discussion of Kern's own voicing choices in the original sheet music and why they matter. The tune is chosen specifically because it modulates far more than a typical standard — four separate key centers across a 36-bar AABA-ish form — which makes it Larsen's worked example for how to *find and name a pivot chord*, and for the recurring "modulate up a major 3rd" device that shows up across a lot of the American songbook. The back half covers a full bar-by-bar chord-scale map for soloing (harmonic minor, Lydian, melodic minor, Dorian-over-a-diminished-substitute), a detailed dissection of the ♭3°/♯4° (subdominant diminished) chord that closes the form, and a closing note on why Kern wrote the last A section in bare triads rather than fully spelled chords — a deliberate ambiguity device. Density is high throughout; almost no bar goes undiscussed.

## Techniques

### Form (analyze the melody first, not the chords)
- **Find the key from the ending, not the start.** All The Things You Are opens on an Fm7 that sounds like a tonic but isn't — the actual key is identified by the final cadence and the final melody note (both land on A♭), so the song is **A♭ major**, even though the first chord you hear is a vi-chord-shaped minor 7. General method stated explicitly: don't assume the opening chord is the tonic — confirm key from where the piece actually resolves. → [[Keys]], [[Cadences]]
- **Determine ABA form from the melody alone, before analyzing any chords** — stated as the correct order of operations for any standard, because the melody is fixed while the harmony under it can be reinterpreted/reharmonized. Here the form reads as ABA but is "less clear" than a symmetric ABA like "What Is This Thing Called Love" (identical A sections) — the two A sections share melodic contour and harmonic *shape* but sit in **different keys** (first A ends in C major, second A is the same idea moved up a step, ending in G major), which is what makes the form ambiguous on a first pass. → [[Musical Form]]
- **Full form map:** A1 (8 bars, A♭ major → ends in C major) → A2 (8 bars, same shape moved to E♭ major → ends in G major) → Bridge (8 bars: 2-5-1 in G major, then a fast cadence to E major) → A3 (8 bars, same opening as A1/A2 but extended +4 bars into a tag ending, closes in A♭ major, no further modulation). → [[Musical Form]]

### Modulation (the video's main subject)
- **"Modulate up a major 3rd" is a common, recognizable device across the standard repertoire — name-checked here as its own pattern, not unique to this tune.** A♭ major's A section resolves up a major 3rd into C major via a G7 that's understood in A♭ (as V7/iii, expected to resolve to Cm) but is redirected to resolve to C major instead. Larsen names this as a repeating move worth recognizing on sight in other tunes — the captions garble the exact title list (rendered as "coal polos I love you," "I'm old-fashioned," "gone with the wind"), most plausibly referring to a Cole Porter tune ("I Love You"), Jerome Kern's own "I'm Old-Fashioned," and "Gone With The Wind" (the standard, not the film score) — *(transcript-garbled proper nouns, flagged as uncertain)*. → [[Modulation (Key Change)]]
- **The pivot-chord identity is genuinely ambiguous here, and Larsen argues explicitly for one reading over another based on how it changes what you play.** Two candidate pivot chords for the A♭→C modulation: (1) **G7 as the pivot** — understood as A♭'s own V7/iii (expected to resolve to Cm), which then gets reinterpreted as C major's plain V7 once it resolves up to C instead of down to Cm — Larsen's preferred reading; (2) **D♭maj7 as the pivot** — the IV chord in A♭, reread as the "Neapolitan" (♭II) in C. **Why it matters practically, not just academically:** reading G7 as the pivot means playing it altered / from C harmonic minor (♭9, ♭13 — "sounds the most smooth" per Larsen) since it's still functioning as A♭'s dominant right up to the moment of resolution; reading D♭maj7 as the pivot means you're already "in" C major by the time you hit G7, so you'd play it as a plain C-major-scale dominant — which Larsen calls "unclear" and the less satisfying way to play the transition. **General principle stated: delay the surprise until the resolution, not the dominant** — an altered/tense dominant chord already telegraphs "this is modulating," so the "aha" moment lands better if it's saved for where the chord actually resolves. → [[Modulation (Key Change)]], [[Secondary Dominants]]
- **Evidence for which reading the composer intended, pulled straight from the melody notes, not just chord theory** — on the first A's G7, the melody carries only the 3rd and 7th (no color tone either way, inconclusive on its own); but the **second A's equivalent chord (C7, modulating E♭→G) has a ♭9 (a leading tone, D♯/E♭) sitting in the melody**, which would clash badly against a plain major reading — Larsen reads this as Kern himself hearing the dominant as altered/harmonic-minor-flavored, supporting the "delay the surprise" pivot-chord reading above as the composer's actual intent, not just a stylistic preference. → [[Guide Tones]]
- **A tonic-major-to-tonic-minor pivot, used as connective tissue mid-phrase, not a full modulation** — landing on C major (end of A1), the very next harmonic move treats **C minor as an accepted "borrowed" color on the same tonic** (major/minor mixture on one root) to slide into E♭ major for A2; the general principle: once a key center is established, its parallel-minor tonic is an available pivot chord into a new key a minor 3rd up, because "pieces that have both major and minor progressions on the tonic" are common enough that the ear accepts it without a hard modulation feel. → [[Parallel Minor]], [[Modulation (Key Change)]]
- **A deceptive ii-V that resolves to a MAJOR chord instead of the expected minor — used identically at two different points in the form (end of A2→bridge in G, and end of bridge→A3's F minor), the tune's other core modulation device.** In G major, the bridge's own internal cadence sets up what should be vi (E minor) via a ii-V, but resolves instead to **E major** — a deceptive-resolution modulation, not a plain vi substitution. General framing: hearing a ii-V normally primes the ear to expect a minor tonic at the resolution; redirecting that same cadence to the parallel MAJOR of the expected minor chord is a reliable surprise-modulation device, reusable in original writing. → [[Modulation (Key Change)]], [[Cadences]]
- **A one-note voice-leading pivot used to snap out of a key with minimum harmonic machinery — change a single chord tone and the whole chord's function flips.** To get from the bridge's E major back to the last A's F minor in just one bar: lower the 5th of the E-major triad (B→B♭), turning E major into an altered dominant-quality chord functioning as **C7alt**, which then resolves to **F minor**. Read a second way as the *tritone substitution of the dominant of the dominant* (i.e. C7 subs for F♯7, which would be the "expected" V7/V on the way to B7→E); either reading, the mechanism is the same one-note change. General technique: a single altered/lowered chord tone can convert a stable tonic-quality triad into a functioning altered dominant with almost no harmonic "seam," useful for a fast one-bar key exit. → [[Modulation (Key Change)]], [[Chord Substitution]] § *Tritone substitution*
- **Full key-center map of the tune, stated plainly as the "read this before anything else" summary:** A♭ major → C major → (hint of C minor) → E♭ major → G major (start of bridge) → E major → (one-bar pivot) → A♭ major (last A, stays there to the end). → [[Modulation (Key Change)]]
- **The general modulation-analysis method, stated as a repeatable workflow, not just applied here:** find the pivot chord (the one chord that reads sensibly in both the outgoing and incoming key), and treat a genuine modulation as something you can clearly *hear* — as opposed to a tune that runs through a lot of different-looking chords (borrowed chords, passing chords, substitutions) but never actually leaves its home key, which Larsen contrasts directly with his own "Stella By Starlight" analysis (linked in the video, not yet in this vault) as the counter-example of a harmonically busy tune that mostly doesn't modulate at all. → [[Modulation (Key Change)]], [[Chord Progressions]]

### Chords / turnaround structure
- **A♭ A-section skeleton:** `Fm7 – B♭m7 – E♭7 – A♭maj7` (vi–ii–V–I in A♭, i.e. a plain [[Turnarounds|1-6-2-5]] starting from the vi) `– G7 (pivot, V7/iii reinterpreted) – Cmaj7 (new I)`.
- **An unusual turnaround variant — the strong cadence lands on the WEAK bar (bar 4), not the expected bar 3.** Named explicitly as a quirk worth knowing: this A section's opening 1-6-2-5-style turnaround resolves its first real cadence on a metrically weak 4th bar rather than the more typical 3rd-bar resolution (contrasted with the bridge's own 2-5-1, which resolves on the expected strong bar) — Larsen frames the vagueness of that early resolution as deliberate, tied to the same "disguise the key with triads" device below. → [[Turnarounds]]
- **The "real book" chord symbol for the pre-cadence chord in the coda (D♭m7) is flagged as simply wrong, with a correction and the reasoning for it.** Larsen argues it should read as **D♭m6** or, functionally equivalent, **G♭7** — both readings point to the same underlying device: **"IV minor" as a way back to the tonic**, the same minor-subdominant family already documented on [[Secondary Subdominants]] and [[Borrowed Chords]] (Fm/Fm6/Fm-maj7/B♭7-backdoor/F°/A♭maj7/D♭maj7 in the key of C — here transposed, D♭ is the IV of A♭). Explicitly generalized: real-book chord symbols for this family of chord are frequently written wrong/simplified, and it's worth re-deriving the "actual" harmony by ear rather than trusting the page. → [[Secondary Subdominants]], [[Borrowed Chords]]
- **Coda tail (the extended 4 bars of the last A):** `D♭maj7 (IV) → D♭m6/G♭7 (iv-minor, "the way back to the tonic") → Cm7 (iii, also tonic-function) → B♭m7♭5 (ii half-diminished) → E♭7alt (V, delayed resolution) → A♭maj7 (I)`.

### The closing ♭3° / ♯4° diminished chord (the tune's signature "quirk" chord)
- **Two enharmonically-related names for the same passing diminished chord, and why Larsen deliberately picks one over the other.** The chord before the final ii (B♭m7♭5) is most simply read as a **♭III° (B°, i.e. "flat-3-diminished" off the Cm)**, but the *stricter theoretical description* is a **♯IV° (sharp-4-diminished)** — Larsen explains he avoids the sharp-4 label here specifically because "sharp 4 can happen in a few different ways" and prefers to derive it concretely as **A♭7♭9 minus its root (a rootless A♭7♭9), i.e. G♭°7 built off the ♭9 of the tune's own home dominant** — same underlying device as [[Diminished Chords]]'s "7♭9 is itself a rootless diminished chord with an added root a major 3rd below," here applied to derive the exact chord in this tune's coda from first principles rather than naming it directly. Resolves down into B♭m7♭5 with the diminished shape's own root sitting on the **7th of the base** (an inversion, not root position). Functions as a **subdominant diminished**. → [[Diminished Chords]]
- **The chord's melody note is always the leading tone/7th of the key — stated as the actual reason composers reach for this exact chord instead of harmonizing that scale degree a different way.** Checked directly against two other standards that use the identical device on the identical melody-scale-degree: **"Stella by Starlight"** (opening two bars — this same ♯4° functioning as a full 2-5-1 substitute) and **"I Remember You"** — both have the 7th-of-the-key sitting in the melody at this exact spot. The stated alternative (harmonizing that note as a plain dominant chord with the 3rd exposed in the melody) "doesn't sound as nice," which is Larsen's explanation for why the diminished substitution recurs across multiple standards rather than being a one-off Kern quirk. → [[Diminished Chords]], [[🎵 SIGMUND ROMBERG - Softly As in a Morning Sunrise]] *(no existing vault note yet for "Stella by Starlight" or "I Remember You" — flagged, not created, per the "don't force-create for a one-off tune-reference" guidance)*
- **A named soloist's trick for "announcing" a coming parallel-minor substitution on the diminished chord before it actually arrives, credited to Keith Jarrett (and reportedly also heard from Kurt Rosenwinkel).** Instead of playing the diminished chord straight, substitute a **C minor triad in its place** (turning the Cm(iii)→dim passing motion into a Cm→Cminor-again "parallel" move) — the trick is that this pre-announces the harmonic move by making the *preceding* Cm chord already imply the parallel substitution that's coming, so the ear hears one continuous **Dorian** color running underneath both chords rather than two separate harmonic events. Framed as "thinking ahead": the player commits to the parallel-minor substitution one chord early so the whole two-chord unit reads as a single Dorian passage rather than a diminished-chord surprise. → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Modal Jazz]]

### Chord-scale map for soloing (bar-by-bar, A♭ major reading)
- **A1:** Fm7–B♭m7–E♭7–A♭maj7 → straight A♭ major scale throughout (no separate minor reading needed — the melody's strong 3rds/7ths already spell the harmony clearly, described as a general strength of this tune for less experienced improvisers, since the melody alone carries the harmonic sound). → [[Scales]]
- **G7 (pivot):** C harmonic minor (giving ♭9/♭13, "the smooth sound," per the pivot-chord discussion above) — resolving to Cmaj7, played straight major, OR (a named alternate color, explicitly flagged as unexpected but usable) **Lydian on the Cmaj7**, moving the whole chord down a half step into **Cm11** for a "nice but not the most obvious" transition. → [[Scale Modes Reference]] § Lydian
- **A2:** same A♭-major reading, transposed; the C7 pivot (E♭→G) reads the same way as the G7 above, harmonic-minor-flavored, matching the ♭9 the melody explicitly puts in the chord.
- **Bridge:** G major 2-5-1 (D7→G, plain major); the D7 carries a leading-tone ♭9 color in the melody but on a weak beat, so it's not a strong prompt to alter the dominant — altering it anyway ("since it's a cadence") is presented as a legitimate optional choice, not mandatory. The F♯ chord (written F♯m7 in the real book but functionally **F♯ half-diminished**, a straight minor ii-V cadence) leads to the deceptive E major landing.
- **C7 → Fm (bridge's one-bar pivot into the last A):** F harmonic minor, the expected source once a dominant resolves to a minor tonic.
- **Last A:** back to A♭ major throughout; the D♭m6/G♭7 (iv-minor) chord specifically reads as a **melodic minor** sound (D♭ melodic minor); Cm7 reads as plain A♭ major scale (tonic-function, no separate minor scale needed); the ♭3°/♯4° diminished reads as **C harmonic minor** derived by ear from the A♭ major scale (swap B♭→B, D♭→D — two altered notes, "easy to work with") — noted as **enharmonically interchangeable with A♭ harmonic major**, essentially the same sound with one different note; final ii-V-I plain.

### A composer's-choice observation (sheet-music voicing, not a performance technique)
- **Kern reportedly wrote the last-A opening in bare triads, not fully-spelled extended chords — argued as a deliberate ambiguity device, not laziness.** A plain triad (Fm, not Fm6/Fm7) leaves the listener genuinely unsure for a couple of bars whether they're in F minor or A♭ major (they share every note in a bare triad reading) — the ambiguity resolves only once the progression moves to B♭m7 and then D♭, confirming A♭. **Turning the tonic-minor chord into a minor-6th voicing (the "normal" jazz habit) would destroy this ambiguity immediately**, because a minor-6th chord isn't diatonic to the relative major reading — so the "incomplete" triad voicing is functioning as a deliberate extra layer of subtle, delayed modulation-feel *inside* a section that doesn't actually modulate at all. Larsen adds he hasn't personally heard a transcribed solo that uses melodic minor over that opening tonic-minor chord (a color the ambiguous voicing would technically support) and invites viewers to flag one if they know of it. → [[Chord Voicings]], [[Musical Form]]
