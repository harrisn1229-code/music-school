# Modal Jazz

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Jazz Harmony]]. Harmony that **stays still on purpose** — one chord or mode for many bars, with interest coming from melody, voicing and texture instead of from chord changes.

This is the jazz idiom closest to how modern loop-based music actually works, which makes it the most directly useful part of the strand.

For modes in a pop context, see [[Modal Pop]]. For the modes themselves, [[Diatonic Modes]].

## The reaction it was

Bebop had reached a point where tunes changed chords every two beats and improvisation was largely about navigating them. Modal jazz (from the late 1950s — *Kind of Blue* is the reference recording) removed the changes almost entirely: *So What* is 16 bars of D Dorian, 8 of E♭ Dorian, 8 more of D Dorian.

**When harmony stops moving, everything else has to carry the music.** That constraint is the whole subject.

## What replaces functional harmony

### 1. The mode is the material

There's no ii–V to outline, so the line is built from the mode's own character — specifically its **characteristic note**, the one degree that distinguishes it from the plain major or minor:

| Mode | Characteristic note | Against |
|---|---|---|
| Dorian | natural 6 | natural minor |
| Lydian | ♯4 | major |
| Mixolydian | ♭7 | major |
| Phrygian | ♭2 | natural minor |

**Land on it and the mode is established; avoid it and the music collapses into plain major or minor.** In D Dorian the note is B — hit B and it's Dorian, avoid B and it's D minor. → [[SCL-C05 — Characteristic notes]]

### 2. Quartal voicings

Stacking fourths instead of thirds. The classic *So What* voicing is three perfect fourths with a major third on top: **E A D G B** from the bottom.

Why fourths: a stack of thirds names a chord and therefore implies a function, and function is the thing modal music is trying to avoid. **Fourths sound open and unassigned** — they state the mode without committing to a chord. They're also why modal jazz has its particular hollow, floating sound.

Transfers directly: quartal voicings under a vocal sit in a mix beautifully because they're sparse and have no strong 3rd fighting the melody. → [[Chord Voicings]], [[Quartal Chord Voicings]] (fretboard shapes, the *So What* voicing fully derived, and a reharmonization trick for using quartal shapes over ordinary tertian chords)

### 3. Pedal points and drones

With no root motion, a held bass note costs nothing and anchors everything. Move the upper voices freely above it.

### 4. Rhythm and space carry the form

When harmony can't signal a section change, dynamics, density and texture have to. This is the same problem loop-based production has, solved the same way. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]

## Modal interchange is a different thing — don't confuse them

- **Modal jazz** — the harmony *stays on one mode* for a long time.
- **Modal interchange / borrowed chords** — you *borrow a chord* from a parallel mode and return. → [[Borrowed Chords]] · [[SCL-C07 — Modal interchange as scales]]

They share a word and almost nothing else. The first is a static approach to form; the second is a chromatic spice within functional harmony.

## Why it matters for the music you actually make

**A loop is modal harmony.** A four-bar loop on Fmaj7 or a two-chord vamp is exactly the situation *So What* is in — the harmony isn't going anywhere and the interest has to come from somewhere else. Modal jazz is 60 years of worked solutions to that problem.

The three that transfer cleanest:

- **Commit to a characteristic note.** A loop that never plays the ♯4 isn't Lydian, it's major with a Lydian label. The colour has to be *sounded*.
- **Voice in fourths when the chord is static.** Removes the harmonic commitment that gets tiring over sixteen bars of the same chord.
- **Change one thing per section.** With no chord changes, one new element — a texture, a countermelody, a register shift — reads as a whole new section.

## Modal islands — treating each chord of a non-functional vamp as its own mode

A different application of "harmony stays still" than the rest of this page: instead of one static mode for a long stretch, a **short vamp with several *different* chords, none of them functionally connected to each other**, each held long enough to be its own local tonal center. Rather than searching for one key that explains the whole progression, treat each chord as a small **island** — pick its scale independently, and don't worry about how it connects to its neighbours.

**A private tool for choosing notes on an island chord that doesn't sit in any obvious key:** invent a small, admittedly fictional fragment of functional harmony between two adjacent chords — hear this move "as if" it were a ii–V, or "as if" it were a IV→iv, in some key that doesn't actually govern the rest of the progression — purely to have *a* reference for scale choice and target notes. **Never claimed as the real analysis, just a usable one** — the same method applies to genuinely non-functional written tunes (Wayne Shorter, Herbie Hancock), not only to a composer's own vamps.

**Two supporting devices that make islands cohere without functional glue:**
- **Place the actual scale/tonal shift on a weak bar rather than a strong one**, when the goal is to obscure the tonal center rather than clarify it — keep the strong internal move scale-static and let the shift happen somewhere the ear isn't watching.
- **A single voicing shape moved chromatically/in parallel across different roots** can be the real thread holding a set of otherwise-unrelated island chords together, even when it wasn't planned that way going in — worth checking for after the fact, not just before writing.

**Why this differs from the "static mode" case above:** *So What* holds one mode for many bars; a modal-islands vamp changes chord (and mode) every bar or two, but each chord still gets treated with the same "commit to its own material, don't chase a global key" logic — the island is just shorter-lived.

## From videos

- **"So What" and "Impressions" as the clearest real case of deliberately avoiding functional harmony, contrasted with the mechanical reason all-minor-chord music is otherwise rare.** Miles Davis's "So What" vamps almost its full 9 minutes on Dm7→E♭m7, each chord treated as a **key center** rather than a functional chord — development comes from exploring modes/scales over the fixed center, not from harmonic movement; a 30-second major-chord intro keeps it from being a pure all-minor piece. John Coltrane's "Impressions" is harmonically identical minus that intro, making it a genuinely pure all-minor example. Framed against why this matters: the three common cadences (perfect/deceptive/plagal) all require a major chord, so genuinely functional harmony can't stay all-minor — modal jazz's key-center vamping is a deliberate way around that constraint entirely, rather than an accident of a mostly-minor progression. → [[Cadences]] — David Bennett Music Theory, [[Why are Minor Chord Songs so rare?]]
- **A fourth independent confirmation of "tonic dominant" language, this time generated by a line-construction device rather than a scale choice — the same "closer" phrase that normally ends a bebop line flipped to open one instead, with an arpeggio extending outward from it.** Framed directly as "a great example of tonic dominant language... a dominant chord that doesn't resolve to the one chord, it just kind of stays in the sound of the dominant" — stated useful for extending a dominant line before an eventual resolution, or over a static dominant section like the B section of [[Rhythm Changes]]. The device itself (a "line closer" used to *open* rather than *close* a phrase) is new vocabulary for the page, full writeup on [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §7. Worked over Charlie Parker's "Donna Lee" head — full song breakdown on [[🎵 CHARLIE PARKER - Donna Lee]], which also carries this page's own already-logged Donna Lee tonic-dominant bullet below (the B♭7 sitting where Bbm7 "should" be). → [[Bebop Vocabulary]] §7, [[🎵 CHARLIE PARKER - Donna Lee]], [[Rhythm Changes]] — Nathan Borton, [[How To Improve Your BEBOP LINES and Phrases with DONNA LEE - The Ultimate Bebop Guide...]]
- **"Modal islands" named directly as the composing/soloing method for a set of vague, non-functionally-connected vamp chords — three original vamps worked as a case study, each treating every chord as its own local tonal center rather than searching for one key that explains the whole thing.** Explicit statement of the method: *"we made it into sort of small islands that don't really connect entirely, but then it still sort of works."* Three supporting devices demonstrated: (1) an **invented functional fragment** used purely as a private scale-choice tool — e.g. hearing an unrelated D-major-to-D♭7 move "as if" it were a ii–V in a key that doesn't actually govern the progression, just to generate a usable altered scale (full mechanics on [[Chord Substitution]] § *From videos*); (2) **scale shifts deliberately placed on the weak bar** rather than the strong one, to keep the tonal center obscured rather than clarified; (3) **a single ambiguous sus/minor-11-type voicing shape moved chromatically across different roots**, discovered only in hindsight as the real thread holding one of the three vamps together, producing contrary motion between the moving chord and a half-step-below bass approach. Also names a genuinely non-jazz, folk-derived cadence (minor v → minor i, no leading tone, from Danish/Swedish folk music) used to resolve one of the islands — see [[Borrowed Chords]] § *From videos* for the full worked cadence. Full three-vamp breakdown, chord-by-chord, on the source note. → [[Chord Substitution]], [[Chromatic Mediants and Pop Modulation]], [[Borrowed Chords]], [[The Axis Progression]] — Jens Larsen, [[Jens Larsen - Modern Jazz Harmony - Chord Progressions and Analysis]]
- **Superimposing a completely unrelated chord's triad/arpeggio over an unchanged bass note as a deliberate modal-color device, distinct from this page's "tonic dominant" reinterpretation below — here the superimposed sound isn't even diatonically related to the underlying chord.** Over a static C-minor bass line, solo using basic **F triad/F7 material** — not because F7 is functionally "correct" (it isn't there), but because emphasizing F creates a second focal point that contrasts against the C-minor bass without ever technically clashing (nothing in a plain F-triad line falls genuinely outside the sound). **Fragility named directly:** the superimposed line has to stay very basic/triadic — reach for generic ("vague") bebop vocabulary instead and the ear stops hearing the superimposition, it just sounds like more minor-key playing. States outright that the identical superimposed material could also just be heard as plain Dorian over the same chord — **the distinction between "superimposed F7" and "Dorian" is entirely which notes get emphasized and how the line is phrased, not the notes themselves**, the clearest single statement in the vault of this page's own "commit to a characteristic note" principle working in reverse (deliberately *obscuring* which mode/chord is being played by choosing what to emphasize). → [[Blues Form and Harmony]], [[Tonic Minor Soloing]] — Jens Larsen, [[The Modern Modal Approach - Minor Blues - Jazz Scales, Exotic Scales - Guitar Lesson]]
- **"Dominant two chord" — a plain diatonic ii chord replaced with a dominant 7th on the same root, treated with the identical tonic-dominant/lydian-dominant logic as the bullet below rather than as an altered V pretending to resolve.** In Jerome Kern's "Nobody Else But Me" (key of C), the ii chord is **D7 instead of Dm7/Dm7♭5** — flagged directly as a device that "doesn't act like a normal 5:1... it doesn't really want to pull to one, it just kind of stays there," and intermediate players commonly mis-hear it and alter it toward a 5–1 sound, which the source calls out as the wrong read. Sound named directly: **lydian dominant / tonic dominant**, carrying the **9, 13, and ♯11**. Scale: the same **melodic minor built off the dominant chord's own 5th** as this page's other tonic-dominant bullet (A melodic minor over D7). The **same treatment is reused later in the same lesson for a backdoor dominant** (B♭7 → Cmaj7, measure 26) — confirming, from a second independent source, that "dominant two chord" and "backdoor dominant" are two chord-symbol contexts for the identical underlying sound. Full tune breakdown → [[🎵 JEROME KERN - Nobody Else But Me]]. → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Turnarounds]] § *Backdoor*, [[Altered Chords]] — Nathan Borton, [[The Jazz Standard That Turns Students Into Pro Players]]
- **"Tonic dominant" — a static, non-functioning dominant 7th chord treated as its own stable modal color rather than as tension wanting to resolve, the same "harmony stays still" logic this page documents applied to a single non-diatonic chord rather than a whole tune/section.** Named directly for a B♭7 sitting where a diatonic ii chord (Bbm7) would normally go in "Donna Lee" — flagged as "weird" because it doesn't belong to the key, then reframed: "this chord is not really... a normal five chord [that resolves]... instead it sits in its own sound and can be stable." Two scale choices given, generalized explicitly beyond this one chord ("whatever dominant chord we're playing"): (1) the chord's own dominant/bebop-dominant scale, or (2) the **melodic minor scale built on the dominant chord's own 5th** (worked example: F melodic minor over B♭7, since F is B♭7's 5th — the same "melodic minor off the 5th" source as the Lydian-dominant/altered-scale material on [[Scale Modes Reference]]). Confirmed as a portable concept, not a one-chord coinage — the same term recurs later in the same video for a backdoor dominant (G♭7) treated identically. → [[Altered Chords]], [[Scale Modes Reference]], [[Turnarounds]] § *Backdoor* — Nathan Borton, [[THIS Song Turns Students Into BEBOP Pros...]]
- **The "So What" quartal voicing, fully derived, plus a D Dorian fretboard-shape set for comping/improvising over it.** Bill Evans's signature comping chord for Miles Davis's "So What" (*Kind of Blue*, 1959) is a stack of three perfect 4ths with a major 3rd on top (E-A-D-G-B) — move the top note down an octave to the low E string at the same fret and it reorders into a genuine 5-note stack of pure perfect 4ths. The lead-sheet symbol Dm11 is functional-harmony vocabulary approximating the modal sound (derive it: Dm7 with the 5th replaced by the 11, a whole step up) — more accurate to say "playing in D Dorian" than "playing over Dm11." Fretboard application: 3-note quartal chords mapped through D Dorian on strings 2-3-4, usable as comping shapes, melodic-line arpeggios, or top-note melody targets. **A reharmonization trick that generalizes past this one tune:** harmonize the minor pentatonic scale in stacked 4ths (two 4ths below each scale tone) to color any minor chord, not just a Dorian-labeled one — this introduces a natural 6, i.e. treats the chord as Dorian by ear even when it isn't officially so, which the video frames as simply fine to do. Full worked shapes, the one-shape-eight-tertian-chords reinterpretation table, and both inversions of the pentatonic quartal shape on the new note [[Quartal Chord Voicings]]. → [[Quartal Chord Voicings]], [[Chord Voicings]] — Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared, [[Quartal Harmony - all about quartal chords on guitar]]
- **The characteristic-note table (Dorian's natural 6 vs. natural minor/Aeolian's ♭6) explains why the same minor-7 chord shape gives you a different upper-neighbor note depending on function, not quality.** Worked in a chromatic-enclosure exercise (chord tone → note above → chord tone → half step below → chord tone) applied to a chord's 5th: over a **ii-chord** minor 7 (Dorian — e.g. Cm7 as ii of B♭), the note above the 5th is a **whole step** (the natural 6th); over the same-shaped chord functioning as a **tonic minor** (Aeolian — e.g. the A-minor tonic chord in "Manhã de Carnaval"), the note above the 5th shrinks to a **half step** (the ♭6th). The chord symbol alone (Cm7) doesn't disambiguate — only knowing which mode the chord is actually drawn from (ii vs. i) does.
