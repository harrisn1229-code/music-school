> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **How fast the chords change, independent of tempo — loop production wants it slow**
> Slow means one or two chords across several bars, because the loop repeats dozens of times and fast changes keep pulling attention back to the harmony.
>
> **Q2.** **Predictability is the feature — rhythm, topline, timbre and arrangement take over**
> Once the ear stops tracking the harmony, after about two passes, the progression becomes a bed rather than a series of events.
>
> **Q3.** **A sustained bass note held while the chords above it change — an EDM build is a dominant pedal**
> Sitting on V and refusing to resolve, so the drop supplies the release.
>
> **Q4.** **`C – G/B – Am – F` — the ear takes the lowest note as the root**
> The bass descends C–B–A–F while the chords stay I–V–vi–IV, so changing the bass changes the perceived chord even though nothing above it moved.
>
> **Q5.** **Reharmonize the last bar · insert a secondary dominant · move the bass — the first is highest-leverage**
> Changing where the loop lands reshapes the whole cycle rather than one moment in it.
>
> **Q6.** **The 3rd — and a topline on the 9th adds colour without changing function**
> With the 3rd out of the way the pad stops competing with the topline over the chord's identity. The 9th isn't in the triad, so it floats rather than settling.
>
> **Q7.** **I–V–vi–IV · vi–IV–I–V · IV–I–V–vi · V–vi–IV–I — only which chord is heard as home**
> The pitch content is identical. Whichever chord the loop starts on and returns to becomes the tonic.
>
> **Q8.** **Relative major and minor — no pivot chord, no new material, no modulation machinery**
> The relative minor of C major is A minor. Same key signature, so the note pool never changes; only the resolution target does.
>
> **Q9.** **Mixolydian, or equivalently the parallel minor — it has no leading tone**
> Nothing pulls up into the tonic, so the progression moves without gravity. That absence is what "modal" sounds like.
>
> **Q10.** **B natural against B♭ — Dorian versus natural minor. The Aeolian cadence is ♭VI–♭VII–i**
> Dorian's raised 6th makes the chord on the 4th degree major, and that major IV under a minor tonic is the whole colour difference. The Aeolian cadence spelled out in A minor is F, G and Am, all diatonic to natural minor.
>
> **Q11.** **A chord a 3rd from the tonic with a quality the key doesn't provide**
> In C those are E, A, E♭ and A♭ major. The subtler alternatives to a truck driver are shifting the mode instead of the key, approaching the new tonic through its own dominant, or pivoting on a shared chord.
>
> **Q12.** **`A A A A / D D A A / E D A E` — I7, IV7, V7**
> Three four-bar phrases, with bar 12 as the turnaround back to the top. The three chords are A7, D7 and E7.
>
> **Q13.** **Because blues doesn't follow major-key functional harmony — the dominant 7th is the home sound**
> The ♭7 is colour rather than tension, so a I7 isn't a chord that needs resolving. It's where the music lives.
>
> **Q14.** **Quick change moves bar 2 to the IV · 8-bar blues is its own form · minor blues is i7 iv7 v7**
> The 8-bar form is real and separate rather than a truncation — "Key to the Highway". Minor blues often adds a ♭VI at bar 9, and the V frequently stays dominant because you still want the leading tone pulling home.
>
> **Q15.** **A figure in the last two bars, 11 and 12 — it usually lands on V**
> Its job is to break the resolution so the ear expects bar 1 again. Landing on the dominant leaves the form leaning forward instead of settling.
>
> **Q16.** **AAB — bars 1–4 state a line, 5–8 repeat it, 9–12 answer it**
> The repeat is what builds the expectation the third phrase pays off, and the space the form leaves is where the response lives.
>
> **Q17.** **The ♭3, ♭5 and ♭7 — and the ♭5 can never be a resting note**
> A blues scale: A C D E♭ E G. That's minor pentatonic plus the ♭5, which works as a passing tone only.
>
> **Q18.** **The friction is the sound · blues isn't functional major/minor · the note is a region, not a pitch**
> The grind between ♭3 and ♮3 is what "bluesy" means. The scale is imposed over the chords rather than derived from them. And players bend the ♭3 up toward the ♮3 and sit between them, so the blues has microtonality built in.
>
> **Q19.** **Rock took the form and the pentatonic vocabulary wholesale · hip hop inherited it through sampling**
> Early Beatles and Stones material is blues form with different production. Soul and funk records built on blues harmony got sampled, so the ♭3 and ♭7 vocabulary and the call-and-response phrasing survive in the loops even where the 12-bar form doesn't.
>
> **Q20.** **The melody's shape over time — strong means balanced, an arc with one clear peak**
> Independent of the actual pitches. What goes up comes back down, rather than drifting or sitting flat.
>
> **Q21.** **A short fragment used to generate much more music — repetition, sequence, inversion, diminution**
> *Also accepted:* interval contraction · interval expansion · augmentation · rhythmic metamorphosis · imitation
>
> **Q22.** **Root settled · 3rd colours it · 7th leaning · 9th open — landing on the root constantly is the reason**
> It agrees with the harmony on every downbeat, so nothing is ever held in tension long enough to want resolving.
>
> **Q23.** **Over changes the harmony supplies it · over a loop the melody must generate its own**
> Rhythm, contour and phrasing. Over changes you can hold one note and let the chords recontextualize it.
>
> **Q24.** **Where the chorus sits relative to the verse**
> Higher reads as more intense, and much of what makes a chorus feel like one is that it's above what came before. Moving it up a third often does more than new notes, so it's the cheapest thing to rule out.
>
> **Q25.** **Climb · shorten the note values · ascending sequence · withhold the tonic — or strip back instead**
> The counter-move is to empty everything right before the chorus so it arrives against space rather than against a wall, which is often better in loop-based production because the drop is the arrival.
>
> **Q26.** **Repetition · rhythmic distinctiveness · singable range and contour — tap it with no pitches**
> If the rhythm alone is still recognisable, it's rhythmically distinct. Repetition outranks everything else.
>
> **Q27.** **Change the rhythm · widen it · move where it sits against the chord — the order is the content**
> Everyone already knows the list. What's hard to know is that rhythm outranks pitch by a wide margin, and it's the fix people reach for last because pitch feels like where melody lives.
>
> **Q28.** Order of sharps: **F C G D A E B**
> Order of flats: **B E A D G C F** — the exact reverse.
>
> **Q29.** **E major** — F♯ C♯ G♯ D♯
> Four flats is **A♭ major** — B♭ E♭ A♭ D♭
>
> **Q30.** **F♭ = E.** Correct in **C♭ major**, whose fourth degree is F♭.
> *Also accepted:* A♭ minor · the ♭6 of A♭ minor
> A heptatonic scale uses each letter exactly once, so the fourth degree of C♭ major has to be written on the letter F. Its pitch is E natural, and E natural spelled as an F is F♭. The near miss worth knowing is G♭ major, which contains C♭ but not F♭ — its seventh degree is F natural, and it has six flats rather than seven.
>
> **Q31.** m2 → M7 · P5 → P4 · M3 → m6 · d5 → A4
> The numbers sum to 9 and the quality flips in every case.
>
> **Q32.** Perfect: **unison, 4th, 5th, octave.** Major or minor: **2nd, 3rd, 6th, 7th.**
>
> **Q33.** **Tonic** (C, Am, Em) · **Subdominant** (F, Dm) · **Dominant** (G, B°)
>
> **Q34.** **The plagal cadence**
> Gentler because IV contains no leading tone — there's no semitone pulling up to the tonic, so it settles rather than resolves. Hence its use in gospel and rock.
>
> **Q35.** **B and F** — B pulls up to C, F pulls down to E
> They're the 3rd and the 7th of the chord, and their instability is the engine of the cadence.
>
> **Q36.** **D7 (D F♯ A C)**, resolving to **G**
> It's the dominant of the dominant, which is why the F♯ appears — it's the leading tone of G rather than a note of C major.
>
> **Q37.** **Borrowed from the parallel minor — a borrowed chord, or modal interchange**
> It's the iv of C minor brought into C major. The ear accepts the parallel key's colour because the tonic hasn't moved.
>
> **Q38.** A harmonic minor: **A B C D E F G♯**
> Solves: the raised 7th gives minor a real leading tone and therefore a functioning V chord. Creates: an augmented 2nd between F and G♯.
>
> **Q39.** D Dorian: **D E F G A B C** — characteristic note the natural 6th, B
> E♭ Lydian: **E♭ F G A B♭ C D** — characteristic note the ♯4, A natural
>
> **Q40.** **For the rest of that measure only — and only on that exact line or space**
> The bar line cancels it, and it doesn't carry to the same letter in another octave. The octave half is the one that catches people.
>
> **Q41.** **Dotted half is 3 beats · double-dotted half is 3½**
> A dot adds half the note's own value, so 2 + 1 = 3. The second dot adds half of what the *first dot* added, so 2 + 1 + ½ = 3½. Not 4.
>
> **Q42.** **Back to the sign · play to the *To Coda* mark · then jump to the Coda**
> The standard misread is going straight to the Coda, which is why the chart scan checks the roadmap before a note is played.
>
> **Q43.** **It sounds an octave lower than written — effectively a permanent 8vb**
> Direction is the entire content of the fact, and the reverse is the common wrong answer. It's why guitar parts fit in treble clef at all.
>
> **Q44.** **Key and time signature · roadmap · range · rhythm hotspots · a holdable tempo**
> Steps 2 and 5 are the ones that save the read. The roadmap stops you playing into a repeat you didn't see, and picking a tempo you can hold through the hardest bar stops you outrunning yourself in bar 9.
>
> **Q45.** **6/8 counts eighths and is felt in two dotted-quarter beats · 12/8 is four**
> Divide the top number by 3 and the real beat count appears.
>
> **Q46.** **Emphasis where the meter doesn't expect it — and the meter has to stay in the listener's head**
> Three forms: accent a weak position, place a note on a weak position and tie it over the strong one, or rest where the arrival is expected. Remove the steady pulse and there's nothing to play against, so it just sounds arbitrary.
>
> **Q47.** **Boom-bap ~85–95, backbeat on 2 and 4, 8th hats · trap ~130–145 half-time, snare on 3, 16th hats**
> Boom-bap's hats are straight, swung and laid back, with an offbeat open hat and sampled drums. Trap layers 32nd and triplet rolls over a 16th bed.
>
> **Q48.** **Both describe the same collection — major with a ♭7 — reached from two directions**
> *Rubric (5):* says Mixolydian is the parallel major scale with a flattened 7th (1) · says a borrowed chord is a chord taken from the parallel mode over an unchanged tonic, and ♭VII is the chord built on the ♭7 of the parallel minor (2) · says parallel mode thinking makes the two descriptions the same operation, since a borrowed chord is just a triad built on an altered scale (2)
> The two readings only look like rivals if you take one to be about scales and the other about chords. Parallel mode thinking dissolves that. In parallel terms, C Mixolydian is C major with the 7th degree lowered, and the triad built on that lowered 7th is B♭ major. Borrowed-chord thinking arrives at the same B♭ by a different route: keep the tonic and take a chord from C minor, whose ♭7 also gives B♭. So the chord is identical and only the framing differs — the modal reading emphasises the collection the whole passage is drawing from, the borrowing reading emphasises a single import into an otherwise diatonic context. Which you use depends on how much of the passage is affected. One B♭ in an otherwise plain C major progression reads as borrowed. A whole loop built on I–♭VII–IV reads as Mixolydian, because the ♭7 is no longer an exception.
>
> **Q49.** **Take the 3rd out of the pad and put tension in the topline — the vocal defines the harmony**
> *Rubric (5):* says the pad instruction removes the 3rd so nothing competes with the topline over the chord's identity (2) · says the topline instruction targets 9ths and 7ths so the melody isn't merely restating the chord (2) · says following only one leaves either a pad and vocal fighting, or a line with no tension anywhere in it (1)
> They're two halves of a single decision about who states the harmony. The pad instruction clears the space: a triad under a busy vocal asserts major or minor on every beat, in the same register the topline is working in, and the two end up arguing about a question only one of them needs to answer. The topline instruction then fills that space with something other than agreement — a 9th or a 7th on the strong beats, so the line has somewhere to go instead of confirming what's already sounding. Do only the first and the pad is out of the way but the topline is still landing on roots, so the section is open and dull. Do only the second and the topline is finally doing something interesting while a triad underneath keeps contradicting it. Together they produce the loop-based ideal: harmony implied rather than stated, and the voice as the thing that decides it.
>
> **Q50.** **Blues imposes the collection from outside the chords, where modal thinking derives it from a root**
> *Rubric (5):* says parallel thinking builds a scale from the tonic by altering degrees, and derivative thinking takes a parent scale and starts on a different degree — both derive the notes from a harmonic relationship (2) · says blues imposes a scale over the chords, so the notes aren't derived from the harmony underneath at all (2) · says this is why the ♭3 over a dominant chord isn't a mistake, since the note was never meant to belong to the chord (1)
> Course 03 offers two ways of deriving a collection and both are derivations. Parallel thinking starts at the tonic and alters degrees — D minor with a raised 6th. Derivative thinking starts from a parent scale and relocates the tonic. Either way the notes are answerable to a harmonic frame. Blues doesn't work like that. The scale is laid over the changes as a fixed vocabulary, and it stays put while the chords move underneath it, so the same ♭3 sounds over a I7 with a natural 3rd in it. In derived thinking that's a contradiction — you have both the major and minor third of the chord sounding. In blues it's the point: the grind between the two is what "bluesy" means, and it's the third of the reasons the ♭3 isn't wrong. The other two follow from the same premise. The blues isn't functional major or minor, so there's no key-based rule being broken, and the ♭3 is a region rather than a pitch, bent up toward the ♮3 and left sitting between them.
>
> **Q51.** **Both exploit an expectation the listener is already holding**
> *Rubric (5):* says syncopation needs the meter present because displacement is only heard relative to an expected position (2) · says a rhythm rewrite works by moving notes off the positions the ear is anticipating (2) · says the pulse is what both depend on, so neither works if the underlying grid isn't clear (1)
> The connection is that rhythm is the parameter with a built-in reference. Pitch has to establish its own context before a note can sound surprising, but a listener is tracking a pulse from the first bar, which means every rhythmic placement is already being measured against an expectation before you do anything. Syncopation is the explicit use of that: put the accent where the meter doesn't expect it, and the displacement is heard as an event. A rhythm rewrite on a dull melody is exploiting exactly the same thing less formally — the line was circling even note values on the beat, agreeing with the grid on every attack, so nothing in it was ever unexpected. Displace the phrase, vary the note values, cut a rest into it, and the same pitches start producing events. Both also come with the same caveat: the pulse has to remain audible. Displacement against nothing is arbitrary, which is why the fix is to move notes relative to a clear grid rather than to loosen the grid.

> **Q52.** **One asks how fast the chords move; the other asks what the melody has to do about it**
> *Rubric (5):* says harmonic rhythm describes the rate of chord change (1) · says the loop-versus-changes distinction is about whether the harmony supplies motion to the topline (2) · says slowing the harmonic rhythm shifts the burden of motion onto the melody, so a topline written over faster changes tends to collapse over slower ones (2)
> They're one parameter seen from either side of the arrangement. Harmonic rhythm is the harmony's statement about itself: how often it changes. The loop-versus-changes distinction is the melody's problem given that statement: if the chords move, the topline can hold a note and let itself be recontextualized, and if they don't, it has to generate its own movement from rhythm, contour and phrasing. So they aren't two facts, they're a cause and its consequence. The prediction follows immediately. Take a topline written over a progression changing every bar and drop it onto the same chords held for four bars each, and it will go slack — not because anything about the line changed, but because the thing that was moving it slowed down. Nothing was wrong with the melody. Its motion was borrowed, and the loan was called in.
