# How many ways can you Reharmonize a II-V-I in C major? - Modernize your chord progressions

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8II1aRK8au8)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8II1aRK8au8)

## Synopsis

Jens Larsen takes one fixed ii–V–I in C major (Dm7 → G7 → Cmaj7) and works through an exceptionally long, systematic catalog of ways to substitute the V chord (and, further out, the whole cadence) — explicitly framed as a reference list for composing/reharmonizing a standard or for finding new soloing color, not a single "here's the trick" lesson. The video moves in three concentric rings, each further from home: (1) recolor G7 itself without changing the root — sus, harmonic-minor dominant, altered, diminished-scale dominant, whole-tone dominant; (2) replace G7 with a different chord entirely — tritone sub and its own sub-variants, the large "four minor" (borrowed-from-parallel-minor) subdominant family (Fm, backdoor B♭7, ♭VI major 7, the Neapolitan D♭maj7, phrygian-dominant G7sus4♭9), and the folk-flavored "five minor" chords (Gm, B♭); and (3) a generative meta-trick — once you've already substituted G7 with an altered-scale or diminished-scale color, reharmonize it AGAIN by pulling a different chord out of that same substitute scale (e.g. from G7 altered's own scale, sub in A♭m(maj7) or Bmaj7♯5 or B♭7sus4♭9 instead of a literal G7 chord), plus combining two "four minor" chords into new hybrids (D♭maj7 + B♭7 → B♭m; D♭maj7 turned major-quality → D♭maj7♯5). ## What's taught

**Ring 1 — recolor G7 without changing the root**
- **G7sus4** — same as Dm7/G or Fmaj7/G (no 3rd, so "not strong," but a useful color Larsen says he personally underuses). *(~1:00)*
- **G7 with a ♭9 and ♭13 but a natural 5th** — borrowed from harmonic minor (the raised leading tone of C harmonic minor sitting under G). *(~1:40)*
- **G7 altered** (♭9, ♯9, ♭5/♭13) — plus building it from **upper-structure triads out of the altered scale**: E♭ and D♭ major triads over the G bass give a vaguer, less-literally-spelled altered color while still carrying the alteration. Stated rule for telling the color families apart by ear: **altered = ♭9+♯9+♭5+♭13 together**; **harmonic-minor dominant = ♭9+♭13+natural 5**; **diminished-scale dominant = ♭9+13 together** (below) — pick one family and commit, because a bare "G7♭♭9" alone is ambiguous between all three. → [[Altered Chords]], [[Upper Structure Chords]], [[Guide Tones]]
- **G7 from the diminished (half-whole octatonic) scale** — characteristic sound is **♭9 + natural 13 together** (the most typical color); less-common extras available in the same scale: ♭5/♯11 and ♯9; also **a C minor triad voiced over the G bass** as an upper-structure reading of the same scale. → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Upper Structure Chords]]
- **Whole-tone dominant** — G7 built from the whole-tone scale, an unusual "only one chord quality in the whole scale" sound Larsen says he rarely reaches for; name-checks Billy Strayhorn and Wayne Shorter's "Juju" as tunes leaning on long whole-tone stretches. **Stated meta-point:** even though the root stays G through this whole first ring, it still counts as reharmonization — you're changing the chord's *sound* (extensions/alterations), not just its function, and that's a legitimate half of the technique. → [[Reharmonization]] § 1

**Ring 2 — replace G7 with a different chord (root change, cadence disguised)**
- **Tritone substitution: D♭7** — the D♭ Lydian dominant color is enharmonically identical to G7 altered (same melodic-minor source, "almost impossible to tell apart" as a soloing target). Framed explicitly as the natural first move once you want to disguise a cadence rather than just recolor it. → [[Chord Substitution]] § 2
- **Full secondary ii–V into the tritone sub**: A♭m7 → D♭7 → Cmaj7, keeping the original Dm7 in front as a longer chain (Dm7 → A♭m7 → D♭7 → Cmaj7). → [[Chord Substitution]] § 2, [[The ii-V-I]]
- **D♭7sus4** — the tritone sub played as a sus chord (loses the G that ties it back to the key, described as "going even further away"). → [[Suspended Chords]]
- **The "four minor" group** — subdominant chords borrowed from the parallel (C) minor key; Larsen names this his single most-used reharmonization tool, "safe" and "stable" despite feeling harmonically distant: → [[Borrowed Chords]], [[Chord Substitution]]
  - **Fm7** — most common voicing is Dm7 → Fm(7) → C, drawn from F melodic minor (the "four minor" dorian-flavored sound).
  - **Fm(maj7)** — same F melodic-minor source, a less stable/more colorful variant than the minor-6 or minor-7 readings.
  - **Fm7 from C natural minor/Aeolian** — a third source scale for the same chord symbol, different color.
  - **B♭7 (backdoor dominant)** — the second-most-common four-minor chord; usually voiced with a natural 13, sourced from F melodic minor as a Lydian dominant (non-resolving dominant, so it can carry ♯11/♭5). Variant: **B♭7sus4** in place of the plain reading.
  - **A♭maj7 (♭VI)** — bossa-nova-flavored, common in pop too. Variants: **A♭maj7♭5** (spicier); **A♭maj7♯5** (from F melodic minor's minor-major color — heard as a C major triad suspended over an A♭ bass); **A♭maj7♯9♯11 chords out of C harmonic minor** (an A♭ major triad with a G major triad stacked on top — ambiguous subdominant/dominant function).
  - **D♭maj7 — the Neapolitan subdominant** — F minor (the four-minor chord) voiced with its ♭6 (D♭) in the bass; jazz treats this bass-position chord directly as D♭maj7 in the key of C. Variant: **D♭maj7♭5**. → [[Neapolitan Sixth]]
  - **G7sus4♭9 ("Phrygian" dominant)** — from harmonic minor, played as a sus rather than a plain dominant; scale = G Phrygian, same notes as E♭ major/C natural minor. Called out as safe in most melodic contexts (only clashes if the melody carries a 9 or 3rd) and specifically good as **a pedal point for intros** for its "mysterious" quality. Variant with a natural 13, from F melodic minor. → [[Suspended Chords]]
- **"Five minor" chords (folk-flavored)** — the dominant played as a straight minor triad instead: **Gm**, then its relative-adjacent **B♭ major** (Gm's ♭III) — a pairing Larsen associates strongly with bossa nova. → [[Borrowed Chords]]

**Ring 3 — the generative meta-trick: reharmonize an already-substituted chord again**
- **Pull a second chord straight out of the same substitute scale you just used, and use THAT instead of literal G7.** Demonstrated three ways over G7 altered's own scale (G altered = A♭ melodic minor): **A♭m(maj7)** (works because it's harmonically adjacent to G7 in context), **Bmaj7♯5** (called out as an even more common reharmonization than the previous one), **B♭7sus4♭9** (very much a "melodic minor" flavored substitute). → [[Chord Substitution]], [[Altered Chords]]
- **Same trick applied to the tritone substitute's own scale**: instead of the plain D♭7, pull its **ii chord, A♭m**, or **Bmaj7** (heard as a chromatic approach climbing from A♭m up to Em — itself the upper-structure triad of Cmaj7) and use those in place of D♭7. → [[Chord Substitution]] § 2, [[Upper Structure Chords]]
- **Diminished-family cross-reference**: the diminished scale that contains G7 also contains B♭7 (already claimed by the four-minor family above) and D♭7 (the tritone sub) — all four dominants a minor 3rd apart share the same diminished scale. The one genuinely different member of that family is **E7 voiced as a 13♭9 diminished sound**, sometimes used to connect a plain E7 (III7 of C major) to G7 as a substitute pairing. → [[Diminished Chords]]
- **Combine two four-minor chords into a new hybrid.** D♭maj7 (the Neapolitan four-minor chord) + B♭7 (the backdoor four-minor chord) combine, by ear, into **B♭m** — voiced/heard as "a D♭ minor color sitting on top of B♭," classified and used as its own four-minor-type substitute. → [[Borrowed Chords]], [[Chord Substitution]]
- **Flip a "minor" four-minor chord to major quality.** Take D♭maj7 (four-minor / Neapolitan, minor-flavored via its ♭6-in-bass origin) and instead voice it as **D♭maj7♯5** — reframed as a "four MAJOR chord with a ♭6 in the bass" rather than a minor one; described as taking "slightly altered four-minor sounds" and testing what happens turning them major-quality. → [[Neapolitan Sixth]], [[Borrowed Chords]]
- **A deliberately vague ambiguous-resolution chord**: straight F major (the plain IV, not four-minor) voiced with the four-minor's ♭6 (A♭) underneath it in the bass — close in practice to a G/A♭7-type sound — described as intentionally vague, where "the lower level of the chord resolves one way and the higher level resolves another." Given as a closing example of the general exploratory method (try combining/altering substitutes and listen for what still works) rather than a fixed recipe. → [[Reharmonization]], [[Chord Substitution]]

**General framing points (apply across the whole catalog, not tied to one chord):**
- Even with 20+ options shown, Larsen states directly you should still master the plain, unsubstituted ii–V–I first — substitution is additive vocabulary, not a replacement for basic fluency. → [[The ii-V-I]]
- Everything demonstrated works as soloing vocabulary over a plain backing (playing the substitute sound in your head/lines) exactly as well as it works as a comping reharmonization — the catalog serves both uses.
- No fingerings, tab, or fret numbers given in this video (chord-symbol/scale-source catalog only, played but not diagrammed) — treat as a naming/theory reference, not a voicing-shape lesson.
