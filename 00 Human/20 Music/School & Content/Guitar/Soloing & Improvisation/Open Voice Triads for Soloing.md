# Open Voice Triads for Soloing

Part of [[Jazz Harmony]] and [[Chord Voicings]]. **"Open" here means *spread*, not *close-position*** —
different sense of the word from [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]] and [[Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords]], both of which use **close-position** triads (three adjacent scale-tones, one note
per string, no string skipped). This page's triads are the same three notes (root-3rd-5th, or an
inversion) but **spread across a wide interval with a string skip in the middle** — e.g. root and 5th a
tenth-ish apart with a skipped string, then the 3rd a further stretch up — which is what makes them
sound wide/harp-like and also what makes them awkward at first: the picking hand has to learn
string-skipping and large-interval jumps it wouldn't need for a close-position shape. Same harmonic
content as the close-position pages, different physical shape and a different sound.

## Two uses — chords vs. solos are different right-hand problems
Nate's source video is the soloing half of a two-part lesson; the companion (not yet in the vault) covers
using the same open-voice shapes **as chords/comping**. The concept (spread root-5th-3rd triad shapes)
is identical between the two uses — what's specific to soloing is the exercise list below, aimed at
training the picking hand to execute the string-skip cleanly at speed, not just grip it as a static chord.

## Exercise 1 — major and minor triads through their inversions
Pick one major triad and one minor triad in the working key and run each through **root position, 1st
inversion, 2nd inversion**, all in the open/spread voicing (not close-position). Worked in the key of F:
**B♭ major** (the IV chord) and **G minor** (the ii chord, i.e. [[The ii-V-I|the ii of this key's 251]]). Two things to notice while drilling this:
- **Every triad has more than one open-voice fingering for the same notes** — e.g. a B♭ major open
  triad can be played on one string set one way, or restrung/refingered a different way that lands the
  exact same three notes on a different part of the neck. Learn more than one shape per triad/inversion
  because a given melodic context will sometimes favor one fingering over the other.
- **The physical layout always has one note per string plus exactly one string skip somewhere** — that
  skip is the defining physical feature of an open-voice triad and the main technical thing being
  drilled here (vs. the no-skip, adjacent-string layout of a close-position triad).

## Exercise 2 — root-position open triads diatonically through the whole neck
Once single-triad inversions are comfortable, harmonize the **F major scale** in root-position open
triads and play the resulting seven triads consecutively up the entire neck, keeping the **same
spread/string-skip configuration** at every scale step (root, then a string-skip up to the 5th, then up
to the 3rd) rather than switching fingerings chord to chord — the point is neck overview, not tidiness
within one position. *(General starting point: this doesn't have to stay root-position-only — try the
same drill starting from an inversion, or with a different spread configuration, once the root-position
pass is solid.)*

## Which triads to use over each chord of a 251 — restates [[Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords]]'s four rules, worked live over F major's own 251
Practice progression throughout: **Gm7 → Gb7 (or C7 altered, same notes via tritone sub) → Fmaj7** — F
major's [[The ii-V-I|251]]. The triad-selection logic is the identical four-rule system already
documented on [[Upper Structure Triads from Seventh Chords]] (minor7 → major triad a minor 3rd above
root; dominant7 → diminished triad a major 3rd above root; major7 → minor triad a major 3rd above
root), demonstrated here as a *live worked application over one real progression* rather than derived
in the abstract, plus a genuinely new fifth source (the [[Diminished Chords|diminished scale]]) folded
in on the dominant chord:
- **On Gm7 (the ii):** the chord's own top three notes (3-5-7 = B♭-D-F) are a **B♭ major triad** —
  matches the rule exactly (minor7 → major triad a minor 3rd above the root). Add the 9th (A) and the
  chord's 9-3-5 stack reads as a **D minor triad**. Add the 11th (C, i.e. the chord's 4th degree) instead
  of the 9th and the resulting 11-3-5 stack reads as an **F major triad**, the tonic itself, usable as a
  color triad over the ii chord.
- **On Gb7 / C7 altered (the V, played via tritone substitution — same trick used on [[Chord Substitution]] and [[Passing Chords]]):** root-position Gb7's top three notes (3-5-♭7 = B♭-D♭-Fb/E)
  are a **B♭ diminished triad** — matches the rule (dominant7 → diminished triad a major 3rd above
  root). Add the 9th (A♭) for a **D♭ minor triad**; add the ♯11 (C) for an **E augmented triad** (the
  chord's altered color, [[Augmented Chords|augmented]] because the ♯11 sits a symmetric augmented-triad
  interval above the 3rd-7th stack).
- **On Fmaj7 (the I):** **F major** (the chord itself), **A minor** (the chord's own upper-structure
  triad — matches the rule, major7 → minor triad a major 3rd above root), and **C major** (the 5th-based
  triad) all work directly.
- **Extra dominant-chord source — triads out of the diminished scale, same device already documented on
  [[Diminished Chords]] and [[Scale Modes Reference]]:** over the V chord, borrow a major or minor triad
  a **minor 3rd away** from the diminished scale's symmetric triad family (already worked there as the
  B♭-D♭-E-G group) — worked here as **E♭ major** (minor 3rd up) and **A major** (minor 3rd down) as
  extra usable triads over the C7-altered sound, on top of the four-rule triads above.

## Four worked example lines over the 251 (Gm7 → Gb7/C7alt → Fmaj7)
Auto-captions on this stretch of the transcript are garbled in places (fast spoken run-through of licks
while playing) — settings/notes below are captured qualitatively where the transcript loses the exact
pitches; treat this section as a map of the *devices* used per line, not a note-for-note transcription.
- **Line 1:** opens on a **B♭ major open-voice triad**, runs down a **G minor** line, then an **E
  augmented / C augmented triad** (same symmetric triad, either name) over the dominant, then a **Gb7
  arpeggio in an inversion**, resolving into Fmaj7 with a shape built a **stacked 4th above the 9th**,
  then moving into what Larsen calls an **"A sus" triad sound** over the major 7 — a triad he likes
  specifically for major-7 color because it voices the chord's **3rd, 7th, and 13th** together, giving a
  richer major-7 sound than the plain triad.
- **Line 2:** a **D minor 7 arpeggio** with two notes encircling the 3rd (an [[Bebop Vocabulary |enclosure]]-style approach into the chord tone) resolving down through the scale, then **Gb major
  open-voice triads** into a **D♭/C♯ minor triad**, then on the Fmaj7 resolving to the **9th** via a
  **G sus** sound, then a **three-note-per-string pentatonic run** — aimed deliberately at covering a
  wide pitch range across the line (starts low, ends high).
- **Line 3:** **Gm7 arpeggio**, an **inversion of a D minor 7 arpeggio**, then on the C7 a **B♭ triad**
  moving into an **E♭ major triad**, encircling the **9th of Fmaj7**, and on the Fmaj7 itself a **D sus
  ("D7sus4-up-an-octave")**-flavored line — the [[Diminished Chords|octatonic/diminished]] sound used
  here specifically for the dominant portion of the line.
- **Line 4:** opens with what Larsen calls a **"minor comp pattern"** (a G minor idea), then an **F
  major triad** resolving its **11th down to the 3rd** (chosen because it works better melodically than
  resolving up), then on C7 the **diminished sound again** — an **E diminished triad** into an **A major
  triad reinterpreted as A minor** — and on Fmaj7 resolving first to the **major 7th**, then continuing
  up to the **9th**.

## Worked over a full tune: Out of Nowhere (G major7 / B♭m7-E♭7 / E7 altered)
A second worked application, over the opening changes of the standard **Out of Nowhere** rather than a static 251 vamp — see [[🎵 JOHNNY GREEN - Out of Nowhere]] for the tune's own harmonic analysis. Two new reusable principles come out of this pass that the F-major-251 lesson above doesn't cover:
- **Voicing-choice caution:** in an open-voice triad, one of the three notes sits much lower in register than the other two — that low note can accidentally read in context as a **new implied root** rather than as part of the intended triad, an unwanted side effect worth actively listening for when choosing which shape/inversion to grab, not just a technical detail.
- **General principle — closer-related triads first:** open-voice triads work better when they're **harmonically closer to the underlying chord**; save the more distant/outside upper-structure open triads for chords with more time to resolve them. Demonstrated directly: over the static **G major7**, open **B minor** (top of the chord) and open **D major** are the first choices, with **E minor** as a further option, including a leading-tone move (**B minor open → A major open**) and a smaller melodic addition (**E minor open with an added F♯** worked in as a single note rather than a second full triad) to keep the phrase reading as one balanced melody. Over the brief **B♭m7-E♭7**, the same **open B♭ minor triad works across both chords**, resolving via a close-position **F major triad**. Over **E7 altered**, the choices stay deliberately close to the chord — **F minor** (contains the 3rd), **D half-diminished**, and **A♭ major/A♭ augmented** — rather than reaching for a distant upper-structure shape on a fast-moving altered dominant.
- **Source of the melodic-resolution instinct, named directly:** classical repertoire — some Bach, some choral writing — is cited as the model for the "big leap, then step back down" shape that keeps a wide-interval open-voice line reading as a natural melody rather than an abstract interval study.
- **The angularity-vs-coherence principle, stated directly and worth carrying into any wide-interval triad work (open-voice or not):** the more/larger the intervals in a line, the more care is required to keep it reading as a real melody rather than "chaos" — judged by ear, by playing a phrase back multiple times, not derived from theory. **Mark Turner** is named as the model for controlled angularity that still makes complete melodic sense; **Kurt Rosenwinkel** is flagged (as an impression, not a stated fact) as having absorbed a lot of that specifically from Turner. Beginner guidance: start with shapes closer to the chord and resolve into a clear statement, rather than stacking leap after leap immediately.
- **A deliberate "surprise" use of an open-structured upper-structure triad, over the same G major7:** playing an **A major triad** (the Lydian upper structure) while leaving out the chord's own B-minor part reads as a strong effect specifically when it lands **after** a line that sounds like it's resolving normally (e.g. off an altered-dominant line pulling toward the tonic) — landing on the Lydian color instead of the expected plain tonic triad. Same device with the **Lydian augmented** sound resolving to a **B major triad**: "two elements still home, but the sharp 5 is really out." → [[Triad Melodic Devices for Jazz Soloing]] §7 for the full Lydian/Lydian-augmented/augmented-scale triad catalog this draws from.

## Practice philosophy: blend, don't isolate — and write your own
Two closing points Larsen makes explicitly, both reusable beyond this specific triad device:
- **Mix open triads into everything else you're already doing** (scales, arpeggios, other devices) —
  don't treat them as a separate mode you switch in and out of. The goal is a line that sounds like one
  vocabulary, not "now I'm doing the triad trick."
- **The real learning happens writing your own lines, not just replaying the examples above.** Playing
  someone else's worked lines is fine as a starting point, but the practice that actually installs the
  sound is composing your own short lines over the same 251 and testing them by ear — "does this sound
  good to me, does it work" — the same self-directed-composition instinct as [[Solo Analysis and Concept Extraction]]'s approach to internalizing vocabulary from transcription.
