# The Montgomery-Ward Bridge

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Jazz Harmony]]. Also called the **commercial bridge**. A stock **I7–IV7–II7–V7** used as the B section of a 32-bar AABA standard.

```progression-songs
```

Tin Pan Alley writers reached for it because it solves a specific problem cheaply: you have two A sections written and you need eight contrasting bars to finish the tune. This progression supplies them without inventing new material — it just walks the circle of fifths and hands you back to the last A.

The "Montgomery-Ward" name is the joke: a mail-order catalogue bridge. Off the shelf, works every time, nobody's proud of it.

## The shape

**I7 – IV7 – II7 – V7**, in C: `C7 – F7 – D7 – G7`, two bars each across eight bars.

Every chord is a **dominant 7**, which is the whole point — none of them is functioning as a stable tonic. In the key of C, `C7` is not home; it's V7-of-IV, already leaning. → [[Secondary Dominants]]

The expanded eight-bar version puts a ii in front of the last two dominants:

`v7 | I7 | IV | IV | vi m7 | II7 | ii m7 | V7`

which is the same skeleton with **ii–V pairs** substituted in — the standard dressing-up move. → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Chord Substitution]]

## Why it works: two fifth-pairs and a seam

The root motion is the reason this thing never sounds wrong, and it matters more here than the chord qualities do.

Take the four roots in C — **C, F, D, G** — and note that `D → G → C → F` is an unbroken chain of descending fifths. The progression plays that chain **starting from the middle**:

| Move | Interval | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| I → IV (C→F) | down a 5th | dominant resolution |
| **IV → II (F→D)** | **down a m3** | **the seam** |
| II → V (D→G) | down a 5th | V-of-V |
| V → I (G→C) | down a 5th | the cadence home |

**Three of the four moves are fifths.** The one that isn't — `IV → II` — is the seam where the chain restarts, and it's the only interesting decision in the whole progression.

Compare [[Turnarounds]]: the base turnaround `I–vi–ii–V` walks down in thirds *then* jumps a fourth. This one is the inverse — mostly fourths with a single third. Same destination, opposite texture.

## For bass

Fifth root motion is the strongest motion there is, which is why walking bass eats this progression alive. Three practical things:

- **Land the II7's major 3rd.** In C, that's the **F♯** over `D7`. It's the only note in the progression outside the key, and it *is* the sound of the bridge. Pass through it and the bridge sounds diatonic and dull; land on it and it announces itself.
- **The seam is yours.** `IV → II` is a minor-third drop with room in it. Chromatic approach from a half step either side, an octave leap, or a walk down `F – E – E♭ – D` — this is the one bar where the progression isn't telling you what to play.
- **Everything else can be root-and-fifth and still sound right.** The harmony is doing the work. This is a good progression to practice *restraint* over. → [[Root-Fifth Bass Lines and the 3-Step Style Rhythm Formula]], [[Chromatic Bass Lines]]

## Where to hear it

All five use it as the **bridge**, not the verse — that's characteristic:

- [[🎵 FATS WALLER - Honeysuckle Rose]] — the textbook case, bare `I7–IV7–II7–V7`
- [[🎵 DUKE ELLINGTON - Satin Doll]] — the same bridge with the companion ii chords added back in
- [[🎵 JIMMY MCHUGH - On the Sunny Side of the Street]]
- [[🎵 LARRY SHAY - When You're Smiling]]
- [[🎵 DOC DAUGHERTY - I'm Confessin' (That I Love You)]]

## Outside jazz

Rare as a loop, common as a *lift*. The progression's defining feature — a non-diatonic II chord a whole step above the tonic, approached from IV — is the same move behind a lot of pre-chorus writing that needs to raise the temperature before a chorus without changing key.

The diatonic softening, **I – IV – ii7 – V7** (minor ii instead of II7), loses the F♯ and with it most of the character. It's smoother, more neo-soul, and much less distinctive. Worth knowing as the alternative but it's a different progression emotionally. → [[Loop-Based Harmony]]
