# Dig It — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

Barely a song — a 50-second window cut out of an eight-minute jam, faded in at one end and out at the other, with a spoken tag lifted from a different arrangement recorded two days earlier. Pollack's angle is that the value here is **editorial rather than compositional**: the musical content is thin, the edit is what makes it a gesture, and it exists largely to set up the album's next track.

**Form.** Circular and undifferentiated — one four-bar pattern looping in pairs, with no sections to speak of. The fade-in does structural work: it announces you're hearing a slice of something much longer, and the circularity reinforces the illusion of open-ended duration. Pollack is explicit that this is **not** the complete-but-tiny "bonsai" form he assigns to "Wild Honey Pie" — those are whole songs in miniature, this is a crop.

**Harmony.** Three chords and nothing else: I – IV – V – IV. The V doesn't resolve down to I; it falls back to IV and the loop restarts, which is part of why it never closes. A vamp whose dominant retreats instead of resolving will run indefinitely without ever asking to end.

**Melody.** Pinned to the tonic note in talking-blues fashion — more chanted than sung — with intermittent ♭3 and ♭7 colouring against the major harmony.

**Arrangement.** A deliberately muddy everything-in-the-room backing track with organ, piano and drums out front. The released excerpt sits in a syncopated 3/4, though across the full take the meter drifts toward a 4/4 march. The spoken bit is spliced so its beginning overlaps the band's fade-out while its ending is left completely bare — the exposure is the point.

**Provenance.** The official track misrepresents it as a solo turn; the idea goes back to a January 1969 Twickenham improvisation over a twelve-bar vamp with names traded antiphonally.
