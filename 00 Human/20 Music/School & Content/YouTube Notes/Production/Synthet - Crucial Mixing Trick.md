## Synopsis

A panning-as-mixing-tool lesson: rather than reaching for volume first, placing elements at different points across the stereo field is framed as one of the most important parts of a good mix — demonstrated by comparing a backing track with panning vs. summed to mono (narrower, more crowded, competes harder for space in the center). Covers a stereo-to-mono utility knob for quick centering/width checks, automating width narrower going into a chorus for a "wide reveal" payoff on the drop, and a personal rule of keeping low bass and kick centered regardless of how much panning is used elsewhere.

## Techniques

- **Pan before reaching for volume to fix crowding** — placing elements at different points across the stereo field, even with small adjustments, does real separation work that volume alone can't — demoed A/B: the same backing track panned vs. summed to mono is narrower and forces more elements to compete in the center. → [[Width and Stereo]]
- **Pan backing vocals out from the center, subtly** — un-panned (centered) backing vocals vs. panned-out backing vocals is a subtle but real difference in how the vocal stack blends.
- **A stereo-width wheel/knob is a quick mono-check and centering tool** — a dedicated control (visible by adjusting the mixer's view setting if not shown by default) can instantly bring a stereo source to mono/center; useful both as a mono-compatibility check and as a creative automation target.
- **Automate width narrower approaching a chorus, for a "reveal" on the drop** — a common genre trick: progressively narrow the stereo image heading into a chorus so the full-width image at the chorus itself reads as a payoff/expansion rather than a static width throughout.
- **Keep low bass and kick centered as a personal default** — regardless of how aggressively other elements are panned, low bass/kick staying dead center is called out as the author's own habitual rule (not a hard law, but a strong default).
