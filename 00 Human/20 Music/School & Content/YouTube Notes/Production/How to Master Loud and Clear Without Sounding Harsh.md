# How to Master Loud and Clear Without Sounding Harsh

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cy-S6YmVe2U)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cy-S6YmVe2U)

## Synopsis

Another Sage Audio mastering-loudness explainer, covering the same general "louder without harshness" ground as the channel's other videos already in this vault ([[Limiting]], [[Master Chain]], [[Clipping and Loudness]]) but built around three specific mechanical explanations rather than a settings checklist. First: why pushing a signal into a brick-wall limiter ceiling risks two distinct distortions — inter-sample peaking (a signal so consistently loud it exceeds what the bit depth/sample rate combination can represent, even when channel meters show nothing wrong) and aliasing (distortion products that exceed the frequency ceiling a given sample rate allows for get reflected back down the spectrum as harsh, unpleasant artifacts) — and why oversampling plus look-ahead address both. Second: a clear before/after framing of low-level (upward) compression versus downward compression for loudness — upward compression amplifies the quiet parts while leaving peaks untouched, so the result is less dynamic but keeps its transients intact, and is a natural setup move for a transient-shaping pass afterward (an "expansion of just the peaks") to make kicks, snares, and other quick-transient sources even more distinct. Third, and the most genuinely new material for this vault: a worked derivation of *why* a limiter's release time floor sits around 50 ms, grounded in wavelength/period math rather than asserted as a rule of thumb — a frequency's period (the time for one full oscillation) is `1 ÷ frequency`, so 20 Hz (the bottom of human hearing) works out to a 50 ms period, and a release time shorter than that risks altering a wave's amplitude mid-cycle (audible distortion). This gives the vault's existing "50 ms sweet spot" release-time numbers (see [[How to Limit Your Master]], [[Pro-L 2]]) a mechanical *why* they didn't have before.

## Techniques

- **Inter-sample peaking, mechanically:** pushing a signal into a brick-wall ceiling can make it so consistently loud that it exceeds the maximum amplitude the session's bit depth *and* sample rate can accurately represent — even when the channel meters show no distortion at all. A lower sample rate encodes the signal less accurately, worsening this. Fix: increase the limiter's **oversampling** (more accurate quantization, reduces inter-sample-peak distortion) and increase **look-ahead** (gives the limiter more time to process/react before the peak arrives, reducing unwanted clipping). If neither is enough, true-peak limiting is the fallback — but it may reduce transient impact. (~0:50)
- **Aliasing distortion, mechanically — the "reflects down the spectrum" explanation.** At a low-enough session sample rate (44.1 kHz cited as the example), clipping/other distortion generated by pushing a signal hard can produce content that exceeds the frequency ceiling that sample rate is able to represent; when that happens, the excess content doesn't just disappear — it gets **reflected back down the frequency spectrum** as aliasing distortion, which "almost always has an unpleasant and harsh sound." Oversampling solves this the same way it solves inter-sample peaking, letting you push a master louder without the harshness. (~2:10)
- **Low-level (upward) compression vs. downward compression for loudness.** Mastering engineers commonly reach for downward compression first — control the dynamics so the overall level can be pushed up without clipping — but there's a better route to a louder master: low-level/upward compression captures the *quieter* parts of the signal and amplifies them while leaving the peaks at the same level. The detail gets pushed forward, so the overall signal reads louder, without the artifacts downward compression creates. (~3:00)
- **What you get after low-level compression, and how to build on it with transient shaping.** The result of low-level compression is a less dynamic mix/master, but — unlike heavy downward compression — one whose transients are *not truncated*. From there, transient shaping (described as "a special form of expansion" that captures and expands only the peaks of the signal) can make those transients even *more* distinct — called out specifically as ideal for increasing the clarity of **snares, kicks, and other instruments with a quick transient**. Sequenced together: low-level compression first for loudness/detail, transient shaping after to keep (or boost) the transient punch that would otherwise get lost. (~3:40)
- **Deriving the limiter release-time floor from wavelength/period math — the "why," not just a number.** A frequency's period (time for one complete oscillation) is `1 ÷ frequency`, converted to ms by ×1000. Worked example: 20 Hz — the bottom of human hearing — completes one oscillation in `1/20 = 0.05 s = 50 ms`; a 200 Hz wave completes one in 5 ms. If a limiter's release time is set *shorter* than the period of a frequency present in the signal (worked example: a 10 ms release against 20 Hz content), it can alter that wave's amplitude in the middle of its own cycle — which is what causes audible distortion. (~5:30)
- **The release-time floor and ceiling this derivation implies.** Set release time to **at least 50 ms** so every frequency within the range of human hearing (20 Hz–20 kHz) can complete its cycle without getting cut mid-wave. But don't go too long either: an overly long release keeps attenuating well past the peak that triggered it, so it starts compressing later transients/peaks that had nothing to do with the original trigger — read as a loss of definition and detail. The same underlying logic applies to attack time, but restates this vault's existing mechanical point (see [[Pro-L 2]]): on most limiters, the "attack" control isn't a real attack stage at all — attack is near-instant — it's actually the time *before the release envelope begins*. (~6:30)
