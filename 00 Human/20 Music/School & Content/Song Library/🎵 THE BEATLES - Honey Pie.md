# Honey Pie — The Beatles

## Pollack's analysis

Pollack has little patience for Paul's period pastiches but rates this one on craft: the harmonic vocabulary is genuinely that of twenties and early-thirties show and dance tunes rather than a cartoon of it — **♭VI, minor iv in a major key, secondary dominants and a tertiary dominant** all inside an eight-bar verse. The period tell is a **slow, separate-tempo intro whose words and music appear nowhere else**, something the Beatles otherwise barely attempted.

**Form.** An extended two-bridge model: long intro, three verses stacked between the bridges, then two more to close, with three verse sections mostly instrumental standing in for the dance breaks the genre expects. The song has **no outro** — it simply stops on the downbeat where another verse would have begun. The 12-bar intro is AAB, the pattern camouflaged by the half-spoken delivery of its second A. Verse and bridge are both 8 bars, but the verse is four short phrases and the bridge is two long ones.

**Harmony.** The **♭VI chord works in both directions inside one verse**: rising, as an appoggiatura chord shoving up into the tonic; falling, as the approach to V ([[HAR-C08 — Borrowed chords and modal interchange]]). Same chord, two functions, no extra material. The middle of the verse is a four-step walk around the **circle of fifths**, period cliché used on purpose ([[HAR-C07 — Secondary dominants]]). The bridge is a bassline exercise ([[POP-C04 — Four chords with a moving bass]]): the line puts most of the chords in inversion and gives the section an unusually high centre of gravity — play it in root position and it collapses. Where a verse leads into a bridge, its last bar is rewritten so a single chromatic voice stitches the two sections together and the seam disappears.

**Melody.** Wide range, worked in a jumpy triadic style that flatters the voice rather than a stepwise crooner line.

**Arrangement.** A stylised stage-band fox-trot pushed far enough to read as surreal. Saxophones enter tight and high, spend most of the song on background wash or answering phrases, and only get the actual tune in the final verse. The intro's second phrase runs through a megaphone/worn-78 effect — obvious now, technically adventurous in 1968.
