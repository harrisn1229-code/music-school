# Georgia on My Mind Fingerstyle Guitar Tutorial (With Sheet Music & Tab)

## Synopsis

Jared Borowski (Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared) walks measure-by-measure through his own **solo fingerstyle arrangement of "Georgia on My Mind," transposed to the key of C**, teaching it "as if we're sitting in a room together" — notation/tab call-outs plus fretboard-level fingering commentary. It's a from-scratch chord-melody arrangement (melody on top, harmony and a moving bass filled in underneath, one guitarist covering all three parts), and Jared repeatedly narrates the *why* behind each choice, not just the *what*: which finger to leave up so it's free for the next reach, when to sacrifice a harmony note versus the melody, how to mute a bass note cleanly, how to shift position smoothly without an audible bump. He explicitly withholds the chord symbols from the printed sheet music ("it would just muck up the sheet music too much") and instead points listeners to a companion video — **[[Georgia on My Mind Chords (Guitar Lesson) - Easiest way to play the REAL chords|"Georgia on My Mind Chords (Guitar Lesson)"]]** (same channel) — to learn the harmony on its own first. The tune's own origin story as *his* arrangement is folded in at the end: he transposed it to C, learned the chords, sang the melody over them, and the fingerstyle arrangement "came to life on its own" from that process — offered as a general song-internalization strategy, not just a one-off anecdote. Free sheet music/tab is available via his site (not fetched here — external resource). No production/mixing content — pure solo-guitar arranging and execution technique.

## What's taught

- **Learning-strategy backstory: transpose every new song to the key of C as a personal study habit**, independent of what key the guitar arrangement ends up landing in — for solo-guitar arranging specifically, the key that's chosen is whichever "sits best in standard tuning" based on where the resulting bass movements fall on the open strings, so C for this tune isn't arbitrary, it's the key where the walking-bass shapes below work in open position. *(~2:09–2:31)* → [[🎵 HOAGY CARMICHAEL - Georgia on My Mind (Guitar)]]
- **Core fretting-hand efficiency rule, stated as general advice for all guitar playing: never fret a note you aren't using.** Demonstrated on the opening C chord — no middle finger down even though a "familiar" open-C shape would include it, because the fingered note isn't part of what's actually being played; the finger goes down only the instant it's needed. *(~2:31–2:59)* → [[🎵 HOAGY CARMICHAEL - Georgia on My Mind (Guitar)]]
- **Opening move: an alternating-bass, Travis-picking/Chet-Atkins-style texture** — pluck the C chord (strings 5-3-2-1), fourth finger up to the 3rd fret, then the picking-hand **thumb** plucks fret 2 string 4, producing a brief alternating-bass feel before the walking line continues. *(~2:59–3:24)* → [[Fingerstyle Thumb Independence]]
- **A chromatic walking bassline is the actual harmonic glue between chords**, not a fixed chord shape — moving from C to E major, the bass descends chromatically and, at one point, there's *no chord at all*, just a bass note (D) carrying the harmony's motion; a chromatic B♭ passing tone is used similarly between other chords, described explicitly as "outside the key," a quick passing tone. *(~3:24–5:07)*
- **Keep melody and bass genuinely independent voices** — described as the defining trait of "great solo guitar playing, fingerstyle and classical guitar": the two lines move on separate physical/rhythmic timelines rather than locking together, executed here with a wrist rotation to reach a pinky note (D, 3rd fret 2nd string) while a first-finger note keeps holding elsewhere. *(~5:07–6:17)* → [[Fingerstyle Thumb Independence]]
- **Priority order when a stretch is physically impossible: melody first, harmony second, bass last.** If a chord voicing can't be fully held because of a stretch, let a harmony note cut off before letting the melody cut off, and try not to sacrifice the bass note either — the sound "will still really sound like the song" as long as the melody survives. Also: a melody note ringing slightly short is acceptable as long as it isn't "audibly weird" against the familiar tune. *(~6:17–7:43)* → [[Classical Guitar Expression & Muting]], [[Chord Melody Arranging]]
- **Classical picking-hand finger naming, p-i-m-a-c, explained via its Spanish origin** (pulgar/thumb, índice/index, medio/middle, anular/ring, meñique/pinky) — on an E minor 7 voiced entirely on open strings (all-zero tab), a "c" printed above the top note marks the pinky, which is why the letters don't map 1:1 onto English finger names even though index/middle happen to line up. *(~7:43–8:19)* → [[Reading Notation on Guitar]]
- **When a pinky-heavy voicing is too hard, drop a middle note, never the melody and (usually) not the bass** — the concrete version of the sacrifice-order rule above, applied specifically to voicings that ask for all five picking-hand digits (the E minor 7 and a later A7♭9 both use the pinky on top). *(~8:19–9:27)*
- **A7(♭9)-type voicing: root is optional, melody isn't** — when the chord needs a bar plus the pinky for the melody note, the root can be dropped entirely if the stretch is too much; the melody note stays non-negotiable. *(~8:54–9:27)* 
- **First-ending shape (D minor 7, open-D root, 5th-fret upper notes) taught as a genuine shape-practice opportunity** if it's unfamiliar — three-finger voicing (fingers 2-4-3), reached by shifting the already-in-place middle finger up to fret 5 rather than resetting the whole hand. *(~9:27–10:10)*
- **"Free finger" planning: leave whichever finger you'll need next unassigned rather than parking it on a note it doesn't have to hold**, illustrated moving into a G13 voicing — the first finger is deliberately left free to reach the G root, at the cost of a slightly awkward jump for the second finger elsewhere. Also: **strumming a voicing (instead of fingerpicking it) is an acceptable substitution as long as any unwanted open string gets muted first** — demonstrated on this same G13 shape, where the open A string must be damped if you strum rather than pick around it. *(~10:10–11:00)*
- **Thumb-side muting of the low E while playing an A root** — to avoid muddying an A-rooted chord, the picking-hand thumb touches/damps the low E string with the side of the thumb at the moment it plucks the A, rather than leaving the E to ring underneath. A second, distinct bass-muting technique from the "thumb-hop" method already documented on [[Classical Guitar Expression & Muting]]. *(~11:24–11:49)* → [[Classical Guitar Expression & Muting]]
- **"Rearticulating" a chord to fake a sustained note you can't actually hold** — over the song's signature "Georgia... on my mind" hook, the arrangement has to re-strike a chord (the "haunting," out-of-key neighbor chord under "mind, mind, mind") multiple times purely because the fingers need to move, even though ideally it would ring once and sustain; Jared reframes this explicitly as a musical device rather than a limitation — "like a background singer, an orchestra... rearticulating something," an ornamentation behind the melody rather than a flaw to hide. *(~13:59–14:29)* → [[Classical Guitar Expression & Muting]]
- **"Tracking" a position shift: keep a fretting-hand finger (here, the pinky) lightly touching the string — not pressed down — while sliding it to the new fret/position**, so the hand arrives already anchored instead of jumping blind. Flagged explicitly as "an awesome little pro tip for guitarists changing positions." Used moving a pinky from fret 3 up to fret 8 on the same string ahead of a position change into the B section's tail. *(~17:05–17:54)* → [[Classical Guitar Expression & Muting]]
- **Notation reading conventions for position/barre markings, confirming and extending what's already documented on [[Reading Notation on Guitar]]:** a Roman numeral preceded by "C" (e.g. C1, "half C5") means barre at that fret; a bracket over specific strings (not always the literal top or bottom half) shows exactly which strings the partial barre covers rather than assuming a clean half; a Roman numeral **without** a C/B prefix (e.g. "V2" = position 7 in the video's example) is a pure position marker, not an instruction to barre — included in notation specifically when the same chord shape is playable in more than one neck position, so the reader knows which one the tab assumes. *(~19:41–21:17)* → [[Reading Notation on Guitar]]
- **Song structure: first/second-ending repeat over the A section (measures 1–5, twice), then a B section, then DC al Coda** — play through to a "to Coda" marker placed after measure 5 on the repeat, then jump straight to the Coda symbol at the bottom rather than replaying the whole form. *(~11:49–12:59, ~21:29–22:13)*
- **Final cadence into the Coda is a genuine ii–V voice-leading move** ("a 2-5... true voice leading"), fingered as a smooth single-finger-at-a-time shape change (drop finger 4, shift finger 2, lift finger 1) rather than a hand reset — praised by Jared as one of his favorite moments in the arrangement. *(~21:53–22:13)*
- **Explicit companion-video routing: learn the chords on their own first.** Jared points viewers to his separate [[Georgia on My Mind Chords (Guitar Lesson) - Easiest way to play the REAL chords|"Georgia on My Mind Chords (Guitar Lesson)"]] video and recommends strumming/singing the plain changes (or having someone else comp while you sing the melody) *before* tackling this arrangement, so the harmony is already internalized rather than being learned for the first time inside a technically demanding chord-melody piece. *(~22:38–23:21)*
- **Arrangement origin story, offered as a transferable song-learning method:** transpose the tune to C → learn/play the plain chords → sing the melody over them to internalize it → let a fuller solo arrangement "come to life" out of that process, rather than sitting down to compose an arrangement cold. Jared frames the two skills (song fluency and arrangement fluency) as mutually reinforcing. *(~23:26–24:38)* → [[Internalizing a Tune Through Repetition]]
