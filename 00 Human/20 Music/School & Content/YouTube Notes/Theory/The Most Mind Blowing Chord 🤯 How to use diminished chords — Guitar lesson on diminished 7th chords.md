# The Most Mind Blowing Chord 🤯 How to use diminished chords | Guitar lesson on diminished 7th chords

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ENTwuGimE1E)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ENTwuGimE1E)

## Synopsis
Jared Borkowski's complete guitar-side tutorial on diminished chords, run as a deliberate seven-part build: define the three diminished chord types and where they come from, learn the three physical dim7 shapes on guitar, understand why those three shapes silently give you all their inversions for free, use dim7 as a chromatic/secondary chord to add tension, use it as the much more common *passing* chord between two diatonic chords, use it to modulate to genuinely distant keys, and finally watch Jared use all of that live in free improvisation. The throughline the whole video keeps returning to is the diminished 7th chord's **structural symmetry** (three stacked minor 3rds, a fourth returns to the root) — every technique in the video, from the free-inversion trick to the four-way modulation "wormhole," is a direct consequence of that one structural fact. Strongest material for Nate: the applied/secondary-diminished-chord framing (a direct diminished-family cousin of [[Secondary Dominants]]), the concrete chromatic-passing-chord drill (run it through a key on three different string sets), and the worked "most distant key" modulation demo, which is the same symmetric-inversion mechanic already on [[Diminished Chords]] from Jacob Collier's video, now shown as a live worked example instead of just stated as a rule.

## What's taught
- **Diminished triad = root + ♭3 + ♭5, built from stacking two minor thirds** — e.g. off C: C (root) – E♭ (♭3, minor 3rd up) – G♭ (♭5, another minor 3rd up); it's the vii° triad that occurs naturally as the 7th-degree chord of every major key (demonstrated via his own "Chords with Color" chart walking C major's diatonic triads: C – Dm – Em – F – G – Am – B°). [~1:56] → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Chords by Key]]
- **Diminished 7th ("fully diminished") stacks a THIRD minor 3rd on top of the triad** — root–♭3–♭5–𝄫7 (double-flat-7), a perfectly symmetric chord: three consecutive minor-3rd jumps, and a fourth minor 3rd lands back on the root an octave up. The odd "double-flat-seventh" label exists because that note is the chord naturally built off the 7th degree of the harmonic minor scale — counted straight off the diminished chord's own root (1-2-3-4-5-6-7) the note lands on the 7th scale step, but harmonic minor already has a flat-6, so it can't be called a natural or flat 6 — it has to be some flavor of "7," and since it sits a half step below the already-flat 7, it's "double-flat 7." As an *interval* this same distance is called a **diminished 7th**, which is where the chord's name actually comes from — it contains both a diminished-5th (from the triad) and a diminished-7th interval. [~3:44] → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Seventh Chords]]
- **Half-diminished (m7♭5) has only ONE of the two "diminished" intervals** — root–♭3–♭5–♭7, a REGULAR flat-7 (not double-flat). "Fully/full diminished" (the dim7) has TWO diminished intervals in it (the ♭5 diminished-5th AND the 𝄫7 diminished-7th); half-diminished keeps just the ♭5 and swaps in an ordinary ♭7 — hence "half." A clean naming mnemonic worth having on the reference note. [~9:22] → [[Diminished Chords]]
- **Minor-third arpeggio drill, single-string then cross-string** — climb the dim7 arpeggio in pure minor thirds (3 frets each hop) along one string first, to see/feel the symmetry directly; then repeat crossing strings freely (whatever minor-third shape is convenient string to string) from the lowest reachable note up to the highest and back down. This is literally playing a dim7 arpeggio end to end and doubles as fretboard-mapping practice. [~2:00] → [[Chord Voicings]]
- **Song example — "How Insensitive" (Antonio Carlos Jobim, bossa nova)** opens D minor (i) → C♯dim7, which is literally i → vii°7-of-harmonic-minor (the dim7 built off the 7th degree of D harmonic minor); loop those two chords and solo with the D harmonic minor scale over them to hear the relationship directly. Bossa nova as a genre leans heavily on dim7 chords generally. [~7:44] → [[Diminished Chords]]
- **Three physical dim7 shapes on guitar, all practical/fingertip-friendly** — (1) 6th-string-rooted, A-string skipped, playable either barred (1st finger bar + 3rd finger) or fully with fingertips (3rd, 1st, 4th, 2nd fingers); (2) 5th-string-rooted, all fingertips, an easy "box" shape; (3) a higher voicing off the same root, also all fingertips. Practice: rotate through all three off the same root, then move the root around the neck. [~10:43] → [[Chord Voicings]]
- **The symmetry gives every inversion for free — no new fingerings to learn** — contrast with a normal chord (his example: major 7), where getting 1st/2nd-inversion voicings means learning genuinely new, harder shapes each time. A dim7 is stacked minor 3rds all the way up, so sliding ANY of the three shapes above up exactly 3 frets (one minor 3rd) produces the exact same four notes reordered — an automatic inversion with zero new fingering. Practice: walk each of the three shapes up in minor thirds, crossing strings the same way as the earlier single-note drill. Cited as the sound behind a lot of "old movie soundtrack" scoring, heard in a Julian Lage/Bill Frisell record moment, and used exactly this way (walking the shape up before resolving) in **"Michelle" (The Beatles)**. [~13:52] → [[Chord Voicings]], [[Diminished Chords]]
- **Universal resolution rule — track your lowest note; it resolves up a half step or down a whole step to a major or minor chord** — because of the symmetry, whichever note you're tracking in the shape (any of the four is functionally equivalent depending on inversion) can resolve UP a half step OR DOWN a whole step into a real chord. The simplest practical approach is to just watch your lowest note and apply that rule. This one fact is the mechanism behind every technique that follows — applied/secondary diminished chords, passing chords, and the modulation "wormhole." [~19:00] → [[Diminished Chords]]
- **Applied/secondary diminished chord ("secondary diminished 7th")** — same logic as a [[Secondary Dominants|secondary dominant]] (V7/x), but built from a dim7 instead of a dominant 7th: place a dim7 a half step below (or whole step above) ANY diatonic chord in the key to tonicize/target it for a moment. Worked example: a plain I–vi loop (his "playing Hallelujah" example, C–Am) gets an applied dim7 dropped in front of the vi chord — either by stretching the progression out (a genuine new chord/beat added) or by squeezing it in as a one-beat passing tone right before the target, leaving the underlying progression length unchanged. [~19:39] → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Secondary Dominants]]
- **Most common real-world use: chromatic diminished PASSING chords** — connect two diatonic chords that sit a whole step apart by inserting a dim7 built a half step above the first chord's root, pointing at the second (e.g. in C major, C → D minor gets a passing C♯dim7 in between). Where two diatonic chords are already a half step apart (e.g. iii→IV in a major key) no passing chord is needed — they're already adjacent. Recommended practice routine: run this through an entire key three separate times, once each on the 6th-string-rooted, 5th-string-rooted, and 4th-string-rooted dim7 shapes — for every diatonic chord pair a whole step apart, insert the connecting dim7 a half step above the first. Song examples: **"Till There Was You"** (I, passing dim7, ii); **"Blackbird" (The Beatles)** stacks two of these back to back — IV, passing dim7, V, passing dim7, vi. [~21:39] → [[Diminished Chords]]
- **Tonicizing vs. modulating — the applied/passing dim7 use above is tonicizing, not modulating.** Tonicizing = briefly making a NON-tonic diatonic chord feel like "home" for a beat, then continuing in the same key (you land on the ii or vi chord and it's clearly a passing moment, not a new key). True modulation is a real, sustained key change — the next technique. [~23:48] → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Modulation (Key Change)]]
- **The dim7 as a modulation "wormhole" to genuinely distant keys — worked live demo.** Starting in C major, target the MOST distant key on the circle of fifths (F♯, directly opposite C): start on the dim7 that sits a half step below C (which would normally just resolve straight back to C) and instead resolve it to F♯/G♭ by choosing a different one of its four symmetric "exits." Same trick demonstrated resolving to B major instead of the "expected" ii-chord target. Practical takeaway: land on any dim7, pick which of its notes you're treating as the resolving tone and which direction (½ step up / whole step down) to send it, and you can land smoothly in a genuinely distant key; once there, sit in the new key for a while (a section, an improvised passage) before finding your way back. This is the same symmetric-inversion mechanic already documented on [[Diminished Chords]] from Jacob Collier's "four-way crossroads" video, and on [[Modulation (Key Change)]]'s enharmonic-modulation section — this video shows it worked live rather than just stating the principle, and confirms the "any of the four notes → any of 4 destination keys" math from the ground up. [~24:46] → [[Diminished Chords]], [[Modulation (Key Change)]], [[Circle of Fifths]]
- **Personal improvisation habit: use the dim7 as a constant harmonic pivot, not tied to a fixed key** — describes his own free-improvisation approach as thinking in real-time functional harmony: on any chord, ask what it could resolve to and where it could go, without needing to already be "in" a key first; the dim7 is his most-used tool for this because it can point almost anywhere. Also called out as genuinely practical on **background-music/functional gigs** (weddings, restaurant sets, repertoire sets) — using a dim7 to bridge smoothly from whatever key/tempo you just finished into the next tune without stopping to reset. [~27:17] → [[Diminished Chords]]
- **Confirms the dim7-as-rootless-7♭9 relationship from the performer's-eye side** — notes (pointing to his own separate video rather than re-deriving it) that these same three dim7 shapes double as rootless voicings of dominant-7♭9 chords. This matches and reinforces [[Diminished Chords]]'s existing Joe Pass-sourced content on the reverse framing (a 7♭9 chord = a dim7 with an added root a major 3rd below). → [[Diminished Chords]]
