## Why some intervals sound consonant (the overtone series)
A single note is never just its fundamental pitch — it carries a stack of quieter overtones above it, each a mathematically related multiple of the fundamental frequency. Playing the fundamental against successive overtones shows a clear consonance gradient, and it's the physical basis for why some intervals feel "natural" and others feel tense:
- **Octave** (2× the fundamental) — the most consonant possible relationship; the two notes' overtones align almost perfectly.
- **Perfect 5th** (3× the fundamental, octave-reduced) — the next-most natural interval; together with the octave, it's the foundation of ancient and Western tonal music.
- **Major 3rd** (5× the fundamental) — still resonant, but audibly less locked-in than the octave/5th.
- **Minor 7th** (7× the fundamental) — the overtone series' own natural 7th is already a bit flat of the tempered minor 7th; starts to read as dissonant/tense.
- **Major 9th** (9× the fundamental) — further out in the series, more dissonant still, especially when voiced close (down an octave, sitting right next to the root).
The farther an interval sits from the fundamental in its own overtone series, the more the two notes' overtone stacks stop lining up — which is the acoustic root of the consonance/dissonance spectrum used throughout [[Chords]] and [[Chord Progressions]]. → You Suck at Producing, [You Suck at Music Theory No. 2: Creating Tension](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9eTRtWcuzWc)

## Interval quality: Perfect/Major/Minor/Augmented/Diminished
- **Counting the number** — count scale degrees (lines/spaces) from the bottom note as 1, ignoring accidentals, up to the top note: a 3rd, a 6th, etc.
- **Perfect intervals** — unisons, 4ths, 5ths, and octaves are "perfect" when built from only the white keys, **except** F–B (that pair is a half-step off from every other 4th/5th — it's the tritone, aug 4th/dim 5th). A half-step larger than perfect = augmented; a half-step smaller = diminished. There's no such thing as a diminished unison.
- **Imperfect intervals** — 2nds, 3rds, 6ths, 7ths are never "perfect," only major or minor (still augmented/diminished a half-step beyond that). Shortcut: an interval is **major** if the top note is in the bottom note's major key; **minor** if the bottom note is in the top note's major key. Work it out with accidentals stripped first, then re-add them one at a time and track how each shift changes the size.
- **The Rule of Nines** — inverting an interval (moving the lower note up an octave above the upper) swaps its number to whatever completes a sum of 9: **2nd↔7th, 3rd↔6th, 4th↔5th**. Quality flips too: major↔minor, augmented↔diminished, perfect stays perfect.
— Toby W. Rush, [[Music Theory for Musicians and Normal People - Enrichment|Music Theory for Musicians and Normal People]], p.10–13

- **Cents as the underlying unit behind "half step," plus a microtonal aside.** Every semitone in 12-tone equal temperament equals 100 cents (most guitar tuners display offset in cents when tuning); the half-step/100-cent semitone is the smallest interval in the standard diatonic system but not the smallest possible one — some ethnic scales use microtonal intervals below a half step, playable on a guitar with custom-placed frets. — QJamTracks, [[INTERVALS for Guitar –crystal clear and ultimate guide]]
- **A worked audio example of inversion, generalizing the Rule of Nines above to consonance/dissonance and quality.** Take a C5 power chord (root C low, fifth G high) and flip it — C on top of G instead — and the interval becomes G→C, a perfect 4th (7 half steps read the other way). Demonstrated across pairs: **a consonant interval always inverts to another consonant interval, a dissonant one to another dissonant one** (major 2nd C→D, dissonant, inverts to minor 7th D→C, also dissonant); **quality flips** (major↔minor, augmented↔diminished; perfect stays perfect); the **augmented 4th/tritone is its own inversion** — unique among intervals, because it splits the octave exactly in half. Practical takeaway: an inverted pair (e.g. minor 6th E→C and major 3rd C→E) can serve the same musical purpose at a different width, useful for voicing the "same" harmonic idea narrower or wider. — QJamTracks, [[INTERVALS for Guitar –crystal clear and ultimate guide]]
- **Per-interval sound-character and practical-use pairings, all played from one root, rather than treating intervals as abstract distances.** Minor 2nd: most dissonant two-note interval playable (short of microtonal bends) — dark/tension riffs. Major 2nd: less dissonant, and **the same pitch as a 9th when stacked in a higher octave on a 7th chord** — the plain-interval vs. chord-tension distinction is purely octave placement, not a different note. Minor/major 3rd: the interval that makes a triad read as minor vs. major. Perfect 4th: substitutes for the 3rd to build a **sus4** chord (major 2nd does the same for **sus2**). Tritone (aug4/dim5): maximally dissonant, common in metal for tension, resolves by stepping to the 4th or 5th. Minor 6th shares the minor 3rd's "sad" color; **major 6th shares the major 3rd's "happy" color but, stacked on a minor triad's root, is specifically the note that turns natural minor into Dorian** — the defining color-tone move for that mode. Minor 7th/major 7th mainly appear stacked on a triad to build "jazz" 7th chords. — BERNTH, [Ultimate Beginner's Guide To Intervals On The Guitar!](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6ftJebnX-MI)
- **The same octave/perfect-5th consonance this note's overtone-series section derives from one note's own harmonic content, reframed as a direct frequency ratio between two different notes.** Where the section above frames the perfect 5th as 3× a single note's fundamental (octave-reduced), this bass-focused lesson frames it as a **3:2 ratio between root and fifth** — one note vibrating exactly 50% faster than the other — landing at "the sweet spot" between maximally-stable simple ratios (1:1 unison, 2:1 octave) and increasingly dissonant complex ones. - **Generic vs. specific intervals, retaught bass-first, plus the bass-specific shortcut of playing intervals as fretboard shapes instead of naming them.** Generic intervals count letter-name distance only (A→B = a 2nd, A→C = a 3rd… A→A = an octave) — matches this note's number-counting method above. Specific intervals add the half-step count + quality on top (A→C = 3 half-steps = minor 3rd; A→C♯ = 4 half-steps = major 3rd; A→E = perfect 5th; A→E♭ = diminished 5th) — same quality-naming rules as this page's table/rules above. **The bass-specific reframe: any interval played on the bass makes a fixed visual pattern — a specific number of frets and strings apart — so the neck itself is an interval ruler and a bassist can navigate by shape/pattern recognition without ever learning the interval names.** Named example: the **"box shape"** for a perfect-fifth/minor-third/major-second cluster, described as used by "millions of bassists who don't know any interval names" — stated as a legitimate, common alternative route, not a shortcut for beginners only; even many working bassists navigate this way rather than by interval ID. → [[Chords]] (the same shape-over-names logic applied to triads/arpeggios), [[Scales]] (the analogous movable box shape for the major scale) — BassBuzz, [[Music Theory for Bass]]
- **Deriving a chord shape's own interval labels from scratch, by counting up the major scale from the chord's root, crossing strings, and naming any note that falls a half step off a natural degree relative to the nearest 1/3/5/7 (never the scale degree before/after it).** Worked on five open-position chords (A, B, C major; D minor; F♯m), including the concrete flat-3 case and the rule for what happens when a natural 5 is already present and a second near-5 note needs a name (♭6, not ♯5 — with the ♭6-vs-♭13/extension-naming choice deferred as a separate, later layer, see [[Extended Chords]] just below). - **Compound-interval names (10th, 12th, 13th) and chord-tone-extension names (9, 11, 13) are two different naming systems for the same physical note — don't conflate them.** A **compound interval** term describes literal distance: a "10th" is always a compound 3rd (3rd + octave), a "12th" a compound 5th, a "13th" a compound 6th — pure counting, no harmonic context needed. A **chord-tone extension** term (9/11/13, or altered ♯11 etc.) describes the note's *functional role in a chord* instead, and does **not** require octave-plus distance — an extension can be voiced right next to the root (or below it) and keep its extension name. The two systems only look interchangeable because they usually land on the same note by coincidence (a plain 9th chord tone often *is* voiced a literal 9th/2nd up) — but core chord tones (1/3/5/7) never take a compound-interval name even when voiced far from the root (a 3rd an octave-plus up is still called "the 3," never "the 10"), and there's no chord tone called "12" at all (a 5th a 12th up is still just "the 5"). Full extension-naming rule (when 2/4/6 becomes 9/11/13 vs. stays 2/4/6) → [[Extended Chords]]. → [[Extended Chords]], [[Add Chords]] — Sound Guitar Lessons With Jared, [[Guitar Theory! - Demystifying Compound Intervals vs Chord Extensions]]
- **John Scofield's signature sound, analyzed: two independent melodic voices moving in contrary motion (a chromatically descending lower layer under a separate blues-phrase upper layer), built from intervals/dyads rather than full chord grips or single-note scale lines.** Over the opening phrase of his solo on "You Speak My Language" (B♭ blues): a dissonant 2nd interval into the tritone on E♭7 (its two guide tones), then only the top note keeps moving against the held bottom note (landing on a chromatic E), before a genuinely **polyphonic** passage — two voices moving independently at once, not just a decorated single-note line — recurs later in the same solo with the voices moving in opposite directions through a turnaround. Bill Frisell is named as another player who leans on the identical polyphonic-interval device but sounds completely different doing it — the takeaway is that phrasing/dynamics, not the interval technique itself, is what makes a line read as one player's voice vs. another's. - **Drill intervals against a fixed root, both directions, to see the chord family each one unlocks** — hold a root (say C) and walk chromatically down through all 12 intervals against it (octave→maj7→♭7→maj6→♭6/♯5→P5→♭5→P4→maj3→min3→9/maj2→♭9/♭2), naming the chord family each interval opens (maj7 chords, dominant/min7 chords, maj6/dim7 chords, augmented/altered-dominant voicings, basic triads, half-diminished, sus4/11 chords, major/minor triads, sus2/9 chords, altered-dominant ♭9 chords); then run it in reverse — hold the SAME note constant as the top voice and walk the root/bass through all 12 notes underneath it, showing that one fixed note is a legal, usable tone (root, 3rd, 5th, 7th, or an extension) of 12 different chord qualities depending purely on what's under it. This is the mechanical justification for [[Reharmonization]]'s "melody note must be a usable tone of the new chord" rule — same note, radically different function, just by changing the bass. Practice prescription: pick a note, build every chord type it can be a member of, then cycle bass notes under it and listen. — Jack Ruch, [[Write Great Chord Progressions and Reharmonize Melodies by Understanding Intervals!]]
- **"You need a flat third to make a minor chord" turned into a single physical fretboard move, applied across all five CAGED guitar chord shapes.** Instead of treating major↔minor conversion as an abstract label, find which fretted note in a chord shape is the major 3rd (count up the major scale from the root) and flat that one note by a fret — every other note in the shape stays put. Worked across all five CAGED shapes (A, G, E, D, C), including a doubled-3rd special case on the G shape (two instances of the 3rd in one 6-string voicing) and an extension of the identical rule to bare triads. Full shape-by-shape breakdown → [[CAGED Minor Chords by Flatting the Third]]. — Jesse Glick, [[What everyone misunderstands about CAGED in under 5 minutes]]
- **An interval trained in isolation sounds nothing like the same interval heard in tonal context — train scale degrees against a tonic instead of (or alongside) raw intervals** — a minor 6th floating free reads as "dark," but the identical distance as the 3rd-to-root of a major chord (E→G inside C major) reads as bright/major; the brain always assigns a root/tonic to whatever it hears and interprets everything relative to that, so pure interval ID transfers poorly to real listening on its own. Full method: [[Scale Degree Ear Training]]. — Max Konyi & David Bennett, [[David Bennett on Music Theory, Composition & the YouTube Life Sonic Sorcery Podcast No. 04]]
- **Feel the overtone-series basis for consonance directly on a guitar** — find a natural harmonic (the one near the 5th fret is the major-3rd/5th-partial), play the open string underneath it, and sing the harmonic's exact pitch against the open string; when the sung pitch locks in perfectly the beating disappears and it "rings like a bell" — a direct, audible demonstration of why equal temperament's major 3rd (~14–16 cents sharp of pure/just intonation) sounds subtly less locked-in than a cappella/choral pure intonation, which isn't constrained to the tempered grid. — David Bennett, [[David Bennett on Music Theory, Composition & the YouTube Life Sonic Sorcery Podcast No. 04]]
