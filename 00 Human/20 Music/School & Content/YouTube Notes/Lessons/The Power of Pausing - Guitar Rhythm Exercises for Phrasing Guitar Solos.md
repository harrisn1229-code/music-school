# The Power of Pausing | Guitar Rhythm Exercises for Phrasing Guitar Solos

## Synopsis

A single lesson lifted from module 3 (phrasing) of Jared's "Nail the Changes" improvisation course — explicitly "phrasing exercise number one," titled around the idea of **using space/rests as sound**. It's a direct companion to [[Singing Along With Your Playing (Phrasing)]] (also from this course, also targeting the same "good phrasing" instinct) but takes a completely different, rhythm-only route: instead of vocalizing, this drill builds phrasing by **deliberately dropping notes out of a constant improvised line to create rests**, starting from the same "constant atonal improv" base referenced across this course. Jared explains the exercise with a slide (rhythm notation, pass conditions, testing method) then demonstrates all three levels live on a nylon-string guitar with a pick, working up through 80/100/120 BPM. The video is short and utilitarian — almost entirely the drill itself, with a brief closing reflection on how a rhythmically-correct phrase can still land in a musically unsatisfying spot, and why that's worth noticing rather than treated as failure.

## What's taught

**The base concept — "the power of pausing," space as a rhythmic sound.** Continuing from the prior module's "constant atonal improv" (playing continuously, any pitch, no rests — the same base [[Singing Along With Your Playing (Phrasing)]]'s step 1 uses), this exercise **subtracts** notes to create deliberate rests, rather than adding new vocabulary. Every phrase is 2 bars: bar 1 is free-pitch eighth notes (reach for any note, pitch ungraded), bar 2 opens with a fixed rhythmic figure and then rests. Only the rhythm is controlled — pitch is always free — isolating rhythmic phrasing from note choice.

**Three cumulative levels (~1:20–5:20 explained, ~5:20–10:00 demonstrated):**
1. **Level 1 — land and rest.** Bar 1: 8 eighth notes (any pitch). Bar 2: land on beat 1, rest the remaining 3 beats. Pass: play it correctly 4 times in a row, no mistakes, at any comfortable tempo. Demonstrated at 80 BPM.
2. **Level 2 — chain in a second rhythmic variation.** Play Level 1's phrase 4 times in a row, then chain straight into a second phrase 4 times in a row: same shape, but bar 2's landing is a single quarter note instead of two eighth notes. Both 4-rep blocks have to be clean. Demonstrated at 100 BPM.
3. **Level 3 — chain in a third variation, all three together.** Adds a third phrase shape (bar 1 opens with a quarter note instead of two eighth notes, then eighth notes; bar 2 lands on two quarter notes instead of one) and requires all three phrase shapes — Level 1's, Level 2's, and this one — 4 reps each, clean, in a row. Demonstrated at 120 BPM. The tempo increases (80→100→120) are the video's own demonstration choice, not a mandated ladder — any comfortable tempo passes a level.

**Practice/testing method:** no metronome needed while practicing; use one only when testing whether you've passed a level, and log the tempo you passed at so you can come back later and try to beat it. Passing once at any tempo is treated as a genuine, complete win — speed is a separate goal to chase afterward, not a requirement to check the box. Optional: layer in the tone/slur/accent "feel" elements from the prior module once the rhythm is solid (not required, but "sounds really good and really musical" combined).

**Closing reflection:** the final demonstrated phrase in the video lands, in Jared's own words, in "a weird spot" that "feels left hanging" — despite being rhythmically exact. Framed as useful information rather than a failure: separate "did I execute the rhythm correctly" (what the drill actually grades) from "did that land musically" (a separate, worth-noticing judgment) — carrying that noticing into real playing is named as part of the real payoff of the exercise.
