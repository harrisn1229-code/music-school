# Minor 6 Pentatonic Scale

**A five-note alternate to the minor pentatonic scale, made by one edit: drop the ♭7 down a half step to the major 6.** Where [[Dorian from the Minor Pentatonic Box]] *adds* the major 6 as an extra color note on top of the existing pentatonic (six or seven notes total, ♭7 still present), this technique *replaces* the ♭7 outright — a straight five-note scale swap, not an added note. Worked in **D minor** over a dominant/blues backing track (chord voicing carries the ♭7 and the major 3rd, i.e. a D7-type sound) — a direct fingering-level sibling to [[Single-Note Pentatonic Substitutions Over Changes]]'s "one note edit" philosophy, but applied as a **wholesale scale identity swap for the whole phrase**, not a chord-tracking device triggered by a chord change.

## The core edit
D minor pentatonic: **D (root) – F (♭3) – G (4) – A (5) – C (♭7)**. Minor 6 pentatonic: **D – F – G – A – B** — the same shape with **C (♭7) replaced by B (major 6)** everywhere it occurs in the box. Credited source: heard from **Albert Collins** and **B.B. King**, both of whom "lean heavily on that major 6 interval" in their blues lines. Sonic effect: a brighter sound than straight minor pentatonic, most audible when the minor 3rd is bent — the bent minor 3rd against the new major 6 produces **a tritone interval**, described as giving "a really unique sound."

## Why it fits a dominant/blues backing
Demonstrated over a track voicing the **♭7 and the major 3rd** in the chord (i.e., a dominant-7 chord) — a common blues/funk harmonic context. Over that chord, the minor 3rd of the pentatonic scale is already commonly bent slightly to lean toward the chord's major 3rd (standard blues note-bending practice); the minor-6 substitution is offered as a second, complementary way to lean the pentatonic sound toward the underlying dominant harmony, alongside (not instead of) that minor-3rd bend.

## Practice method: pair every lick with its minor-6 twin
The whole lesson is structured as **five paired lick examples** — play the lick in straight minor pentatonic first, then replay the identical phrase with every ♭7 dropped to the major 6, so the ear and the hand both register the swap directly against the familiar version. This paired-comparison drill is the actual practice method, more valuable than any single lick:

1. **Lick 1 (10th-fret box, A/D/G strings):** slide up to 12th fret A string, 10th fret D string, 12th fret D string, 10th fret G string; repeat with a reach to 12th fret G string, then descend an octave and continue down into the next position lower, bending the minor 3rd throughout. Minor-6 version: same fingering, every C dropped to B — "a pretty big change in the fingering" for one changed note, with the bent-minor-3rd-against-major-6 tritone landing on the bend.
2. **Lick 2 (same box, starts high):** starts on the ♭7 (13th fret B string), descends the pentatonic bending the minor 3rd, then shifts up one position for a second phrase using a **hammer-on/pull-off embellishment (G to A♭, the 4 up to the ♭5)** and a **string-jump move (minor 3rd → 4 → 5, i.e. F-G-A, back to root)**. Minor-6 version starts on the major 6 instead of the ♭7 and changes the same notes throughout — described as "really nice things that fall under the fingers." Explicitly noted: **blend minor pentatonic and minor-6 pentatonic together** once both are under the fingers — they aren't an either/or choice.
3. **Lick 3 (top three strings only):** bend the ♭7 (13th fret B string) up, grab the root (10th fret high E), go to the minor 3rd (13th fret high E), repeat the root/♭7 pair, then a quick pull-off (13th fret to 10th fret B string), then shift position descending to grab the ♭7 an octave down rather than staying in one position. Minor-6 version bends **from the major 6 instead of the ♭7** — same half-step-bend mechanic, different starting note, producing the same minor-3rd/major-6 tritone color. Framed explicitly as a way to **break pentatonic muscle memory**: the altered fingering forces stretches from index to pinky rather than the usual index-to-ring reach, changing "the whole physical nature of playing" the line.
4. **Lick 4 (one position down, adds the 9th):** starts ♭7-root-minor 3rd-then the **9th (E in the key of D)** — note this lick already goes beyond the plain 5-note pentatonic by incorporating the 9. Repeats a version of lick 3's material an octave down, ending on ♭7 and the 5th. Minor-6 version: every ♭7 in the lick becomes the major 6, described as falling nicely under the fingers at this particular position, again with the index-to-pinky stretch discipline.
5. **Lick 5 (starts low, ends sliding up a position):** ascends straight up the minor pentatonic from root to root bending the ♭7 slightly, then descends **skipping the 4th** (root-♭7-5th, then down to the minor 3rd on the same string via a finger-roll from the side of the fingertip to the tip — for grip/control on a note you want to bend or vibrato), then a second phrase up on the top two strings (♭7-root-5th) sliding up a position (15th/13th fret high E, 15th fret B string) and back down to the ♭7. Minor-6 version: drop every ♭7 (C) to B throughout — "all the same stuff, we're just changing that one note."

## Practical takeaways
- **This is a whole-scale identity swap, not a per-chord note edit** — unlike [[Single-Note Pentatonic Substitutions Over Changes]] (swap one note exactly when a specific chord arrives), minor 6 pentatonic is chosen as an alternate *color* for a phrase or a whole solo section over a static dominant/blues harmony, then optionally blended back with straight minor pentatonic.
- **Every existing minor pentatonic lick is a candidate for this treatment** — take a lick you already know, find every ♭7 in it, and drop each one a half step to the major 6; the method generalizes to any lick, any key, any position.
- **Fingering changes more than the sound does.** Because the ♭7 and major 6 sit a half step apart but often require different fingers to reach cleanly in a box shape, the minor-6 version of a lick frequently forces new stretches (index-to-pinky instead of index-to-ring) — deliberately useful for breaking fixed pentatonic muscle memory, not just a tonal upgrade.
- **The tritone hook:** bending the minor 3rd while sitting in minor-6 pentatonic briefly sounds the minor-3rd-to-major-6 tritone — call this out explicitly as the "unique sound" moment worth targeting on purpose, not just a byproduct.
- **Reference listening:** Albert Collins and B.B. King, both cited as players who lean heavily on the major-6-over-minor-pentatonic sound in blues lines.
