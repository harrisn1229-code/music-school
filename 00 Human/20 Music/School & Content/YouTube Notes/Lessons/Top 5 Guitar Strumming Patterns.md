## What's taught — the 5 strumming patterns

### 1. Basic down-up strum
**D U D U D U D U** — continuous alternating eighth-note strum, described as "the foundation of every other strumming pattern." Played perfectly even it sounds flat/robotic; two concrete fixes make it musical:
- **Accent every other down stroke.** Creates a felt "drum beat" (backbeat-like pulse) inside a purely mechanical stroke pattern.
- **Emphasize different strings at different times** rather than hitting the full chord evenly on every stroke — vary which strings get weight, stroke to stroke.
- As a further embellishment once comfortable, he drops in an extra 16th-note strum mid-pattern to spice it up — a taste of how simple patterns get dressed up once the base motion is solid.

### 2. Double down-up strum ("the most common strumming pattern of all time")
**D D U U D U** — Jared calls this the single most ubiquitous strum in popular music, usable fast or slow across styles. The mechanical secret: the strumming hand never stops moving in a **continuous, steady alternating down-up motion** (the same physical motion as pattern 1) — the "double" strokes aren't the hand changing direction twice in a row, they're the hand passing over the strings **without striking them** on certain passes ("ghost strokes"), then striking again on the next same-direction pass. So the felt D-D and U-U pairs are really down/(ghost up)/down and up/(ghost down)/up, riding on top of one continuous motor pattern rather than being mechanically distinct strokes. This is the core technique insight of the whole video — every "compound" pattern below is really the same continuous hand motion with strategic silent passes.

### 3. Gallop strumming pattern (metal/hard rock)
**D D U** repeated, rhythmically a **quarter note followed by two sixteenth notes** — named for its horse-galloping feel; very common in metal, but usable in other genres too.
- **Palm mute at the bridge**, lightly touching the strings (not fully choking them) for the classic muted chug — pairs especially well with heavy distortion for a hard-rock sound.
- **"Orchestrating" the chord**: strum the full chord on the initial downstroke, then play only the bottom strings on the following hits (rather than hitting the same full voicing every time) — a technique Jared says he's covered elsewhere as general guitar-technique advice, and one that applies to any strumming pattern, not just this one: choose whether you're hitting lower or higher strings on a given stroke rather than always hitting everything.
- Works with any chord type, not only power chords — he demonstrates breaking the straight gallop up rhythmically mid-phrase to create more of a "song feel" before returning to the base pattern.

### 4. Carter-family-style strum (flatpicked folk/country)
Same **D D U** gallop rhythm as pattern 3, but reinterpreted: the first downstroke becomes a **single plucked bass/root note** (not a full strum), and the following down-up becomes a strum on just the **upper strings**. Pattern: **root (bass note) → down-up (upper strings)**, repeated.
- Alternate the plucked bass note between the chord's root and another chord tone for variety (demonstrated on an F chord).
- Named for the Carter Family folk/country picking style; played with a flat pick here (Jared notes a fingerpicked version is also possible, not demonstrated).
- **Great accuracy drill for the picking hand** — hitting a specific single bass string cleanly before strumming.
- Combines well with muting technique. Pairs naturally with **walking bass notes between chord changes** (Jared references a separate video on walking between chords for this — not yet in this vault, so not linked).

### 5. Funky 16th-note strum with built-in polyrhythm
A continuous 16th-note down-up motion (count: "1 e & a 2 e & a 3 e & a 4 e & a") where **only every third 16th-note articulation is actually struck** — the rest are ghost passes. Striking every 3rd note out of a 4-per-beat grid creates a felt **3-against-4 polyrhythm** (a different implied time signature nested inside the actual 4/4), which is what gives the pattern its syncopated funk feel.
- **Chord voicing:** bar just the **top four strings** for a minor 7 chord shape — described as a great voicing specifically for this funky 16th-note strumming feel (demonstrated on an A minor voicing).
- Structure: four groups of the 3-against-4 figure ("chord-click-click, chord-click-click, chord-click-click, chord-click-click" — each "chord" a down/up strike, each "click" a ghost pass), then a **resolving group on beat 4** that breaks the 3-pattern with a denser down-down-up-down-up-down-down-up-down-up-down run to land back in a clean 4/4 feel before the phrase repeats.
- **Optional embellishment:** add a pickup 16th-note flourish at the beginning or end of the phrase for extra funk color.
- Jared's stated favorite of the five ("saved it for last"); he's also used this same pattern as a worked example in a separate video of his on octaves (not yet in this vault, so not linked) — mentions it pairs especially well with sliding octave shapes for a heavy-rock-to-straight-funk range of sounds.

## General technique layered across all 5 patterns

- **Strum with the fingers, not just the pick.** Strum down with the **backs of the fingernails** (even without real nails), and up with the **back of the thumbnail**. Any single finger or combination works — Jared uses just the middle finger, the middle two fingers, or the first finger interchangeably depending on feel; no single "correct" finger. All five patterns above transfer directly to finger-strumming. *(This duplicates/reinforces the strumming-hand-finger technique already captured on [[Grip Tension Release and Strumming-Hand Basics]] from a separate Q&A video — see that note for the fuller writeup; this video adds no new mechanical detail on it beyond confirming it applies across all 5 named patterns here.)*
- **Dynamics and orchestration make or break a strumming pattern.** Pluck specific notes on purpose (as in the Carter-family pattern), play low-then-high to let high strings ring while continuing to strum the bottom, and vary which strings get hit and how hard — deliberately, not as a byproduct of imprecision. Don't strum every string evenly all the time unless that's a deliberate choice for the sound wanted.
