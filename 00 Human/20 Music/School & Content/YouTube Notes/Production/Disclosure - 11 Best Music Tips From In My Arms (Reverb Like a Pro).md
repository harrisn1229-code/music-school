# Disclosure: 11 Best Music Tips from "In My Arms" (& how to use reverb like a PRO)

## Synopsis

A dense, session-view walkthrough of "In My Arms" covering percussion width/character, UK garage kick philosophy, reverb as a rhythmic tool rather than just a space tool, deliberate pitch-modulated "analog imperfection" on drum machine hits, and a clever workaround for wanting to automate a printed synth's filter after the fact. ## Techniques

- **Two kicks per track, each doing a different job: one for punch, one for top end** — rather than one kick trying to cover the whole range, a short/punchy kick and a separate top-end-focused kick are layered, with the punchy one's length automated to get shorter/longer through the arrangement. → [[Kick Design]], [[Layering and Stacking]]
- **A near-constant, unbroken bassline needs a very short, clicky, punchy kick — not a long one.** When the bassline itself is already sustaining continuously, a long kick fights it for low-end space; a short click-forward kick lets the bassline own the sustained low end instead. Contrast: a straight four-on-the-floor house track can support a longer kick because there's no competing sustained bass underneath it. Rule of thumb: it's genre/arrangement-dependent, not a fixed setting. → [[Kick Design]], [[Genre Drum Patterns]]
- **In UK garage specifically, the kick can sit almost entirely below 100 Hz and barely register as "hit" on its own — the bassline underneath is what actually delivers the chest-thump feeling.** A genre-specific inversion of the usual "kick carries the low end" assumption. → [[Genre Drum Patterns]], [[Bass Design]]
- **Real percussion stereo width comes from using two physically different sounds panned left/right, not the same sample duplicated and panned.** Panning one shaker sample hard left and a copy of itself hard right gives some width just from the original performance's stereo bleed, but panning **two different shakers** (or a shaker + tambourine) left/right — each sitting in its own frequency range — gives dramatically more width because the ear can tell the two sources apart. → [[Width and Stereo]], [[Sound Selection Guide]]
- **Heavy overdrive/distortion on shakers and tambourines specifically for fullness, not grit.** Deliberately over-driving quiet, thin percussion layers brings out low-mid body/fullness that a clean shaker doesn't have — framed as a fullness move, not a lo-fi/character move. → [[Saturation and Distortion]]
- **A big plate reverb on the drum bus, plus individual Valhalla reverb sends per track — different reverb roles at different stages.** Individual elements (claps especially) get their own short send (EMT 140-style plate, "B" setting, roughly 2 seconds), while the whole drum bus additionally gets a large plate for cohesion — two reverb layers doing two different jobs (per-element character vs. whole-kit glue). → [[Reverb]]
- **Tremolo placed AFTER a reverb return turns the reverb tail into a rhythmic, pulsing element — reverb as a groove tool, not just a space tool.** A tremolo effect on the reverb return (not the dry signal) set to 16th notes chops the reverb's decay into a rhythmic pulse; explicitly framed as reverb doing double duty — adding both space and groove simultaneously. → [[Reverb]], [[Rhythm and Groove]]
- **Pre-delay on a clap reverb can be pushed hard enough to become a deliberate rhythmic effect, not just "more space."** Rather than treating pre-delay as a subtle spacing parameter, pushing it far enough that the reverb's onset itself lands on a chosen rhythmic subdivision turns it into an audible groove element in its own right. → [[Reverb]], [[Rhythm and Groove]]
- **LFO-modulated pitch on a drum-machine hit (909 hi-hat, also used on rides/cymbals) for deliberate "analog imperfection."** An LFO mapped to a sample's pitch makes a normally pitch-stable hit (909 hi-hat in Battery) wander slightly — explicitly done to fight the "sample, sample, sample" static repetition feeling and make a digital drum machine read as a little more alive/analog, "like something's a bit wrong" on purpose. Also applied to ride cymbals/cymbals via pitch (an alternative to the more common pan-modulation move), and sometimes to release time too. → [[Modulation]], [[Genre Drum Patterns]]
- **Roll off inaudible sub-bass mud even when it "looks" like it should be there** — checked visually (a spectrum analyzer showing energy a small speaker won't reproduce anyway) and cut, on the reasoning that content down there does nothing but eat headroom if nothing in the room can actually play it back. → [[Masking and Carving]], [[Gain Staging]]
- **Deliberately leave raw, imperfect, "out of time" hits unquantized when sidechain ducking already glues the mix together** — a specific percussion layer (called "dead dims") is intentionally left un-quantized/un-tightened because the track's sidechain compression already pulls everything into a cohesive pocket regardless of exact timing; quantizing it would have made it feel less alive for no audible mix benefit.
- **Automate saturation/overdrive Wet/Dry as a substitute for filter cutoff automation on audio you can no longer edit as a synth.** After committing a synth part to audio (one good take, no way back into the original patch's cutoff/resonance), [[Decapitator]]'s dry/wet mix was automated up and down through the track instead — the rising/falling saturation amount mimics the perceptual effect of a rising/falling filter cutoff closely enough that, given the choice again, he'd still pick the overdrive-automation approach over having direct cutoff access. → [[Decapitator]], [[Automation]]
- **Ozone 9 Imager as a general-purpose stereo-widening tool, not just a mastering-stage device** — reached for on individual elements (a shaker, a tambourine, even a reverb return) using "Mode 2," pushed hard, whenever something needs to sound wider or have its highs lifted; a go-to rather than a rare mastering-only reach. → [[Ozone 9 Imager]]
