# Course 02 — Chord Function

Teaches what chords **do**, not how to spell them. Every diatonic chord has one of three jobs, and once you hear the job instead of the letter, a progression becomes a shape you can manipulate.

**What it assumes**

- [[Course 01 — Derivation Rules]] — interval quality and key derivation have to be in place before function means anything.

**What this unlocks**

- **Four other strands, each an application of this one.** POP is harmony over loops and vamps · BLU is harmony with a non-functional grammar · JAZ is harmony with substitutions layered on · MEL is melody written *against* harmony. None is teachable before this.
- The harder chord material stops being memorisation and becomes derivation.
- Practically: deliberate harmonic decisions in a loop, instead of finding chords by ear and keeping whatever sounds good.

> [!info] The gap this course closes
> Spelling chords fluently while being unable to say what any of them *does* is the standard gap at this stage — the vocabulary without the grammar. It's also why the four strands above stay out of reach until this is done.

## The lessons

### Lesson 1 — Function

**Read**
- [ ] [[Chord Progressions]] → *Roman numeral analysis*, *Harmonic function (T / SD / D)*
- [ ] [[Chords by Key]]

**The material**
- The idea the whole course rests on: **every diatonic chord does one of [[HAR-C01 — The three functions: T, SD, D|three jobs]]** — tonic, subdominant or dominant.
- **Tonic** is home: I, plus its substitutes vi and iii. **Subdominant** is departure: IV and ii. **Dominant** is tension that wants to resolve: V and vii°.
- The normal cycle is **T → SD → D → T** — most of Western popular music in four symbols.
- Hearing chords as *functions* rather than letters turns a progression from a list into a shape. It also makes substitution obvious: **chords sharing a function can stand in for each other**, which is why I → vi barely disturbs a loop while I → V transforms it.
- The payoff is transposition-proof. "vi" behaves the same way in every key; "Am" only tells you which key you happen to be in.

**Concepts**
- [[HAR-C01 — The three functions: T, SD, D]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — take four progressions you've written. Label every chord T, SD or D. Find the ones that don't follow T→SD→D→T and work out what they do instead.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — play a four-chord loop you know on piano, then re-voice it so every T chord is root position and every D chord is first inversion. Hear how much of "function" your hands were already doing by ear.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 02 — Lesson 1 Test]]

### Lesson 2 — Cadences

**Read**
- [ ] [[Cadences]] → *The four types*, in full — it's a short note

**The material**
- A cadence is how a phrase ends: the punctuation of harmony. Four of them, and they're the difference between a phrase that stops and one that lands.
- The [[HAR-C02 — Authentic cadence|authentic cadence]] (V–I) is maximum resolution, and the default ending of most Western phrases.
- The [[HAR-C03 — Plagal cadence|plagal cadence]] (IV–I) is gentler because IV has **no leading tone** — nothing pulls up by a semitone, so it settles rather than resolves. That's why it's everywhere in gospel and rock.
- The [[HAR-C04 — Deceptive cadence|deceptive cadence]] (V–vi) swerves, and it's believable precisely because vi shares two of its three notes with I. The ear accepts a near-miss.
- **The [[HAR-C05 — Half cadence|half cadence]] is the one worth internalising.** It ends *on* the dominant rather than resolving to it, which makes an antecedent phrase demand an answer — and that's what builds 8- and 16-bar sections out of 4-bar ones.
- Underneath all four sits [[HAR-C06 — Why V–I resolves: the tritone|the tritone]]. G7 contains one between **B and F** — maximally unstable, and each note is a semitone from a tonic chord tone. B rises to C, F falls to E; both resolve by half step into consonance.
- That's *why* V–I feels inevitable rather than merely conventional. It's also the same fact that explains tritone substitution later.

**Concepts**
- [[HAR-C02 — Authentic cadence]]
- [[HAR-C03 — Plagal cadence]]
- [[HAR-C04 — Deceptive cadence]]
- [[HAR-C05 — Half cadence]]
- [[HAR-C06 — Why V–I resolves: the tritone]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — find one of each cadence in songs you know. Then take a progression ending V–I and replace it with V–vi. Listen to what that does to the phrase.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — on guitar, play the same eight-bar phrase four times, ending with an authentic, plagal, half and deceptive cadence. Record all four to one Ableton track, play them back and name each one blind.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 02 — Lesson 2 Test]]

### Lesson 3 — Leaving the Key

**Read**
- [ ] [[Secondary Dominants]] → *The idea*, *Why it works*, *The full dominant-function roster*
- [ ] [[Borrowed Chords]] → *The workhorses*
- [ ] [[Parallel Minor]]

**The material**
- Two ways to use a chord from outside the key, and they do opposite things.
- **[[HAR-C07 — Secondary dominants|Secondary dominants]] borrow tension.** Any diatonic chord can be treated as a temporary tonic by putting *its* dominant in front of it. In C major the chord on A is Am; raise its 3rd to C♯ and you get A7, which now points at Dm. That's **V/ii**, and the C♯ is a borrowed leading tone.
- **[[HAR-C08 — Borrowed chords and modal interchange|Borrowed chords]] borrow colour.** Keep the tonic, raid the parallel minor: Fm (iv), A♭ (♭VI), B♭ (♭VII), E♭ (♭III) in C major. The key centre never moves, so it reads as shade rather than a key change. The classic move is **IV → iv → I**.
- The distinction is the whole lesson: **secondary dominants add pull, borrowed chords add colour.** One creates motion, the other creates mood.
- A useful tell: a secondary dominant is nearly always a *dominant 7th* that isn't diatonic, and it resolves down a fifth. A borrowed chord usually isn't a dominant at all, and it resolves to the tonic or simply sits there.

**Concepts**
- [[HAR-C07 — Secondary dominants]]
- [[HAR-C08 — Borrowed chords and modal interchange]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — in a C major loop, put E7 before Am and hear it pull. Then swap an F for an Fm and hear it darken. Same one-chord change, completely different jobs.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — in a C major loop in Ableton, drop E7 in front of Am and print it as a clip. Then swap the F for Fm and print that. A/B both against the plain loop.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 02 — Lesson 3 Test]]

### Lesson 4 — Melody Against Harmony

**Read**
- [ ] [[Non-Harmonic Tones]] → *The 9 types*
- [ ] [[Modulation (Key Change)]] → *Common types*, *How to hear/analyze it*

**The material**
- **[[HAR-C09 — Non-harmonic tones|Non-harmonic tones]]** are the melody notes that aren't in the chord underneath — and they're most of what makes a melody sound like a melody rather than an arpeggio.
- The one to know cold is the **passing tone**: approached by step, left by step, same direction. Most of the other eight are variations on how you arrive and how you leave.
- Then the distinction that closes the course: [[HAR-C10 — Tonicization vs modulation|tonicization versus modulation]].
- **Tonicization** points at a chord for a moment — a secondary dominant leaning on ii — then leaves. **No new key.**
- **Modulation** relocates the tonic and *confirms* it with a cadence. The ear resets.
- It's a spectrum rather than a binary, set by **how long you stay and whether you cadence there**. Four bars of V/V–V is genuinely borderline.
- What makes a modulation smooth is the **pivot chord** — diatonic to both keys, so it can be heard either way. Approach it in the old key, reinterpret it, cadence in the new one. Am is vi in C and ii in G, which is why C→G is the easiest modulation there is.

**Concepts**
- [[HAR-C09 — Non-harmonic tones]]
- [[HAR-C10 — Tonicization vs modulation]]

**Homework**
- [ ] **Review** — take a melody you've written and label every note chord-tone or non-harmonic. Then modulate a loop from C to G using Am as the pivot, and confirm with D7–G.
- [ ] **Apply** (~15 min) — write a four-bar melody over that C loop, then modulate to G using Am as the pivot and confirm with D7–G. Label every melody note chord-tone or non-harmonic as you play it back.
- [ ] **Test** — [[Course 02 — Lesson 4 Test]]
