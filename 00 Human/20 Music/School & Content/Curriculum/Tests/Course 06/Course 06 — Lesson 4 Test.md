> **Q1.** **Mixolydian — major with a ♭7 — or equivalently borrowed from the parallel minor**
> Both readings are valid and both produce the same chord. It's the single most common non-diatonic chord in rock and pop.
>
> **Q2.** **No leading tone — nothing is pulling up into the tonic**
> A functional V puts a half-step under the tonic and the ear follows it home. ♭VII has no such pull, so the progression moves without gravity. That absence is what "modal" sounds like.
>
> **Q3.** **The borrowed ♭VII, alongside descending chromatic bass under a held chord**
> *Also accepted:* the line cliché · "Michelle" · "Something"
>
> **Q4.** **One has a functional dominant and the other doesn't**
> *Rubric (5):* says C–G–F contains a real dominant with a leading tone pulling to the tonic (2) · says C–B♭–F has no leading tone, so nothing pulls (2) · names the result as drift or modal colour rather than resolution (1)
> The difference is entirely about gravity. In the second version the G chord carries the note a half-step below the tonic, and the ear treats that as an instruction — the progression has somewhere it is going, and the F afterwards reads as a step on the way back. In the first version the B♭ removes that half-step entirely. Nothing in the chord wants to move anywhere, so the loop stops being a journey and becomes a colour field. That is why modal writing tends to avoid functional dominants altogether: one V chord is enough to drag the whole thing back into a key.
>
> **Q5.** **B natural against B♭ — Dm–G is Dorian, Dm–Gm is natural minor**
> One note is the entire colour difference. Dorian lifts; Aeolian stays dark.
>
> **Q6.** **The raised 6th — it turns the chord on the 4th degree from minor to major**
> Dorian is natural minor with that one degree raised, and the major IV against a minor tonic is what makes it sound hopeful and forward-leaning rather than bleak. That's why Dorian vamps are everywhere in neo-soul, house, lo-fi and modern R&B.
>
> **Q7.** **♭VI–♭VII–i — F, G and Am in the key of A minor**
> All three chords are diatonic to natural minor. It climbs stepwise into the tonic with no dominant at all, which is a completely different feel to V–i, and it's the bed under a large amount of trap, metal and film scoring.
>
> **Q8.** **Emphasize the characteristic note · use the chord it produces · avoid the parent's dominant**
> *Rubric (5):* says the characteristic note must be present and emphasized, or the mode isn't audible (2) · says to use the chord that note produces, since that's what communicates the mode (2) · says to avoid the parent key's dominant, which drags the ear straight back (1)
> The trap is that a mode played over a progression that keeps resolving to the parent major just sounds like the parent major. Playing the right notes is not enough, because the same seven notes describe seven modes and the ear picks whichever tonal centre the harmony keeps confirming. So the mode has to be asserted harmonically, not scalewise — the ♭7 or the ♮6 has to be audible, the chord it produces has to appear, and the one chord that would undo all of it has to stay out. Think parallel rather than derivative: "D minor with a raised 6th", not "the white notes starting from D".
>
> **Q9.** **A chord a 3rd from the tonic with a quality the key doesn't provide**
> In C those are E, A, E♭ and A♭ major. The diatonic chords a 3rd away are Em and Am, so changing the quality or chromaticizing the root is what makes one chromatic.
>
> **Q10.** **It shares a common tone with the tonic — and it arrives rather than resolves**
> One note holds while everything else shifts, which is why it reads as a lift, a widening or a scene change instead of a wrong turn. There's no dominant pull in it at all, and that's exactly why film and game scoring lean on them.
>
> **Q11.** **An unprepared step up for the final chorus — a cliché because it announces itself**
> No pivot, no dominant, no reinterpretation; the song just lurches upward. Being a cliché means overused, not broken — if the song wants an unsubtle final lift, this is the tool that does it.
>
> **Q12.** **Shift the mode instead of the key · approach the new tonic through its own V · pivot on a shared chord**
> A modal shift keeps the tonic and changes the collection, so it isn't really a modulation at all. Approaching through the new key's own dominant takes one chord and announces the arrival. The pivot chord is the classical method and the smoothest — Am is vi in C and ii in G, so following it with D7 and G lands the change without a seam. The relative-key switch is a fourth option and barely a modulation at all.
