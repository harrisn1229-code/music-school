# Songs that use the Dorian mode

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n_Zzztd5ZCk)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n_Zzztd5ZCk)

## Synopsis

David Bennett defines Dorian precisely as natural minor with a raised 6th degree — the single note that separates it from plain Aeolian — then walks through how that one note gets deployed across genres: as a two-chord vamp (funk, Pink Floyd), withheld from the melody until a payoff moment (Mad World, Blue Jeans), carried purely by the chords while the melody stays ambiguous (Drive), directly contrasted against Aeolian within one song (Eleanor Rigby, Thriller), and as a genuine tonal center rather than a chord in modal jazz (So What).

## Techniques

- **The raised 6th is the whole story.** Dorian = natural minor with scale degree 6 raised a half-step; that's the only note that differs from Aeolian, so it's the note to listen for (or deliberately place) when writing/identifying Dorian.
- **The Dorian vamp: i–IV.** A major IV chord (only possible in Dorian, since Aeolian's iv is minor) is the fastest two-chord way to state Dorian — minor i sets the key, major IV supplies the raised 6th. Common in funk (arguably more than plain minor) and vamped for extended stretches in Pink Floyd songs. A minor ii chord (minor rather than diminished, because of the raised 6th) is an alternative source of the same color.
- **Deliberately delaying the 6th in the melody as a chorus payoff** — "Mad World" and "Blue Jeans" both carry Dorian in the chords from the start but withhold the melody's first raised-6th note until the chorus, making the chorus land brighter/more satisfying by contrast with the verse.
- **Chord-only Dorian** — "Drive" (R.E.M.) never touches the raised 6th in the vocal melody at all; a major IV chord alone recontextualizes the otherwise-ambiguous minor melody as Dorian for the whole song.
- **Ambiguous pentatonic minor resolving into Dorian at a structural moment** — "Woodstock" (Joni Mitchell) avoids both Dorian's raised 6th and Aeolian's flat 6th (staying pentatonic) until the vocal finally lands on the Dorian 6th at the end of the first chorus.
- **Direct Dorian↔Aeolian contrast as a mood device** — "Eleanor Rigby" shifts C♯ (Dorian) to C♮ (Aeolian) line to line for an unsettled/eerie effect; "Thriller" sits mostly in C♯ Dorian but briefly dips to the Aeolian minor iv for the same bright/bleak contrast.
- **Modal jazz uses Dorian as a tonal center, not a chord** — "So What" (Miles Davis): 16 bars D Dorian, 8 bars E♭ Dorian, 8 bars back to D Dorian, with soloists exploring the scale rather than resolving through a functional chord progression — genuinely mode changes, closer to key changes than chord substitutions.
- Other examples: "Riders on the Storm" (The Doors) — descending intro line walks straight down E Dorian. "Scarborough Fair" — the "rosemary and thyme" melody lands on the Dorian raised 6th.
