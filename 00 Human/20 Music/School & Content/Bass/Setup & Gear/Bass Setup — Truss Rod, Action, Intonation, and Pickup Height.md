# Bass Setup — Truss Rod, Action, Intonation, and Pickup Height

A DIY basic-bass-setup procedure from BassBuzz — five ordered steps (truss rod → bridge saddle action → nut check → intonation → pickup height) that fix most of what makes a cheap bass feel and sound bad: buzzing/rattling notes, strings too far off the fretboard, out-of-tune fretted notes, and weak or distorted amp tone. **Order matters** — each step subtly affects the measurements for the ones after it (truss rod affects action, and truss rod + action + nut all affect intonation), so doing them out of order means re-doing earlier ones. Tools needed: a ruler, a screwdriver, a couple of Allen wrenches, a tuner, and a business card. The video's own blind test (see below) is the argument for bothering with this at all: a properly set-up cheap bass can beat an un-set-up expensive one in a blind feel/sound test with an experienced player.

## Step 1 — Truss rod (sets the neck's forward bow)

The truss rod is a metal rod inside the neck that counteracts string tension. Set wrong, it causes either buzzing (too straight) or strings sitting too far off the neck (too much forward bow).

**The truss rod test trifecta** (do this before touching anything):
1. Press the E string down right behind the **1st fret** (capo, or wedge in a pick/ruler).
2. Press the E string down past the **12th fret** with your plucking hand.
3. Measure the gap at the **7th fret** with a business card. Can't slide it in at all → neck too straight, **loosen** the rod. More than 1–2 business cards of clearance → too much forward bow, **tighten** the rod. 1–2 cards of clearance → correct, leave it.
   - *Alternate check:* tap the E string against the 7th fret — a small click = correct; no click = too straight (loosen); lots of click/travel = too much bow (tighten).

**Adjusting:** find the truss rod adjuster (headstock, body-end/heel, or — on a vintage-style neck — you may have to pull the neck to reach it). Use the correctly-fitting *thicker* Allen wrench (no wiggle, to avoid stripping it). Start any adjustment with an 1/8 turn to de-stick it, then return to start. **Tighten clockwise** (too much bow → straightens the neck), **loosen counterclockwise** (too straight → lets it bow forward), **1/8 turn at a time** — firm pressure is normal, forcing it or big turns is not. Retune and re-run the trifecta after each adjustment, always **in playing position** (not flat on a bench), always **in tune** first. Truss rod changes take hours to fully settle — make small moves and re-check later rather than chasing the measurement in one sitting.

## Step 2 — Bridge saddle height (action)

Bridge saddles set **action** — how far the strings sit above the fretboard — more directly than the truss rod, though the truss rod still affects it. Target: **medium action** — easy to press but plucks hard without rattling. *(Low action = light touch required, more buzz risk. High action = harder to fret, more plucking headroom before buzz.)*

**Measuring:** fret/capo the 1st fret, measure from the top of the **12th fret wire** to the bottom of the string. **~2–3 mm (≈3/32") on the E string** is a good medium starting point *(general starting point from the video, not a universal spec)*; the G string can run slightly lower since it's a lighter string. Inner strings (A, D) should sit **slightly higher** than the outer strings (E, G) — the fingerboard has a curve (radius) built in.

**Adjusting:** smaller Allen wrench (some bridges need a locking screw loosened first — check yours). **Clockwise = raises** the saddle (string was too low), **counterclockwise = lowers** it (string was too high). Move a **1/4–1/2 turn at a time**, and keep both sides of a saddle even (same turn amount each side). Retune after every adjustment (saddle height changes pitch), remeasure, then pluck every fret medium-hard checking for rattle — press with full fretting pressure so weak technique doesn't get misread as an action problem. Buzz on multiple frets → raise action slightly; clean and want it lower → try dropping it. Repeat per string: E, then G, then A and D.

## Step 3 — Nut clearance (diagnose only — can't self-fix)

Checks whether **open strings** buzz (slot too low) or intonation/action is being thrown off (slot too high). Unlike the other four steps, **you can diagnose this yourself but can't fix it without special files/tools** — this step exists to tell you whether to book a pro visit, not to hand you a fix.

**Check:** business card, no fretting, slide between the string and the **1st fret wire**. Slides in cleanly → fine. Too tight to fit → slot may be low (only a real problem if the *open* string actually rattles — otherwise leave it). Lots of clearance → slot is too high, needs re-cutting by a pro.

## Step 4 — Intonation (per-string length at the bridge)

Frets only deliver in-tune notes if each string is exactly the right length — this is why intonation is step 4, after truss rod/action/nut are all locked in (they all subtly change effective string length).

**Check per string:** tune the open string on a real tuner → fret the **12th fret** without bending (should read the same note, an octave up) → also check the **19th fret** as a second data point. All three in tune = that string's intonation is correct.
- Fretted notes read **flat** → string is too long → **shorten** it.
- Fretted notes read **sharp** → string is too short → **lengthen** it.

**Adjusting:** Phillips-head screw at the bridge saddle moves the saddle back/forward. Flat → **loosen** (counterclockwise) to shorten the string. Sharp → **tighten** (clockwise) to lengthen it. Slack the string slightly while turning so the saddle isn't fighting full string tension (protects the bridge finish). After adjusting, seat the screw by tapping the screwdriver into the head. Cycle **adjust → retune → recheck fretted notes** until dialed, without bending while fretting — expect several passes per string, and repeat for all four strings.

## Step 5 — Pickup height (tone and volume out of the amp)

The bass can play perfectly and still sound bad if pickup height is off: **too low → thin, quiet tone. Too high → distortion and pop/click sounds from strings hitting the pole pieces.**

**Spec starting point** *(this bass's own spec — check yours; not a universal number)*: ~6/64" (E side) / ~5/64" (G side), or ~2.5 mm / ~2 mm, measured from the pole piece tops to the bottom of the string with that string fretted at the last fret.

**Manual/by-ear method** (works with no spec at all):
1. Solo one pickup — start with the neck pickup.
2. Play at real intensity, including slap if that's part of the part.
3. Distortion or string-on-pole contact → lower it (tighten the mounting screws). Weak/thin/quiet → raise it (loosen the screws).
4. Once set, A/B the two pickups for roughly matched volume, then check the blend.

**Split (P-style) pickups:** what looks like two pickups is often one split pickup shaped to the fingerboard radius — set the **inner pole pieces slightly higher** than the outer ones for even output string-to-string.

## The blind test — why this is worth doing

BassBuzz set up a $300 bass and a $1,000 bass using exactly this procedure, then blindfolded an experienced bassist (Geo) for an A/B. Handed the $300 bass first, he was genuinely impressed — said he'd happily gig on it, no complaints. Only after being handed the $1,000 bass did his read shift ("high fidelity," "like driving a Cadillac") — and it was *that* comparison, not the first bass in isolation, that revealed a gap. The takeaway stated on camera: not knowing how to set up your own bass means depending on paying someone else for years; being able to do it yourself means dialing a bass in exactly the way you want it, and re-fixing it yourself instead of paying for every touch-up. Directly reinforces [[Bass Tone Chain Priority]]'s Link 1 finding (fingers/technique >> gear) — a cheap, well-set-up instrument closes most of the gap to an expensive one; this note is the mechanical maintenance half of that same "gear matters less than you think, technique/setup matters more" thesis.
