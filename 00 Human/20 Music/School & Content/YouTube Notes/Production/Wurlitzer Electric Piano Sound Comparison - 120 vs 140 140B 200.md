# Wurlitzer Electric Piano Sound Comparison: 120 vs 140 140B 200

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=18Ioq6uJlFY)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=18Ioq6uJlFY)

## Synopsis

A collaboration with Ben Bove (retrorentals.net — the Fender Rhodes/Wurlitzer tech and historian of choice for touring artists) A/B comparing the major Wurlitzer electric piano models — 120, 140B, 200, 200A — by ear and by engineering history. Corrects a factual error already present in the vault's own Keyscape patch note (the 140B is solid-state, not tube) and surfaces real recording/mic'ing technique alongside the gear history.

## Techniques

- **140B vs. 200/200A**: both solid-state, same underlying pickup technology, but genuinely different sound — the 140B (the model that predates the 200 series) has more "bite" and an action that feels closer to an acoustic piano.
- **200 vs. 200A**: the 200A relocated the preamp board to reduce noise — an amp-construction iteration, not a fundamentally different tone. A "vintage" noise floor is more often a symptom of an aging/failing amp than inherent character; modern replacement amps (Vintage Vibe and similar) are recommended over treating hiss as part of the sound.
- **Not every Wurlitzer has a proper effects loop returning through its internal speakers** (unlike a Rhodes) — some units' aux/pedal output just feeds a recording desk, bypassing the onboard speaker cabinet. A custom board was built for one unit specifically to add a correct-impedance send/return so pedal effects come back through the internal speakers without needing to pad the pedal's input level.
- **Parallel dry/wet signal preservation, borrowed from how guitarists reamp**: mult the line out BEFORE the effects loop so a clean, full-transient dry copy survives alongside the effected signal — once an effect is printed you can't remove it, so keeping the dry path is the same insurance as a guitarist's DI-for-later-reamping.
- **Deliberate low-end control via mic'ing choice**: close-micing a Wurlitzer's small internal speakers (literally car-door-speaker sized) naturally rolls off deep low end compared to the line/DI signal — used deliberately to keep the part from conflicting with a bass part, rather than EQ'ing it out afterward. A kill switch on the internal speakers allows pure DI recording with no speaker bleed.
- **The true tube-era Wurlitzers are the 120 and 145** (this video corrects the common assumption that the 140B is tube — it isn't). The 120 specifically carries the distinctive "Ray Charles 'What'd I Say'" character, but has noticeably less sustain than the solid-state models and is famously inconsistent unit-to-unit — individual reeds vary enough that a tech has to spot-check each one for quality; described half-jokingly as "a boat anchor" by one tech advising against buying one for reliable gigging use, despite having a raw, distinct early-rock character the later solid-state models don't fully replicate.
- **Overall verdict from the two players in the video**: a good non-A 200 (like the one demonstrated, ~early 1970s) is a strong all-purpose choice; both agreed on preferring it within seconds of playing it.
