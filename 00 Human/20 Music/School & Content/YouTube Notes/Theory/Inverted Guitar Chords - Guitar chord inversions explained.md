# Inverted Guitar Chords - Guitar chord inversions explained

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xIwjyUOpiHE)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xIwjyUOpiHE)

## Synopsis
A 2021 entry in Jared's ongoing "theory of harmony and chords on guitar" series, focused on a single distinction most guitar-inversion content skips: a chord **shape** that's technically an inversion (some chord tone other than the root under your fingers) is not automatically a **functional** inversion — that only happens when the bass note actually sounding underneath the whole piece of music is that chord tone. Play an "inverted" D shape while the bass player holds the root, and nothing has actually inverted; it's just a rootless-sounding voicing. The video works this distinction through six real-song/etude examples (Bob Dylan, Randy Newman/"You've Got a Friend in Me," The Beatles' "In My Life," a Stairway-style progression, "My Funny Valentine," and Carcassi's Etude in A), each demonstrated both with and without the inverted bass note so the ear can hear what the inversion actually buys — smoother, more singable, stepwise bass motion instead of chord-to-chord root jumps. Closes with the "neighbor IV chord" idea (keep the key's root under the bass when moving to IV) and an explicit reframe: guitarists mostly practice inversions as fingering shapes (jumping between a chord's inversion voicings while the bass, if any, ignores it), which is a genuinely different — and equally worthwhile — skill from writing/hearing functional inversions in real music.

## What's taught
- **Definition:** an inversion is a chord that does *not* have its root on the bottom — root = "one," everything else (3, 5, 7…) counts, and whichever of those is lowest determines the inversion (1st = 3rd in bass, 2nd = 5th in bass, 3rd = 7th in bass, 4-note chords only).
- **Two guitar-specific ways to find inversion shapes:** (1) take a chord shape, identify each string's chord-tone role, and on each string find the next chord tone up (5→1→3→5…) — the **same "advance every note one slot" method already documented in [[Chord Voicings]]** from the Jack Ruch blues-inversions video, and now also its own full worked lesson (D/C/Cmaj7, added 2026-08-11) → [[Guitar Inversions - Music Theory Guitar Lesson]]; (2) move the top note of a shape down an octave (or use unison-interval shapes) to land on the next inversion down (still not its own video in this vault).
- **The video's core teaching point — shape-level inversion vs. functional inversion:** a guitar voicing can be *labeled* an inversion (some chord tone other than the root under your fingers) while *functioning* as plain root position, because function depends on the lowest note of the **whole ensemble/song**, not the guitarist's own voicing. If the bass player is still playing the root, the guitarist's "inverted" shape is just a rootless-sounding voicing of the root-position chord — not an inversion in the sense that matters musically. This is a gap not previously called out explicitly on [[Chord Inversions]] (which documents guitar-as-omission and voicing choice, but not this shape-vs-function split) — added there as a new subsection.
- **The actual musical function of inversions:** smoother, more singable, stepwise bass-line motion under changing chords, instead of the bass leaping root-to-root every change. Written with slash notation (`C/G` = "C over G" = C chord, G as the specified lowest note) — even when that note is already in the chord (the 5th here), the slash calls out that it's specifically the bass note wanted, for melodic reasons.
- **Six worked song/etude examples, each played with the inversion and then without it for direct A/B comparison** (paraphrased chord letters where the captions were unambiguous; flagged where garbled):
  - **Bob Dylan, "Don't Think Twice, It's All Right"** — "Ain't no use to sit and wonder why, babe": I – V(1st inv.) – vi – vi7(3rd inv., ♭7 in bass, "Am7/G") – IV. A genuinely descending, stepwise bass line under the phrase; without the inversions the same changes lose "that connecting step" and jump around more.
  - **Randy Newman, "You've Got a Friend in Me"** (Toy Story) — "it's so cool, it's so cool" sits on a **first-inversion E♭ major** chord; called out as an example of subtlety beating the expected strummed-and-jumping guitar default, and as voice leading in the literal sense (the term comes from vocal writing).
  - **The Beatles, "In My Life"** — the **A7 third inversion (A7/G)**, a secondary dominant to the IV chord ("five of four") — already fully documented (same chord, same analysis) on the song's own note: [[🎵 THE BEATLES - In My Life]]. This video is a second, independent source for that same chord; the "chromatic chords" video Jared references for the F♯m6 and the IV→iv borrow is a separate video not yet in this vault.
  - **"Stairway to Heaven"-style progression** — demonstrated live with a moving/descending bass line under a held triad shape (no chord names spoken during the demo itself — flagged low-confidence, kept qualitative rather than guessed).
  - **Jazz standard "My Funny Valentine"** — the standout example: Dm – Dm(maj7)/C♯ – Dm7/C (3rd inv.) – Dm6/B♭, a genuinely chromatic descending bass line (D-C♯-C-B♭) under what's mostly the *same* D minor triad shape held in place — until the final half-step drop, where the identical triad shape reinterprets as a **B♭ major 7 chord** (relative major) purely because the bass moved under it. Without the inversions, the same progression "still sounds cool" but "really misses something" — the bass no longer targets the target chord the same way.
  - **Matteo Carcassi, Etude in A major** (classical guitar staple) — second chord is an **E7 first inversion**; played root-position instead, "it just would not be the same" — classical repertoire treats every voice with this level of intentionality far more than typical guitar-strum contexts do.
- **"Neighbor IV chord"** — moving to the IV chord (D, in the key of A) without jumping the bass to D's root: hold the bass on A instead (A is D's own 5th), giving **D/A — a second-inversion D**. Framed loosely as "a neighbor IV chord" when the change is quick; the underlying idea is keeping the key's own root under the bass through a IV move rather than relocating it.
- **Closing reframe:** guitarists usually mean the *shape* sense of "inversion" — jumping between a chord's inversion voicings purely as fingerings/fretboard-mapping practice (e.g. cycling through a D7 chord's inversion shapes while still "just thinking of D," not asking a bass player to actually sound each one) — which Jared explicitly separates from the *functional* sense this video is really about (a specific moment in a real piece where the bass line itself has an inversion happening). Both are worth practicing; they are not the same skill. A future video on jazz chord-inversion shapes is teased.
