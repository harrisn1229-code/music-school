# Vocal Harmony

Part of [[Music Theory]] → [[Chords]]. How to build a second (or third) melodic line that supports the lead without just copying it — the vocal-arrangement counterpart to [[Chord Progressions]]. Companion to [[1 Harmony Vocal Track]] (the recording/mix-chain side of this) and [[Layering and Stacking]].

## Picking the interval

- **Thirds are the default** — stack a harmony voice a third above (or below) the melody; rich and consonant, and the standard first move.
- **Major 3rd = 4 semitones, minor 3rd = 3 semitones** above the melody note. Ear-reference: the opening interval of "Happy Birthday" is a major 3rd.
- **Stay diatonic** — pick major or minor 3rds to match the underlying key/chord at each point, not a fixed interval start-to-finish. A harmony built from *only* major 3rds against a diatonic melody goes out of key and sounds wrong; mixing major/minor 3rds by ear (or reading them off the chord) is what keeps it in the scale.
- **Sixths = the same relationship, inverted an octave** — functionally interchangeable with a third, different register.
- **Fifths are more neutral** — a 3rd carries emotional color (major = brighter, minor = sadder); a 5th supports without pushing the emotional read either way. Use a 5th when you want harmonic weight without more "feeling."
- **Chord-tone harmonies** — for a first attempt, or a fuller stack, harmonize using the exact notes of the underlying chord (root/3rd/5th) rather than a fixed melodic interval; build up from one added note to the full chord stack.

## The four motion types

How the harmony line moves *relative to* the melody:

| Motion | Both voices move... | Feel |
|---|---|---|
| **Parallel** | same direction, same interval | the default "harmony a third above" approach — usually works, but can go dissonant against the chord at some points (nudge that spot to a 4th/5th instead) |
| **Similar** | same direction, different interval | the fix for a dissonant parallel spot |
| **Oblique** | one voice holds a note, the other moves around it | a sustained note under a moving lyric/response — doubles as a [[Arrangement and Song Form\|call-and-response]] device, works on instruments too |
| **Contrary** | opposite directions | still blends despite diverging; a distinct color from parallel harmony |

## Building it without a second real take

- **Reharmonize a duplicated vocal in Auto-Tune** — duplicate the lead take, then manually/graphically retune the *duplicate* onto a target scale degree (e.g. the 5th) instead of singing a second performance. Optionally shift the duplicate's register/formant toward a different voice character for separation. → [[Pitch and Formant]]

## Delivery

- **Tight-mouth vowels blend better** — wide-open vowels are harder to harmonize cleanly; aim for a narrower mouth shape ("la la la," not a wide "ah") for a tighter blend between voices.

## From videos

- **Interval taxonomy with reasons, plus contour matching/oblique harmony as a named pair.** Thirds are the default (deviate specifically when the "correct" third clashes with the chord underneath — Beatles' "Two of Us"); sixths are inverted thirds (same notes, shifted an octave — "Islands in the Stream"); fifths are deliberately AVOIDED precisely because they're too consonant — two voices a 5th apart can blend into an uncanny single "mega-voice" (used deliberately for that effect on Bowie's "Blackstar"); 3-part harmony leans on thirds/fourths, occasionally dropping to unison for emphasis (boygenius). **Contour matching** (harmony line shares the lead's up/down shape) is the default; **oblique harmony** breaks it deliberately — the harmony holds one fixed note while the lead melody moves freely (Green Day's "Basket Case," Sinatra's "Something Stupid," Beatles' "Please Please Me"). **Unison + manual double tracking**: two voices on the identical line never align perfectly, producing a phasing/thickening effect (Elton John/Kiki Dee) — when it's the SAME singer performing the part twice, that's manual double tracking (Nirvana, Beatles' "Back in the USSR"); a straight copy-paste doesn't work, the two takes need genuine performance variance to phase against each other. → [[Artificial Double Tracking (ADT)]] for the automated technique invented to replace this — David Bennett Music Theory, [[How do vocal harmonies work]]
