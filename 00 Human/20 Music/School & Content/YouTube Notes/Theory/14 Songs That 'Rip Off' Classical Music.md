# 14 Songs That 'Rip Off' Classical Music

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yknBXOSlFQs)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yknBXOSlFQs)

## Synopsis

A survey of pop songs built on note-for-note or near-identical classical melodies — the throughline being that none of these ever result in a lawsuit, because the classical source material is in the **public domain** (composition copyright expires 70 years after the composer's death, and almost all pre-20th-century classical music is well past that mark).

## Techniques

- **The public-domain rule, stated precisely, as the reason none of this catalog triggers legal risk**: composition copyright lasts 70 years after the composer's death in most countries — Pachelbel (d. 1706), Leoncavallo, Chopin (d. 1849), Bach, Beethoven, and Rachmaninoff (whose case here is the one exception below) are all comfortably past that line. → [[Sample Clearance]] (added a new "public domain" section covering this exception in full).
- **A recurring specific borrowing mechanism across nearly every example: pop version = a simplified, less-ornamented version of the classical melody, not an unrelated new tune.** Maroon 5's "Memories" ↔ Pachelbel's Canon in D (same chord progression; the pop melody is literally the classical melody with notes removed). Elvis Presley's "Can't Help Falling in Love" ↔ Martini's "Plaisir d'Amour" (same relationship — simplified, de-ornamented). Queen's "It's a Hard Life" ↔ Leoncavallo's *Pagliacci* aria (identical melodic contour, but Mercury changes chords once per bar instead of twice — harmonic rhythm halved, same net simplification principle).
- **Meter/timing changes as a specific way to disguise a melodic lift without changing the notes**: Elvis's "Surrender" (from de Curtis's "Torna a Surriento") keeps the melody near-identical but shifts from the original's 3/4 into 4/4, and inserts an extra bar after each line — breaking up the phrase rhythm is enough to make a melodically-close lift feel structurally distinct.
- **A songwriter's own claimed inspiration story can be wrong about the actual mechanism, even when the influence itself is real**: John Lennon said "Because" (Beatles) was Beethoven's "Moonlight Sonata" chords played backwards (after hearing Yoko Ono play it) — but the video's harmonic analysis shows this isn't literally true; the real relationship is that "Because" takes Moonlight Sonata's opening arpeggio shape and elaborates it differently (climbing back down instead of repeating, adding an extra bass note) rather than reversing the chord sequence. Worth remembering when treating an artist's own account of a borrow as ground truth — the *fact* of influence and the *claimed mechanism* of it are separable, and only the transcript/notes can confirm the latter.
- **The one exception in this catalog where the source ISN'T safely public domain**: flagged in the video's own chapter list (Eric Carmen vs. Rachmaninoff) as a different case from the others — Rachmaninoff's death (1943) is recent enough that some of his catalog's public-domain status is more complicated/jurisdiction-dependent than the centuries-old sources above it, worth treating as a caution against assuming "classical = automatically free" without checking the specific composer's dates.

## Catalog (pairs cited)

Maroon 5 "Memories" ↔ Pachelbel, Canon in D (same chords + simplified melody) · Queen "It's a Hard Life" ↔ Leoncavallo, *Pagliacci* ("Vesti la giubba") · Elvis Presley "Can't Help Falling in Love" ↔ Martini, "Plaisir d'Amour" · Elvis Presley "Surrender" ↔ de Curtis, "Torna a Surriento" (meter changed 3/4→4/4) · Radiohead "Exit Music (For a Film)" ↔ Chopin, Prelude in E minor (near-identical opening, diverges after) · Beatles "Because" ↔ Beethoven, "Moonlight Sonata" (Lennon's own "played backwards" claim not borne out by the actual chords, per this video's analysis) · Procol Harum "A Whiter Shade of Pale" ↔ Bach, Orchestral Suite No. 3 ("Air on the G String") — see also [[Songs Inspired By Bach]] for a fuller writeup of this same pair · a segment on Pachelbel's "Minuet in G" as a second widely-reused source · Eric Carmen ("All By Myself"/"Never Gonna Fall in Love Again") ↔ Rachmaninoff (Piano Concerto No. 2 / Symphony No. 2) — the one pair in this list where public-domain status is genuinely more complicated than the others.
