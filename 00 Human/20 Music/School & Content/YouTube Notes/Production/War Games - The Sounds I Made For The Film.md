# War Games: The Sounds I Made For The Film

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QZBETbh0kaI)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QZBETbh0kaI)

## Synopsis

A history-and-workflow walkthrough of scoring 1983's *War Games* (Matthew Broderick/Ally Sheedy, composer Arthur B. Rubinstein) on a Synclavier II — Marinelli's original 1980s-era floppy disks still load and play. Covers how the Synclavier functioned as an early all-in-one DAW (sequencer + sound design + multitrack recording in one machine), the FM synthesis patches used for the song "Video Fever," clock-syncing a LinnDrum off the Synclavier's own click via a Dr. Click unit, and the constrained, arrange-while-building workflow that limited polyphony forced.

## Techniques

- **The Synclavier as an early all-in-one DAW**: sequencer with transport-style physical buttons (record/punch/continue/start/stop/fast-forward/rewind) modeled on a 24-track tape machine's transport rather than a menu/command interface — deliberately more physically direct than learning keyboard shortcuts. Floppy disks stored per-project cues, labeled with reel/cue numbering (e.g. "M22" = reel 2, cue 2) matching the physical film-reel workflow of the era (a feature film spanning 5-6 twenty-minute reels).
- **All-FM synthesis patches for the "Video Fever" cue**: 10 tracks total — an FM bass, an opening riff bass line, a "ska guitar"-style rhythm part, doubled tracks, an icy transitional "snowflakes" texture, and a delayed-vibrato "effect" sound triggerable live from the keyboard (not just sequenced).
- **Clock-sync chain**: the Synclavier's own internal click feeds a Dr. Click unit, which converts it to a 48 ppqn (pulses-per-quarter-note) clock and drives a LinnDrum in sync — the same clock-sync approach later demonstrated on a modern analog-dubstep rig (see [[How I Create Dubstep on Analog Synths (without a computer)]]), showing the technique's lineage back to 1980s hardware sync standards.
- **Arrange-while-building workflow, forced by limited polyphony**: the Synclavier's voice count (32 for a long stretch) meant complex, multi-track arrangements would run out of voices, so the workflow was to bounce completed parts to multitrack tape in stages (bass, then the next figure, etc.), printing EQ/effects/reverb at bounce time rather than deferring to a later mix pass — because studios typically had only one hardware reverb unit shared across everything. Live instruments (drums, guitar, bass) were added on top of the bounced synth tracks for the final blend of digital precision + analog point/attack + live-player dynamics.
