# the secrets behind Mk.gee's HYPNOTIC production!?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=969p5Q4JSZU)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=969p5Q4JSZU)

## Synopsis

loner reverse-engineers Mk.gee's production from the outside in — Mk.gee has done almost no interviews, so the source material is one onstage gear Q&A, listening to *Two Star & the Dream Police*, and an old Mk.gee Instagram Live. The video builds a from-scratch Mk.gee-style track piece by piece: a baritone-range guitar bass part run through a "controlled chaos" effects chain built around a distorted, noise-gated reverb (the actual pedal Mk.gee named, an FX Ranger, reverse-engineered because the built-in noise gate was never mentioned in his own description of it); 80s-pop-influenced chords (lifted from The Police's "Every Breath You Take"); texture as a defining idea — one-off, once-only Sonic moments rather than a continuous layer, covering glitched-out lossy vocal chops, faint filtered background vocals, and "so weird you'd skip past it" modular-style synth presets; live drums recorded with a "play light, then push it hard" saturation philosophy that depends on a velocity-sensitive sample library, not fixed one-shots; a from-scratch recreation of Mk.gee's CZ-5000-based synth sound (Casio CZ-5000) built by layering two CZ-emulation VSTs and running them through flanger → chorus → envelope-mode-noise tape → plate reverb → distortion → heavy end-of-chain compression; and a set of specific arrangement tricks — short random-sound song openers, bringing the bass in slightly early for a smoother transition, a guitar-feedback intro, tonal synth-percussion intros, and bounced/distorted drum-fill ear candy. The single most valuable idea in the video is the **noise gate placed FIRST in a reverb+distortion send chain**, so the "crazy distorted reverb" only fires on the loudest notes — this is presented as *the* secret behind the whole Mk.gee sound, everything else (dynamic-range performance, texture layering, the CZ chain) builds around it.

## Techniques

**The core guitar/bass chain ("Controlled Chaos"):**
- **Baritone-range guitar as the bass instrument.** Mk.gee's actual gear (from his one onstage interview): a 60s reissue Fender Jaguar modified to a baritone range — this is what gives his low, almost bass-like guitar tone. No baritone guitar owned here → substitute a baritone-range sampler/VST for the same low-register character.
- **Front-of-chain: preamp → compressor → amp, preamp driven hard enough that only the loudest notes break up.** Crank the preamp specifically so the loudest played notes get a crunchy, distorted texture — quieter notes should stay comparatively clean. This *requires* a wide dynamic range in the performance (see below) or the effect never triggers on anything. *(general starting point — no exact gain figures given)* → [[Decapitator]], [[Gain Staging]]
- **Wide dynamic range is a hard requirement, not a mixing nicety, for this whole chain to work.** Called out twice in the video as "hugely important" — some notes need to be played really quiet, some really loud, because the preamp-crunch AND the noise-gated reverb below are both keyed off note volume. A flat, compressed performance defeats the entire effect. → [[Gain Staging]]
- **Overall tone stage: chorus → flanger → tape emulation**, added after the preamp/comp/amp stage for general color/movement before the secret-sauce reverb gets added. → [[Chorus-Ensemble]], [[Phaser-Flanger]], [[Kramer Tape]], [[Modulation]]
- **THE secret source — a noise-gated distorted reverb send, gate placed FIRST in the chain.** Mk.gee's actual pedal (named on camera): a Death By Audio-style "FX Ranger" — "a crazy distorted kind of crunched-out reverb." What he never mentioned: it has a **noise gate built in**, so the distorted-reverb effect only activates above a certain input volume. To replicate: create a send/return track (Ableton: right-click → Route to This Track) → **Reverb → Distortion → Compressor** on the return, then put a **Noise Gate BEFORE all of it** (first device in the chain) with the threshold set so **only the loudest notes cross it and trigger the distorted reverb** — quiet notes pass through dry, loud notes suddenly bloom into a crunched-out reverb wash. This is what makes a static bass part "feel alive." → [[Gate]], [[Reverb]] (effect), [[Saturation and Distortion]]
- **Push the send's Reverb + Distortion more aggressive for chords than for the bass part** — same gated-reverb-send recipe, but on an 80s-pop chord part (see below) the reverb/distortion stage was driven harder to help fill out the arrangement's space, versus a more restrained setting on the bass.

**Chords / 80s-pop influence:**
- **Lift a recognizable 80s-pop chord progression as a writing shortcut, then run it through the same gated chain.** Took the chords from The Police's "Every Breath You Take" directly, re-recorded them with a wide dynamic range (same discipline as the guitar bass part), then ran the same send-based gated distorted-reverb chain over them, pushed more aggressive than on the bass to fill the track's space. → [[Chord Progressions]]

**Texture — defined as one-off moments, not a continuous layer:**
- **Texture = "moments that may only appear once but take you deeper into the sonic world," not a background wash.** Mk.gee describes *Two Star and the Dream Police* as "a Sonic story book," and the textures are the quirky, standout moments that stick with you — a useful, concrete redefinition of a word producers throw around vaguely ("adds so much texture"). → [[Ear Candy and Hiding Synths]]
- **Glitched-out, lossy vocal chop as background texture (inspired by "How Many Miles").** Record a vocal chop with a melody you like → basic processing (reverb via [[Little AlterBoy|Ultra Boy]]-style pitch/reverb chain + compression) → run it through a **lossy-codec plugin** (Goodhertz "Lossy," presets auditioned until one hit) for that "old MP3/nostalgic phone" sound → layer in a short segment of a **separate texture loop pitched up two full octaves** on top. → [[Ghz Lossy 3]], [[Ear Candy and Hiding Synths]], [[Layering and Stacking]]
- **Faint, filtered background vocals as a second vocal-texture technique (from "New Low").** Simpler than the chop above: record a plain vocal phrase, apply simple processing including a **room reverb**, and sit it low/filtered in the background — a different, lighter way to work vocal texture into a track than the chop-and-lossy move. → [[Ear Candy and Hiding Synths]], [[Reverb]] (effect)
- **"Skip-past" synth presets as a deliberate texture source.** Mk.gee's synth texture layers are ones that "nine times out of 10, if I clicked on it, I would skip straight past it" while browsing presets — i.e. deliberately picking the *weird*, glitchy-sounding preset over the pleasant/obvious one. Sourced from clicking through sound-effects presets in **Analog Lab** banks until one of these "wrong" sounds landed. Reasoned as a byproduct of Mk.gee's actual modular-synth setup (glitchy, imperfect moments are inherent to modular gear); the preset-browsing move is the accessible substitute for not owning a modular rig. → [[Ear Candy and Hiding Synths]], [[Plugin Wishlist]]

**Live drums — "play light, then push it hard":**
- **Play/program drum hits SOFT for tone, then boost the level afterward, rather than hitting hard for volume directly.** Pulled from an old Mk.gee Instagram Live where he described his whole drum-sound philosophy as "just saturation... play light and then push it hard." Sounds counterproductive (why play quiet if you're going to boost it?) but the reasoning is real: a softly-struck drum has a fundamentally *different tone character* than a hard-hit one, not just a lower volume — so playing light gets the desired tone, and boosting afterward (gain, then saturation) gets the desired level, two separate problems solved by two separate moves instead of one hit trying to do both. *(general starting point — no numeric gain figures given)* → [[Saturation and Distortion]], [[Gain Staging]]
- **This technique REQUIRES a velocity-sensitive sample library, not a static one-shot kit.** A one-shot's tone doesn't change with velocity — only the volume does — so the "play light for tone, push hard for level" trick is inaudible on a plain one-shot kit. Needs a proper multi-velocity-layered VST drum instrument where low-velocity hits are audibly a *different* tone than high-velocity hits, not just quieter. Demonstrated on the stock Kontakt drum kit — A/B'd kick at high vs. low velocity, described as "night and day." → [[Kontakt 7]]
- **Group all live-drum elements (kick + snare roll + normal snare + hi-hat) to one mixer/bus track, then compress and saturate them TOGETHER, not per-element.** After building the loop from individually-triggered hits, everything routes to a shared bus so the compression and saturation (the actual "McGee sound" ingredient per his own Instagram description) is a group/glue process rather than element-by-element seasoning. → [[Glue Compressor]], [[Saturation and Distortion]]

**CZ-style vintage synth section (a "B-section" recipe):**
- **Source instrument named on the record: a Casio CZ-5000.** Mk.gee's song titled "CZ" uses a real Casio CZ-5000 (phase-distortion synth) for its rich vintage character. No hardware/plugin CZ owned — flagged to [[Vintage Gear Worklist]] (Casio CZ-101 already queued there as the closest tracked unit; CZ-5000 is a different specific model in the same family).
- **Layer two DIFFERENT CZ-emulation VSTs rather than relying on one.** Base layer: "Virtual CZ" (a CZ-emulation VST), preset **"Sharp Angle"**. Second layer: a *different* CZ emulation, from Arturia (captioned "artua"). Explicitly noted that even stacked, the two emulations don't get anywhere near the richness of the real synth on their own — the richness has to come from processing (below), not the source layering alone. → [[Plugin Wishlist]]
- **The CZ processing chain, in order: flanger → chorus → tape (envelope-mode noise) → plate reverb → distortion → heavy compression.** (1) Flanger and chorus first for movement/width. (2) A tape-emulation stage — the video uses "Reels" specifically because its **noise knobs have an envelope mode**, so tape hiss/noise only sounds *while a note is playing* instead of a constant fuzzy noise bed underneath everything (closest owned tape plugin without a documented envelope-noise mode: [[Kramer Tape]] — note the gap). (3) A **plate reverb** next — "plates tend to be nice and clean," used here as the clean-space stage before the chain gets dirtied up again. (4) Distortion, then (5) **heavy compression placed at the very END of the chain** — the compression is what "brings out all the nice textures from the noise and the flanger" earlier in the chain, i.e. driving a hard compressor after multiple coloration stages reveals detail those stages added rather than just leveling the part. → [[Phaser-Flanger]], [[Chorus-Ensemble]], [[Kramer Tape]], [[Little Plate]], [[Decapitator]], [[Glue Compressor]], [[Saturation and Distortion]]
- **Leave the first note of every bar empty — a CZ-specific arrangement detail, not a generic rule.** Copied directly from listening to "CZ": the first note of each bar is left silent in the synth part, leaving room for the bass to start the loop rather than the synth and bass colliding on beat 1.
- **Layer a second, loud lead synth on top for a dynamic "jolt."** Applying the same wide-dynamic-range principle used on the guitar chain: add other synth layers with the lead part played/programmed noticeably loud against the quieter surrounding parts, for a jump in intensity the listener physically feels.
- **B-section drums: a free modular drum machine VST, used for its tonal (pitched) sounds specifically.** Mk.gee uses modular digital drum machines on certain tracks (named example: "New Low") for a glitchy-but-still-vintage character; recreated with an unnamed free modular drum-machine VST, leaning on its tonal/pitched drum sounds rather than its noise/percussive ones.

**Arrangement tricks:**
- **Start songs with a short, random, "walked-in-mid-scene" sound — drums, a synth, or anything that fits — rather than a clean intro.** Identified as a pattern across "pretty much all" of Mk.gee's intros. Demonstrated by starting a section (styled after "Alesis") with just kick + snare hitting, no other context yet. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **Bring the bass in slightly EARLY, ahead of the beat/section change, for a smoother transition.** loner's own addition on top of the Mk.gee-inspired intro move — the bass entering a hair before the "official" downbeat of the new section reads as a smoother handoff than a hard-cut entrance. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]], [[Pocket and Microtiming]]
- **Guitar-feedback intro (inspired by "DNM").** Mk.gee opens some songs with guitar feedback — an abrasive sound used tastefully as an intro texture rather than an accident. To record: run the guitar into an amp with recording feedback loop set up, then physically hold the guitar up to the speaker until feedback builds — no dedicated feedback pedal needed, just amp + proximity. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **Tonal synth percussion as an intro layer (inspired by "How Many Miles").** That track opens with tonal (pitched, not just noise-transient) synth percussion; recreated using a synth's "memory mode" preset bank, picking a percussion-flavored sound from it. → [[Arrangement and Song Form]]
- **Bounce a snare roll to audio and distort it for a bit-crushed ear-candy drum fill (inspired by "Alesis").** Since the loop already has a programmed snare roll, printing/bouncing it to audio and running it through a distortion stage turns it into a distinct, bit-crushed-sounding fill that "pops through" as ear candy in the A-section, rather than sound-designing a separate fill from scratch. → [[Ear Candy and Hiding Synths]], [[Saturation and Distortion]]
