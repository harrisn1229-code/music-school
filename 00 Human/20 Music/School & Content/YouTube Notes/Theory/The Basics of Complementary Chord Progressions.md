# The Basics of Complementary Chord Progressions

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0RWf94d8QNg)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0RWf94d8QNg)

## Synopsis
A short songwriting-focused lesson on why (and how) to write a *different* chord progression for each song section instead of looping one progression for the whole track. Max argues variety of feeling section-to-section is the payoff, then gives a concrete method: choose deliberately whether/when each section touches the tonic chord, since that single choice determines how "arrived" or "still traveling" a section feels. Sets up [[Understanding Chords - The Number System]] as the next video in his sequence.

## What's taught
- **Give each section its own progression on purpose.** A single vamp for the whole song (also covered from the loop-based-production angle in [[Loop-Based Harmony]]) is easier to write but "kind of boring" — writing a distinct progression per section (verse/chorus/bridge/intro/outro) gives each one its own emotional identity and creates a flow of changing feelings across the song instead of a static one. → [[Chord Progressions]], [[Musical Form]]
- **Withhold the tonic from a section to make its eventual arrival land harder.** Worked example: a 4-chord verse in C major that never touches the I chord (F–Dm–Em–G), followed by a 2-chord chorus that does (C–F) — because the ear tracks where "home" is, hitting C for the first time at the chorus reads as an arrival/payoff. Not a rule to apply every time, but a deliberate lever: decide per section whether landing on (or avoiding) the tonic serves the feeling you want. → [[Keys]], [[Chord Progressions]]
- **Every chord within a key has its own distinct "feel" relative to the tonic, even chords that share the same quality.** E minor, D minor and A minor are all minor chords in the key of C, but each feels different against the C tonic because of *which* scale degree it sits on — worth consciously auditioning alternatives from the same key rather than defaulting to the first minor chord that comes to mind.
- **Deciding whether adjacent sections should repeat or avoid the same ending/starting chord is also a deliberate choice** — ending a section on the tonic and starting the next section on it too (for continuity) is just as valid as deliberately avoiding a repeat (for freshness); the point is to make the choice on purpose and evaluate whether you like the result, not to follow a rule.
