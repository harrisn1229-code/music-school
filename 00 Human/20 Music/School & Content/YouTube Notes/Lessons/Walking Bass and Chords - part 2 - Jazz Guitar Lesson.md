## What's taught

**Core organizing idea: most turnaround bass motion is just "connect to the next root via the 5th (or ♭5) of the current chord."** Larsen states this as the single takeaway of the first example: systematically using the 5th (or flatted 5th) to move from chord to chord in a turnaround "is still going to sound fairly logical" and — just as important for solo guitar — "quite playable," i.e. it's the default, low-friction choice because the 5th sits in a comfortable spot relative to most jazz chord grips. This is the same "root, approach-note, new root" mechanic already documented on [[Jazz Comping — Rhythmic Motif and Walking Bassline]] § *Bonus: walking bassline with the chords*, here demonstrated with an actual reason (playability + logic) rather than left as an unexplained rule.

**Example 1 — systematic 5th-based bassline over the Rhythm Changes A section (`B♭ G7 | Cm7 F7 | B♭ G7 | Cm7 F7 | B♭ B♭7 | E♭ E♭m6 | B♭/F G7 | Cm7 F7`):**
- **B♭ → G7:** approached via the 5th of B♭ (F) leading up toward G. *(uncertain exact note per the garbled transcript — reconstructed from "use the fifth of the B flat to go up there.")*
- **G7 → Cm7 → F7 → Dm:** the fifth used again at each step ("again fifth," "also by the fifth") — the systematic-5th pattern repeating through the vi-ii-V-ish motion inside the turnaround.
- **Dm → G7:** a **chromatic leading note, A♭**, used instead of the 5th — called out explicitly as a deliberate exception to the systematic-5th rule, i.e. even inside "always use the 5th" there's a spot where a half-step chromatic approach reads better.
- **G7 → Cm7:** the 5th again.
- **F7 → Fm (the borrowed-chord/mode-mixture move, [[Borrowed Chords]]):** **use the 5th, not the 3rd, as the connecting note** — stated as a specific rule, not a one-off choice: "the third is going to sound a bit weird… it's going to mask the fact that we're changing to a minor chord." *(Actionable rule: on a dominant→same-root-minor move, the 3rd telegraphs the wrong chord quality on its way in — the 5th is harmonically neutral and lets the minor 3rd of the target chord itself announce the quality change instead of the bass doing it prematurely.)*
- **F7 → B♭7:** approached via the **3rd** of F7.
- **B♭7 → E♭maj7:** Larsen **stays on the same bass note** across this change ("I'm staying on that note… I repeat the notes") rather than moving — because this stretch of the progression (`B♭ → B♭7 → E♭ → E♭m6`, [[Rhythm Changes]]'s borrowed-iv move) is *already* moving chromatically on its own, so the bassline doesn't need to add motion of its own. Same "repeat when the harmony already supplies the movement" logic as the Joe Pass repeated-note rule already on [[Jazz Comping — Rhythmic Motif and Walking Bassline]], and the same mechanic as [[Chromatic Bass Lines]] (a held/repeated bass tone under harmony that moves chromatically above or around it).
- **Resolving to B♭maj7 with F in the bass** — a slash-chord/inversion device: voiced as B♭ over F, which Larsen points out is harmonically **the same notes as a D minor triad over F** — i.e. the same pitches read two different functional ways depending on what's implied above. → cross-links [[Chord Inversions]].
- **→ G7 (top of the next chorus):** via the 5th, chromatically up to the root.
- **G7 → Cm7:** a chromatic leading note into Cm7.
- **Cm7 → F7 → B♭ (next A):** the 3rd of F7 used to move up into the top B♭ for the next chorus.

**Example 2 — an alternate, more melodic bassline over the same form, deliberately using more chromatic/leading-note motion and fewer 5ths:**
- **B♭ → G7:** moved **down** with leading notes toward the root — B♭, then A♭, down to G via the **3rd of G7** as the final approach note (a descending chromatic-ish line into the target, contrasting with example 1's upward 5th-based approach to the same change).
- **G7 → Cm7:** the 5th.
- **Cm7 → F7:** down to F via the **3rd of F7**, used as a leading note into B♭ — and here Larsen names the "big win" of this whole bassline style explicitly: **playing the 3rd of the current chord as the connector, when that note is also a chord tone of the target chord**, creates a big, satisfying melodic interval leap into the new chord while still sounding fully "inside" both chords at once — dual-function connecting tone. He describes the effect as "almost like a dominant resolving," i.e. the connecting note briefly makes the line sound like it's walking through an implied secondary-dominant relationship (B♭ → [implied D7] → G) even though no such chord is actually played — a genuinely melodic, non-mechanical option worth reaching for whenever a chord tone happens to be shared between the current and target chords. *(General starting point: before defaulting to the 5th, check whether the 3rd, 5th, or 7th of the current chord is also a chord tone of the next chord — if so, that shared tone often makes the most melodic connector.)*
- **Cm7 → F7:** via the 3rd of Cm7.
- **F7 → Fm:** the 5th again — same rule as example 1, restated as a consistent habit rather than a one-off: avoid the 3rd on a dominant→same-root-minor move.
- **Fm → B♭7:** the 5th.
- **B♭7 → E♭maj7 → E°7 ("e diminished"):** approached via the **3rd of B♭7**.
- **Repeating notes on B♭ with F in the bass** — the same slash-chord/repeated-note device as example 1, reused identically.
- **→ F7:** via the 5th.
- **F7 → B♭ (next chorus, as a triad):** via the 5th again.
- **B♭ → D7 (top of the bridge):** **E♭ used as a chromatic upper-neighbor leading note down into D7** — the hand-off point from the A section into the Rhythm Changes bridge ([[Rhythm Changes]] § *The B section*), a half-step-above approach into the bridge's first dominant.

**Practice and stylistic framing (applies beyond Rhythm Changes):**
- **Mix the two approaches deliberately, don't lock into one.** Larsen names this directly: playing example 1's "very systematic, always-the-5th" approach for five minutes straight gets tiring to listen to and to play; mix in the more melodic/chromatic connector style from example 2 once the systematic version is solid. This is the same "lock in the plain pattern, then vary" sequencing logic already central to [[Jazz Comping — Rhythmic Motif and Walking Bassline]] — here applied to *note choice* rather than *rhythm*.
- **Practice method: write basslines out deliberately before improvising them.** "You need to sit down and just write some baselines, just practice playing them… then you can start changing bits and pieces" — compose fixed basslines first, internalize them, then start altering pieces once comfortable, framed as "fitting patterns together." Directly reusable as a practice step alongside the rhythmic-motif practice sequencing already on [[Jazz Comping — Rhythmic Motif and Walking Bassline]].
- **Repertoire targeting: pick medium-tempo standards built on this turnaround-plus-fourth-degree-and-back shape.** Larsen calls out that "a lot of jazz standards" share this exact structure (a turnaround section plus a move up to the IV and a diminished move back — i.e. the Rhythm Changes A-section shape, not necessarily Rhythm Changes itself) and that they tend to sit at medium tempo specifically, which is what makes simultaneous walking bass + comping actually playable — fast tempos with bass + chords together on solo guitar are "difficult" and, per his own duo-guitar playing experience, not that commonly needed since most playing isn't happening at very fast tempos anyway.
- **Beyond-root bass motion flagged as a further level, not covered here:** composing basslines "the way bass players do," including *not* defaulting to the root on beat 1, is named as "a whole other level" available once the root-based version is comfortable — explicitly deferred to a future lesson, kept simple here on purpose because it's more playable.
