## Synopsis

Jared Borkowski runs through the four most common, most foundational Travis-picking fingerpicking patterns — named after guitarist Merle Travis, the style's signature trait is a thumb that keeps a constant alternating bass (root–fifth) going while the fingers fill in chord tones on top, with melody optionally layered on top of that (the more advanced destination players like Chet Atkins took it). ## What's taught

**The Travis-picking premise.** The picking-hand thumb holds a constant alternating bass — for any given chord, root then fifth (1–5), back and forth, unchanging in rhythm regardless of which of the four patterns is layered on top. The fingers fill out the chord above that bass, and melody can be added on top of *that*, giving a solo guitar the effect of two or three musicians (bass, chords, melody) from one instrument — "the signature of this Travis-picking sound."

**String-grouping convention (applies to all four patterns).** Which strings get used depends only on where the chord's root sits, not on the pattern:
- **6th-string-rooted chord** (e.g. **G**) — thumb alternates strings **6 and 4** for the bass (skip the 5th/A string entirely), fingers work strings **3 and 2**.
- **5th-string-rooted chord** (e.g. **C**) — contained entirely in the four middle strings: **5, 4, 3, 2**.
- **4th-string-rooted chord** (e.g. **D**) — top four strings only: **4, 3, 2, 1**.

**Minimal left-hand fretting tip.** Demoed on the G chord: don't fret notes the pattern doesn't actually use — Jared holds down only the 3rd fret on the 6th string (not a full "textbook" G shape with extra fingers down) since the picking pattern only touches strings 6/4/3/2 anyway. Keeping the fretting hand minimal keeps the picking pattern clean and leaves fingers free for later voicing changes.

### Pattern 1 — Inside-Outside
Finger sequence: **Bass(root) → Inside(string 3) → Bass(5th) → Outside(string 2) → repeat** (using the 6th-string-rooted grouping as the reference; same idea shifted for 5th/4th-string chords). Named for the finger part, since the bass line is identical across all four patterns. Sounds intentionally plain/mechanical played dead-even — that's fine, it's the foundation the other three build from. Demoed on G (6th-string), C (5th-string), and D (4th-string), then run through the full chord progression of Buck Owens' "Love's Gonna Live Here" at half speed then double speed, then under the sung first verse.

### Pattern 2 — Outside-Inside
Exact mirror of Pattern 1: **Bass → Outside → Bass → Inside → repeat.** Same string groupings, same bass line — "you already know what it's going to be... you're just flipping outside and inside." Demoed through the same progression, then under the sung chorus.

### Pattern 3 — Pinch pattern
Flagged as **the most useful of the four** — "if there's one you're ever going to learn it should be this one." Built from two Outside-Inside cycles played back to back, except the very first attack is modified: instead of separately picking Bass then Outside, thumb and **m** (middle) finger pluck **together** in a pinching motion ("the pinch"), and the following beat is left to sustain/ring rather than getting a new attack ("weight"). Full 8-eighth-note figure:
1. **Pinch** — thumb (bass/root) + outside-string finger, plucked simultaneously
2. *(sustain — no new attack, let the pinch ring)*
3. Thumb (bass, alternates to the 5th)
4. Inside
5. Thumb (bass, back to root)
6. Outside
7. Thumb (bass, 5th)
8. Inside

**Jared's preferred variant: drop the very last note** (event 8, the second "Inside") — creates extra syncopated bounce, and on faster tempos opens a beat of breathing room to switch chords cleanly. Can be played straight or with a light swing feel — Jared demos it swung. Demoed on C and D, then through the progression slow/fast, then under the sung second verse — this is also the pattern used in the video's own cold-open intro.

### Pattern 4 — Descending arpeggio
Three-note repeating figure that arpeggiates the chord top-down: **Pinch (thumb bass + outside string together) → Inside (middle voice) → Thumb (bass alone) → repeat.** Because the figure starts on the highest-pitched note (the outside string) and ends on the lowest (the bass), the perceived pitch motion descends each cycle — "the top note of the thumb bassline is part of this arpeggio... descending arpeggio structure," i.e. the bass isn't just rhythm here, it's the bottom of a melodic shape. Demoed on G, then through the progression slow/fast, then used to accompany an improvised guitar solo over the same progression (rather than singing, since the source song only has two vocal sections — verse and chorus).

**Practice routine (implicit across all four).** For each pattern: play it slowly through a full chord progression → double the speed → apply it directly to a real song's verse/chorus/solo. Suggested learning order given the material builds this way: patterns 1 and 2 first (mirror images of each other, build the core alternating-bass/finger-independence coordination), then pattern 3 (explicitly built out of two cycles of pattern 2 plus the pinch), then pattern 4 (adds genuine melodic/arpeggiated shape on top of the same mechanics).
