# Practice Advice From Chick Corea

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gXcOOV_Wnmk)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gXcOOV_Wnmk)

## Synopsis

Jazz guitarist Jens Larsen sits down with Yoran and Timothy (a saxophonist) from a channel he refers to as "Sharpen Eleven" to work through Chick Corea's widely-circulated list of "good, cheap advice for playing in a group" — a list Corea reportedly modeled on Thelonious Monk's famous advice-to-sidemen list. The video was shot before Corea's death and released with an added intro after he passed in February 2021, framed as both a practical checklist and a window into how he thought about music. The three go through the list item by item, mostly in agreement, and spend most of the runtime unpacking *why* each piece of advice is hard to actually do, not just what it says — this is a discussion/commentary video, not a technical lesson with exercises, licks, or notation. No plugins, gear chains, or production content — it's pure improvisation philosophy and ensemble-playing practice, squarely instrument/theory territory. The most valuable through-line, repeated in different words across almost every point, is **restraint as a skill that has to be practiced deliberately**, not something that just shows up once technique is good enough — and the group is explicit that this is *sideman* advice as much as it is soloist advice: several points (balance, blending, "make the other musicians sound good") are framed as things a good accompanist does that rarely get taught directly in lessons.

## What's taught

**The full Chick Corea list, in the order discussed, each with the group's practical gloss:**

1. **"Play only what you hear."** Not "play the right notes" but actually developing an internal ear for the line before playing it — tied directly to how the group frames transcribing: not to collect licks, but to identify by ear which parts of a solo you *actually like*, then go find out theoretically why, so you build your own taste rather than a vocabulary of borrowed licks. *(general starting point: after transcribing a solo, don't move on until you can say specifically why the phrase you liked works — not just "it sounds cool.")*
2. **"If you don't hear anything, don't play anything."** Cited alongside a Jim Hall quote — *"Don't just play something. Sit there."* — as the most aspirational and least literally-practiced point on the whole list; nobody in the conversation claims to actually do this, and it's framed as something that only becomes possible with real confidence, because in a panicked jam-session state the instinct is exactly the opposite (fill space to prove you belong).
3. **Using space in a creative way** (the group's own summary of the space-related points, which recur across the list) — framed as the single hardest thing on the list to execute because the natural instinct under pressure is to fill every gap, not leave one.
4. **"Don't let your fingers and limbs just wander. Place them intentionally."** Flagged as reading as a very piano/drummer-specific instruction — Corea reportedly thought about the piano rhythmically, almost like a drum kit, which the group says makes this point harder to map onto guitar/sax directly, but the underlying idea (motion should be a decision, not a habit) still generalizes.
5. **"Don't improvise on endlessly. Play something with intention. Develop it, then end it — take a break. Don't play something. Sit there."** — same intention theme as #1/#2; the group ties this to a real teaching tension: bebop pedagogy (e.g. the Joe Pass books Jens most often assigns) deliberately drills endless unbroken eighth-note lines as a *learning tool* for building vocabulary and breaking a student out of habitual note choices, even though the end goal (per this list) is the opposite — so continuous eighth notes have a real practice purpose, but shouldn't be mistaken for the finished style. Cross-referenced against real players: a Charlie Parker solo has far more space and shorter motivic phrases than people assume; Pat Martino is cited as the clear "long continuous eighth-note line" reference point on guitar, contrasted with Parker's more motivic phrasing.
6. **"Leave space, create space, intentionally create places where you don't play."** (explicitly numbered "five" by Jens in the video, i.e. the list's 5th point) — the group says this is genuinely something you have to schedule practice time for, not something that happens by osmosis; nobody present has a standing practice routine for it despite agreeing it matters. A concrete practice method suggested: from Mick Goodrick's *The Advancing Guitarist* — take a rhythmic phrase and reduce it to a bare rhythmic staff with rests marked in (no pitches), then improvise, filling in only the rhythmic template, forcing rests to happen at fixed points regardless of what you feel like playing. Jens says he's tried this by taking a transcribed solo (a Baseman solo, per the transcript), stripping it down to *only* its rhythm, and improvising new pitch content over that fixed rhythmic skeleton.
7. **"Make your sound blend. Listen to your sound, and adjust it to the rest of the band and the room."** Framed as generic-sounding advice that's actually rarely taught concretely — the group's specific gloss: know your *role* in the band/song structure first, then adjust. A drummer's advice to Jens in conservatory, from a fellow student rather than a teacher: "your chords have to connect with the snare drum" — and, analogously, a bassist should connect with the hi-hat in a jazz context (vs. the kick drum in a more pop/rock context). Room acoustics get called out directly: playing school-gym concerts with terrible acoustics taught Jens to keep his tone mid-focused and cut low end aggressively, specifically because a double bass is extremely easy to bury with excess low end from a guitar amp in a room with bad bass response — sound-check specifically for this, and know how to fix it quickly once you hear the problem.
8. **Balancing multiple simultaneous sound sources** (Corea's original point references balancing a drum kit or multiple keyboards against each other) — mapped to guitar as gain-staging your comping-vs-soloing volume: the group calls out a very common failure mode where a guitarist raises volume to solo, then drops back to comping *louder* than their pre-solo level, because "back to normal" during a solo doesn't recalibrate correctly by ear. A cautionary anecdote: an amp with an extremely non-linear volume taper (quiet up to a point, then a sudden jump to painfully loud within a fraction of a turn) is flagged as a real practical hazard worth testing for before a gig, not just a mixing habit to fix.
9. **"Don't make any of your music mechanically adjust through patterns of habit. Create each sound, phrase, or piece with a choice, deliberately."** The group's strongest pushback on the whole list: several of their favorite players (Allan Holdsworth, Pat Metheny, Michael Brecker's "false-fingering"/overtone effects, Charlie Parker's near-identical phrase over the IV chord of rhythm changes across many recordings) demonstrably *do* rely on mechanical, habitual patterns and still sound great — the conclusion reached is that the point is really a caution against playing on autopilot early or reflexively in a solo (right at the start, before earning it), not a blanket ban on having recurring signature moves; *placement in the solo*, not the existence of the habit, is what separates a "lick" from a mechanical crutch. Related: players with the least mechanical, most "surprising" phrasing were named as Warne Marsh, Paul Desmond, and Chet Baker — cool-jazz players who follow the logic of the last phrase played rather than a rehearsed shape; Bill Frisell, John Scofield, and Jim Hall were named as guitarists in the same camp.
10. **"Guide your choice of what to play by what you like, not by what someone else will think."** Discussed as a confidence/experience issue more than a technical one — playing to what a specific room or crowd seems to want (the group describes clubs where 16th-note "burning" playing gets a visibly better response regardless of what the music actually calls for) is named as a real trap that pulls a soloist away from their own taste. A Mark Turner quote is referenced (from an interview, paraphrased): Turner said he dislikes the standard "build up to an explosion" solo arc and deliberately avoids that shape — cited as an example of a player consciously choosing a different intensity contour than what an audience typically expects.
11. **"Use contrast and balance the elements — high, low, fast, slow, loud, soft."** Singled out explicitly as **the single best piece of advice on the entire list.** The concrete practice method given: record yourself soloing, then go back and audit the recording against a checklist — register (am I stuck in one range?), tempo/density (same rhythmic activity level throughout?), dynamics (same volume?), tension/release arc (same shape every time?), note count (same density?). The point isn't that every solo needs extremes of all six — some great players (Mark Turner is cited again here) barely touch the ends of the spectrum and still sound great — the point is *knowing which of these you default to and which you never touch*, so the choice becomes conscious. Explicitly linked to why self-critique doesn't work in real time while improvising: you can't create and evaluate simultaneously any more than a writer can edit while drafting the same sentence — record first, critique after, as two separate passes.
12. **"Play to make the other musicians sound good. Play things that will make the overall music sound good."** Called out as the most purely "sideman" item on the list — summarized bluntly as "how not to get fired." Tied to "leave your ego at the door," and to reading the room when a rhythmic idea you're pushing isn't landing with the rest of the band: the instruction given is to stop forcing it and adapt to where the band actually is, rather than continuing to impose an idea nobody else picked up.
13. **"Play with a relaxed body. Always release whatever tension you create."** Flagged again as reading as piano-specific (possibly rooted in classical training and posture consciousness), but the saxophonist in the conversation connects it to real physical strain from instrument ergonomics — a baritone sax at ~10 kg worn around the neck for a set is cited as a genuinely unnatural physical load, and posture problems in jazz musicians (citing visibly poor postures in archival photos of Phil Woods and Gerry Mulligan, vs. a notably relaxed, upright Charlie Parker) are discussed as a real, under-addressed occupational-health issue, not just a "feel" note. A guitar-specific observation: switching from a standard-neck guitar to an ergonomic-neck instrument (a Strandberg) after ~20 years of a bent-forward playing posture was described as "liberating," letting the player sit upright with the neck raised rather than parallel to the floor.
14. **"Create space, begin, develop, and end phrases with intention."** A specific self-critique and a specific fix are both given: Jens says his own weak point is *how phrases start and end* — starting reactively/on-autopilot and then just dropping the phrase somewhere near the end without deciding where it was headed. The fix he practices: **decide the target/last note of a phrase before playing the beginning**, effectively planning backward from the ending rather than forward from the start — explicitly compared to the foundational skill of learning to resolve into a II-V-I target chord tone. Named as something the best phrase-shapers (Michele Petrucciani, Pat Metheny, Michael Brecker) sustain across a whole line, described as staying "aimed at the next point" the entire way through rather than losing focus partway.
15. **"Never beat or pound your instrument. Play it easily and gracefully."** The group flags a real tension here — Corea's own playing is described as frequently *not* subtle, sometimes deliberately hard-hitting and percussive (consistent with him describing his own approach to piano as drummer-like), which reads as advice aimed at *other* musicians' comping/accompaniment behavior rather than a rule Corea applied to his own soloing. Read as time-and-place advice, not an absolute.
16. **"Create space, then place something in it."** — discussed as the most actionable, practical version of the space-related points: let a comping pattern or groove run uninterrupted for a bar or two, deliberately hold back, then drop in a short, isolated phrase and let it breathe rather than filling continuously. Suggested as roughly a 50/50 target between playing and space, not the near-100%-playing default most improvisers gravitate to. A guitar-specific way to "create space while still playing": open-voiced chords, or (on saxophone) a single sustained note held through a chord change, letting the listener hear the harmony move underneath a static note.
17. **"Use mimicry sparsely. Mostly create phrases that contrast with and develop the phrases of the other players."** Referenced against the well-known Miles Davis/Herbie Hancock footage where Hancock mirrors exactly what Miles just played and Miles visibly reacts with annoyance — occasional rhythmic mimicry (e.g. picking up and running with a soloist's 2-against-3 figure) can work as a one-off device, but comping that continuously "parrots" a soloist reads as insecure rather than supportive, and denies the soloist the contrasting space that actually helps them. A Michael Brecker anecdote (secondhand, from an interview) is cited as a real-world case where this backfired: Brecker found Hancock's tendency to closely track his harmonic movement (rather than holding independent ground) frustrating when Brecker was deliberately playing "outside" the changes, because it removed the tension he was trying to create.
- **Closing synthesis, stated directly as the group's own bonus rule:** *"If you don't know what to play, just don't play."* Framed as not a cop-out but a real, underused option — leaving space is better than filling it with something uncertain, and it takes real discipline to reach for it since most improvisers' natural instinct pulls the other way. Monk and Dave Brubeck (behind Paul Desmond specifically) are given as reference points for comping that's deliberately extremely sparse — Brubeck is described as "barely comping" behind Desmond, opening up huge amounts of space for the soloist.

**Two named practice resources called out directly (not owned-plugin-equivalent, but worth flagging as reading material):**
- **Mick Goodrick, *The Advancing Guitarist*** — cited specifically for its "effective uses of space" exercise: two blank staff pages with only rhythmic notation (rests marked) to improvise pitches over. Instrument-agnostic despite the title.
- **A Joe Pass method book** (unnamed specifically) — Jens's most-recommended book for building bebop vocabulary; its example solos are deliberately continuous eighth-note lines with no breaks, useful precisely *because* it's the opposite extreme of "leave space," as a vocabulary-building drill.
- **A Chick Corea writing/booklet** referenced but not the source of this specific list — described as ~50 pages where Corea frames composing and improvising as a game with rules set by the composer/bandleader, which the players then operate within.
