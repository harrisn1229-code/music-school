# TECHNIQUE MISTAKES 89% of Guitarists make & how to fix them!

## Synopsis

Despite the generic title, this is specifically a legato/fretting-hand video: a pull-off snapping-motion basic (already covered on the vault's checklist), then the video's real payoff — the fretting thumb should actively **track/slide along** with the fingers as they move across strings during a legato line, not stay locked in one fixed spot.

## Techniques

- **Pull-offs need an active snapping motion, not a passive lift** (matches [[Fretting and Picking Hand Mistakes Checklist]] point 12 already — not logged as new).
- **The core insight: the fretting thumb should move WITH the fingers across strings during a legato line, not stay locked in one position.** Demonstrated on a full-fretboard legato scale run: the thumb (positioned in the area behind the middle finger) visibly slides along as the fingers move from the low E string toward the high E string, rather than staying anchored at the low-E-string starting position.
- **Two stated reasons this matters:**
  1. **Consistency.** A moving thumb keeps the same wrist angle and finger-position feel on the low strings as on the high strings — nothing has to change technique-wise moving across the neck. A locked thumb forces a different wrist angle/finger position per string, which is a source of "I can play this on the low strings but not the high strings" inconsistency.
  2. **Forces a light grip.** A tight fretting-hand grip physically can't move the thumb — so deliberately letting the thumb travel with the fingers structurally prevents over-gripping, making relaxed technique an emergent result of the motion rather than something to separately remember.
- **Transfers beyond legato** — the same light-grip benefit applies to sweep-picking arpeggio shapes, even though the thumb typically stays more fixed for a given shape there (it's the *grip lightness*, not the literal thumb travel, that carries over).
- **Practice progression:** start with a straight 3-note-per-string scale (worked example: G harmonic minor) ascending/descending, focused purely on the thumb tracking the fingers; add short triplet bursts within it for extra challenge once comfortable; progress to practical scale-based phrases/patterns run through the full scale (not just straight up/down) as the more advanced version.
- **Named failure mode:** under a fast/technically demanding passage there's a real temptation to tighten the grip back up — worth deliberately resisting, staying relaxed, since that's exactly where the light-grip benefit matters most.
