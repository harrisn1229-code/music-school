# Music theory concepts ranked by importance (tier list)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ItsMmqTOgKo)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ItsMmqTOgKo)

## Synopsis

David Bennett runs ~28 music theory concepts — note names, chord types, scales, rhythm/notation topics, and advanced jazz/tuning ideas — through an S-to-F tier list ranked purely by how essential each one is to know, not how hard it is to learn. His stated framing: this doubles as a study-sequencing roadmap — start at S tier and work down, stopping wherever you like, since even a partial pass through the list expands your musicianship. The recurring test he applies is **substitutability**: a concept ranks lower when you can fake your way through without it (a plain triad stands in for a 7th chord; a root-position chord stands in for an inversion), and higher when nothing else covers for it (you cannot play a chord you don't know the notes of).

## Techniques

**S tier (need to know):** Note names — can't do anything more advanced without them. Triads (major/minor/augmented/diminished, sus2/sus4) — essential chord vocabulary for performing or writing almost any song. Ear training / relative pitch — even basic by-ear ID (key, simple progressions) massively expands how you conceptualize music versus only ever reading it. Major scales (all 12) — pre-learns the finger/mental patterns most songs are built from and underlies improvising.

**A tier:** Seventh chords — common and useful, but substitutable with a plain triad in a pinch. Chord inversions — same substitutability logic (root-position works if you don't know the inversion, just loses character). Intervals — names the gaps between notes, valuable for by-ear ID and for transposing on request ("can we take this up a major third?"). Pentatonic/blues scales — simplified, versatile major/minor-scale subsets, the guitarist's entry-level scale. Minor scales (natural/harmonic/melodic) — same pre-learning value as major scales, for minor-key material.

**B tier:** Upper chord extensions (9/11/13, add-chords) — substitutable down to a 7th chord, "nice to know." Modes of the major scale (Dorian, Phrygian, Mixolydian, etc.) — skippable if you already know major/minor (a Dorian passage reads as "minor with a raised 6th"), valuable when writing/improvising specifically in a mode's flavor. Transposition — frequently needed (matching a singer's range on a cover) but not essential to playing a song as written. Key signatures — essential if you read sheet music, irrelevant otherwise; landed mid-table for being polarized rather than moderately important. Roman numeral analysis — abstracts a progression away from its specific key (a I-IV-vi-V sounds the same in any key), very valuable for ear-based thinking. Note values (rhythm notation) — reading rhythm off the page matters less than reading pitch; if you already know how a song goes, rhythm comes intuitively. Clefs (treble/bass/alto) — essential for reading sheet music at all, irrelevant if you play by ear or off tab.

**C tier:** Extended modes (modes of harmonic/melodic minor) — same flavor-value as major-scale modes, but come up far less often. Exotic scales (whole tone, octatonic, bebop, etc.) — the miscellaneous bucket, useful for specific colors, rarely load-bearing. Articulation (staccato/legato) — matters for how a note is played, less than playing the right note at the right time. Time signatures (naming/counting) — you can feel a familiar meter without naming it; the real value cited is knowing unusual signatures (5/4, 7/4) exist as an option, which can spark ideas you wouldn't otherwise reach for. Tuplets — same logic, playable by ear without the label, needed mainly for reading sheet music. Rhythmic solfège ("the three," "the and of four") — an efficient verbal shorthand for beat placement, mostly a professional-environment skill.

**D tier:** Cadences / functional harmony — far more load-bearing in classical/jazz than pop/rock, where it loses relevance. Dynamics (forte/piano markings) — matters most for faithfully reproducing a classical composer's intent from the page; playing your own interpretation, you already have an intuitive sense of loud/soft. Polymeter (unified tempo, parts cycling in and out of sync) — advanced, but worth knowing if you'll be playing in a different meter than the rest of the band, so the "falling out of sync" moment doesn't read as a mistake and cause panic.

**F tier (nice to know only):** Polyrhythms (e.g. 4-over-3) — ultimately just a tuplet dressed up, playable/writable without the label. Tritone substitution — a jazz-specific reharmonization move (G7→C becomes D♭7→C), irrelevant outside that niche. Microtonality — rare in Western practice, relevant mainly when pushing outside Western tuning entirely. Harmonic series (overtones) — nice-to-know on piano/guitar, more load-bearing on horns/strings tuning against it. Temperament (equal vs. just intonation) — only actionable on instruments that can bend pitch (violin); fixed-tuning instruments like piano can't do anything with the knowledge anyway.
