# Songs that will help you identify ascending intervals

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PhDIm_2qS5s)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PhDIm_2qS5s)

## Synopsis

A song-anchor mnemonic system for interval ear training: instead of drilling intervals as abstract distances, memorize one famous melody's opening two notes per interval, then compare a new interval you're trying to identify against that anchor. Covers all 12 intervals inside the octave, ascending, plus a bonus 13th interval (minor 9th) the presenter considers worth knowing despite being outside the octave. Part 1 of 2 — [[Songs that will help you identify descending intervals]] is the companion video.

## Techniques

- **The method itself**: pick one well-known song per interval where the melody's first two notes ARE that interval — when trying to identify an unknown interval by ear, mentally run through the anchor songs until one matches.
- **Minor 2nd (semitone)** — *Jaws* theme (John Williams): the whole theme pivots back and forth on a semitone (E→F). Easiest interval to spot — "if it sounds like it couldn't get any smaller, it's a semitone."
- **Major 2nd (whole tone)** — first two notes of "Ferrari"; also the gap between the first two notes of any major scale.
- **Minor 3rd** — "Mad World" (Tears for Fears/Gary Jules): "All around me are..." — F to A♭. Carries an inherently "minor" color even in isolation.
- **Major 3rd** — "Sir Duke" (Stevie Wonder), opens by outlining a major chord; "Let's Dance" (David Bowie) — outlines a dominant 7 chord, but the first two notes (E♭→G) are still a major 3rd.
- **Perfect 4th** — "Summer Nights" (Grease) bass line's opening two notes.
- **Tritone (aug 4th/dim 5th)** — most dissonant common interval, "foreboding." "YYZ" (Rush) — the whole intro vamps on the tritone. *The Simpsons* theme's opening tag rises a tritone then resolves to the perfect 5th — good demonstration of tension (tritone) → release (5th). Side-note: the "banned by the Church" tritone myth is debunked (credited to Adam Neely) but the association persists because the interval genuinely sounds unusually dark.
- **Perfect 5th** — Star Wars main theme's opening two notes (B♭→F); also the *E.T.* theme's opening two notes. Sounds "at rest"/consonant — can be confused for an octave since only the octave is more resolved-sounding.
- **Minor 6th** — context-dependent character (tense-sounding rising out of a minor key, purely resolved in isolation). "Baker Street" (Gerry Rafferty) sax line's opening two notes; "The Entertainer" (Scott Joplin) main melody — reads as very resolved here because it lands on the 3rd of the tonic chord.
- **Major 6th** — also context-dependent, tends to want to resolve down to the 5th scale degree. "The Holly and the Ivy" (traditional carol), first two notes.
- **Minor 7th** — "Somewhere" from *West Side Story* (Bernstein), opening two notes — wants to resolve. "Can't Stop" (Red Hot Chili Peppers) bass line's opening two notes (E→D), resolving to the octave.
- **Major 7th** — quite dissonant in isolation, sounds like it wants to resolve up to the octave. "Take On Me" (a-ha) — the famous high chorus melody opens on a major 7th leap, part of why that chorus is notoriously hard to sing.
- **Octave** — "Somewhere Over the Rainbow" — sustained/drawn-out opening octave leap, called "almost the most foolproof song example" for the interval because it isn't brief.
- **Bonus 13th interval — minor 9th (octave + minor 2nd)**: flagged as worth knowing separately from its in-octave equivalent (minor 2nd) because it sounds meaningfully LESS dissonant than a bare minor 2nd despite being built from the same two pitch classes — the octave separation removes the "rub" of adjacent notes. "Killing in the Name" (Rage Against the Machine) bass line — D up to E♭ a minor 9th (not minor 2nd) above.
- **Why compound intervals (beyond the octave) are rarely taught**: (1) most compound intervals sound nearly identical in character to their in-octave equivalent (a major 10th sounds about as consonant as a major 3rd), so ear-training systems lump them together; (2) intervals are fundamentally about melody, and wide leaps beyond an octave are hard to sing/hear as melodic, so real songs rarely use them — except the minor 9th, which genuinely sounds different enough from the plain minor 2nd to be worth a dedicated anchor.
