# Major Triads Across the Fretboard (String-Group Inversions)

**Learn one major triad's three inversions (root-position, 1st inversion, 2nd inversion) on each of the four 3-string groups, so any major chord is playable from at least a dozen fretboard locations rather than one memorized open-position shape.** Companion to [[Close Position Triad Voice-Leading Etude]] and [[Spread Triad Voice-Leading Etude]] (composed voice-leading studies) — this page is the raw shape-inventory those etudes assume, plus the practical spacing payoff for riff-writing.

## The four string groups, three voicings each

Worked on A major (A-C♯-E) across all four adjacent 3-string groups — **E-A-D · A-D-G · D-G-B · G-B-E**:

- On each group, find the root in three different octave locations, and at each one a different note ends up on the bottom of the stack: **root-on-bottom** (root-3rd-5th), **3rd-on-bottom** (3rd-5th-root), and **5th-on-bottom** (5th-root-3rd).
- Because the guitar is tuned in fourths except for the B string, the **D-G-B and G-B-E groups reuse the same three voicing shapes as E-A-D/A-D-G but with the top note shifted one fret** to compensate for the B string's major-3rd tuning interval — same notes, same octave, same voicing type, different fingering.
- **Finding the root in every position first is the prerequisite step** — use the octave-shape relationships already covered in the vault's note-location material (open string ↔ 12th fret, and the string-pair octave shapes) rather than searching blind.

## Why this matters beyond "more shapes to know"

- **Extends directly to bigger chords.** Once a triad inversion is solid, adding the 7th (or further tensions) on top doesn't require learning a whole new chord — it's the same triad shape plus one note, matching the extension-on-a-known-shape method in [[Chord Voicings]] and [[Best DARK Metal Chord - Spice Up Your Evil Chords Now!]].
- **Non-root-position voicings sound less generic.** Defaulting to root-on-bottom voicings (the easiest to find) is the common habit; deliberately reaching for 3rd-on-bottom or 5th-on-bottom voicings of the same chord produces a noticeably different, more interesting color from identical notes.

## Practical payoff — spacing a chord riff for speed and interest

A riff written using only root-position triads anchored to the low E string forces the fretting hand to travel the full neck between chords, capping how fast it can be played cleanly. Fix: **pick each chord's voicing based on proximity to the previous chord**, not by defaulting to the root-position shape — worked on a G-B♭-F-C-G progression, chaining voicings so each new chord's nearest available inversion (root/3rd/5th-on-bottom, on whichever string group is closest) is chosen over the "obvious" root-anchored one. Result: dramatically smaller hand movement between chords, faster playable tempo, and a more interesting sound than repeating the same voicing shape transposed up and down one string.

## Practice recommendation

10-15 minutes/day finding all inversions of one triad across the neck, rotating the root note daily.
