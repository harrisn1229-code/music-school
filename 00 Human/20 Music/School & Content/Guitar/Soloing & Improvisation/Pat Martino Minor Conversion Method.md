# Pat Martino Minor Conversion Method

A guitar-vocabulary system drawn from Pat Martino's book **Linear Expressions**, reduced to four practice methods: (1) map the fretboard through **minor 7 chord shapes**, (2) visualize scale/line shapes physically around those chord grips, (3) reduce every chord quality to a minor-chord equivalent (**minor conversion theory**), and (4) generate lines from Pat's book-specific composed etudes, which the book calls **activities**. The throughline across all four: instead of learning separate vocabulary for major, dominant, and half-diminished sounds, everything gets funneled into **one minor-chord vocabulary reservoir**, played from whichever chord-form shape is physically closest — the same CAGED-adjacency instinct as [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]] and [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]], but keyed to minor-7 grips and a theory rule for reinterpreting non-minor chords as minor, rather than to CAGED major/dominant grips directly.

## Minor conversion theory: every chord quality reduces to a minor chord

Pat's core theoretical move, stated directly in the video: **"everything can be minor."** Any diatonic chord quality maps to a minor chord built off a different scale degree of the same root:

| Chord you're looking at | Play minor vocabulary rooted on... |
|---|---|
| **Major** (maj7/maj6) | The minor chord built off its **6th degree** (e.g. F major → D minor) |
| **Dominant 7** | The minor chord built off its **2nd degree** (e.g. C7 → D minor) |
| **Half-diminished (m7♭5)** | A **minor 6 chord** built off its **♭3rd** |

*(Table is exactly as narrated in the source video's spoken summary — captions carry the rule qualitatively, not written out on-screen, so re-verify against the book's own tables before leaning on any single interval as gospel.)* The worked ii–V–I example below applies the rule loosely rather than literally note-for-note: over a **dominant chord built on the ii chord's own root** (the V of a major key, a whole step above the ii), Pat plays the **same minor-7 vocabulary as the ii chord itself** — so a single reservoir of Gm7 lines covers both Gm7 (ii) and C7 (V) in a Gm7–C7–Fmaj7 turnaround, and switches to Dm7 vocabulary only once the tonic (Fmaj7) arrives, because Fmaj7's minor-conversion target (minor off its 6th) is D minor. → [[Chord Substitution]], [[Seventh Chords]], [[The ii-V-I]]

## Method 1 — Map the fretboard through minor 7 chord shapes

The book opens with the **inversions of one minor 7 chord** (worked example: **Gm7**) rather than scale boxes. Each Gm7 shape is shown together with the notes of its **relative major scale** (B♭ major, since G minor and B♭ major share a key signature) laid out around that specific chord grip — so the scale isn't learned as an abstract box, it's learned as "the notes that live around this exact chord voicing." Practice directive: run the scale shape tied to each chord form **both forwards and backwards**, paired with the voicing itself, moving up the neck through every chord-form inversion, and repeat the whole process in multiple keys (not just G minor). → [[Chord Voicings]], [[Chord Inversions]], [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]] (same "shape = fretboard landmark" logic, worked with triads instead of 7th-chord grips)

## Method 2 — Visualize lines around the guitar shapes

Folded together with Method 3 in the source lesson: every "activity" (see below) is meant to be learned **with the chord shape and its scale visualized simultaneously**, not as an isolated fingering — the point of Method 1's shape-mapping is that by the time you reach a line, you already know which chord grip and scale box it's physically sitting inside.

## Method 3 — "Activities": composed lines tied to each chord form

Pat's book gives fixed, composed melodic lines (**activities**) built to reinforce the chord-shape/scale mapping from Method 1 — one activity per chord-form inversion. Nathan Borton's added practice layer on top of just learning them as written:

- **Start by learning only the opening phrase of each activity**, not the whole 5–6-measure line, and learn that opening phrase in **as many keys as possible** before going further.
- **Relearn the same opening phrase starting from a different string** than the one it's written on — e.g. take the chord-form-1 activity's start (originally on the low E string) and replay the identical line starting on the **A string** instead. The physical relocation lands you inside a **different chord-form shape** (moving to the A string put the line around **chord form 4** of Gm7 in the worked example) purely because the string change repositions which chord grip the notes now sit closest to — the same relocate-the-lick-across-CAGED-zones move as [[Triads as a Fretboard Map]] and the CAGED-adjacency sections of [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]], applied here to a composed etude fragment instead of an ear-copied blues lick or a moving-progression arpeggio.
- **Exit the fragment either of two ways**: walk down the scale shape tied to whichever chord form you've landed in, or play straight into the activity's own written ending as originally composed, now transposed to the new position.
- **Repeat the relocation drill from every string** the chord form can start on (E string, A string, D string demoed in the video), building a small library of the same phrase living in multiple neck positions/chord forms.

## Method 4 — Applying activities over a ii–V–I (worked example)

Worked over **Gm7 → C7 → Fmaj7** (a major ii–V–I in F) using minor conversion theory to decide which minor vocabulary plays where:

- **Gm7 and C7 both get Gm7-tonality lines** — because C7 (the V) converts to the same minor-vocabulary target as Gm7 (the ii) under Pat's minor-conversion rule (see table above), so there's no vocabulary switch needed across the ii–V half of the progression, only at the resolution.
- **Fmaj7 (the I) gets Dm7-tonality lines** — F major's minor-conversion target is D minor (minor built off F's 6th degree).
- **First pass:** take the **chord-form-1 activity's opening phrase**, play it across all three chords, switching minor key center (Gm7 → Dm7) exactly at the Fmaj7 arrival, and finish each chord's phrase by walking down that chord form's tied scale — same exit method as Method 3.
- **Second pass, same progression:** swap in the **chord-form-4 activity's opening phrase** instead of chord-form-1's — chosen specifically because chord form 4 is the shape that sits over the ii–V half of the progression in this key — then move that same line to **Dm7** once Fmaj7 arrives, exactly as before. Framed directly as the payoff of the whole method: "being able to piece the fragments of the activities together in this way can be a very effective way to start generating Pat-style lines." → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]] (the *same* Gm7–C7–Fmaj7 progression is the worked example on that page's own "whole page" video bullet and on [[The ii-V-I]]'s "composing solos" bullets — a useful A/B: CAGED-arpeggio outlining vs. Pat's minor-conversion-vocabulary approach to the identical changes)

## Chunking a long activity into fragments

The hardest of the four methods, by the video's own framing: memorizing every activity in full. A single chord form's activity runs **5–6 measures**, too long to internalize as one block, so the recommended fix generalizes the fragment-relocation logic from Method 3 to a longer line:

1. **Split one long activity into ~4 smaller chunks**, worked on the chord-form-2 activity in the source video.
2. **Treat each chunk as its own independent lick/vocabulary line** — not a fragment of something bigger, a standalone phrase in its own right.
3. **Drill each chunk starting from a different string**, exactly as in Method 3 — e.g. "chunk two" relocated to start on the **low E string**, which centers it around **chord form number one** instead of wherever it originally sat.
4. **Link chunks back into the activities they came from using a common tone** — find a note the chunk shares with the surrounding activity line and use it as the splice point, the same common-tone connective-tissue principle used to glue separate memorized vocabulary phrases together on [[Connecting Licks Into Longer Lines]] (that page's "proximity linking"/scale-walk devices are the general case; this is Pat-specific material spliced the same way).
5. **The resulting chunk is portable across every chord that shares the target minor quality**, not locked to the one chord it was learned on — the worked chunk (built from Gm7-tonality material) is shown playable over **Gm7 itself, C7 (its minor-conversion dominant), or B♭ major 7 (its relative major)** — three different chord qualities, one physical shape, using the minor-conversion table above to justify each substitution.
