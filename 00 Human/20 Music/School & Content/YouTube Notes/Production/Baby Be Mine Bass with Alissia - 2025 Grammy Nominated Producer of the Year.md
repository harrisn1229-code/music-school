# "Baby Be Mine" Bass with Alissia - 2025 Grammy Nominated Producer of the Year

## Synopsis

Anthony Marinelli walks producer Alissia through recreating the Minimoog bass from Michael Jackson's "Baby Be Mine" (*Thriller*) by ear, live — tuning, envelope timing, and resonance dialed in while she plays the part. Interspersed with genuine Thriller-sessions history (LM-1 drum programming with an outboard sync/click track, no MIDI) and repeated arrangement philosophy about giving every part its own space.

## Techniques

- **Minimoog single-trigger mode drops the envelope on a fast fill unless sustain is raised for that passage specifically.** In single-trigger mode, legato playing (a fast fill) doesn't re-trigger the envelope between notes, so with sustain too low the sound "dies" between notes in a busy passage; the fix demonstrated live is to raise sustain specifically for the fill, distinct from the sustain setting used on the main groove — different envelope settings per passage density, not one fixed setting for the whole part.
- **Never track keyboard control voltage on a bass patch — confirmed again, independently, as "critical."** With key tracking engaged, playing higher notes on the bass patch made it read as "too bright... it's just not a bass" — the same rule already documented on [[Bass Design]] from other sources, reconfirmed here as one of the two settings (alongside filter resonance) called out explicitly as "key" to the sound.
- **Time the envelope's release so the bass note ends before the beat it's leaving room for — specifically named as leaving a hole for the snare to hit into.** A bass note that "carries over past the quarter note" reads as "too sustained, too present" and crowds the snare's transient; the fix is a combination of decay/release shaped so the note is already receding by the time the snare needs its own space. Direct case study, on the actual Minimoog patch, of the arrangement-space discipline already documented from Steve Porcaro's account of the same album's sessions.
- **LM-1 drum machine workflow with no MIDI**: sync via an outboard clock/click track (cited as 24 or 48 pulses-per-quarter-note clock) rather than MIDI, so a part can be re-synced later by feeding it a click; everything else played and programmed directly into the drum machine's own sequencer ("song mode").
- **Arrangement philosophy, restated from the sessions**: every Thriller-era part is deliberately soloable and distinct — "you could solo every stem and it's really its own part" — credited as much to Rod Temperton's arrangement decisions (knowing when to clear space and bring a new element in) as to any individual sound design choice.
