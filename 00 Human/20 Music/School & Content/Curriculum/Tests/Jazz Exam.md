> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **Cmaj7 or C6 — a four-note chord. The 7th is the default and the triad is the special case**
> Everything downstream assumes four notes: guide tones cannot exist without a 7th, and a tritone substitution has nothing to preserve without one. Reading a jazz chart as triads is not a simpler reading, it is a wrong one.
>
> **Q2.** **Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7 · Dm7♭5 – G7♭9 – Cm7**
> *Also accepted:* Cm6 or CmMaj7 for the minor tonic · Dmø7 for the minor ii
> Because of the root motion: D → G → C is the circle of fifths in miniature, each root falling a fifth, and the cell stacks two of the strongest progression in tonal music back to back.
>
> **Q3.** **Any m7 followed by a dominant 7th a fourth above it — the deceptive move, and the unresolved ii–V**
> Whatever the pair points at is the intended I even when it never sounds. Deceptive lands on vi or ♭VI instead; unresolved moves straight to the next ii–V, which is why a bebop head can look like it changes key every two bars when it does not.
>
> **Q4.** **The 3rd and the 7th — the exception is m7♭5, where dropping the ♭5 states a plain m7 instead**
> The 3rd says major or minor and the 7th says major, dominant or minor. The root says nothing about quality and the 5th almost nothing, which is why the 5th is the first note dropped. Cm7♭5 and Cm7 share E♭ and B♭, so only the ♭5 separates them.
>
> **Q5.** **C → B → B and F → F → E**
> Nothing leaps. One voice holds while the other moves a half step, then they swap. **Each chord's 3rd is held over as the next chord's 7th, and each chord's 7th falls a half step onto the next chord's 3rd** — that is what root motion by fifths does, and it is the mechanical reason the cell sounds inevitable rather than merely conventional.
>
> **Q6.** **Root + 3rd + 7th, no 5th and no extensions — best as root–7th–3rd**
> It is the right voicing rather than the lazy one whenever something else needs the register: under a busy vocal, or with a second chordal instrument in the arrangement. Extensions stack on top of it — shell plus 9th, shell plus 13th.
>
> **Q7.** **Enough shared tones and the same function, function first — the failure is substituting everything**
> A substitute sharing three notes that resolves the wrong way is worse than one sharing two that resolves right. The melody is the test on top of that. Wall to wall, substitution reads as directionless rather than sophisticated.
>
> **Q8.** **They contain the same tritone · only dominants qualify · it buys chromatic bass motion**
> G7's tritone is B and F; D♭7 has F and C♭, and C♭ is B — the 3rd and 7th simply swap roles. The substitute always sits a semitone above the target of resolution, so `Dm7 G7 Cmaj7` becomes `Dm7 D♭7 Cmaj7`, roots walking D – D♭ – C.
>
> **Q9.** **♭II-of-ii, the substitute for VI7 on the way to ii — and any major triad a semitone above the target**
> It is commonest precisely because it is undramatic: it sits inside a progression as a chromatic step from iii down to ii and most listeners never register it. The minimum form does not have to be altered, or even a dominant 7th.
>
> **Q10.** **How you arrive at it — chromatically it is a tritone sub, by fifths from ♭VI it is a Phrygian cadence**
> The audible difference is whether the roots move chromatically or by fifths on the way in. It sounds complete because every note of a major triad on ♭II falls a semitone into the tonic triad — ♭2̂→1̂, 4̂→3̂, ♭6̂→5̂ — three simultaneous semitone resolutions with no tritone and no leading tone.
>
> **Q11.** **Fm7 – B♭7 – Cmaj7, iv7 – ♭VII7 – I, both borrowed from C minor**
> Reach for it when V7 sounds too final or too bright: it is warmer, plagal-flavoured and more ambiguous. B♭7 contains A♭, the ♭6 of C, which resolves down to G. The lighter shorthand is the backdoor dominant — ♭VII7 straight to I with no minor iv in front.
>
> **Q12.** **I – vi – ii – V — it ends a section pointing back at the top; write it long, then compress**
> In C, `Cmaj7 Am7 Dm7 G7`: the root falls a third, then rises a fourth twice, and it ends on V, which wants the next chorus's I. Compressing a good turnaround gives a good short one — two bars, one bar, or V alone on beats 3–4. Expanding a bare `Dm7 G7` gives you nothing to work with.
>
> **Q13.** **Bars 1–4 are I–vi–ii–V twice; bars 5–8 answer by going to IV and back through the borrowed iv**
> The full second half is I → I7 → IV → iv → I → V/ii → ii → V. Four bars of a static loop do not get boring because four bars of real harmonic movement answer them, and that question-and-answer inside one eight-bar unit is the transferable part.
>
> **Q14.** **III7–VI7–II7–V7, two bars each — the harmonic rhythm drops 4× and that alone reads as new**
> In B♭ that is `| D7 | D7 | G7 | G7 | C7 | C7 | F7 | F7 |` — four dominants, each resolving down a fifth into the next, and only F7 is diatonic (it is the key's V7). No key change, no tempo change, no new instrumentation: just diatonic-and-busy switching to chromatic-and-slow.
>
> **Q15.** **AABA and 16+16 — the first half must not resolve to I; it has to reach V or a V-substitute**
> Otherwise the second half has nowhere to launch from, and the second half's ending is the tune's real cadence. Knowing the container also tells you how much of the chart is genuinely new material before you read a chord.
>
> **Q16.** **A passing chord connects and is optional; a substitution replaces — passing chords go on the weak beat**
> Keep the real chord on the heavy beat and the ear tracks the actual harmony, hearing the passing chord as colour. It settles who has to know: decoration you can add alone while comping, a substitution has to be agreed or the bass and the chords will disagree about what the tune is.
>
> **Q17.** **The melody note must be a chord tone or a functioning extension of the new chord — so work melody-first**
> Not a note the new chord actively contradicts: a natural 9 over a ♭9 chord, or a natural 4 sitting on the 3rd. Everything else — function, bass motion, density — is negotiable, so write the melody out and choose chords against it rather than the other way round.
>
> **Q18.** **Cmaj7 root · Am7 ♭3 · Fmaj7 5th · Dm7 ♭7 · A♭maj7 3rd · F♯m7♭5 ♭5**
> Same note, six meanings — the cheapest reharmonization move there is, because the topline never changes. The systematic version is delayed resolution: hold the melody on its expected tonic note and harmonise it with anything except the tonic chord.
>
> **Q19.** **When the tune is the point it pulls focus — so reharmonize the repeats, not the first statement**
> Let the listener learn the plain version, then change what is underneath it. It reads as development rather than confusion, and it hands you a second section for free.
>
> **Q20.** **The mode's character, quartal voicings, pedals and drones, and texture — it is not modal interchange**
> Modal jazz holds one mode for many bars, a static approach to form. Modal interchange borrows a chord from a parallel mode inside functional harmony and returns. They share a word and almost nothing else.
>
> **Q21.** **Dorian natural 6 · Lydian ♯4 · Mixolydian ♭7 · Phrygian ♭2 — avoid it and it collapses to major or minor**
> Land on it and the mode is established. In D Dorian the note is B: hit B and it is Dorian, dodge B and it is D minor. A loop that never plays the ♯4 is not Lydian, it is major with a Lydian label.
>
> **Q22.** **E A D G B — three perfect 4ths with a major 3rd on top**
> A stack of thirds names a chord and naming a chord implies a function, which is exactly what modal music is avoiding. Fourths sound open and unassigned, so they state the mode without committing to a chord.
>
> **Q23.** **A seven-note scale plus one chromatic passing tone — the eighth note keeps chord tones on the beat**
> Play a seven-note scale in straight eighths and by the next bar the chord tones have shifted onto offbeats. Bebop dominant on G is G A B C D E F F♯ G. The extra note goes wherever it puts chord tones on strong beats; if yours already land there, you do not need one.
>
> **Q24.** **It surrounds a target note before you play it — an approach note is the one-note cousin**
> For a target of C: D♭–B–C, D–B–C, B–D♭–C, or D–D♭–B–C. An approach note precedes the chord tone by a half step below (strongest, it borrows the leading-tone pull), a half step above, or a scale step. Both belong on the note you want heard, usually a chord tone on a downbeat — not on every note.
>
> **Q25.** **Chromatic embellishment of static harmony — a sustained half-step line, not a one-note ornament**
> Take one note that matters, a chord root or a guide tone, and walk it by half step across several beats or bars while the harmony stays still. An enclosure or an approach note targets a single arrival on a single chord tone; CESH is closer to a bass-line device borrowed into the melody.
>
> **Q26.** **Learn by ear · chunk · analyse each chunk against its chord · reduce to one named concept · improvise**
> The skipped step is the fourth, the reduction. Learning a solo by ear is not enough on its own — the concept, not the lick, is the thing worth keeping, and a set of per-chord concepts is a template you can play through repeatedly.
>
> **Q27.** **The guide tones are the same pair either way — D♭7 swaps their roles rather than disturbing them**
> Dm7 is F and C, G7 is B and F, Cmaj7 is E and B, so the lines run C → B → B and F → F → E. Substitute D♭7 and its guide tones are F and C♭, which is B — the identical pair, with the 3rd and the 7th exchanged. The two lines are unchanged and only the root moved, which is why the substitution costs the progression nothing: it is a new bass note under a tension that was already there.
> *Rubric (5):* spells both guide-tone pairs (2) · notes the pair is identical with the roles swapped (2) · concludes the tritone sub changes the root under an unchanged tension (1)
>
> **Q28.** **e.g. A7 for Am7 (secondary dominant) · D♭7 for G7 (tritone sub) · B♭7 for G7 (backdoor)**
> Others that work: Em7 for Cmaj7 or Am7 for Cmaj7 (diatonic third-apart substitution), E7–A7 for Cmaj7–Am7 (III7–VI7), interpolating a ii in front of each dominant, or a diminished passing chord between the pairs. The point is that none of them is a memorised turnaround: each is one of Lesson 3's rules applied to the same four-chord skeleton, so the whole catalogue can be generated rather than learned.
> *Rubric (5):* three variants that genuinely change chords (2) · each names the device it used (2) · the skeleton's function or destination is preserved (1)
>
> **Q29.** **Legal: Am7, where E is the 5th, and Fmaj7, where E is the major 7th. Illegal: Fm7**
> Other legal answers include A7 (E is the 5th), Em7 (E is the root), C♯m7♭5 (E is the ♭3) and A♭maj7♯5 (E is the ♯5, which is a functioning altered extension). Fm7 is F A♭ C E♭, so a held E natural sits a semitone above the chord's own ♭7 and the chord actively contradicts it. That is the rule: the melody note has to be a chord tone or a functioning extension, and nothing else about the substitute matters until that is satisfied.
> *Rubric (5):* two legal chords with the E named as a chord tone or extension in each (2) · one illegal chord with the clash identified (2) · states the constraint itself (1)
>
> **Q30.** **The bridge slows the harmonic rhythm 4×; modal jazz changes one non-harmonic element instead**
> The Rhythm Changes bridge introduces no new key, tempo or instrumentation — it goes from two chords a bar to one chord per two bars, and the slowdown alone reads as a different section. Modal jazz has no harmonic rhythm left to change, so a texture, a countermelody or a register shift does the same job. Both say the same thing to a loop-based producer: **contrast does not require new material.** Halve the harmonic rhythm, or add one element, and the section changes.
> *Rubric (5):* names the bridge's harmonic-rhythm slowdown (2) · names modal jazz's texture, density or register route (2) · states the shared conclusion that contrast needs no new material (1)
>
> **Q31.** **One rule — a change only reads as a change against a plain version the listener already has**
> Substitution reads as sophisticated against plain harmony and as directionless wall to wall, because with nothing plain left there is no baseline for it to be measured against. Reharmonizing the first statement fails the same way: the listener never learns the plain version, so the reharmonization is not heard as a departure, only as the tune. Both are the same mechanism — contrast is relative, and spending it everywhere spends it nowhere.
> *Rubric (5):* identifies one shared mechanism rather than two rules (1) · states it as contrast requiring a baseline (2) · applies it to both the substitution and the reharmonization case (2)
>
> **Q32.** **CESH — walk one inner voice down by half step. An enclosure will not do it: it targets one arrival**
> CESH is chromatic embellishment of static harmony, which is exactly the constraint: a sustained half-step line under a chord that is not moving, so motion is generated without touching the harmony. An enclosure or an approach note is a one-note ornament aimed at a single arrival, so it decorates a moment rather than filling four bars, and a bebop scale answers a different question again — where chord tones land in a running eighth-note line. CESH is also the device that respects the modal decision from Lesson 6 instead of undoing it.
> *Rubric (5):* names CESH and describes the chromatic inner line (2) · names a device that does not fit, with a reason (2) · connects it to holding the harmony still deliberately (1)
