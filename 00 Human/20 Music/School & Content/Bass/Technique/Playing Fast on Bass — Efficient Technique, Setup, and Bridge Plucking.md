# Playing Fast on Bass — Efficient Technique, Setup, and Bridge Plucking

BassBuzz's three named levers for raising a bassist's playable speed ceiling, framed directly against the idea that speed is a talent you either have or don't — the video is explicit that no shortcut substitutes for years of deliberate practice (using Thundercat's shredding as the reference point), but names three concrete, trainable/settable things that get a player closer faster than raw reps alone. None of the three is about finger *strength* — they're about removing wasted motion and wasted resistance from the system, three different links in the chain (fretting-hand motion, instrument setup, plucking-hand position).

## Lever 1 — relaxed, efficient fretting-hand technique

Minimize how far the fretting hand has to move to reach each note, rather than reaching with big, loose motions. The video doesn't break this down into a specific drill, but the underlying failure mode it's describing is the same one named explicitly in [[Fretting-Hand Strength, Stretch, Stamina, and Finger Control]]'s Exercise 4 (the "flying fingers" fix — the "cup of tea" pinky flail and "flip the bird" middle-finger flail): any finger that lifts higher/wider off the fretboard than the next note requires is wasted travel, and wasted travel is what puts a hard ceiling on how fast a line can be played, not raw finger speed. Treat this lever as "go run Exercise 4" rather than a separate drill — same mechanism, same fix.

## Lever 2 — set the bass up with low-to-medium action

**Action** = the height of the strings above the fingerboard. Lower action means the fretting hand needs less vertical travel to fully seat a note (smaller, more efficient movements) and the string offers less resistance to the plucking hand, so both hands do less physical work per note — which raises how fast the whole system can move before technique breaks down. This is a one-time setup change (or a tech's job), not a practice-session drill, and it's the one lever of the three that touches the instrument rather than technique. Deliberately in tension with [[Jamerson's Root-Fifth-Octave Exercise]]/[[Bassline Writing]]'s Motown tone recipe, which calls for the *opposite* — **high action** — as part of James Jamerson's signature Funk Brothers tone; that's a genre/tone tradeoff (higher action = firmer, more resistant string = part of that thick, dug-in P-bass sound) working against this lever's speed goal, not a contradiction — pick the setup for what the part needs, tone or speed.

## Lever 3 — pluck near the bridge

Plucking closer to the bridge (rather than over the neck/pickup) means the string has less give at that point along its length, so the plucking fingers do less work per pluck and can cycle faster — the same physical principle as why a string feels "tighter" the closer you get to either fixed end. **Tension with tone — and now a real data point on it (see From videos below).** This lever is framed as a speed-position choice, not a tone recommendation, and this video doesn't address the tradeoff directly — but plucking position also changes *timbre* (brighter/more percussive near the bridge, warmer/rounder over the neck), and a separate BassBuzz breakdown of Chris Squire's tone on "Roundabout" names bridge-position picking as one of his four concrete tone steps, independent of this note's speed framing. Cross-reference [[Bass Tone Chain Priority]] Link 1 (fingers/plucking hand as the single highest-leverage link in the whole tone chain) — that note covers *attack quality* from plucking-hand mechanics; this lever is about plucking *position* specifically, a related but distinct control on the same hand. **The full tone-side resolution of this tension now has its own note:** [[Plucking Position — Fingerboard, Neck, and Bridge Tone Shaping]] confirms bridge position reads as bright/punchy/thin (this lever's speed rationale and that note's tone rationale are the same physical position, two different reasons to reach for it) and adds the two positions this note doesn't cover — fingerboard (warm/bassy, the opposite end from a speed standpoint too) and neck (the balanced default) — plus a genre-brief cheat sheet and worked examples for choosing among all three.

## How the three levers stack

None of these is presented as sufficient alone — they're three independent places to remove friction from the same system: the fretting hand travels less (Lever 1), the string resists less on the way down (Lever 2), and the string resists less on the way up when plucked (Lever 3). Combined, they lower the physical cost of every note in a fast line, which is what actually raises the achievable tempo ceiling — same "remove friction, not just add reps" logic as [[String Crossing and Plucking Patterns]]'s rake-vs-alternate pre-decision fix (that note targets *indecision* as the friction source; this one targets *physical resistance* as the friction source — different bottlenecks, same "friction is the enemy of speed" frame).
