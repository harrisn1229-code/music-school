# Composing with SMOOTH Voice Leading | Inverted Chords Guitar Composing Tutorial

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=A-kZTB_EgOE)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=A-kZTB_EgOE)

## Synopsis

Jared Borkowski reverse-engineers the two intro compositions from an earlier "chord inversions" video of his (referenced repeatedly but not itself in this vault yet — see Source note below) into a full worked composing method: take an ordinary open-chord progression, re-voice it as a three-note voice-led line by choosing inversions that minimize how far each individual note travels between chords, then — as a distinct second pass — fill the remaining wide gaps (a whole step or more) between voice-led notes with extra passing chromatic tones, without needing to name the resulting chord at all. The video works the same progression in two keys (G and D) with full inversion-by-inversion narration of which note moves, by how much, and why, plus a live improvised gap-filling demo in each key. This is a **composition method** video, not a soloing or comping lesson — it's the most concrete, step-by-step treatment in the vault of the "harmony as a byproduct of moving melodies" idea that recurs across [[Chord Inversions]]'s existing entries from this channel (the milonga analysis, the "10 favorite lessons of 2023" compilation). Distinct from the channel's [[Close Position Triad Voice-Leading Etude]] and [[Spread Triad Voice-Leading Etude]] — those are composed etudes that mechanically chain the full diatonic third-cycle of a key for drilling; this video voice-leads one real, cadence-driven progression and then treats gap-filling as a separate compositional layer on top, with no diatonic-coverage goal.

## What's taught

**Core concept — voice leading as independent melodic lines:** treat each note of a chord as its own "voice"/singer in a melody, not as an indivisible chord shape — "there's multiple Melodies happening at once and when those Melodies are stacked on top of each other it happens to make chords... that's kind of how Harmony and chords were first realized in music history." Good voice leading = for each note, find the closest note it can move to (by step — half or whole step) to become part of the next chord; the smaller the individual moves, the more "singable" the line and the smoother the progression sounds. Because most chord changes require a chord tone other than the root in the bass to make the shortest moves work, **voice leading requires inversions** — hence the pairing with [[Chord Inversions]].

**Progression #1 — key of G, default open chords:** G → D (V) → G7 → C (IV) → C minor (borrowed iv) → Dsus4 → D → D7 → back to G.

**Three-note voice-led version of the G progression (115 BPM), move-by-move:**
- **G major** (root voicing) — root, 5th, 3rd top to bottom.
- **G → D (1st inversion, 3rd in bass):** bottom note stays the same (a held common tone — "excellent voice leading"), one note moves down a half step, the top note moves down a whole step.
- **D → G7 (3rd inversion, ♭7 in bass):** bottom note moves down another half step to become the ♭7 of G7; middle note moves down a minor third; top note moves down a whole step. Result voices the ♭7, 3rd and root — **the 5th is deliberately omitted**, which Jared calls totally fine on a dominant/seventh chord ("if we need to get rid of the five that's okay" — points to his own shell-voicings video for more).
- **G7 → C (1st inversion, 3rd in bass) — "a beautiful voice leading moment":** the bottom and top notes of the G7 voicing are a **tritone** apart; each resolves a half step in the *opposite* direction from the other (bottom down a half step, top up a half step) while the middle note holds — the classic tritone-resolution engine (dominant 7th → tonic) demonstrated at the single-note level rather than as chord theory. Lands on 3rd-root-5th of C major.
- **C → C minor:** only the bottom note (the 3rd) moves down a half step (major 3rd → minor 3rd); the other two notes are held common tones. Smallest possible move for a major→minor color shift.
- **C minor → Dsus4:** "notice how the bass notes are all moving down by step... very cool to have a progression where all of the bass notes on the bottom are moving down chromatically" — bottom note moves down to the root of D, middle note moves down a minor third to the 5th of D, top note holds.
- **Dsus4 → D:** just the top note resolves down to the 3rd of D; everything else sticks (the textbook sus4→3rd resolution, voice-led).
- **D → D7 → G (the four-voice/full-chord finish):** a 4th voice (the ♭7) is added — "if it was actually just three singers, this singer here could go up and sing that and get rid of the five for a second" — then resolved to a full strummed G bar chord for thickness. **Strict 3-voice resolution alternative given too:** D's root moves down to G's root, D7's ♭7 moves to the 3rd of G, D's 3rd moves to the root of G — described as sounding "kind of perfect" as a resolution. In practice Jared strums the full bar chord rather than sticking to exactly 3 voices — the voice-leading logic still holds underneath the fuller strum.
- **Full recap chain:** G major → D 1st inv. → G7 3rd inv. (♭7 in bass) → C 1st inv. → C minor 1st inv. → Dsus4 → D → D7 → G.

**"The coolest part" — filling the gaps with passing chromatic tones (a second, separate pass on top of voice leading):** once a progression is already voice-led (every note moved to the closest position in the next chord), any voice whose move is a **whole step or a minor third** (not just a half step) has room for a **passing tone** filling that gap — "you can even connect everything by never moving more than a half step." Demonstrated live and unplanned ("I don't have this planned out or anything, I'm just going to add notes in between movements") — Jared explicitly does **not** name each resulting chord as he plays, framing that as the point: **you don't need to know the theory to use this.** Full resulting chord list when he does analyze it afterward (illustrating how far one pass of gap-filling can push the harmony): G major → G minor → D major → F♯ minor → F♯ diminished → G7 → C major → C minor → B augmented → E♭ major → G minor → Dsus → D → G. Flagged by Jared himself as "kind of overdoing it" as a demo of the ceiling — in practice he recommends filling in "a couple here and there," not maxing out every gap.

**Progression #2 — key of D, same shape:** D → A (V) → D7 (thought of as **V7/IV** — "five of four, so five of the G chord" — since G is IV of D) → G minor (**borrowed minor iv**, the same functional slot C minor filled in the G-key version) → Asus4 → A → back to D. Same underlying shape as progression #1 transposed, minus the extra D7 add-a-voice flourish at the very end.

**Voice-led version in D (105 BPM, right-hand arpeggiated accompaniment):**
- **D major** — root, 5th, 3rd (same shape logic as the G-key opener).
- **D → A (1st inversion)** — 3rd, root, 5th.
- **A → D7** — voiced with the ♭7 in the bass, 3rd and root stacked above.
- **D7 → G minor** — bring the bass note down to make the borrowed minor iv.
- **G minor → Asus4 → A → D** — resolves back to the D shape, closing the loop.

**Gap-filling demo #2 (key of D):** same technique — "we could add that D minor if we like that," add a stepwise passing note before jumping to the A chord, add a whole-step-up passing note on top for a deliberately **dissonant** color before resolving to G / G minor, work through an **augmented chord** as a passing sonority before the major chord, then bring the bottom note down a half step and another note down a half step to land on a sus chord. Explicitly called out sounding **"very classical"** with the arpeggiated accompaniment — Jared connects this directly to classical voice-leading practice: "that's exactly what classical music is doing, it is finding those extra little voice leading spots." One specific shape named: what functions as a **B♭ major** shape gets its bottom, middle, and top notes moved **individually, one at a time**, rather than jumping as a block straight to the next sus shape — called out as carrying more harmonic impact than moving multiple notes at once when the harmony changes.

**Composition philosophy (the throughline):** "Harmony... was kind of created as a byproduct of doing that with melody" — historically, melody moved first and harmony emerged as a side effect; the modern songwriter can **reverse-engineer** that process: start with a chord progression, voice-lead it (connect chord to chord by moving individual notes the shortest distance, via inversions), then fill the remaining gaps with passing tones for more harmonic color and "impact." Explicitly framed as one of Jared's favorite composition concepts in general, not guitar-specific, though he applies it to the fretboard here. **Practice takeaway, stated directly and generally applicable to any known chord:** take any chord shape you already know, find where two voice-led notes are a whole step or more apart, fill in an extra passing note, and listen to what it creates — no need to identify the resulting chord theoretically first.

**Resource mention (free, not gear — no wishlist action):** "Chords with Color" — a free chord chart at soundguitarlessons.com covering all diatonic chords and common extensions (add9, maj7, maj9, etc.) across five keys; offered as a source of raw chord-option material to combine with this voice-leading/gap-filling method for songwriting, theory study, or ear training.
