# Learn Your Bass Fretboard Notes (Easy Starter Method)

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tcok98Vk6uA)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tcok98Vk6uA)

## Synopsis

An earlier, longer, more call-and-response-drilled BassBuzz take on the same "money notes" fretboard system documented in full in [[Bass Fretboard Note Memorization System]] (built from the channel's later, tighter [[Memorize the Bass Fretboard in 9 Minutes]]). Where that later video compresses the five-step system into 9 minutes, this one spends nearly 20 minutes building the same first-four-frets anchor set from the ground up — starting from a piano keyboard explanation of the 12-note musical alphabet and sharps/flats (rather than assuming that theory as background), drilling the open strings and the money notes with repeated out-loud pop quizzes, and closing with a live worked example: finding a guitarist's called-out chord (C, F, G) inside the money zone and picking which fret to actually play when more than one option exists. It's pitched at a total beginner who doesn't yet have the piano-keyboard mental model this whole system depends on, which makes it a useful "step 0" companion to the more advanced note even though the two videos teach the identical anchor-note content.

## What's taught

- **Piano-keyboard framing for the 12-note musical alphabet.** 12 notes total — 7 white keys (letter names A–G, looping back to A) and 5 black keys. Going up in pitch moves forward through the alphabet, going down moves backward. The white-key letters alone don't cover the black keys, so sharps (♯, raise a note one key) and flats (♭, lower a note one key) fill the gap — e.g. raising D one key = D♯, lowering G one key = G♭.
- **Enharmonic equivalents, explained via the raise/lower framing rather than stated as a rule.** The same black key can have two names depending on which direction you approached it from: lowering D lands on D♭; raising C lands on the *same physical key*, called C♯ — "two names, one note."
- **"Buddied close" mnemonic for the no-black-key gaps.** B→C and E→F have no black key between them ("they really like each other and they wanted to stay extra friendly and buddied close") — the same fact [[Notes & Pitches]] already states plainly, given here as a memorable phrase for beginners rather than a bare rule. Drilled both ascending (A A♯ B C C♯ D D♯ E F F♯ G G♯ → loop to A) and descending (A A♭ G G♭ F E E♭ D D♭ C B B♭ → loop to A), each step called out loud with a pause for the viewer to answer before the video does.
- **One fret = one piano key.** Explicitly stated as the bridge from the piano-keyboard mental model to the bass neck: since you can't see white/black keys on a fretboard, the rule to hold onto is that moving up one fret is identical to moving up one piano key, whether that lands on a natural or a sharp/flat.
- **Open strings: E A D G, "eat and drink grapes."** Same string-name content and mnemonic already documented in [[Bass Playing Position, Setup, and Tuning Basics]] — drilled here with a call-and-response naming quiz (point at a string, viewer names it, first slow then fast).
- **Money notes: three naturals per string in the first four frets** — same anchor set as [[Bass Fretboard Note Memorization System]] Step 1: E open/F fret 1/G fret 3; A open/B fret 2/C fret 3; D open/E fret 2/F fret 3; G open/A fret 2/B fret 4. Heavily drilled with repeated pop quizzes in both directions (name → fret, fret → name), across all four strings in order and then in random order.
- **Why three naturals per string, not four (the reasoning, spelled out) — new detail not in the existing note.** The would-be 4th natural on each string (continuing the alphabet one more letter past the money zone) always lands on the 5th fret, and the 5th fret of a string is always the same pitch as the open string one string up: 5th fret E string (A) = open A string; 5th fret A string (D) = open D string; 5th fret D string (G) = open G string. You've therefore already "got" that 4th note for free from the next string's open note, so there's no need to memorize a separate fret position for it — framed as an intentional design choice in the anchor set, not an arbitrary cutoff at 3.
- **Applying sharps/flats to the money zone.** Any memorized natural can be raised a fret (sharp) or lowered a fret (flat) from itself — e.g. G on the E string's 3rd fret → G♯ one fret up, G♭ one fret down. Drilled across strings: A on the G string 2nd fret → A♯ 3rd fret / A♭ 1st fret; F on the D string 3rd fret → F♯ 4th fret, but explicitly **no F♭** exists (F sits right next to E, "buddied close," so there's no black key to lower into) — B on the A string 2nd fret → B♭ 1st fret; G on the E string 3rd fret → G♯ 4th fret. *(Simplification flag: this video treats F♭/B♯/C♭/E♯ as simply not existing. [[Notes & Pitches]] documents that these spellings ARE real — required in certain keys to keep the "each letter used once" rule intact (e.g. F♯ major's 7th degree must be spelled E♯, not F) — so this video's "there is no F flat" is a beginner-level simplification, correct for the money-zone/enharmonic-shortcut purposes taught here but not universally true.)*
- **Live jam application — finding a chord's root note in the money zone and choosing among options.** Worked example: given a C–F–G(–F) chord progression called out by a guitarist, find each chord's root as a bass note: C → 3rd fret A string (the only C in the money zone). F → two options in the money zone (1st fret E string, 3rd fret D string) — **stated rule of thumb for beginners: when more than one option exists, pick the lower-pitched one, "that's always the safest choice."** G → also two options (3rd fret E string, open G string) — same rule applied, picks the 3rd fret E string over the open G. Then plays one note per bar/chord change along with the progression, live, as a jam-readiness test. *(The auto-transcript garbles the literal called chord order in this closing section into nonsense — "going to AB," "then am" — so the exact bar-by-bar sequence isn't reliably reconstructable; the reusable takeaway is the method — root-find each named chord inside the money zone, prefer the lower option — not a specific transcribed sequence.)*

No sound/sample/tone-selection content and no production/mixing content — pure instrument-fundamentals pedagogy, same territory as [[Memorize the Bass Fretboard in 9 Minutes]] but slower, more beginner-oriented, and built from the piano-keyboard model up rather than assuming it.
