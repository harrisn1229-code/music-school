# Chord Progressions - Beginner's Guide - Part 1 (What Are Chords?)

## Synopsis

Part 1 of Max Konyi's 5-part beginner chord-progressions series. Pure conceptual groundwork: harmony = any two-or-more notes sounding together, a chord is identified by root note + quality (any quality can sit on any root), major and minor are the two primary qualities in modern music and each always sounds the way it sounds regardless of root, chords carry emotional weight because of the intervals between their notes, and a chord's feeling is subtly modulated by the chords around it and the key it's heard in (foreshadowing chord progressions in later parts). No settings, exercises, or formulas yet — this is definitions only, aimed at someone who has never thought about what a chord "is."

## What's taught

- **Harmony = 2+ notes sounding simultaneously**; a chord is a harmony. Some theorists insist chords need 3+ notes, but the video treats 2 notes as a valid (implied) chord.
- **A chord is named by root note + quality** (major, minor, suspended, diminished, augmented, 7ths, 9ths, 11ths, 13ths, altered…) — any quality can be built on any root note (e.g. F major 7 vs. E major 7 — same quality, different root).
- **Major and minor are the two primary qualities in modern music** — major sounds bright/positive/uplifting, minor sounds darker/sadder on the same root, and this holds true on every root note (a minor chord always sounds like "minor" wherever you put it).
- **Chords generate emotion through the intervals between their notes** — C alone is neutral; adding E creates one relationship (interval); adding G creates three simultaneous relationships (C–E, E–G, C–G), and altering any note changes the feeling because it changes those relationships. *Why* specific intervals produce specific feelings is left as an open/unanswered question — the practical takeaway is just to get ears familiar with what different chords feel like.
- **A chord's feeling is contextual, not fixed** — the same C major chord sounds subtly different heard in the key of C vs. heard in the key of G, because it's now in relationship with a different tonal center. This sets up why chord *progressions* (sequences that repeat) matter — each chord has its own sound, but also relates to the chords around it.
