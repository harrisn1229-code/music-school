# Producing and recording piano with Greg Wells

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1xeCIHtbwXE)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1xeCIHtbwXE)

## Synopsis

A short, on-the-floor tracking-session clip from producer/mixer Greg Wells via Mix with the Masters — a sibling video to [[Bringing vocals out of the speakers with Greg Wells]] (also Wells, also this channel), but concrete and hands-on where that one was pure philosophy. Wells walks through recording a grand piano part for a track he's rebuilding from a ten-month-old laptop demo, covering three things in sequence: how he's choosing to *play* the part (economically, leaning into repetition, deliberately avoiding harmonic density), the physical mic array capturing it (a three-zone Gefell setup — hammer pair, room pair, and a distant low-end mic), and the signal path getting it to Pro Tools (a Neve 88R console and a Chandler TG-1 compressor run in limit mode). The most valuable part of the clip is its second half: a real mid-session problem (a big chord choking the compressor on the featured mic pair) and the fix (backing the compressor off and re-tracking), used as a launching point for a stated philosophy about committing to tracked sounds while staying willing to backpedal — and a closing reflection on mistakes as a normal, even valuable, part of the recording process.

## Techniques

- **Play piano economically for a modern sound.** [0:00–0:35] "Piano is a very different beast, it's a huge sonic footprint, so I want to try and play it economically — not big flashy chords, just kind of open — and to me that sounds more modern. Unfortunately when there's too much harmonic information, it starts to sound dated — too many chords, too many major thirds, too many things. On this kind of song I think it's better to keep it kind of spare." Concrete move: an ostinato, repeating a fifth in the right hand rather than voicing full chords, deliberately leaning into "the monotony of it." → [[Arrangement and Song Form]], [[Piano Recording]], [[Masking and Carving]]
- **Hammer mics: two Gefell UM92s, the closest and most-featured pair.** [~1:00] Positioned right at the piano hammers — the pair carrying the most detail, and (per the session's own story below) the pair most at risk of choking a downstream compressor on a loud chord. → [[Gefell UMT70S]], [[Piano Recording]]
- **Room mics: a pair of Gefell UM300s.** [~1:00] Captures the room/ambient picture; not run through the compressor. → [[Piano Recording]], [[Drum Mic Technique]]
- **Low-end mic: a Violet Amethyst, deliberately placed about nine feet from the piano.** [~1:15] "Down at the foot of the piano, but about nine feet away from me, we have a Violet Amethyst mic to pick up the low end — and we're gonna probably add some low EQ to that, just for extra sort of tone if we need it." A distant placement used purely to isolate low-frequency content as its own signal for separate EQ treatment, rather than relying on the closer pairs for low end. → [[BASS Slot]], [[Piano Recording]]
- **The Gefell mic lineage: same origin company as Neumann, same M7 capsules.** "I've been a fan of Gefell mics since engineer Steve McMillan turned me onto my first good microphone for vocals... it's called a UMT 70S, fantastic mic that sounds a lot like a Neumann 87 at about a third of the price... Neumann and Gefell were the same company at some point, I don't know the exact details of the history, but they splintered — they all use M7 capsules." → [[Gefell UMT70S]], [[Neumann U47]], [[Neumann TLM 102]]
- **Console signal path: Neve 88R preamp + light shelving EQ.** "We are going to take advantage of this really pristine Neve 88R console that's here, and the mic pres are fantastic, the EQ is also fantastic — we're probably going to add a bit of top and a bit of bottom." A gentle additive move, not a carve. → [[UAD Neve 1073]], [[EQ]] (effect), [[Piano Recording]]
- **Compress the hammer mics only — the direct/detail pair, not the room or low-end mics.** Same direct-gets-the-heat, ambient-stays-alone split [[Drum Mic Technique]] documents for a live kit — applied here to piano. → [[Piano Recording]], [[Drum Mic Technique]]
- **Limit mode, not compression mode, on the Chandler TG-1 hardware compressor.** "There's a Chandler TG-1 here — limit mode is the way to use that thing. I, for whatever reason, I don't really get the compression mode in it, but limit is like magic. I don't want to do it too much because you can overcook it." A stated mode preference on a unit with both. → [[UAD Teletronix LA-2A]], [[Compression]] (effect), [[Plugin Wishlist]], [[Piano Recording]]
- **Clock straight to Pro Tools and try to finish processing before it hits the DAW.** Clocking with an Antelope 10MX (also his studio's clock in Los Angeles) — "try and get all the processing done before we put it in the box... recording the way you want it to sound, rather than tweaking after the fact." → [[Committing and Printing]]
- **The chorus choke, and the fix: back the compressor off and re-track, don't patch it in the mix.** A huge A♭ minor-seven chord at the top of the chorus overloaded the hammer-mic signal through the TG-1 — "it made the thing choke a little bit, it was like zzt, and then it would come back in, it almost sounded like the fader." It sounded great everywhere else in the take, but the choke kept bugging him ("whenever something is just kind of like gnawing away at me, like a little persistent itch, I just want to fix it"), so he re-tracked the piano through the same signal path with the TG-1 backed off — level-matching (and likely a follow-up clip-gain pass) afterward. → [[Piano Recording]], [[UAD Teletronix LA-2A]], [[Committing and Printing]]
- **Commit-to-tracked-sound philosophy, with an explicit escape hatch for backpedaling.** "I'm a real fan of recording the way you want it to sound rather than kind of tweaking after the fact — of course I always wind up tweaking after fact anyway, but I like to commit to what it feels like it needs to be for the record... usually it works out, but every once in a while I have to backpedal, because it's hard to know what a sound is going to need to do once other things are in the track and sort of competing for bandwidth." → [[Committing and Printing]]
- **Mistakes as a normal, even valuable, part of the process — closing reflection.** "Some of the best things I've ever come up with in my life were completely embarrassing mistakes that I would have thrown into the trash bin, but somebody else in the room said, 'what was that, we should use that.'... I think screwing up is part of moving forward, especially in the recording studio — you got to try stuff, and not everything's going to work out the first time, and that's the way it's supposed to be." → [[Committing and Printing]]
