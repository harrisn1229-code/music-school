## What's taught

### Flea's own slap/pop technique (his words, on camera)
- **Slap:** strike the low string with the thumb, specifically using **"the bone that's in the middle of your thumb."**
- **Pop:** hook the **middle finger** underneath the target string and pull up — Flea's own line: *"I will slap down with my thumb, and then with my middle finger it will go underneath the D string and pull up."*
- This is Flea's personal variant of a technique he explicitly says "is done several different ways" — not presented as the one correct method, just his own.

### BassBuzz's lightning-round fundamentals refresher
- Fresh **round-wound strings**, **low-to-medium action**, **tone knob up** — the gear/setup side of a clean slap tone.
- Mechanically: **rotate the forearm at the elbow** to strike the string with the **side of the thumb**, set the pop finger under the target string, then **rotate the forearm back up** to execute the pop — a single continuous down/up cycle, the same core idea as [[Teeter-Totter Slap-Pop Technique]] (that note's full "one continuous cycle" breakdown), described here in compressed form and in Flea-specific language.

### The drill itself — pattern, octave shape, and fretting
- **Two-bar repeating pattern**, all octaves: ignoring the first note, it's **pop-thumb-thumb** repeated, ending pop-thumb, where the final thumb doubles as the next repeat's first note — one continuous repeating cell rather than two distinct bars.
- **Octave-finding rule:** from any note, its octave is **up two strings and up two frets** (or, going the other way, **down two strings and down two frets**).
- **Fretting rule:** once past the open string, fret the octave shape **index-pinky**, not index-ring — index-ring "wastes energy" for this shape. Flagged as a common student mistake.
- **Chromatic walk:** play the two-bar cell **twice** starting on the open-E octave, move the whole shape **up one fret**, play it twice again, repeat — all the way up to the **12th fret**, then back down the same way.

### Tempo ladder
- **~70 BPM** — explicit beginner starting point.
- Progress in **2–3 BPM increments** at a time.
- **~140 BPM** — named intermediate target.
- **~218 BPM** — "Flea tempo," the speed of the pattern as played in the source clip; framed as the ceiling target ("if you can go faster than that, you get to replace Flea in the Chili Peppers").

### Four things to self-check while drilling
1. **Accuracy** — hitting only the strings intended, no stray hits.
2. **Consistency** — every note ringing out for its full duration, not choked short.
3. **Breathing** — a real, non-joke reminder; most players stop breathing normally by the halfway point of a full pass.

### Real Flea lines built from this exact pattern
- **"Get Up and Jump"** (Red Hot Chili Peppers' 1984 self-titled debut) — the thumb-pop rhythm of the line matches the drill almost exactly, minus one small hammer-on at the end.
- **"Dark Necessities"** and the Chili Peppers' cover of **"Higher Ground"** — a related variant of the same cell, **thumb-thumb-pop** (starting on the thumb instead of the pop) rather than pop-thumb-thumb.
- Referenced as pattern matches only — no tab, key, or full breakdown given for any of the three songs in this video; treated as ear-training/pattern-recognition evidence, not song lessons in their own right, so no dedicated song notes were created from this alone.

### Three recipes for turning the drill into original lines
1. **Static bass** — keep every slap on the open E string as a fixed "static" bass note, and move only the pops around a chosen scale. Worked with E minor pentatonic (E, G, A, B, D).
2. **Moving octaves** — instead of a fixed slap note, move the whole octave shape (both slap and its pop octave) through the scale, using the same two-strings-up/two-frets-up shape to find each note's higher octave.
3. **Be the Flea** — the deeper takeaway: invent an original 1–2 bar slap/pop combination of your own, and drill *that* up to speed the same way, moving it around the neck. The real lesson isn't copying Flea's specific notes, it's copying the process he used to invent the pattern in the first place.
