> [!danger]- 🔒 Answer key — do not open until you have answered every question
> **Q1.** **Partly convention, partly physiology — a singer audibly works harder up there**
> The ear hears the effort, so height and strain read as the same thing whether or not a voice is involved.
>
> **Q2.** **Verses sit lower, choruses lift — so check where the chorus sits relative to the verse first**
> A large part of what makes a chorus feel like one is simply that it's higher than what came before it. Moving it up a third often does more than writing new notes.
>
> **Q3.** **Widening the range — bigger leaps and a wider span read as dramatic**
> It works independently of how high the line actually goes. A melody circling three notes feels contained no matter what register it's in.
>
> **Q4.** **Climb in register · shorten the note values · repeat a fragment in ascending sequence · withhold the tonic**
> The first three make the line busier and higher as the verse runs out; the fourth makes sure nothing has resolved by the time the chorus arrives.
>
> **Q5.** **Nothing feels resolved, so the chorus landing on the root becomes the release**
> Resolution is a resource. Spending it in the verse means the chorus has nothing left to deliver.
>
> **Q6.** **Strip everything back right before the chorus so it arrives against space, not against a wall**
> *Rubric (5):* describes subtraction immediately before the chorus rather than a build into it (2) · says the chorus then arrives against space (1) · says the drop is the arrival in loop-based production, so subtraction beforehand creates the contrast a build would spend early (2)
> Building and stripping are both ways of making the chorus feel like an event, and they work on the same principle from opposite directions — what matters is the size of the change at the boundary, not the absolute energy either side of it. In loop-based production the section after the boundary is usually the loudest and densest thing in the track, so climbing into it leaves nowhere to go: the verse ends near the ceiling and the chorus can only match it. Emptying the bar or two before the drop widens the gap for free. It also agrees with how the genre is already listened to, where the moment of arrival is the point and everything before it is preparation.
>
> **Q7.** **Repetition · rhythmic distinctiveness · singable range with clear contour — repetition outranks everything**
> A hook has to come back, and be short enough to hold after a single pass. If it can't be sung back it isn't a hook at all.
>
> **Q8.** **Tap it with the pitches stripped out — if it's still recognisable, it's rhythmically distinct**
> Passing proves the hook survives being remembered badly, which is the condition it will actually be recalled under.
>
> **Q9.** **Contrast against what surrounds it, and simplicity usually beating complexity**
> If the verse is busy and low, a sparse high hook lands harder. The hook is the part that has to survive being remembered badly, so complexity is a liability.
>
> **Q10.** **Change the rhythm · widen it · move where it sits against the chord**
> After those three: add space so rests make what surrounds them land, displace the phrase off the downbeat, and reharmonize underneath it for the same melody with a different meaning.
>
> **Q11.** **Rhythm — and it's reached for last because pitch feels like where melody lives**
> Most dull melodies are dull rhythmically, circling even note values on the beat, but the instinct when a melody isn't working is to look for better notes.
>
> **Q12.** **Change the rhythm first and stop as soon as it works — usually before step three**
> *Rubric (5):* runs the steps in order, starting with rhythm and stopping at the first fix that solves it (2) · explains that the order encodes which fix pays off most, so working down it is cheaper than trying everything (2) · notes that the list without the order would send you to pitch first, which is the common mistake (1)
> With twenty minutes the order is the whole method. Start by rewriting only the rhythm — displace notes off the beat, vary the note values, leave a rest where the line currently runs straight through — and play it back before touching a single pitch. If that fixes it, you're finished and the melody you already had turns out to have been fine. If not, widen the line next: one real leap, a top note higher than anything it has reached so far. Only then start moving landing notes onto 9ths and 7ths. The ranking is the answer because everyone already knows the list. What's hard to know is that rhythm outranks pitch by a wide margin, and that knowing it saves you the hour usually spent auditioning better notes for a line whose problem was never which notes it used.
