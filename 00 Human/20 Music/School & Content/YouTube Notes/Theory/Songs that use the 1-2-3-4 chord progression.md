# Songs that use the 1-2-3-4 chord progression

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eC4SEVRT9wE)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eC4SEVRT9wE)

## Synopsis

The simplest possible harmonized-scale idea — climb straight up the first four diatonic chords of the major scale (I–ii–iii–IV) — surveyed across classic rock, Beatles, video-game, and contemporary examples, closing with a case study of the same idea "summiting" onto a cadential 6/4 in a *Mario Kart* theme.

## Techniques

- **The progression**: I–ii–iii–IV, literally reading straight up a harmonized major scale — as basic on paper as reading a textbook diagram, but used to real effect in many classic songs because the unbroken ascending contour reads unmistakably as "climbing."
- **Examples**: "Like a Rolling Stone" (Bob Dylan) — climbs I–ii–iii–IV then continues to "summit" onto V. "Boys Don't Cry" (The Cure) — same shape, key of A. "Here, There and Everywhere" (The Beatles, Paul McCartney) — verse climbs the shape twice before diverging. "Sexy Sadie" (The Beatles, John Lennon) — bridge climbs the identical shape, same key (G) as "Here, There and Everywhere" — Bennett floats (without asserting) that Lennon may have been influenced by McCartney's earlier use, or that it's simply an "obvious" progression to land on independently, since it's just a literal scale-read. "Daydream Believer" (The Monkees) — same era, same device, possibly Beatles-influenced. "Lean on Me" (Bill Withers) — climbs the four chords, lingers on IV, then climbs back down partway — creating enough of a distinct melodic shape in the CHORDS themselves (not just the vocal) that it functions almost like a hook.
- **Rainbow Road theme (Mario Kart 64)** — climbs the shape in the key of A, then "summits" onto a **cadential 6/4** (cross-referenced to the dedicated cadential-6/4 video in this same batch) rather than landing plainly on V — voiced as a second-inversion I chord over the dominant bass note, further stretched by a sus4 before finally resolving — described as giving the game's climactic final race theme extra grandeur.
- **You and Me Song (The Wannadies)** — reuses the identical I-ii-iii-IV shape for BOTH the verse and the chorus of the same song (the available transcript excerpt is cut off mid-explanation of what specifically differs between the two uses — not claimed beyond confirming the shared harmonic shape).
