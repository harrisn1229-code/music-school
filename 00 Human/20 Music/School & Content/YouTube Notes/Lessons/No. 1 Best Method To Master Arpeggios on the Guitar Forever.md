# #1 Best Method To Master Arpeggios on the Guitar Forever

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2HhUqnF4FCs)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2HhUqnF4FCs)

## Synopsis

A short (8:27) daily-practice-method lesson from Nathan Borton pitching **focused arpeggio practice** as the single technique that, done consistently, improves playing the most — framed against the common failure mode of having "worked on arpeggios before" without ever exploring them systematically. Rather than teaching one arpeggio shape or one song's changes, the video lays out **three complementary practice angles for covering every diatonic arpeggio of a key**, all worked over the same key (B♭ major, two flats — B♭ and E♭) so the three angles build on each other: (1) a **horizontal "box position" method** — a fixed four-finger box shape that houses one diatonic arpeggio at a time, shifted up the neck to the next box for the next diatonic arpeggio in the key (B♭maj7 → Cm7 → …), varied by direction (all-ascending, all-descending, up-down alternating); (2) a **vertical, single-string-set method** — running one arpeggio up and down a chosen string set, starting not just on the root but on **every chord tone** (root, 3rd, 5th, 7th) in turn, practiced across the E, A, D and G strings; and (3) **two-octave patterns with named starting fingers** — full-neck two-octave arpeggio shapes anchored to a specific starting finger per starting chord tone (root = 2nd finger, 3rd = 1st finger, 5th = 2nd finger, 7th = 3rd finger). The throughline across all three is **visualize, don't just muscle-memorize** — the goal is instant aural/visual access to a key's arpeggio sounds, not rote finger patterns. Borton is explicit that this isn't a one-and-done exercise: it's framed as an ongoing, lifelong background practice meant to be repeated across every key and mode. Distinct from this vault's existing arpeggio material (which mostly targets *navigating a moving chord progression*, e.g. [[Arpeggio Shapes for Playing Over Changes]]'s CAGED-adjacency method) — this video is instead a **systematic daily drill for internalizing every diatonic arpeggio of a single static key**, closer in spirit to (but a genuinely different method from) [[Learn a Valuable Arpeggio Exercise to Map Out the Fretboard]]'s five-CAGED-position system. A mid-video sponsor read (Strange Joy Strings) and a Patreon plug (guitar-pro/PDF transcriptions of the exercises, plus a bonus etude applying the concepts over a blues progression) are both external and not further extractable.

## What's taught

- **The core premise: focused, systematic arpeggio practice — not just "having worked on arpeggios before" — is presented as the single highest-leverage daily habit for improvement.** Arpeggios aren't the whole of improvisation, but they supply the **target notes** that let a listener (and the player) hear the underlying chord changes inside a line — stated both at the open and again at the close of the video as the reason arpeggio fluency matters. *(~0:00–0:30, ~6:15–6:30)*
- **Method 1 — Horizontal "box position" practice, shifted per diatonic chord.** Place all four fretting fingers on the neck to form a fixed box shape; the box defines a small playing zone — moving outside it is the trigger to change strings, not to reach with a finger. Starting on the low E string in **B♭ major** (2 flats: B♭, E♭), play a one-octave **B♭maj7** arpeggio, then, staying in the same box, work through every other diatonic arpeggio available in that box up to the end of the fretboard; when the box runs out of room, **shift the whole box up (or down) one fret** to the next diatonic arpeggio in the key (B♭maj7 → **Cm7** next, continuing up the scale), again starting on the low E string, and repeat for every arpeggio of the key. **Variation drills on top of the base pattern:** play the whole sequence descending-only instead of ascending-only, or alternate — ascend one arpeggio, descend the next. *(~0:35–2:15)*
- **Method 2 — Vertical, single-string-set practice, starting on every chord tone.** Instead of moving horizontally within one box, run an arpeggio **up and down a single string set**. Demonstrated on the low E string with B♭maj7: first starting on the **root**, then explicitly broken into starting on **every chord tone in turn — root, 3rd, 5th, and 7th** — so the same arpeggio is drilled from four different entry points, not just the root-anchored one most players default to. Repeat the same starting-tone drill on the **E, A, D and G strings** "for maximum flexibility and mastery." *(~3:00–3:50)*
- **Vertical shapes chain into two-octave range.** The single-string-set starting-tone shapes **connect vertically** — e.g. a two-octave B♭maj7 arpeggio starting on the **3rd** is built simply by repeating the same one-octave-from-the-3rd shape an octave higher, extending coverage further up the neck rather than requiring a new shape to be learned. *(~3:50–4:05)*
- **Method 3 — Two-octave patterns with named starting fingers, per starting chord tone.** For B♭maj7 starting on the low E string: a full two-octave horizontal pattern beginning with the **2nd finger** (rooted on the low E string); a second two-octave pattern starting on the **3rd (D)**, also on the low E string, but this time anchored with the **1st finger** while staying inside the same box-position logic from Method 1 — opening up more of the fretboard than staying anchored to one root-only shape. **Stated common starting-finger assignments** (transferable rule of thumb, not B♭maj7-specific): starting on the **root → 2nd finger**, starting on the **3rd → 1st finger**, starting on the **5th → 2nd finger** (same finger as the root), starting on the **7th → 3rd finger**. Explicitly noted that other starting fingers are possible and will alter the resulting shape — these are the common defaults, not the only option. *(~4:10–5:20)*
- **Visualize, don't rely on muscle memory.** Called out directly as a practice-quality rule during Method 1 but implicitly governing all three methods: actively picture each arpeggio's shape and chord-tone identity inside each box/position rather than letting the fingers run the pattern on autopilot — the stated payoff is that this develops the *ear* for the key, not just finger recall, giving "instant access to its essential sounds." *(~2:00–2:15)*
- **Portability and practice cadence, stated at the close:** this is explicitly framed as **not a one-time exercise** — an "ongoing, lifelong process" meant to be run "every day with every possible key or mode," which the video notes leaves "almost an infinite number of options" to keep working through — Borton says it's something he keeps going in the background at all times, not a drill that gets "finished." *(~6:30–7:00)*
- **External, not further extractable:** a mid-video sponsor read for Strange Joy Strings (not a technique tip), and a closing Patreon plug for guitar-pro/PDF transcriptions of the exercises plus a bonus etude applying all three methods over a blues progression (gated content, external to the vault). *(~2:15–2:45, ~7:00–8:00)*
