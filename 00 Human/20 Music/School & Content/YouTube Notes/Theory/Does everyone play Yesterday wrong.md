# Does everyone play Yesterday wrong?

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pOtcLmOeScM)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pOtcLmOeScM)

## Synopsis

A software-verified (Melodyne) case study proving that "Yesterday" (Beatles)'s famous opening phrase is universally transcribed and sung wrong — nearly every cover and every piece of published sheet music sings G→F, but the actual first note on two of the phrase's four occurrences in the recording is a sharp F or an A, not a G. Used as a springboard into why sheet-music transcription errors happen and propagate, and why the "correct" version of a melody is itself a slippery concept.

## Techniques

- **The core finding, pitch-verified with Melodyne**: "Yesterday"'s opening phrase (G→F) recurs 4 times in the song; two of those four times (the very opening line, and the start of verse 3) the actual sung pitch is different — a slightly sharp F, and an A→F respectively — not the G→F that appears in every piece of sheet music checked and nearly every cover.
- **A plausible mechanism for why an audible mistranscription becomes universal**: the two "wrong" occurrences are fast/fleeting, making the exact pitch hard for the ear to lock onto; the two correctly-notated G→F occurrences are sung slower and more clearly. The theory offered: listeners' ears/brains subconsciously "fill in" the harder-to-catch fast phrases with the pitch they've already clearly heard on the slow ones, since G→F is close enough to the true pitch that the substitution doesn't sound wrong.
- **Why published sheet music isn't a reliable ground-truth source, structurally**: the songwriter usually doesn't write it — a publisher's transcriber does, working by ear (not specialized pitch-detection tools) and therefore capable of making exactly this kind of error. **Errors propagate via copying**: a transcriber working from an existing (already-wrong) piece of sheet music, rather than the original recording, will reproduce and spread the same mistake rather than catching it. **Sheet music is also not meant to be a 1:1 transcript in the first place** — it's a performance guide that assumes stylistic elaboration, so exact-pitch fidelity was never fully the point.
- **A recorded performance is not the only "correct" version even by the original artist**: Paul McCartney sang the (technically wrong but sheet-music-standard) G→F version live in the 1960s, then switched to matching the original record's actual pitches in later performances (e.g. a 2004 performance) — demonstrating that even a songwriter's own live rendition of their own song legitimately drifts and isn't a fixed, singular "correct" melody to transcribe against.
- **Practical caution for transcription-by-ear work**: when a target phrase repeats multiple times at different tempos/clarity levels within the same recording, don't assume the clearest/slowest instance is representative of all of them — cross-check each occurrence independently rather than pattern-matching the ambiguous ones onto the unambiguous ones.
