# Fox Stevenson: You Will NEVER Use Clipping The Same Way Again (with A/B Tests) [Get A Loud Pro Mix]

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rXbMcPeD3Mw)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rXbMcPeD3Mw)

## Synopsis

Fox Stevenson's central claim, demonstrated with A/B tests: "perceived dynamics" and "measurable dynamics" are different things, and a heavily clipped/limited track can still sound perceptually dynamic even though a meter says it isn't. Digital clipping specifically gets reframed as a legitimate creative/character tool, not an error to avoid — the question isn't "does this measure as dynamic," it's "does this sound dynamic," and those two questions have different answers more often than most producers assume.

## Techniques

- **Perceived dynamics and measured dynamics are different things — a heavily limited/clipped mix can still sound dynamic to a listener even though it isn't dynamic on a meter.** The core reframe behind the whole video: chasing measured dynamic range isn't the same goal as making a track feel dynamic, and a track can be loud, glued, and squashed while still reading as punchy and alive. → [[Clipping and Loudness]]
- **Digital clipping is not inherently bad — it can function as a legitimate character/dynamics tool, not just a flaw to avoid.** A clipped kick, A/B'd against an unclipped version, was preferred for sounding more "contained" — described explicitly as "nice saturated" character rather than damage, once heard rather than just measured. **Reframe clipping/crushing as a stylistic choice tied to genre identity** ("clipping and crushing together sounds like dance music"), not merely a loudness-maximization necessity — the choice of how glued vs. dynamic a mix sounds is an aesthetic decision as much as a technical one. → [[Saturation and Distortion]]
- **Two kicks at identical peak level (both 0 dBFS) can sound dramatically different in perceived loudness purely from frequency balance — high-end content and low-mid character, not level, drive perceived loudness.** A direct demonstration that "loudness" as heard isn't simply a function of peak or RMS level; two technically-equal-level sounds can feel like completely different volumes.
- **An unmastered, ungl glued mix genuinely sounds worse than the same mix mastered and glued together — even though it's technically "more dynamic."** Reinforces that glue/mastering is a real perceptual improvement most listeners prefer, not purely a loudness-war compromise being made reluctantly.
- **Phase relationships between layered/duplicated signals change how clipping and compression behave on the combined signal — sometimes by several dB, even with identical processing settings.** Demonstrated: adding a signal to a phase-shifted copy of itself and clipping the sum produces a meaningfully different (in the demo, ~3 dB louder) result purely from the phase relationship, not from anything about the clipper's settings. Practical stance: don't obsess over phase to the point of creative paralysis, but be aware it's a real variable worth checking when a layered sound reacts to processing in an unexpected way. → [[Phase Alignment]]
- **Deliberately "fold" an aggressive snare into the master limiter/clipper while keeping the sub/bass out of that same aggressive processing.** Rather than pushing overall gain up uniformly (which drags the sub along with everything else), let the limiter/clipper specifically catch and shape the snare's transient, so the snare gets glued/loud character while the sub stays free to keep resonating without being squashed. How much of this "bow-tie" low-end pinching is acceptable is a judgment call tied to how separated you want your elements to feel — not a fixed rule. → [[Limiting]], [[Bass Design]]
- **Deliberately keep a lead element (kick) significantly louder than the rest of the mix, then bring the other elements back in around it** — an unusual, deliberately imbalanced starting point (cited from his own track "Ether") rather than balancing everything toward an even level from the start.
