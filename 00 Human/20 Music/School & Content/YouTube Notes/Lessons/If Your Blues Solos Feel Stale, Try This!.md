# If Your Blues Solos Feel Stale, Try This!

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_x3_REfEzrw)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_x3_REfEzrw)

## Synopsis

Jack Ruch teaches how to break a stale blues solo out of its box by inserting a **jazz-style ii–V–I (2-5-1)** at the point in a 12-bar blues where the **I chord resolves to the IV chord** (bar 4 of the form). Worked entirely in the **key of G**, targeting **C** (the IV chord) as the "1" of a **Dm7 → G7 → C** insertion — the same major ii-V-I shape as [[The ii-V-I]]'s own worked example, applied specifically to the blues's I→IV pivot. Ruch is explicit that this substitution is a **soloist's private reharmonization**: it works "even if the rhythm section isn't playing this harmony underneath" — you can hear/imply the Dm7-G7-C motion in your line while the band just holds the plain blues changes. Four increasingly decorated example lines are built from the same chord-tone/arpeggio foundation, the last one lifted from a Joe Pass line (from the tune "Bluesology," originally in F, transposed up to G here). Reference listening named directly: T-Bone Walker, early B.B. King (*Live at the Regal*), Ray Charles for how this vocabulary sits inside "jump blues" and swing-blues playing without taking it out of style, plus Joe Pass and Wes Montgomery for the jazzier end of the same vocabulary. Full tab/notation is gated behind Ruch's Patreon — no tab data survives in the transcript, only spoken note names/degrees, so the fingering/position detail below is reconstructed from those verbal descriptions.

## What's taught

- **The core substitution: insert a major ii–V–I (Dm7–G7–C) wherever a blues resolves from its I chord to its IV chord.** In a 12-bar blues in G, the I→IV move happens at bar 4 (G→C). Because C is "the landing spot," treat it as the "1" of its own ii-V-I: the "2" is a **minor 7 chord a whole step up from the target** (Dm7), the "5" is a **dominant 7 built a fourth above that** (G7, alterable — "G7 flat nine" for extra tension, or "G7 sharp five" for an augmented-tension color), landing on **C**. Stated directly as usable purely as a soloing device, independent of whether the rhythm section changes its comping — "it doesn't matter if the rhythm section is playing this harmony underneath... you can play these 2-5-1 [lines] even if the band isn't playing those actual chords." → [[The ii-V-I]], [[Chord Substitution]], [[Blues Form and Harmony]]

- **Line 1 — Dm7 arpeggio → diminished-7-off-the-3rd over G7 → chromatic enclosure into C.** Starts on the **5th of Dm7**, down to the **♭3**, down to the **root**, then down to the **♭7** (C) of the Dm7 shape — "right out of this chord shape." From there, **down a half step into the 3rd of G7**. At that point there's a choice: stay strictly inside the chord (arpeggiate straight up a G7 arpeggio) or, as played here, launch a **diminished-7th arpeggio starting from that same 3rd of G7** — described as giving "that cool funky little flat-nine sound" over the G7 (the diminished-7-off-the-3rd device — full mechanic on [[Diminished Chords]]). That diminished shape then **resolves down a half step to the 5th of C**. The phrase closes with a small **chromatic enclosure over the C chord**, targeting the **3rd of C** — approach and surround the target note chromatically before landing on it (device detail on [[Downbeat and Upbeat Enclosures]] / [[Enclosures Through the Scale]]). Framed explicitly as a simple foundational line meant to be embellished and extended once it's comfortable.

- **Line 2 — same shapes, relocated to lower strings, played with shuffle feel.** Moves the same underlying vocabulary down to the lower strings: a **Dm7 arpeggio ascending root → ♭3 → 5th → ♭7**, then a **chromatic enclosure targeting the 3rd of G7** (rather than line 1's straight half-step approach), then the same **diminished-7th arpeggio move** as line 1 back over G7. Because the backing is a shuffle, the line is played with a swung, shuffled rhythmic feel rather than straight eighths. Stated purpose: this kind of line "inserts right into your everyday blues soloing" and gives a "jazzy flavor without really taking it out of the style" — the vocabulary he says T-Bone Walker, early B.B. King (*Live at the Regal*), and Ray Charles all use constantly in swing/jump blues playing.

- **Line 3 — relative-major substitution over Dm7, moved up a position, plus a "Hendrix chord" ♯9/♭9 tension on G7.** A more advanced version, moved up the neck a position with an added chord substitution: over the **Dm7 (ii)**, play **up a minor third and arpeggiate the relative major** — an **F major 7 arpeggio** — described as a common substitution that gives you the **♭3rd, 5th, ♭7th, and 9th of a Dm9 sound** (matching [[Chord Substitution]]'s relative-major-over-minor device documented elsewhere on the same technique family). The line starts by hitting a **G** and sliding into the arpeggio shape, then targets the **3rd of G7** when that chord arrives, played out of a **C-shape CAGED bar-chord voicing** of G7 (the same physical shape Ruch says he comps G7 with routinely). From there, arpeggiate **up the G7 shape: 3rd → 5th → ♭7 → root**, then add **altered tension**: the **♯9 and ♭9** of G7 together — named directly as "the Hendrix chord... that kind of Purple Haze chord" — fingered by **barring the 1st finger down** to grab the ♭9 within the same shape. The tension notes are played with a **hammer-on, pull-off, and slide embellishment, picked only once** for the whole run (a single pick attack driving multiple slurred notes). → [[Chord Substitution]], [[Altered Chords]]

- **Line 4 — a transposed Joe Pass "Bluesology" line: G Mixolydian opening, relative-major arpeggio in a new position, and a G-minor-over-C substitution.** The longest, most advanced line, lifted from Joe Pass's playing on the tune **"Bluesology"** (a swing blues in F originally; Ruch transposes it up a whole step to **G** to match this lesson's key — he'd previously posted it in the original key of F as a short-form video/Instagram post). Structure: **(1)** opens over the G chord with a **G Mixolydian fragment** — descending down toward the **♭7**, connected by a small chromatic passing note between the root and the ♭7. **(2)** moves into the **same F major 7 arpeggio** used in line 3 (the relative-major-over-Dm7 substitution) but played from a **different neck position** — out of a different bar-chord (barre) shape than line 3 used, over the D minor chord (F major 7 = relative major of D minor). **(3)** a run of **chromatic passing material**, described by Ruch as functionally just "playing out of the key of C" at that point. **(4)** drops to a **G minor chord voiced over the C chord** — a substitution Ruch calls "a really nice" one, equivalent to playing **G Dorian**, which gives a **C Mixolydian sound** (same underlying note pool, reframed) — and "sneaks in" the **major 7th of G minor** as a passing color tone inside that line. **(5)** closes with further chromatic connective material back to the top of the form. Reference recording: Joe Pass's "Bluesology." Reference listening for this style generally: **Joe Pass, Wes Montgomery** — "if you listen to guys like Joe Pass or Wes Montgomery when they play a blues, you hear this stuff all over the place." → [[Chord Substitution]], [[Scale Modes Reference]]

- **Stated practice priority: nail the transitions before adding embellishment.** Ruch's explicit generalizing advice, stated directly after line 3: "once you get the basic movements together... those basic connecting of the chords, like targeting the chord tone and landing on it on the downbeat when the chord change happens — once you get those little transitions [locked in], it's way easier to then embellish and add more to the phrase." I.e. rhythmic precision on the chord-tone landing comes first; decoration (enclosures, tension notes, hammer-ons/pulls/slides) is layered on top only once that's solid. → [[Chord-Tone Soloing]]

- **Closing framing.** Ruch calls the whole device "a great way to break out of the box and add some sophisticated, different, jazz-inspired lines to your playing" that "blends right in with your... blues vocabulary" rather than sounding foreign to the style — stated as something he uses in his own playing "all the time."

## Drilling this into your own playing

- **Isolate the insertion point first, out of time.** Loop just the I→IV bar (bar 4 in G: the G-to-C move) with a static drone or slow metronome and run the plain **Dm7 → G7 → C** arpeggio shapes (line 1's shape) until landing on a chord tone of C on the downbeat is automatic — matches Ruch's own stated priority above and the practice model already documented on [[Woodshedding Changes with a Looper]].
- **Drill the diminished-7-off-the-3rd move on its own.** From the 3rd of any dominant 7 chord, run a diminished-7 arpeggio and resolve a half step down to a chord tone of the target — this is line 1/2's signature "flat-nine" color and the most reusable single device here (transfers to any V→I in any key); see [[Diminished Chords]] for the fuller position-by-position version of the same shape.
- **Drill the relative-major-over-minor substitution separately from the rest of the line.** Over any minor 7 chord, arpeggiate the major 7 chord a minor 3rd above its root (F maj7 over Dm7, or transposed: any m7's relative major) until the ♭3-5-♭7-9 sound is familiar on its own, decoupled from line 3's specific fingering — the device generalizes to any minor ii chord, not just Dm7.
- **Isolate the ♯9→♭9 "Hendrix chord" tension shape on its own dominant chord**, practiced as a target color rather than embedded in a full line — grab it with the 1st-finger barre as described, resolve it down into the I chord's 3rd, repeat until it's a reflex.
- **Transpose the whole system to at least 2–3 other keys**, since every line here is built from a movable ii-V-I shape, not a fixed-position lick — e.g. blues in C (targeting F: Am7-D7-G... wait, in C the I→IV move targets F, so the insertion is Gm7→C7→F) or blues in A (targeting D: Em7-A7-D). Relocate lines 1–2's shapes first since they're the least decorated, then lines 3–4.
