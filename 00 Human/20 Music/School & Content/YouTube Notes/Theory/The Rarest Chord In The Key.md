# The Rarest Chord In The Key

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DPpvkpAkJBM)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DPpvkpAkJBM)

## Synopsis

Why vii° (the diatonic leading-tone/"seventh" chord of the key — B° in C major) is, despite being one of the seven diatonic chords, almost never actually used. Works through real examples where it does appear, then gives two concrete reasons for its rarity: it's genuinely awkward on guitar (easy on keyboard, hard to grip on guitar), and even when a songwriter reaches for it, it usually gets "swallowed" into the far more common V7 chord that sits right next to it.

## Techniques

- **Definition/spelling**: vii° is the diminished triad built on the 7th scale degree — B-D-F in the key of C. Diatonic, not chromatic, but rare in practice.
- **Keyboard-idiomatic, guitar-awkward**: on piano, vii° is trivial to find as a stepping-stone triad shape moving down the white notes (Dm→C→B°→Am→G). On guitar it's a genuinely difficult shape to grip, which the video gives as one concrete reason composers writing on guitar rarely stumble onto it. Examples: "Seaside Rendezvous" (Queen, piano, B° as a descending white-note passing chord), "Lover of Mine" (Beach House, organ, same B° in C).
- **Non-white-key examples exist too**: "Do It Now" (Paul McCartney, key of G) uses F♯° as the leading-tone chord, notated on the sheet as F♯m7♭5 (a diminished triad + diatonic 7th = half-diminished, symbol ø7). "Lover, You Should've Come Over" (Jeff Buckley, key of D) uses C♯ø7 the same way.
- **Half-diminished notation confusion**: the natural diatonic 7th on top of vii° is a half-diminished 7th (ø7 symbol — a degree sign with a slash through it), but because that symbol is unfamiliar, it's very often written instead as "m7♭5" (take a minor 7 chord, flatten the 5th) — same chord, different label.
- **Same chord, two valid analyses (leading-tone vs. secondary ii)**: in "Lover, You Should've Come Over," the C♯ø7→F♯7 pair can be read either as the diatonic vii° of D, OR as a secondary ii→V into vi (Bm) — C♯ø7 = ii/vi ("the secondary supertonic," the chord one step before a secondary dominant) and F♯7 = V/vi, together forming a full ii–V–I into Bm even though the song's real key is D major. Same ambiguity in "You Never Give Me Your Money" (Beatles) — analyzable as vii° in C major OR as ii of A minor.
- **Classical precedent**: Mozart's Piano Sonata No. 5 (key of D) repeatedly uses C♯° resolving to D major.
- **Why it's rare, reason 1 — it's "hidden in plain sight" inside V7**: vii° (B-D-F in C) is literally the top three notes of G7 (G-B-D-F). Whenever a songwriter would logically reach for the weaker vii° resolution, they tend to instinctively add the missing root (G) in the bass instead, getting the much stronger V7→I perfect-cadence resolution (descending 5th, G→C) — so vii° keeps getting absorbed into V7 rather than used on its own.
- **Why it's rare, reason 2 — systematic mislabeling**: in "M79" (Vampire Weekend, key of G), an arpeggiated F♯° is frequently transcribed on sheet music as "D7 over F♯" instead — nearly the same notes (just adds a D), but not what's actually being played. Same mislabeling recurs on "Do It" (McCartney) — the chorus's F♯° is written as D7/F♯. Read as a bias: musicians/transcribers default to hearing an incomplete-looking diminished chord as a dominant 7th missing its root, because that unlocks familiar V→I cadence logic, rather than naming the diminished chord for what it actually is.
