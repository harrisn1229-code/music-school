# The Most Useful Chord in Jazz Fusion

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=62fFtZLKWPE)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=62fFtZLKWPE)

## Synopsis
An advanced jazz-fusion harmony lesson on **upper structure chords**: one specific upper-structure triad (E minor, in the key of E, "the flat 6 upper structure") placed over six different possible bass notes, each bass note reinterpreting the exact same upper voicing as a completely different functional chord — flat-6 major, flat-7 dominant, flat-3 (iv minor 6), altered dominant (V7alt of vi), a Spanish/sus dominant, and its flat-9 twin. Max demonstrates each in a short diatonic progression, then plays a full track using four of the six stacked together, and sets a practice challenge to use the technique in an original progression. This fills a real gap in the vault's theory library — nothing currently documents upper-structure/polychord thinking.

## What's taught
- **Core concept — an upper structure is a chord with a different bass note underneath it.** Any chord type (triad, 7th, whatever) can sit "on top" while a different bass note underneath reinterprets the whole stack as a new composite chord; changing only the bass note can either just invert the upper chord (if the new bass note is already one of its own tones) or create a genuinely new chord with new extensions/tensions against that bass. Typically triads are used as the upper structure, but any chord type works. → [[Chord Naming by Context]], [[Chord Inversions]], [[Extended Chords]]
- **The demonstrated upper structure**: an E minor triad, tried over six different bass notes, always placed on the **♭6 degree of a major key** (or the natural 7 in a minor key) as the recommended default insertion point — "possibly the best place to put it in a major key." Worked throughout in the key of E major, so the upper structure sits on C.
- **Bass note 1 — same as the upper structure's own root** → a plain **♭6 major chord** (C major7♯11 / maj7♭5 in the key of E). No fixed resolution requirement; can be dropped in and left hanging, or optionally resolved down a half-step (♭6→5) if a smoother landing is wanted. Common in fusion to deliberately NOT resolve it ("going against the grain is what's hip").
- **Bass note 2 — a 4th below the upper structure's root (the upper structure read as the ♭7 of the bass note)** → a **13th dominant chord** (D13 here). Being a dominant chord, it can resolve many ways: backdoor resolution into the tonic, as a tritone substitute into the chord a half-step up, as a straight V7 into a new key or a modal chord. Demonstrated as a **tritone substitute** resolving into the vi chord. → [[Secondary Dominants]] (dominant-function roster), [[Modulation (Key Change)]]
- **Bass note 3 — a major 3rd below the upper structure's root (the upper structure read as the ♭3 of the bass note)** → a **minor 6/9 chord** (A minor6add9, the iv chord in the key). Described as "super dramatic," ideal at a high-tension/tragic moment; forgiving about what follows — commonly resolves to I but works resolving almost anywhere.
- **Bass note 4 — a major 3rd above the upper structure's root, read enharmonically (the upper structure treated as the ♭3-equivalent/upper-triad of an altered dominant)** → an **altered dominant, ♭7♯5♯9** (G♯7♯5♯9 in this key — functioning as V7alt/vi, i.e. the secondary dominant of the relative-minor's dominant). One of the most common altered-dominant sounds in jazz; typically resolves down a 5th into a minor chord (here, C♯ minor9), though it can also target the IV, the ii, the ♭III (as a tritone sub), or the relative/parallel major. → [[Altered Chords]], [[Secondary Dominants]]
- **Two additional, rarer bass options mentioned but not used in the demo track**: (5) the upper structure read as the **♯4** of a bass note → a "Spanish dominant" (7sus4♭9-type sound, F♯7sus4♭9 in this key); (6) the upper structure read as the **♭9/♭2** of a bass note → the same chord shape a half-step away (B7sus4♭9), functioning similarly. Both are offered as an ear-training challenge ("see if you can figure those out") before being explained.
- **Assembling multiple upper-structure options into one progression**: the demo track alternates a plain diatonic chord with an upper-structure chord, cycling through several of the bass-note options above in sequence rather than picking just one — deliberately "overkill" for demonstration, but a real technique: chain 2+ different upper-structure readings of the *same* upper triad through a progression, driven by whatever bass motion/voice-leading connects them smoothly.
- **Practice takeaway**: write an original progression using at least two (ideally all four/six) of these bass-note reinterpretations of a single upper triad — internalizing the required voice-leading and bass motion is the actual skill, not just knowing the chord names.
