# Songs that use 3/4 time

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LtvtkwQjDIw)
📺 [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LtvtkwQjDIw)

## Synopsis

3/4 as "waltz time" — its genre home (waltz, folk, ceremonial/traditional song, minuet), why rock largely avoids it in favor of 4/4's driving feel (demonstrated by a real 3/4-to-4/4 genre-conversion cover), swung 3/4 as the "jazz waltz," and its near-total absence from modern pop with a couple of notable exceptions.

## Techniques

- **The genre character of 3/4 vs. 4/4, demonstrated by a direct conversion**: 3/4 is described as having an "old-fashioned, sweet, sentimental" sound, lighter and less driving than 4/4. When artists want a more upbeat/rocked-up version of a 3/4 song, they often convert it to 4/4 rather than just playing it faster — "Amazing Grace" (normally 3/4) becomes noticeably more driving and straight-ahead in the Red Hot Chili Pipers' 4/4 arrangement. This is offered as the direct explanation for why 3/4 is rare in rock, since a significant part of rock's characteristic power comes from the 4/4 meter itself.
- **Rock songs that DO use 3/4 lean into its character rather than fighting it**: Jimi Hendrix's "Manic Depression" pairs a fast 3/4 with a rock arrangement to produce a "manic, distressing" feel that suits its subject matter; Metallica's "One" starts in 4/4 and switches to 3/4 partway through.
- **Swung 3/4 = "jazz waltz"**: when the eighth notes within a 3/4 bar are swung (uneven pairs, first note longer) rather than straight, the result is described as a jazz waltz. Elliott Smith's "Waltz #2" is a swung 3/4 example; Nina Simone's version of "Mr. Bojangles" turns what was originally a straight 3/4 country song (written by Jerry Jeff Walker) into a jazz waltz purely by adding swing. **A notation convention worth keeping**: swung rhythms are often notated AS IF straight, with a small marking/symbol at the top of the score indicating the eighth notes should be performed swung — straight notation plus a swing indicator is simply easier to write and read than fully notating triplet-based swing rhythm directly.
- **3/4's other historical dance forms beyond the waltz**: the minuet and the sarabande are also 3/4-based European dance forms. The most famous minuet, "Minuet in G," was long misattributed to Bach but is now credited to Christian Petzold.
- **3/4 in pop music has become genuinely rare**, even though it was more common in the 1950s-60s ("Que Sera, Sera," Doris Day) — modern pop occasionally uses the related-but-distinct 6/8 instead. Confirmed modern 3/4 pop examples: Billie Eilish's "When the Party's Over"; Seal's "Kiss From a Rose" (which later switches into 6/8 partway through — the video cross-references its own separate video on the 3/4-vs-6/8 distinction, since the two are easily confused).
- **Genre/traditional-song home base**: waltzes (Strauss II's "The Blue Danube," originally arranged for male choir before becoming the famous orchestral version — Chopin later wrote waltzes never intended for actual dancing); folk music ("In the Pines"); ceremonial/traditional songs (Scarborough Fair, Happy Birthday, God Save the King/Star-Spangled Banner); "Edelweiss" (written for the 1959 musical *The Sound of Music* but frequently mistaken for a genuine traditional Austrian song, likely BECAUSE of its 3/4 meter reinforcing that impression).
